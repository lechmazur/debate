[P1.1] Religious liberty is strongest where the state would otherwise command a faith community's worship, doctrine, clergy, or membership; it is weakest when that community enters the ordinary marketplace and asks to be excused from rules binding everyone else. The proposition protects the first and refuses to subsidize the second: exemptions should be no wider than the religious function they genuinely protect.
[P1.2] A congregation may choose clergy and teachers of doctrine, set terms of membership and discipline, and decline rites contrary to its teaching. Those are the core functions; forcing those choices would make the state an editor of religion. But a public banquet hall, gym, fee-charging clinic, or commercial employer hiring for jobs unconnected to worship is not being asked to change a creed. It is being asked not to turn a public economic transaction into exclusion.
[P1.3] The line is between a church being a church and a church acting as a market actor.
[P1.4] Broad exemptions get the incentives backwards. They let an institution take the benefits of commercial life—customers, fees, contracts, labor markets—while shifting the costs of private doctrine onto people who never joined that faith. A same-sex couple denied a service, or a qualified employee rejected for a nonreligious job, bears a civic harm, not a theological disagreement. The more remote the activity is from worship, the less an exemption protects conscience and the more it licenses unequal citizenship.
[P1.5] The objection that line-drawing is hard, or that a religious institution does not stop being religious when it serves the public, proves too much. It would make institutional identity alone a veto over equal-treatment law and invite businesses to wrap themselves in religious language. The “tightly tied” test is not a theology exam; it asks whether government is interfering with worship, doctrine, clergy selection, or membership, or merely enforcing equal access in commerce. That boundary leaves religious freedom intact while denying a commercial veto over other people's rights.

[P2.1] The “middle ground” [C1.3] collapses because the motion tests the exemption, not an institution’s self-description. Core religious functions are not Sunday worship only: religious formation, doctrinal governance, teaching doctrine, and membership discipline can occur in schools, charities, shelters, or hospices. A principal hired to shape religious curriculum may be tightly tied; a hospice nurse or banquet-hall booker generally is not. The rule protects roles that carry faith, not mission statements that cover every employee and transaction.
[P2.2] The theologian objection [C1.4] is worse for “sincere religious mission” [C1.7]. The tight-tie test asks what the contested job or service actually does: teach doctrine, lead worship, select clergy, define membership, or offer an ordinary service. The mission test asks officials to grade sincerity, mission centrality, adequate alternatives, “truly for profit,” and “open to the public as ordinary commerce.” That ranks religious self-understandings and invites the official impression of “real religion” that minority traditions should fear.
[P2.3] Nor is identity destroyed [C1.5]. A school remains religious when it may choose those who transmit and model its faith; it is not “a private school with a chapel” because it cannot impose a creed test on every accountant, cleaner, or clinician. The students, patients, and residents in [C1.6] matter, but so do the rejected applicant and the customer told that civic access depends on another person’s religion. Equal status is not satisfied by “someone else may serve you.”
[P2.4] So “only” is justified [C1.7]: it is the limiting principle. The alternative says it defeats the public banquet hall, but a nonprofit hall can call bookings “hospitality mission,” disclaim for-profit status, and point to other venues. Once institutional sincerity sets the scope, third parties must prove their exclusion is serious enough. The proposition keeps the burden where it belongs: show a tight connection to a core religious function, or follow the same equal-treatment rules in broad commercial activity.
