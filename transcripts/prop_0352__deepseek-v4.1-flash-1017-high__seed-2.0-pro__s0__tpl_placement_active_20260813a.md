[P1.1] The claim that permanent metro concentration is inevitable is not an observation; it is a policy choice dressed as geography. Governments have spent decades concentrating transport, universities, research money, public employment, and tax capacity in big cities—then treated the migration that follows as proof that only big cities can work. That is circular. Place-blind policy is not neutral; it reinforces the places that already have density, institutions, and infrastructure.
[P1.2] Concentration also has costs the other side must price. Major metros face housing shortages, congestion, long commutes, strained transit and water systems, and enormous infrastructure bills. Rural regions lose young people, schools, clinics, and tax base in a spiral that makes remaining residents pay more for less. That is not efficiency; it is a transfer of opportunity from places with underused assets to places already overheating.
[P1.3] When a factory or hospital leaves a small town, the loss is not just local; it is a national waste of roads, housing, and civic infrastructure already built.
[P1.4] The levers are ordinary, not utopian: universal broadband and mobile coverage; regional universities and vocational training; childcare and healthcare staffing; procurement anchored in local firms; transport linking towns to one another and to cities; and housing supply in successful small towns. These are not subsidies to failure; they are public goods that markets underprovide in low-density places. A town with a hospital, reliable broadband, and a rail link has a future; the same town after the clinic closes and the bus route is cut does not.
[P1.5] The difference is often administrative, not geographic.
[P1.6] PRO does not require saving every hamlet or freezing a region in amber. Viability means jobs, services, connectivity, and a realistic path for the next generation—not nostalgia. The real question is whether “do nothing and call it inevitable” is acceptable. It is not.
[P1.7] Permanent concentration is a choice, and governments should do more to make smaller places viable.
