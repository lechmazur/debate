The proposition asks only that we keep the rule every liberal democracy already applies to cash, safes, and encryption: tools are legal; crimes are illegal. Punish conduct, not capability.
[P1.2] Three pillars carry this case.
[P1.3] First, principle. Due process requires that the state prove wrongdoing before stripping rights. A default-illegal regime inverts this: every citizen becomes a suspect who must seek permission to hold their own assets. We do not require licenses to keep cash under a mattress or to lock a diary, even though both can hide crimes.
[P1.4] Self-custody is the digital version of the same liberty, and abandoning the conduct-based standard here sets a precedent that swallows encryption, private messaging, and ultimately any tool the state finds inconvenient.
[P1.5] Second, consequences. Forcing everyone into custodial intermediaries concentrates risk catastrophically. FTX, Celsius, and Mt. Gox vaporized billions of ordinary people's savings precisely because users couldn't hold their own keys. Self-custody is also a lifeline where institutions fail: dissidents in authoritarian states, Afghan women locked out of banking, families fleeing hyperinflation.
[P1.6] A ban-by-default punishes the world's most vulnerable to inconvenience its least competent criminals.
[P1.7] Third, futility. Wallet software is open-source code anyone can copy; a private key can be twelve memorized words. Criminals—who already break laws—will keep using them, while law-abiding users lose protection and law enforcement loses the transparent public blockchain trail that has repeatedly cracked major cases, from the Bitfinex hack recovery onward. Prohibition trades visible, traceable activity for a black market.
[P1.8] The motion still allows aggressive enforcement: wallets tied to specific crimes can be seized, traced, and prosecuted. CON must defend something far stranger—presumptive guilt for owning a key. That is the clash, and it decides this debate.
