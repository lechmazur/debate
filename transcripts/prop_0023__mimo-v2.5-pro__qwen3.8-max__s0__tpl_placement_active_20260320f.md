[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a familiar problem for a familiar legal box. FTX’s collapse shows the need for strict consumer protection, but it does not show that securities exchange or broker-dealer regulation is the right tool. The failure was fraud, commingling, and fake audit—not the absence of a securities license. Matching orders and holding balances is not unique to securities; currency exchanges and commodity venues do the same.
[C1.2] Crypto platforms do perform functions that deserve regulation: custody, matching, price discovery, leverage. But securities law is not a generic investor-protection code. It is a specialized system built around issuers, disclosure obligations, registered securities, clearing agencies, and ownership records. Many of the largest traded crypto assets have no issuer whose disclosures can be policed, settle on public networks, and can be held directly by users.
[C1.3] A broker-dealer regime designed around street-name custody and centralized clearing does not simply snap onto token transfers, staking, or smart-contract settlement. Where assets are marketed as investment contracts, securities rules can apply; that is different from making securities law the default for most platforms.
[C1.4] The better answer is not light supervision. It is a distinct, stringent category: mandatory segregation of customer assets, proof of reserves and liabilities, capital and liquidity requirements, anti-manipulation surveillance, cybersecurity standards, licensing for custodians and venues, and clear liability for fraud. Those protections can be imposed directly, without pretending every listed token is a security or every exchange is a stock market.
[C1.5] Forcing most platforms into securities regulation would likely produce compliance theater, litigation-driven uncertainty, and migration offshore or into less transparent decentralized venues. It would also waste enforcement resources on classification fights instead of the actual harms: misuse of customer funds, wash trading, insider dealing, and false disclosures.
[C1.6] The real choice is not between investor protection and lawlessness. It is between fit-for-purpose rules and borrowing an old framework that does not map.
