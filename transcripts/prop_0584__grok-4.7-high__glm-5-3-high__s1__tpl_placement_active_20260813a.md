[P1.1] Price stability is not a luxury central banks can set aside when the labor market looks fragile. Over the next three years they should keep inflation near target even if that means a modest rise in unemployment. Tolerating an overshoot to protect jobs today raises the odds of a larger employment loss tomorrow and a lasting cut to real incomes in between.
[P1.2] Above-target inflation does not stay neatly contained. Once households and firms stop believing the target will be met, wages and prices chase each other. People without strong bargaining power lose ground at once: rents, groceries, and energy move before pay does. A modest rise in unemployment is painful, but it is finite, visible, and partly cushioned by insurance and other stabilizers.
[P1.3] Entrenched inflation is a weekly tax with no automatic offset, and bringing it down later usually destroys more jobs than preventing it now.
[P1.4] Delay is what turns a manageable tradeoff into a bad one. In the 1970s, treating inflation as secondary left expectations unanchored, and the later correction was far sharper than an earlier, smaller tightening would have been. The recent surge taught the same lesson faster. Assurances that prices would fade on their own did not anchor behavior.
[P1.5] The longer policy waited, the higher rates had to climb, and the narrower the path back to target without a harder landing.
[P1.6] Over three years, credibility is the difference between a small adjustment and a large one. A central bank known to defend the target can often restore price stability with less cumulative slack than one markets expect to flinch at the first rise in unemployment. Fiscal policy can support displaced workers. Monetary policy cannot abandon its anchor and still deliver stable real wages or durable jobs.
[P1.7] Keeping inflation near target is how a modest employment cost stays modest.

If the curve is flat, forcing unemployment higher buys very little inflation reduction at a very high human cost.
[C1.5] You would be paying full price for a discount.
[C1.6] Third, the credibility argument is reversible. The 1970s taught central banks to fear looking soft on inflation. A bank that ignores its employment mandate every time the two goals brush against each other has a credibility problem too — with the public whose institution it is.
[C1.7] Finally, "modest" is a promise no central banker can keep. Tightening works through layoffs; nobody sets the dial. The honest position over three years is symmetric: neither target gets automatic priority. That is what I defend.
