We don't ask exhausted new parents to read a consent brochure and proactively request a PKU test; if we did, some children would slip through, suffer irreversible brain damage, and die from conditions a cheap early diet or hormone could have prevented. [P1.2] Two design choices carry my whole case, and I want the debate to live here.
[P1.3] First, opt-out versus opt-in is a question about defaults, and defaults decide outcomes. Opt-in does not maximize freedom—it maximizes inertia and inequality. Opt-out flips the burden so the default protects the child, while a parent who genuinely objects can still refuse with one signature.
[P1.4] Autonomy is fully preserved; what changes is who bears the cost of doing nothing.
[P1.5] Second, the clause "limited to conditions with effective early interventions" is not decoration—it is the firewall against every scary objection my opponent will raise. [P1.6] The scope is defined by a single test: would acting now, in infancy, concretely help this child? If yes, default in. If no, it isn't on the panel.
[P1.7] So the real comparison is stark. Opt-in predictably leaves some treatable babies undiagnosed until symptoms—meaning harm—appear. Opt-out catches them while preserving the right to say no.
