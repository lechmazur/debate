[P1.1] The case for block grants rests on a simple insight: you cannot schedule a breakthrough. Yet that is precisely what short-cycle competitive grants attempt to do—compress scientific discovery into three-year proposal cycles, measure progress in quarterly reports, and reward only work that can be described in language a review panel already expects to hear. Fundamental science cannot thrive under these conditions.
[P1.2] Consider what actually produces transformative knowledge. The transistor emerged not from a targeted research program but from years of patient exploration of quantum phenomena. The basis of modern genomics was laid not because anyone funded a project to map human DNA, but because sustained curiosity about molecular inheritance persisted long enough to outlast skepticism. These outcomes required something competitive grants structurally cannot provide: the assurance that tomorrow's work can build on today's without fear of losing funding mid-discovery.
[P1.3] Block grants solve this problem directly. When laboratories receive stable, multi-year support, researchers stop spending half their time defending their existence to committees and start doing science. Teams form that can pursue problems requiring five, ten, fifteen years of accumulated effort—problems that by definition cannot fit inside a three-year grant cycle. The overhead of perpetual proposal writing disappears.
[P1.4] Junior researchers can be trained properly instead of weaponized into grant-writing machines before they have learned anything worth discovering.
[P1.5] Yes, this means less auditability per dollar. I concede that directly. But the alternative—constant recompetition—creates its own costs that accountability metrics ignore: the research agenda skews toward the fundable rather than the important, talented people leave science entirely, and genuinely novel work gets strangled for failing to predict outcomes in advance.
[P1.6] The accountability argument assumes we know how to measure scientific progress. We do not. What we know is that patient, sustained investment has repeatedly produced the discoveries that changed everything. The burden is on the competitive model to explain why it should be trusted to deliver what history says it cannot.

Consider the 1989 cold fusion announcement: if the researchers involved had secured a 15-year unaccountable block grant, we would have poured hundreds of millions of dollars into a phenomenon debunked by follow-up experiments within months, with no mechanism to cut losses or redirect funds to more promising work.
[C1.2] The “less accountability” PRO dismisses is the exact safeguard that stops such wasteful spending.
[C1.3] PRO’s nostalgic appeals to past breakthroughs like the transistor and genomics also fall apart under scrutiny. The transistor was invented at Bell Labs, which was funded by AT&T but held to strict standards of scientific and commercial value, not given a blank check to pursue any whim for a decade with no oversight. The Human Genome Project, the foundation of modern genomics, was a targeted initiative with clear public milestones and regular accountability checks, not an unaccountable curiosity-driven block grant.
[C1.4] PRO’s own historical examples prove that transformative work does not require forfeiting all accountability, only relief from excessive short-cycle pressure.
[C1.5] Finally, PRO’s claim that competitive grants weaponize junior researchers ignores the far more perverse incentives of unaccountable block grants. Without regular review, funding concentrates almost exclusively in large, prestigious institutions with the clout to secure multi-year block awards, starving early-career researchers, smaller schools, and underfunded fields of resources. Competitive project grants, for all their flaws, at least let individual researchers compete on the merits of their ideas, rather than their institution’s lobbying power. PRO’s model would entrench the very inequities in research funding they claim to oppose, all while eliminating the guardrails that keep public investment productive.
