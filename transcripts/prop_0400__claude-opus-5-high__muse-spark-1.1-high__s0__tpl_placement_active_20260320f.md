The oldest principle of legitimate coercion is that someone must be answerable for it. [C1.1] The promise is a person who can answer "why me?" The reality a legal right to a human decision-maker delivers is slower, more biased, less accountable decisions.
[C1.2] Human discretion in benefits, immigration, and criminal risk is not a gold standard. Study after study of asylum officers, disability examiners, and sentencing judges shows outcomes shift with time of day, local politics, and implicit bias, with massive unexplained variation between adjudicators in the same office. A human can say "I considered hardship" while hiding that they did not. An automated rule, however imperfect, writes its threshold down where it can be tested, litigated, and fixed.
[C1.3] The catalogue of scandals cited proves the opposite point. Robodebt, Michigan, and the Dutch childcare cases were not rogue models escaping human control; humans wrote the unlawful income-averaging rule, set the fraud score, and instructed staff not to intervene. Adding a legal requirement that a clerk click "approved" would have changed nothing except to launder responsibility. Accountability comes from system-level disclosure, logging, and independent audit, not from the fiction that one low-level signatory owns the outcome.
[C1.4] And the mandate has brutal tradeoffs. Agencies decide millions of cases annually. If every adverse decision must wait for human drafting and review, queues lengthen or approvals slow to fund the headcount. For a family facing eviction while waiting on benefits, or a worker flagged for overpayment, delay is the harm.
[C1.5] Worse, when overworked staff must sign hundreds a day, they rubber-stamp — automation bias made law.
[C1.6] Protect people with what works: plain-language reasons, access to data and code, and a genuine right to appeal to a human when they dispute the outcome. That secures contestability without mandating inferior, slower first-instance human decisions for everyone.
