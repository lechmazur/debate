[P1.1] Europe faces a two-front crisis: a resurgent Russia on its eastern flank and a fracturing social contract at home. Voluntary forces and small professional armies cannot solve either. Reintroducing or expanding mandatory national service is the most direct, fastest, and most cost-effective instrument available to European governments to rebuild defense readiness and social cohesion in a single policy stroke.
[P1.2] Consider the defense gap. After three years of war in Ukraine, most European NATO members still cannot field the mass that serious deterrence requires. Reservists, not active divisions, are the backbone of any continental conventional defense. Mandatory service of even six to twelve months generates, over a decade, a trained reserve pool numbering in the hundreds of thousands per country.
[P1.3] Sweden's reactivation of conscription in 2017, Norway's later expansion, and Germany's revived debate reflect a recognition that market-based recruitment cannot deliver numbers or speed when the threat is continental. A conscript trained today is a sergeant in five years and a platoon leader in ten — capability that money alone cannot buy in time.
[P1.4] Now the social case, which the opposition treats as window-dressing but is in fact load-bearing. Modern European societies are stratified by class, region, ethnicity, and increasingly by ideology. Schools are segregated by neighborhood; universities by background. National service forces citizens into shared institutions, shared hardship, and shared responsibility.
[P1.5] The Finnish and Swiss models are not romantic relics; they are living evidence that mixed units, common standards, and uniform obligation produce measurable trust across social lines. France's emerging Service National Universel applies exactly this logic to a generation that otherwise never leaves its information bubble.
[P1.6] The real choice is not between conscription and an ideal volunteer professional force. That force does not exist and will not exist in time. The choice is between mandatory service and a hollow army combined with a society that has stopped asking its young to serve one another. That is the status quo Europe can no longer afford.

[C1.1] Mandatory national service fails on both defense and cohesion because it substitutes quantity for quality and compulsion for commitment. European armies already struggle with retention and specialization; flooding units with short-term conscripts adds bodies that require constant retraining and supervision, diluting the professionalism needed for modern combined-arms operations against a peer adversary.
[C1.2] A six-to-twelve-month term produces soldiers who exit just as they become marginally useful, leaving units with high turnover and weak unit cohesion. The same mechanism that supposedly builds a reserve pool simultaneously drains active-duty training capacity and equipment stocks. Countries that have tried to expand conscription rapidly have faced exactly these bottlenecks: higher maintenance costs, slower deployment readiness, and difficulty attracting the technical specialists required for drones, cyber, and precision fires. Those gaps cannot be fixed by adding more reluctant teenagers.
[C1.3] On the social side, forced mixing does not manufacture trust when the underlying incentives remain misaligned. When service is universal in theory but selectively enforced in practice, resentment concentrates among the groups that cannot easily evade it. Mixed barracks under conditions of low motivation often reproduce existing social hierarchies rather than erase them, with stronger recruits dominating informal structures and weaker ones disengaging. Historical patterns in several European states show spikes in absenteeism, disciplinary incidents, and post-service political polarization, not the broad trust gains claimed.
[C1.4] Voluntary incentives, targeted reserves, and deeper NATO integration address readiness shortfalls without the fiscal drag and social friction that mandatory programs reliably generate. The proposition offers a blunt instrument for problems that require precision.

[C2.2] The complementarity claim between mass conscripts and specialist tracks collapses in practice. Money spent on barracks, uniforms, and basic weapons for short-term service reduces funds available for the targeted bonuses and extended contracts required to retain drone operators or cyber technicians. Sweden’s mixed system still shows persistent shortfalls in precisely those technical fields the proponent says conscription will free specialists to fill.

Where enforcement is stricter, surveys from existing programs record elevated distrust toward institutions among those compelled to serve while peers evade.
[C2.4] Voluntary recruitment and NATO pooling remain the higher-leverage route because they concentrate resources on committed personnel and shared command structures rather than dispersing them across reluctant cohorts. The proposition’s instrument generates the very readiness drag and social friction it claims to cure.
