[P1.1] Cities are running out of surface. Every major metro area has already claimed its usable ground-level real estate for buildings, roads, and sidewalks. When a country needs a new transit line, a new sewer trunk, or a new fiber corridor through a dense downtown, there is often no surface path left that doesn't mean demolishing homes, businesses, or historic structures. Tunneling is not a lifestyle preference at that point—it is the only physically available corridor.
[P1.2] That is the case this debate should turn on: not whether tunneling is cheap, but whether the alternative is even available at a tolerable cost.
[P1.3] Consider what surface construction actually costs beyond the contractor's invoice. Tearing up a street to lay a water main shuts down businesses for months, chokes traffic for a metro area of millions, and triggers years of eminent-domain fights when it touches a single occupied building. These costs are real, they are large, and they are almost entirely absent from the "boring is expensive" comparison CON will run. Boring costs are visible, budgeted, and one-time.
[P1.4] Surface disruption costs are diffuse, recurring, and borne by everyone except the line item that gets criticized in a budget hearing. That asymmetry is exactly why boring looks expensive in isolation and is actually the cheaper path once you count what it prevents.
[P1.5] There's also a compounding return CON's framing ignores: burying utilities and transit doesn't just avoid one disruption, it avoids the next one, and the next, for decades. A power line buried once stops going down in every storm. A transit tunnel doesn't need its right-of-way re-fought every time the city wants to widen a road. And the surface freed up—medians, old rail corridors, disused roadway—can be converted into housing, parks, or pedestrian space that raises land value and tax revenue, which is real money that offsets the capital outlay over the tunnel's lifespan.
[P1.6] High upfront boring costs are the price of admission for a resource—usable urban surface—that no longer exists to buy any other way.
