[P1.1] The fight over devices is already lost if it is fought app by app. Platforms change faster than family rules. A ban on one feed simply moves attention to the next. Controls lag the product, and children learn the workaround before the school note arrives.
[P1.2] Schools and pediatricians who hand parents a rotating hit list are asking families to win a game the industry redesigns every season.
[P1.3] Sleep, boredom, and free play are the better frame because they name what screens displace, and they do not expire when the next app launches. A child asleep by a set hour cannot scroll through it. A child bored long enough to invent a game has already practiced the skill feeds cannot sell. A child with daily free play has a competing claim on time that no content filter has to enforce minute by minute.
[P1.4] These are not soft substitutes for limits. They are the limits, stated as developmental goods rather than as a catalog of forbidden software.
[P1.5] That framing matches what schools and pediatricians can actually do. They cannot police every household app store. They can set one shared expectation: protected sleep, regular unstructured time, and play that is not mediated by a screen. Parents then get a coherent standard instead of a dozen skirmishes.
[P1.6] The question shifts from “Did you delete that app?” to “Was there an hour today with nothing scheduled and nothing glowing?” The second is harder to game and easier to keep across years.
[P1.7] App-by-app advice also trains the wrong attention. It makes the device the center of family life and turns adults into content moderators. Each new rule feels arbitrary because the target keeps moving. Goals anchored in rest and play apply even on days with no phone.
[P1.8] They build impulse control, frustration tolerance, and self-direction—the capacities screen rules silently assume and rarely create.
[P1.9] Harmful products still deserve scrutiny. But the core of digital wellbeing is a life in which devices do not colonize rest, idleness, or play. Fight for those three, and the app battles shrink. [clipped]

[C1.1] Sleep, boredom, and free play are real goods. The claim at issue is stronger: that they should replace app-level guidance as the core frame. That claim fails for three reasons.
[C1.2] First, the proposed goals do not eliminate device battles; they just stop naming them. Getting a child asleep by nine means taking the phone at nine. Protecting free play means saying no to the game that expands to fill it. The parent still stands in the doorway negotiating, except now the clinician has left them without vocabulary for what the device is doing.
[C1.3] "Was there an hour with nothing glowing?" sounds clean until the child asks which app, on which device, at which time — and the adult has been told those questions are the wrong ones.
[C1.4] Second, these goals cover the harms of displacement and say almost nothing about harms of content and design. A child who sleeps nine hours, suffers genuine boredom daily, and plays outside can still be messaged by an adult stranger on a game's chat, served a self-harm feed by an algorithm optimized for engagement, or nudged into spending by a loot mechanic. Sleep does not interrupt any of those. The proposition treats the screen's damage as a time problem, when a large share of the worst damage is a design and content problem.
[C1.5] Telling parents to stop being "content moderators," as the proposition does [P1.7], tells them to stop doing exactly the job that keeps kids safe online.
[C1.6] Third, the frame is unfalsifiable in the wrong hands. "Regular unstructured time" and "daily free play" let a school send home a poster and call the work done, while the family still faces a specific child asking about a specific app tonight. Concrete guidance — which features to disable, which chats to supervise, which apps to postpone — is actionable at the dinner table. Broad goals are actionable in a policy document.
[C1.7] Framing around sleep, boredom, and play subtracts the only tools aimed at the harms that occur while the child is awake, awake, and awake.
