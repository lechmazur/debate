[P1.1] The modern European social model is facing a quiet crisis of erosion. Large companies increasingly bypass traditional employment structures by routing their labor through digital platforms and complex, multi-layered subcontracting chains. This is not an innocent evolution of efficiency; it is a systematic strategy of cost-shifting. By converting standard employees into "independent contractors" or pushing them down a chain of intermediaries, large firms externalize their labor risks and hollow out the funding of our public safety nets.
[P1.2] To protect workers and sustain the European welfare state, the EU must require these large corporations to pay a higher share of social contributions when they choose to use these fractured labor models.
[P1.3] This proposal establishes a crucial economic mechanism: it internalizes the social costs of precarious labor. Currently, when a large firm relies on platform workers or endless subcontracting, it avoids paying its fair share of payroll taxes, pensions, and healthcare contributions. The public purse is left to pick up the pieces when these workers face illness, injury, or retirement. Imposing a higher social contribution rate on companies utilizing these arrangements forces them to pay for the structural instability they introduce to the workforce.
[P1.4] Furthermore, this policy restores market fairness. Right now, ethical businesses that hire workers directly and pay full social benefits are punished with a competitive disadvantage. This creates a race to the bottom. Levying higher contributions on platform and subcontracting models removes the artificial financial incentive to gig-ify and fragment labor.
[P1.5] It ensures that competition in the single market is based on productivity and innovation, not on who can exploit regulatory arbitrage most effectively.
[P1.6] If a large company chooses a fragmented labor model for its own flexibility, it must pay the true price of that model. We must secure the financial foundations of our societies and ensure corporate giants pay their fair share.

[C1.1] The proposition asks Europe to tax precarity instead of ending it. That single confusion is enough to vote CON.
[C1.2] If platform and subcontracted workers are genuinely misclassified employees, the correct response is to classify them as employees and collect the normal social contributions — which is precisely what the EU's Platform Work Directive already moves toward through its presumption of employment. A "higher share" surcharge does the opposite: it legitimizes the fragmented model by putting a price tag on it. Large firms pay the levy, the worker stays a contractor with no sick leave, no unemployment insurance, no pension accrual in their own name, and the state collects a check instead of giving the worker rights.
[C1.3] That is not protection. It is indulgences for precarity.
[C1.4] Second, the policy will hit the people PRO claims to defend. Faced with a punitive contribution rate, a large company has three rational responses: bring work in-house and terminate the platform and subcontractor relationships entirely; shift contracts to non-EU platforms and providers; or pass the cost down the chain as lower piece rates and squeezed subcontractor margins. Small and mid-sized subcontractors — the backbone of European industry, from cleaning firms in Warsaw to logistics SMEs in Lombardy — lose their largest clients overnight.
[C1.5] Gig workers who chose flexibility lose the gigs. The "ethical" direct-hire firm PRO romanticizes is often a multinational; the subcontractor is the local SME.
[C1.6] Third, the instrument is crude and gameable. "Large company" thresholds invite corporate restructuring. Defining which subcontracting chains trigger the surcharge — IT services? construction? cross-border posting? — creates years of litigation and arbitrage that favor exactly the sophisticated players PRO wants to punish.
[C1.7] The clash is simple. PRO wants to monetize a problem. CON wants to solve it. If a worker deserves employee protections, give them employee status.
[C1.8] If a subcontractor is legitimate, do not punish the relationship. A surcharge does neither job well, and damages workers and SMEs in the process.

[P2.5] Third, defending subcontracting under the guise of protecting SMEs [C1.4] misses the predatory nature of the status quo. By penalizing this cost-shifting, we protect legitimate SMEs from being forced into a race to the bottom just to win contracts.
