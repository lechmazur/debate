[C1.1] The proposition sounds like common sense—cap arrivals when costs outweigh benefits. But hiding inside that "simple math" is a mechanism too vague to apply honestly and too blunt to apply well.
[C1.2] Start with the trigger itself. Who determines when crowding, pollution, and housing pressure "outweigh" revenue gains? These variables resist objective measurement. Crowding is subjective; pollution models differ by methodology; housing pressure has multiple competing drivers.
[C1.3] Whoever controls that determination controls the city's economy. The history of tourism regulation shows such powers get captured—by existing hoteliers wanting to eliminate day-tripper competition, by wealthier long-term residents who prefer quieter streets, or by politicians seeking visible action that avoids harder structural reform. A cap justified as protecting residents easily becomes a cartel protecting incumbents. The proposition offers no mechanism to prevent this.
[C1.4] Then consider the instrument. Even granting that intervention is warranted, a hard arrival cap is the crudest possible response. It forgoes revenue entirely rather than extracting it more efficiently. Congestion pricing, port arrival fees, time-dispersal requirements, and designated routing all reduce harm while retaining economic participation.
[C1.5] A cap doesn't solve the underlying problem; it transfers visitors—and their spending—to a competitor city that declines to cap. The problem moves; the host city simply loses.
[C1.6] Finally, the housing displacement argument is misattributed. Cruise day-trippers, by definition, do not rent apartments. The forces converting residential units to tourist accommodation are short-term rental platforms—a categorically different phenomenon. PRO has bundled three distinct problems and proposed one blunt instrument that addresses, at best, one of them.
[C1.7] The residents PRO claims to protect include hospitality workers, port employees, and street vendors whose livelihoods depend on volume. Caps eliminate that volume without solving a single structural problem. The motion's logic is emotionally satisfying but analytically careless—and when policy is designed around emotion rather than precision, the people with the least political power pay the price.
