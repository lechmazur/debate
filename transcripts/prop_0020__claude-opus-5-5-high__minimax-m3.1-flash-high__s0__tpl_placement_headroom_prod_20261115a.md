[P1.1] Strength is not measured by symbols. A union is strong when its members prosper, trust one another, and can absorb shocks without turning on each other. By that standard, the single currency has been the EU's most costly self-inflicted wound.
[P1.2] Start with the design flaw. The euro gave Germany, Greece, Finland and Portugal one interest rate and one exchange rate, while leaving them without a shared budget, without easy labour mobility, and without automatic transfers between regions. That is a currency built for a country that does not exist. In the 2000s, rates suited to a sluggish Germany fuelled credit bubbles in Spain and Ireland.
[P1.3] When the bubbles burst, the countries in trouble could neither devalue nor cut rates. The only tool left was "internal devaluation": crushing wages and jobs until costs fell.
[P1.4] The results were brutal. Greece lost roughly a quarter of its economy. Youth unemployment in Spain and Greece hovered around half for years. Italy has barely grown in two decades of euro membership.
[P1.5] That is not a temporary bump; it is a lost generation inside the world's richest bloc.
[P1.6] Then count the political damage. The crisis turned partners into creditors and debtors. Germans were told they were bankrolling lazy southerners; Greeks saw Berlin and the Troika dictating their pensions. Populist and anti-EU movements surged across the continent.
[P1.7] Resentment that a normal recession would never have produced became a permanent fault line.
[P1.8] Now the counterfactual. Poland, keeping the złoty, let its currency fall in 2009 and was the only EU economy to avoid recession. Sweden kept the krona and prospered. Without the euro, Southern Europe would have adjusted through exchange rates, not mass unemployment, and the EU would have kept its single market and political project without the bitter north-south war.
[P1.9] The euro's defenders will cite stability. But the ECB's heroics were firefighting a blaze the currency itself started.

[C1.1] PRO mistakes the eurozone crisis for proof that the euro caused Europe’s weakness. But property booms, reckless banking, and unsustainable public promises produced that crisis; a common currency did not invent those incentives. Moreover, “internal devaluation” was painful adjustment without default, capital controls, or a break-up of the single market. Countries using floating currencies also suffered banking collapses and fiscal crises.
[C1.2] The euro cannot bear the exclusive blame PRO assigns it.
[C1.3] The euro’s durable benefits are precisely what make today’s EU more cohesive. It eliminated exchange-rate risk inside the single market, reduced conversion and hedging costs, and supported deeper trade and investment. It also gave smaller members access to a credible monetary framework. Governments retained fiscal policy while monetary policy became shared.
[C1.4] That division of labour helped preserve economic integration through repeated crises rather than turning every shock into a contest over exchange rates.
[C1.5] PRO’s counterfactual requires national currencies to remain stable while governments surrender monetary autonomy. That is not a free lunch. During the financial crisis, depreciation could have raised import prices and foreign-currency debt burdens, while competitive devaluations could have undermined confidence in the single market. A currency union prevents that beggar-thy-neighbour spiral.
[C1.6] It also gave European leaders incentives to build common crisis institutions, including stronger bank supervision, common fiscal rules, and shared financing mechanisms.
[C1.7] Finally, abolition now would not restore a tidy pre-euro Europe; it would unwind contracts, redenominate debts, and reopen fights over central-bank assets. The EU is stronger with the euro because it has converted monetary divergence into rules and institutions, even if those rules were born in conflict. The relevant comparison is not an imagined Europe of flawless national policies, but the more integrated, credible, and shock-resistant union the currency helped create.
