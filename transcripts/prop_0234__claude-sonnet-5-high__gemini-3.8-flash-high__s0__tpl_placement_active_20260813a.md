[C1.1] The proposition operates on a comforting fiction: that complex human labor can be standardized like factory parts on a shelf. But mandating that large employers publish rigid pay bands and promotion rates by role does not liberate workers; it bureaucratizes compensation, compresses wages, and penalizes the very ambition and merit that drive individual career mobility.
[C1.2] First, transparency mandates inevitably turn pay bands into pay ceilings. When every band is public and heavily scrutinized, employers face immense pressure to minimize internal friction and compliance liability. The natural corporate response is not raising everyone to the top of the bracket, but wage compression. Companies become unwilling to offer premium compensation to star performers, match outside retention offers, or reward specialized, hybrid skills that defy neat categorization.
[C1.3] Instead of lifting the floor, rigid bands depress the ceiling, converting dynamic private-sector compensation into a sluggish civil-service step-ladder.
[C1.4] Second, mandating promotion rates by role introduces perverse structural incentives. Professional progression is not linear, yet standardizing promotion metrics treats human careers like an assembly line. When employers must report these figures, they will inevitably game them: creating artificial, hyper-specific job titles to fragment reporting pools, or worse, refusing to take hiring risks. A hiring manager will hesitate to take a chance on a non-traditional applicant or a worker returning from a career break if a slower ramp-up threatens to drag down the published promotion rate for that title.
[C1.5] Finally, PRO dismisses managerial flexibility as mere cover for bad faith and exploitation. But genuine flexibility is how organizations reward differentiated effort, initiative, and unique value. Two employees sharing the title of "Senior Analyst" or "Software Engineer" rarely deliver identical output. Forcing employers to fit human talent into standardized brackets denies the fundamental heterogeneity of modern work.
[C1.6] Replacing managerial discretion with rigid state-mandated metrics does not produce fairness. It trades meritocracy for bureaucratic stagnation, hurting the very workers who outperform the baseline.
