[P1.1] When a state has massacred, enslaved, or stolen children, its first obligation is not to its treasury but to truth. A formal apology is the official record correcting itself - saying we did this, it was wrong, and you were wronged. Without that, victims die waiting for acknowledgement, descendants inherit denial, and the public is taught a lie. A democracy that will not tell the truth about its own past cannot claim legitimacy in the present.
[P1.2] The case for silence reduces the state to a corporation managing liability. Legal advisers are paid to see risk; they are not paid to weigh reconciliation, international standing, or moral integrity. Taking their warning as a veto means letting risk managers decide national conscience.
[P1.3] More importantly, the warning that an apology could increase compensation claims is not an argument against apology. If claims become more credible because the state finally admits what it did, that means the claims were just. Avoiding an apology to avoid paying is deliberate injustice. You are protecting today's budget by perpetuating yesterday's theft.
[P1.4] In many systems an apology is not itself a binding admission of legal liability, but even where exposure does rise, that is appropriate exposure. Current citizens did not commit the atrocity, but they inherited the state - its borders, its institutions, its wealth, and its debts. You cannot claim the assets and disown the liabilities.
[P1.5] Far from weakening the state, apologies strengthen it. Germany's post-Holocaust atonement, Canada's apology to Indigenous residential school survivors, Australia's to the Stolen Generations - none erased legal or political fights, but all unblocked negotiations, education, and relationships that denial had frozen for decades. Silence is far more expensive: endless litigation, protest, alienation, and a reputation as a state that must be forced to admit facts.
[P1.6] States should apologize because justice comes before thrift, because just compensation is not a cost to be avoided, and because a country that owns its history is more stable, not less.
