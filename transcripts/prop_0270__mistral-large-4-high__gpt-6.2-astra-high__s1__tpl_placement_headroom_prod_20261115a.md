[P1.1] Parents are losing a rigged game. The result is whack-a-mole: block TikTok, discover BeReal; restrict YouTube, find the next dopamine loop. This approach treats symptoms while ignoring the condition driving children to screens in the first place, and it burns out caregivers who sense they can never keep up.
[P1.2] The alternative is to frame digital wellbeing around what screens displace: sleep, boredom, and unstructured play. These are not luxuries but developmental necessities. Sleep regulates mood and attention; unstructured play builds creativity and emotional resilience; boredom—the uncomfortable space where the mind wanders—is where problem-solving and self-regulation are actually learned. When a child reaches for a device at 10 p.m., it is often not because the app is irresistible but because there is nothing else to do, or exhaustion has made scrolling the path of least resistance.
[P1.3] A child who has learned to tolerate boredom and play outside has internal resources that no parental control app can install.
[P1.4] Schools and pediatricians should therefore set goals that build alternatives rather than erect barriers. A pediatrician who asks, "Is your child getting adequate sleep and daily unstructured play?" offers a sustainable standard that survives the next app update. A school that protects recess and limits homework overload attacks the boredom that fuels compulsive checking. These institutions have the authority to shift cultural norms, not just negotiate nightly truces over screen time.
[P1.5] Telling parents to fight device battles one app at a time asks them to out-engineer Silicon Valley. Framing sleep, boredom, and free play as the core goals gives families something concrete to build toward, and gives children an affirmative reason to put the device down.
