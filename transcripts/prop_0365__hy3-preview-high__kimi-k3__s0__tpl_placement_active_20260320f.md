[P1.1] For a working parent with two kids, a trip to the local multiplex to see the latest blockbuster used to mean choosing a convenient 7 PM showtime at a standard price. Today, that same parent is far more likely to find that 7 PM slot is reserved for a premium large-format (PLF) screening—IMAX, Dolby Cinema, or another upcharged format that adds $6 to $10 per ticket. The standard-price alternatives are pushed to 11 AM matinees or 11 PM late shows, times that are impossible for most families with school or work obligations.
[P1.2] This is the direct harm of letting PLF screens dominate blockbuster scheduling: ordinary moviegoers are priced out of convenient, affordable access to the films they want to see.
[P1.3] PLF formats are a fine optional add-on, but they are not a replacement for standard screenings. When theaters allocate the majority of prime blockbuster showtimes to these higher-margin formats, they are making a deliberate choice to prioritize short-term per-ticket profits over broad audience access. A standard adult ticket in most U.S. markets runs roughly $10 to $14; a PLF ticket pushes that to $16 to $24. For a family of four, that is an extra $24 to $40 per outing—a cost that turns a routine monthly treat into a budget-busting luxury.
[P1.4] For many low- and middle-income households, the math is simple: if there are no convenient standard-price showtimes, they skip the theater entirely.
[P1.5] This is not an argument against PLF existing. It is an argument against letting PLF dominate scheduling to the point that standard screenings become scarce. Theaters have a baseline obligation to keep core moviegoing accessible to the general public, not just to customers willing to pay a premium for extra bells and whistles. When scheduling choices violate that obligation, they must be rejected.

When an opening-weekend crowd overwhelmingly picks the premium room, scheduling a half-empty standard auditorium in the same slot would be a business suicide note dressed up as fairness.
[C1.2] That demand matters because exhibition economics are brutal. Studios take the lion's share of ticket revenue in a blockbuster's opening weeks—exactly when PLF dominates. The premium upcharge is one of the few streams theaters actually keep, and it subsidizes everything else: the matinees, the discount Tuesdays, the small films on screens three and four, the lights staying on at all. Strip it away with a scheduling mandate and the likely result isn't more cheap 7 PM shows; it's closed locations and higher standard prices.
[C1.3] The family PRO worries about gains nothing when their multiplex becomes a husk.
[C1.4] The cross-subsidy point is decisive. Price discrimination—charging enthusiasts more for the deluxe experience—is what keeps the baseline product affordable. [C1.5] And the harm itself is overstated. A standard-price seat to the same film exists—at matinees, on weeknights, on discount days, a week or two later when PLF screens rotate to the next release. What PRO calls being "priced out" is really a convenience premium on the single most demanded slot: opening weekend, prime time. That is not an access violation; it is how every scarce, desirable thing gets rationed.
[C1.6] The real threat to ordinary moviegoers isn't the IMAX upcharge. It's a business model so strangled that there's no theater left to walk into.
