[P1.1] Housing affordability is, at its core, a supply problem. When more people want homes than homes exist, prices rise. The proposition before us simply asks which lever moves the needle further in advanced economies: building more housing by removing bureaucratic barriers, or capping rents and strengthening tenant protections. The answer is supply expansion, and it isn't close.
[P1.2] Consider the mechanism. In cities like Tokyo, where zoning is nationally standardized and permitting is relatively streamlined, housing construction has kept pace with demand for decades. Tokyo adds hundreds of thousands of units in strong years. The result?
[P1.3] Real rents in one of the world's largest metropolitan areas have remained remarkably stable even as population grew. Compare that to San Francisco, London, or Berlin—cities where restrictive zoning, lengthy approval processes, and political bottlenecks choke new construction. These same cities have also experimented heavily with rent control and tenant protections, yet affordability crises have only deepened.
[P1.4] The reason is straightforward economics with a brutal political twist. Rent control helps sitting tenants in the short run, but it suppresses the incentive to build, maintain, and convert existing stock. The empirical literature consistently finds that rent control reduces rental supply over time, creating a two-tier market: protected insiders and everyone else facing even higher prices on the shrinking uncontrolled segment. Tenant protections like just-cause eviction rules and right-of-first-refusal policies have similar dynamics—they raise costs and risks for landlords, which get passed through as higher rents or reduced investment.
[P1.5] By contrast, streamlining permitting and reforming zoning directly attacks the bottleneck. Housing costs are inflated largely because land-use regulations—minimum lot sizes, height caps, single-family zoning mandates, multi-year environmental and community review—artificially constrain what can be built where. When Minneapolis, Auckland, and Oregon moved to legalize missing-middle housing and simplify approvals, permitting activity increased and rent growth moderated relative to comparable cities that did not reform.
[P1.6] The choice is not between helping tenants and helping developers. It is between policies that expand the pie and policies that rearrange slices of a shrinking one.
