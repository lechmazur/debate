[P1.1] Drugs that perform flawlessly in a narrow demographic slice can prove useless—or lethal—for everyone else. Yet clinical trials have long relied on younger, male, and ethnically skewed cohorts, generating drug labels that read like universal facts but rest on a mirage of evidence. The proposition asks whether we should mandate proportionate representation across age, sex, and ethnicity even if it materially slows approvals. The answer is yes, because approval without demographic validity is not regulatory efficiency; it is a public health crisis deferred onto the very patients least likely to have been in the room when the data were collected.
[P1.2] Biology is not uniform. Drug metabolism, symptom presentation, and adverse-event profiles differ meaningfully across demographics. A diabetes therapy cleared on overwhelmingly male data may underperform in women; a heart medication studied in younger cohorts can interact dangerously with the polypharmacy common in older adults. When skewed evidence reaches the market, regulators inevitably face retrospective label restrictions, black-box warnings, or outright withdrawals.
[P1.3] Those downstream corrections cost lives, money, and public trust—delays merely displaced from the trial phase to the hospital ward.
[P1.4] The opposition will cast this as a crude trade-off between speed and diversity. But speed founded on incomplete evidence is reckless haste, not innovation. A trial that takes longer because it genuinely includes the population destined to use the drug produces one robust decision; a fast trial that excludes them produces years of off-label guessing, emergency visits, and documented disparities in outcomes. We already accept that larger safety databases slow development; the same logic applies to representative ones.
[P1.5] Existing accelerated approval pathways preserve flexibility for genuine emergencies; this requirement governs standard approvals, where the duty of thoroughness should prevail.
[P1.6] Mandating proportionate representation treats clinical evidence as a public good rather than a convenience sample. The measured pace required to include the whole population is not an obstacle to progress—it is the condition under which progress deserves the name.

[C1.1] The motion contains its own confession. It asks whether we should mandate proportionate representation *even if that materially slows drug approvals*. That phrase is doing enormous work, and the proposition's opening simply waves past it. Material delay means patients with approved diseases wait longer for therapies that could extend or save their lives.
[C1.2] The harms of delay are not hypothetical; they are patients dying in the gap. Any honest weighing must count those deaths, not treat them as an acceptable administrative rounding error.
[C1.3] The genuine problem here—demographic gaps in trial data—is real and worth solving. But proportionate representation is the wrong instrument, for two reasons.
[C1.4] First, *proportionality does not produce the statistical power the proposition implies*. If a trial is designed to show efficacy in the overall population, enrolling proportionate numbers of, say, elderly women or a smaller ethnic group does not give you a properly powered subgroup analysis. You would need those subgroups massively overrepresented to detect clinically meaningful differences. Mandating proportionality sounds rigorous; statistically, it offers false comfort.
[C1.5] The hard question—does this drug behave differently in this population—goes largely unanswered either way.
[C1.6] Second, better tools exist that do not require accepting material delay. Regulators can mandate dedicated parallel sub-studies without holding the primary approval hostage. Post-market surveillance requirements can be written as conditions of approval, generating real-world demographic data rapidly once the drug is in use. Adaptive trial designs can enrich underrepresented subgroups without resetting the trial clock.
[C1.7] None of these require the proposition's blunt bargain: slower approval for everyone in exchange for proportionality on paper.
[C1.8] The proposition frames the choice as reckless speed versus responsible inclusion. The actual choice is between a policy that materially delays drugs for all patients based on a statistically inadequate substitute for real subgroup analysis, and smarter targeted approaches that generate genuine demographic evidence without that lethal lag.
