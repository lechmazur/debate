[P1.1] Public funding currently rewards universities for filling classrooms, not for educating anyone. That is a design failure. When money follows enrollment alone, institutions have every reason to admit students they cannot serve, keep them long enough to collect the subsidy, and treat dropout and dead-end degrees as someone else's problem. A campus can grow headcount, starve advising, and still be paid while half a cohort vanishes.
[P1.2] Taxpayers then pay twice: first for the unused seat, then for unfinished credentials and debt without payoff.
[P1.3] Graduation rates and post-graduation earnings measure the two things public higher education exists to deliver: completion and a real economic return. A student who never finishes has not received the education we claimed to fund. A graduate who cannot out-earn the alternative has been sold a costly delay. Tying funding to demonstrated improvement on both metrics forces universities to care about those students after the tuition check clears.
[P1.4] Both together close the easy escapes: graduating students into poverty, or parking them in programs that never end.
[P1.5] The word that matters is improvement. This is not a scheme to starve community colleges or punish schools that enroll first-generation students. It is a demand that those institutions get better at graduating the students they already take and connecting them to work that pays. A campus that starts low can still win funding by raising results.
[P1.6] A campus that starts high cannot coast by packing more bodies into lecture halls.
[P1.7] Enrollment-only funding pretends access is the same as opportunity. It is not. Access without completion is a revolving door. Access without earnings is public money converted into private disappointment.
[P1.8] If a university cannot show that more of its students finish and then earn, it has not earned a larger claim on the public purse. Stop paying for promises. Pay for results.

[C1.1] The motion sounds like accountability, but it is a mechanism for abandoning the students public universities were built to serve. Tying funding to graduation rates and post-graduation earnings does not just measure outcomes; it tells universities which students to admit and which to discourage.
[C1.2] Start with earnings. Post-graduation earnings reward the major, not the mission. A university that produces teachers, social workers, public defenders, and rural nurses will look like a failure next to one that produces financiers, even when those teachers are doing exactly what the state needs. If funding follows earnings, every campus has a financial incentive to shrink education, nursing, and humanities programs and expand engineering and business tracks, regardless of whether the state needs more engineers or more middle-school math teachers.
[C1.3] The metric will quietly rewrite the curriculum toward whatever pays best, not toward what the public actually needs.
[C1.4] Graduation rates are worse. They reward institutions that select students already likely to finish and punish those that take the students everyone else has rejected. A community college that opens its doors to every adult in its county, including returning dropouts and single parents juggling two jobs, will post lower graduation rates than a selective flagship admitting only students with 4.0 GPAs. Under this motion, the open-access institution loses money for doing the open-access job.
[C1.5] The flagship gains money for being selective. We have inverted the incentive: public dollars now flow away from access and toward exclusion.
[C1.6] The "improvement" language does not save this. You can improve graduation rates by admitting fewer at-risk students. You can improve earnings by steering students away from low-paying majors. The metric still rewards whoever gets to choose their incoming class.
[C1.7] Enrollment-based funding, for all its flaws, at least aligns with the public purpose: educate the population that shows up. The motion replaces that with a system that pays for selection, not education. That is the wrong direction.
