[P1.1] An asteroid is one of the few global catastrophes that is not merely survivable in policy but preventable in engineering. The motion asks governments to build standing capability—survey, decision, launch, and deflection—instead of leaving planetary defense as an academic watch. The case turns on time. Once a threatening object is found, useful deflection is a race against physics: orbit determination, political decision, spacecraft buildout, launch windows, transfer time, and enough lead time for a small velocity change to become a wide miss.
[P1.2] A research-only system discovers danger while assuming it can improvise a rescue. Infrastructure reverses that: continuous sky surveys find objects earlier, while maintained vehicles, responsive launch arrangements, and rehearsed command protocols turn warning into action before launch windows close.
[P1.3] The stakes are concrete. Chelyabinsk showed that a modest object can produce a city-scale shock and mass injuries. Tunguska showed the regional-devastation class. Larger impactors threaten not one nation but the continuity of civilization.
[P1.4] NASA’s DART mission demonstrated that kinetic impact can change an asteroid’s orbit, but a single test is not a defense system: no assigned target, no fleet, no operational timeline, no rehearsed decision chain. The lesson is that the technology is plausible enough to operationalize and too important to leave at experimental stage.
[P1.5] Opponents will emphasize cost, but the comparison is not budget line versus zero. It is modest permanent readiness against an uninsurable catastrophe. Governments already maintain costly standing capacity against low-probability, high-loss threats—flood defenses, strategic stockpiles, grid protection. Planetary defense has an advantage most risks lack: unused preparedness still preserves options, while unbuilt capability converts a warning into panic.
[P1.6] Research can publish a warning; only deployed infrastructure can prevent impact.

That means any object we could realistically deflect is one we will see coming decades in advance. Decades of warning is precisely the case where you do not need a standing fleet: you design the mission for that object, its size, spin, composition, and orbit, and build it fresh.
[C1.3] A permanently parked spacecraft, built for a generic threat, will be the wrong tool and aging hardware by the time anything arrives. We do not maintain a mothballed hospital for a disease that announces itself thirty years before the outbreak.
[C1.4] Now look at the real risk profile. The objects that hit without warning—Chelyabinsk-class boulders—are too small to detect far out and too small to justify deflection infrastructure; mitigation there is civil defense and monitoring, not launch pads. The civilization-enders are rare on any human policy horizon.
[C1.5] Meanwhile, the same governments PRO wants writing blank checks face pandemics, floods, and grid failures that are orders of magnitude more probable and kill on schedule, not on geological odds.
[C1.6] Nor is unused planetary-defense capacity free. Flood defenses hold back water every year; a deflection fleet drills, decays, and consumes budgets while its decision chain atrophies into untested ritual. Permanent institutions also entrench: standing launch arrangements become procurement pork, and a bureaucracy with a mission but no mission has every incentive to inflate threat estimates.
[C1.7] The genuinely valuable core—sky surveys, orbit determination, and demonstrated deflection techniques—is exactly what a research niche delivers. Detection is the bottleneck; DART showed the concept works when needed. Keep the watch sharp and the shelf of techniques full, and build the defense when physics, not panic, says so.
