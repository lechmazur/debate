[P1.1] Farmland is not stock. It cannot be manufactured, moved, or replaced, and societies cannot eat financial returns. When non-farming institutional investors accumulate it without limit, they convert the foundation of food security into a portfolio hedge, and both resilience and rural life suffer.
[P1.2] First, access collapses. Pension funds, insurance pools and private equity arrive with vastly cheaper capital than a young farmer seeking a first mortgage. They can outbid locals field by field, then lease back at higher rents. Prices detach from what farming itself can repay, tenancy replaces ownership, and would-be farmers become precarious contractors on land their communities once held.
[P1.3] Once that tipping point passes, rural succession does not recover.
[P1.4] Second, incentives diverge. An investor needs risk-adjusted return across a global portfolio; a resident owner needs fertile soil and water in twenty years. That difference shows up in the field: pressure for intensive cash crops, simplified rotations, heavier chemical use, and mining of soil and aquifers for near-term yield. Absentee owners hire managers judged on annual performance, not stewards judged by whether the land still feeds their children.
[P1.5] Third, communities hollow out. Rents and profits flow to distant headquarters rather than circulating through local suppliers, schools and co-ops. Concentrated ownership also means concentrated political power over water rights, leases and supply chains, leaving actual producers with less bargaining power in a crisis.
[P1.6] A cap is modest and market-preserving. It does not ban outside capital; funds can still lend, finance storage and processing, or hold long leases. It simply sets a ceiling on the share of farmland any non-farming institution can own in a given region, preventing financial logic from capturing the land market itself. Resilience demands many rooted owners, not a few diversified portfolios.
[P1.7] Farmland is too essential to be just another asset class.

[C1.1] Capping institutional ownership would make food systems more fragile, not more resilient, because it targets capital instead of conduct.
[C1.2] Farming is enormously capital intensive with low returns. In many regions a single family farm ties up several million dollars in soil to earn 2-4%. Banks will not finance a young grower for that, and farmers are aging out with their retirement locked in land. Institutional investors - pension funds, insurers - are one of the few sources of patient cash willing to buy at retirement and lease back to operators, fund drainage, precision equipment and storage that a leveraged family cannot.
[C1.3] Cut that buyer out by law and you cut land values, destroy farmers' retirement collateral, and tighten credit for everyone left.
[C1.4] The claim in [P1.2] that they outbid and dispossess locals misreads how entry actually works. Ownership is not the only way to farm. For a 28-year-old, renting 300 hectares from a fund that wants a 30-year inflation hedge is far more accessible than borrowing millions to own it. Tenancy is not precarity if lease law is decent; it is the standard path in France or the US Midwest.
[C1.5] A cap forces would-be farmers back into crippling debt just to access ground, accelerating exactly the succession collapse warned of in [P1.3].
[C1.6] Incentives also align longer than [P1.4] suggests. Farmland is valuable only if it stays productive. A fund holding thousands of hectares for decades loses money if soil organic matter falls or aquifers deplete - the asset price collapses. That is why large owners are often first adopters of soil mapping, cover cropping and water-efficient pivots: they can amortize costs a small owner cannot.
[C1.7] Finally, rural communities need inflow, not ownership purity. Profits may leave, but wages, input purchases and taxes stay, and are larger when land is capitalized. If the worry is monoculture or rents, regulate practices and leases directly. A blunt ownership ceiling starves agriculture of the money resilience requires.

[P2.1] The opposition confuses money with rootedness. Agriculture does need capital, but not all capital behaves the same.
[P2.2] A pension fund chasing an inflation hedge is not patient when rates spike or wheat prices dip. It raises rents to hit its target, cuts maintenance, or dumps land to rebalance. That is pro-cyclical capital arriving in booms and strangling operators in busts. Protecting today's inflated land values also protects no one's resilience: high prices are exactly what block the 28-year-old.
[P2.3] A retirement system that depends on selling soil to the highest bidder is already broken; a cap brings prices back toward what farming can repay and opens space for cooperatives, land banks, and successor farmers supported by lending — capital that does not demand ownership.
[P2.4] Nor is renting from a fund equivalent to renting to farm. A neighbor-landlord cares if you are still there in ten years. An asset manager judged on annual yield prefers short leases that allow rent hikes, restricts longer-term planting and soil building, and can terminate across thousands of hectares at once. Tenancy works where law gives security and ownership is dispersed — that is the point.
[P2.5] A cap does not force debt-fueled purchase; it prevents one class of cash-rich bidders from monopolizing leases and sets the condition where decent lease law can work.
[P2.6] Finally, an asset that can be sold need not be cared for. Degraded ground can still fetch a price in a commodity boom, and fund lifecycles run years, not generations. Sensors on a pivot are not stewardship; they are yield optimization.
[P2.7] Regulating practices while letting ownership concentrate leaves the regulator to chase managers with lawyers in another capital. Limit the share, keep the capital in loans and infrastructure, and keep decisions with those who must live with them.

[P4.1] A1: There is no universal number, because land markets are local. A cap means a county or watershed sets a ceiling — say no single non-farming institution beyond a small share, and all such investors combined below a modest share — then phases it in with existing holdings grandfathered. That avoids a crash and gives retiring sellers a buyer: land banks, cooperatives and successor farmers backed by public guarantees and farm credit, not auction to the highest distant bidder. Retirement paid over time from productive farming is more resilient than a one-time windfall that prices out the next generation and then vanishes.
[P4.2] A2: We are not pushing farmers from patient equity into fragile debt. Funds can still lend, finance drainage and storage, and hold limited acreage or long leases under the cap; we only block unlimited fee-simple control. A bank forecloses farm by farm under court rules, while a mega-owner can hike rents or exit thousands of hectares at once when its portfolio target shifts. The truly patient equity is farmer, family and cooperative ownership rooted for decades, financed by loans that do not demand control.
[P4.3] That is why we still win: secure leases and practice standards only work when power is dispersed. France thrives not because funds own freely, but because ownership is capped in effect and tenants have rights. Leave ownership unlimited and the regulator chases asset managers with lawyers in another capital, while rents leave town and soils are mined for yield. Cap the share, keep the capital in tools not titles, and resilience stays with those who must live with the harvest.
