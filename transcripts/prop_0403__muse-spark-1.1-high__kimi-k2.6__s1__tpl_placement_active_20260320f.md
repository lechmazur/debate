[C1.2] A binding community approval process functions as a one-way trap. [C1.3] Locking a team in place does not fix a failing market; it turns the franchise into a zombie asset.
[C1.4] This veto also flips the extortion dynamic PRO fears. A city that knows a team cannot leave becomes the extortionist, piling on new demands or refusing necessary upgrades because the team has no exit. The threat of relocation is sometimes the only leverage that keeps public partners honest.
[C1.5] The policy is structurally self-defeating. If taking public money means surrendering the right to ever relocate, rational owners will either refuse the subsidy—leaving the city with an empty arena—or demand far larger upfront payments to compensate for lost option value. The cities that can least afford to lose a team will pay more, or watch investment flow to jurisdictions without civic exit bans.
[C1.6] If the concern is wasted tax dollars, the proper remedy is to ban subsidies outright or enforce the clawback clauses already written into most agreements. Converting a temporary public investment into permanent civic restraint is overreach that destroys value for everyone except the politicians parading a captive franchise.

[C2.1] PRO tries to absorb "better lease" into its own case [P2.1], but that collapses the critical distinction. A lease is a bilateral contract with fixed terms and predictable penalties. A binding community approval process is a roaming veto that can shift its demands after the ink is dry. Calling that a "lease term" is like calling a recall election an employment contract: it fundamentally changes the nature of the deal by subjecting every relocation decision to raw political bargaining.
[C2.2] The factory analogy [P2.2] fails because sports franchises are not standalone plants. They are nodes in a cartel with shared revenue, territorial exclusivity, and league-wide debt capacity. A factory that cannot relocate can still sell goods online or ship nationwide. A franchise trapped in a market with eroding corporate suites and shrinking media value cannot mail its product elsewhere; it withers in place.
[C2.3] When a team cannot leave, the city does not need to actively extort it [P2.3]. Simple demographic decline does the damage. The team becomes a stranded asset, stripped of resale value and starved of capital improvements, while the league has no mechanism to replace it with a healthier market.
[C2.4] PRO celebrates the deterrent effect [P2.5] as if a subsidy boycott by owners would magically end stadium welfare. The likelier outcome is spatial sorting: investment flows to major markets that do not need public money, while mid-sized cities lose not just leverage but access. The approval process does not make voters wiser; it makes them hostages to a single employer, forced to approve whatever terms prevent the lights from going out.
[C2.5] As for St. Louis [P2.6], the debt on an empty dome is a sunk cost. Trapping a team does not pay that debt; it merely hides the loss behind a façade of civic pride. Real accountability comes from enforceable financial penalties written into the original deal, not from a permanent relocation ban that confuses possession of a franchise with protection of the public purse.
