[P1.1] The employment relationship depends on a basic bargain: workers offer their labor under terms they can understand, and employers make decisions affecting livelihoods through processes workers can scrutinize. When algorithms silently shape who gets hired and who gets fired, that bargain breaks. Workers cannot challenge, improve, or even comprehend decisions they cannot see.
[P1.2] Automated decision systems now screen résumés, score interviews, flag employees for termination, and rank workforce productivity. They process thousands of variables, apply hidden criteria, and do so without the interpersonal accountability a human decision-maker carries. A manager who rejects a candidate can be asked why. An algorithm that does so cannot—even when the outcome carries the same weight for the worker's life.
[P1.3] Disclosure matters because harm hides in opacity. Automated systems can replicate bias at scale. Software that penalizes employment gaps disproportionately screens out women returning from parental leave. Scoring systems that downgrade workers based on rigid scheduling availability systematically disadvantage caregivers.
[P1.4] Without disclosure, affected workers have no way to identify the pattern, much less contest it.
[P1.5] The legal logic already exists. Employers must already provide reasons for termination in many jurisdictions. Creditors must disclose adverse action. Consumer protection laws increasingly require notice when automated tools process personal data.
[P1.6] Employment decisions, which shape housing, healthcare, and family stability, deserve at least the same standard.
[P1.7] The cost of compliance is minimal. Disclosure requires only that employers state an automated system meaningfully influenced a decision. This does not demand revealing trade secrets or publishing source code. It simply ensures workers know when a machine, not a person, shaped their fate.
[P1.8] A worker's livelihood should never be decided by a black box they don't even know exists. When employers refuse to say whether automation drove the decision, the only party who benefits is the one who already holds all the power.

[C1.1] The case against this mandate starts with the phrase that would do all the legal work: “meaningfully influenced.” That is not a defined standard; it is an unpredictable line. Almost every modern hiring and firing decision runs through software in some way. A recruiter uses a keyword search; a manager sorts attendance logs in a scheduling tool; a performance dashboard flags declining output. If those count, disclosure becomes a meaningless notice attached to virtually every application and termination.
[C1.2] If they do not count, employers cannot know which decisions do until a court decides after the fact. A duty cannot be fairly enforced when the trigger is unknowable in advance.
[C1.3] The compliance cost is not as small as claimed. To avoid crossing an undefined threshold, employers would need vendor audits, case-by-case legal review, documentation of human overrides, and litigation risk over whether the required notice should have been given. Mid-size and smaller employers would bear that burden without in-house AI counsel. Cautious employers will over-disclose, and workers will receive so many automated-influence notices that the information becomes worthless.
[C1.4] The mandate creates noise, not transparency.
[C1.5] The comparison to credit adverse-action rules also fails. A credit decision is a discrete final act generated by a known data source. Hiring and firing are collaborative, multi-step judgments. A manager may see a ranked list and still reject someone for interview presentation, communication style, or a better final candidate.
[C1.6] Requiring an employer to announce that automation “meaningfully influenced” that decision forces a false label onto ordinary human judgment.
[C1.7] Finally, the remedy does not deliver what workers actually need. A notice that a system played some role says nothing about which input mattered, whether bias infected the result, or how to contest it. Workers would still face the same opaque outcome, just with a disclaimer attached. If the goal is accountability, the law should require substantive explanations of adverse decisions or audit outcomes for discrimination.
[C1.8] It should not impose a vague disclosure duty that burdens employers while giving workers only the appearance of protection.

Materiality in securities regulation, substantiality in discrimination doctrine, reasonableness in negligence: courts draw these lines case by case, and employers comply every day under equally flexible terms. CON presents a false dilemma: either every scheduling tool triggers a notice, or no one can tell until a court decides.
[P2.2] But employers know exactly when they deploy systems to screen résumés at scale, rank candidates algorithmically, or flag workers for termination based on model output. Those are the decisions this duty targets, and they are identifiable in advance.
[P2.3] The over-disclosure fear is self-defeating. CON argues automation is so pervasive that notices would attach to everything—yet that pervasiveness is precisely why disclosure matters. If algorithms quietly mediate most employment decisions, workers deserve to know. A notice that appears on most decisions reveals a real fact: automation is the invisible infrastructure of hiring and firing.
[P2.4] That is information, not noise.
[P2.5] CON's distinction from credit adverse-action also fails. When software filters applicants before any human sees them, the algorithm is the gatekeeper—whether or not a manager later exercises discretion among survivors. Saying a human made the final call does not erase the automated screen that chose who survived. Disclosure of that gatekeeping is precise, not a false label on human judgment.
[P2.6] Most telling is CON's claim that disclosure gives workers only "the appearance of protection." This reverses the causal logic. Without knowing an automated system was involved, a worker cannot ask whether it discriminated, seek an audit, or challenge its criteria. Disclosure is the precondition for every substantive remedy CON says workers actually need.
[P2.7] You cannot contest what you cannot identify. CON would keep the black box sealed in the name of protecting workers from knowing it exists—and the only party served by that silence is the employer who built it.
