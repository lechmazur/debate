[P1.1] Copyright exists to reward human creativity, not to privatize the output of machines. In the United States, AI-generated works should receive no copyright protection unless a human can document substantial creative control over the final expression. That rule preserves the purpose of copyright, keeps ownership coherent, and stops a rush to lock up endless machine-spun content.
[P1.2] Copyright’s core requirement is human authorship. A work must originate in a person’s mind through choices about selection, arrangement, phrasing, and composition. When a user enters a prompt and an AI system supplies the bulk of the expressive detail, the machine—not the person—determined the protectable elements. The human supplied an idea or request; ideas are not copyrightable.
[P1.3] Without documented control over how the final expression took shape—iterative editing, structural decisions, deliberate selection among outputs, or transformative refinement—there is simply no human author of the expression itself.
[P1.4] Substantial creative control is the workable line. It does not demand that every pixel or sentence be hand-made. It requires proof that a human directed and shaped the expressive result in a meaningful way: choosing among generations, rewriting significant passages, composing sequence and emphasis, or fixing style through concrete interventions. Documentation makes the claim verifiable rather than speculative.
[P1.5] Without it, agencies and courts are left guessing who authored what.
[P1.6] The contrary rule invites disorder. Entities could claim monopolies over vast volumes of barely-touched AI output, flooding markets and chilling human creators who need to build on the cultural commons. Copyright would attach to content that required almost no human creative labor, inverting the incentive structure the doctrine was built to serve.
[P1.7] Protect the human who can show they steered the expression. Deny protection where the machine performed the expressive work. That is the only coherent rule.

[C1.1] Copyright has never required creators to prove they were creative. It asks whether a work is original — a minimal spark — and then protects it. Nobody makes a photographer submit a log of her intentions to show she controlled the light, the lens optics, or the sensor's processing. She pressed a button in a fraction of a second, and the law calls her an author.
[C1.2] The motion invents, for AI alone, a documentation regime found nowhere else in copyright, and then treats the absence of paperwork as proof of absence of authorship.
[C1.3] That is the core defect. "Document substantial creative control over the final expression" is not a line; it is a trap. A novelist who drafts by hand and uses a model to smooth two hundred sentences cannot reconstruct which words were hers. A designer who generated four hundred variants and chose one has made a real aesthetic judgment and kept no record of the discards.
[C1.4] Under this rule, both lose their work to the public domain — not because a machine authored it, but because a human failed at recordkeeping.
[C1.5] Notice who survives that regime: studios and platforms with legal departments, prompt-logging pipelines, and compliance staff. The independent illustrator loses. And the incentive it creates is not more human creativity; it is concealment. Tell people that admitting AI assistance forfeits their rights, and they will simply stop admitting it.
[C1.6] A rule that rewards lying is not a rule that keeps ownership coherent.
[C1.7] The flood argument runs backwards. Denying protection does not reduce machine-made content; it makes every piece of it free for anyone to copy, repackage, and sell at scale, competing against human creators who now have no recourse when their own hybrid work is lifted wholesale.
[C1.8] Existing doctrine already handles this. Ideas are unprotected, thin works get thin protection, and infringement analysis filters unoriginal elements out. The default should be ordinary originality, assessed case by case — not a presumption of forfeiture cured only by a paper trail.
