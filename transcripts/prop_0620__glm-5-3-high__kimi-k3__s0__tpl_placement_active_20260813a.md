[P1.1] The default rule today is that companies may track, profile, and monetize hundreds of millions of people unless each individual finds the buried toggle and switches it off. That default is backwards, and it is backwards in a way only law can fix.
[P1.2] Start with the consent fiction. Consent only does moral work when refusal is realistic and informed. But the modern data economy is engineered to make refusal nearly impossible: dark patterns that bury "reject all" behind extra clicks, take-it-or-leave-it walls where the price of privacy is exclusion from ordinary services, and privacy policies written in tens of thousands of words of legalese that no one reads. When a grocery app, a weather app, and a flashlight app all demand access to your location and contacts, the "choice" on offer is not autonomy—it's a toll booth.
[P1.3] A rule that treats this as valid consent ratifies a formality, not a decision.
[P1.4] Second, the harm is structural, not individual. Targeted advertising works by exploiting inferences—about health, finances, sexuality, vulnerability—that people never disclosed. Cambridge Analytica showed how behavioral profiles become persuasion infrastructure. Discriminatory ad delivery lets housing and job ads skip protected groups.
[P1.5] These injuries are diffuse, invisible, and often undetectable to the victim. Markets cannot discipline harms consumers cannot see, so the burden must shift to the party profiting from the risk.
[P1.6] Third, presumptions are how law handles asymmetric power everywhere else: pollution is not permitted until each neighbor opts out; pharmaceuticals require approval before sale, not after harm. Data harvesting for ads is the same shape—concentrated commercial gain, distributed social risk. Presumptive unlawliness with opt-in consent is the proportionate remedy: firms that deliver genuine value can still ask, clearly and honestly, and let users say yes.
[P1.7] Notice what the proposition does not do. It does not ban targeted advertising. It does not ban data collection for security or service delivery. It simply requires the one thing that makes any of this legitimate: a real, uncoerced yes.
[P1.8] Anything less is not consent—it is capture. The law should stop pretending otherwise.

[C1.3] What replaces it is not privacy; it is paywalls, and the burden lands on those least able to subscribe—lower-income users, the Global South. Advertising is an imperfect subsidy, but it democratizes access; this motion abolishes it by default.
[C1.4] Second, the remedy fails on its own terms. PRO's case is that consent today is formalism—buried toggles, dark patterns, legalese. [C1.5] That is not autonomy restored; it is consent fatigue industrialized. If the disease is engineered consent, more consent requests are not the medicine. Better law targets the engineering—ban dark patterns, require a symmetric "reject all," police inferences about health and vulnerability—without outlawing the underlying transaction.
[C1.6] Third, disproportion and perverse effect. Presumptions of unlawfulness fit pollution and pharmaceuticals because those risks are grave and irreversible; showing a runner an ad for running shoes is not asbestos. The harms PRO names—discriminatory housing and job ads, exploitation of sensitive traits—can be targeted directly, and increasingly are. And notice who survives an opt-in world: giants with logged-in users who can extract consent at scale.
[C1.7] Small publishers and startups cannot. PRO warns of "capture"; its remedy delivers it, entrenching incumbents while starving the open web.
[C1.8] Reject a rule that burns down the free internet to rescue a consent fiction it merely reproduces.
