[P1.1] A thirteen-year-old today can Google herself and find her potty training, her meltdowns, her first period discussed by her mother to two million strangers — content she never consented to, cannot control, and that will follow her into every job interview, first date, and college application. The question is simple: when that child becomes capable of understanding what was done with her image, should the law give her a meaningful voice? The answer must be yes.
[P1.2] The case rests on three pillars.
[P1.3] First, consent. Every other context where a person's private life is broadcast commercially requires their agreement. Family vlogging is the sole industry where the product — a child's intimate daily life — is monetized without the subject's consent, because the person who should protect that consent is the one profiting. Parental authority exists to serve children's interests; when parents earn ad revenue from a child's embarrassing moments, that authority is compromised by a direct financial conflict.
[P1.4] The law routinely steps in precisely where guardians have conflicts of interest — that's why Coogan-style laws protect child actors' earnings. A removal right is the digital-era equivalent.
[P1.5] Second, permanence. A childhood embarrassment once faded with memory. Now it is indexed, searchable, clipped, and remixed forever. Adolescence is exactly when identity formation happens — and when peers weaponize this material.
[P1.6] The harm compounds during the very years the child gains the maturity to object.
[P1.7] Third, this is a narrow, proportionate remedy. We are not banning family content or criminalizing parents. We are saying: once a child reaches adolescence, she may request removal of content featuring her name, routines, or humiliations, and platforms and parents must honor legitimate requests. Adults already enjoy comparable erasure rights in many jurisdictions.
[P1.8] Extending a stronger version to the actual subjects of this content — children who never chose publicity — is the minimum a decent legal system owes them.
[P1.9] Parents have free speech. Children have lives. The law should stop pretending only the first matters.

Vote CON because the proposition protects children in name only: it trades targeted safeguards for overbroad censorship of family life.
