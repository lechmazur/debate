[P1.1] Tax exemption is a bargain, not a birthright. Society agrees to forgo revenue from churches, mosques, synagogues and temples because they are presumed to serve the common good — feeding the poor, sheltering the homeless, counseling the grieving, nurturing conscience. When a religious organization abandons that role to operate as a partisan machine, it breaks the bargain and should lose the benefit.
[P1.2] That is already the principle we apply to everyone else. A secular food bank, museum, or university that endorses candidates, bankrolls campaigns, or turns its offices into a party headquarters loses its charitable status. To let religious groups do what no other charity may do is not religious freedom; it is religious favoritism, a special license to electioneer with subsidized dollars.
[P1.3] And subsidy is exactly what is at stake. Every tax-deductible donation and every property-tax exemption forces neighbors of all faiths and no faith to underwrite that organization. No American should be compelled to subsidize someone else’s campaign rallies dressed up as Sunday services. If pastors want to endorse from the pulpit, coordinate with candidates, distribute partisan voter guides, or funnel tax-free money into elections, they can do so — just not on the taxpayer’s dime.
[P1.4] They can preach, they can organize, they can even form a separate political arm that pays taxes like everyone else.
[P1.5] The line in the proposition is deliberately high: overt partisan actors, not primarily charitable or spiritual institutions. Preaching about poverty, war, abortion, or civil rights is not targeted. Endorsing candidates, acting as a campaign field office, and rating parties from the altar is.
[P1.6] Protecting that line protects both democracy and faith itself, keeping houses of worship from being bought, captured, and hollowed out by politicians.

[C1.1] Tax exemption for houses of worship is not a prize for staying apolitical. It is a limit on the state’s power to tax, audit, and define religion. The proposition hands officials the job of deciding when a congregation has become an “overt partisan actor” rather than “primarily spiritual.” That is not a bright line. It requires the government to parse sermons, score voter guides, and weigh how much of a church’s life is God versus party.
[C1.2] A food bank is not a church. Churches exist to proclaim a comprehensive moral vision. For many traditions that vision includes who may justly govern, which laws are wicked, and whether a candidate is fit. Naming names does not make the teaching less religious.
[C1.3] It makes the tax collector a theologian.
[C1.4] The subsidy claim is false. Forgoing tax on a sanctuary is not writing a campaign check. Neighbors do not underwrite Sunday endorsements any more than they underwrite a newspaper’s editorials by not treating the press as a state organ. Exemption is the baseline that keeps the state from charging rent on conscience.
[C1.5] History shows the test is a weapon. Abolition pulpits, civil-rights congregations, and churches teaching on war or abortion were all accused of abandoning “spiritual” work for politics. The party in power will always find the other side’s religion too partisan. That chills speech and captures faith.
[C1.6] Campaign-finance law already bars churches from writing checks to candidates. Use that. Do not strip property and donation benefits because a pastor endorsed from the pulpit. A “separate political arm” tells believers they keep tax status only if they amputate their prophetic voice.
[C1.7] That is a penalty on religion, not a defense of democracy.

Abolition pulpits and civil-rights congregations condemned slavery and segregation as sin — precisely the moral preaching the proposition protects. [P2.5] A separate taxable political arm amputates nothing. The voice remains; only the subsidy moves. Barring direct checks while subsidizing the building, staff, and mailing list that elect candidates is closing the front door while the garage stands open. What truly captures faith is the current rule: politicians hunting for subsidized pulpits, and congregations remade into applause lines.
