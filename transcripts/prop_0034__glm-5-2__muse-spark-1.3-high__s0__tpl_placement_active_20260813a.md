[P1.1] A person's voice is not a public utility. It is one of the most intimate markers of identity—tied to reputation, trust, and livelihood. When a platform or media company commercially distributes an AI-generated voice clone that imitates an identifiable person, it is trafficking in that person's identity without permission. The proposition is straightforward: if you want to sell someone's voice, ask first.
[P1.2] The case rests on three points the opposition cannot escape.
[P1.3] First, the harm is concrete and asymmetric. A voice clone can be deployed in fraudulent calls, deceptive advertisements, or misleading media that reaches millions before the target even learns it exists. Once distributed, the damage is done—reputation eroded, trust violated, opportunities lost. The individual bears the cost while the platform collects the revenue.
[P1.4] Affirmative consent flips that imbalance: those who profit from an identity bear the burden of securing permission.
[P1.5] Second, the standard is neither novel nor unworkable. We already require consent before using someone's likeness in advertising, before publishing endorsements, before processing sensitive personal data. Voice is as identifying as a face and as personal as a signature. Extending the consent principle to AI voice clones follows established legal logic, not radical expansion.
[P1.6] Third, the proposition targets commercial distribution of identifiable imitations—not parody, satire, commentary, or non-commercial expression. A platform selling access to a synthetic voice clone of a real person for ad campaigns or paid content is engaged in commercial exploitation, not protected speech. The First Amendment does not guarantee the right to sell a simulation of someone else's voice for profit.
[P1.7] The default must be consent. Anything less treats a person's voice as freely available raw material for whoever can copy it. That is not innovation—it is appropriation dressed in technical sophistication, and the people whose voices are taken deserve a say before the marketplace does.

[C1.1] Requiring permission first sounds respectful. In practice it hands every identifiable person a private veto over speech that invokes them. Voices are not just personal property; they are part of shared public life. Politicians are quoted, mimicked, dramatized and debated, and audiences understand the difference between a real statement and a recreation.
[C1.2] Turning every recognizable AI imitation into forbidden merchandise until a release form is signed converts culture about real people into property owned by its subjects.
[C1.3] The commercial-distribution limit does no real limiting. Almost all influential speech today is commercially distributed: ad-supported podcasts, monetized videos, streaming films, audiobooks, news recreations, translated interviews. A human impressionist can satirize a candidate on a paid show without prior consent; under this rule the same satire rendered with AI tools would need a lawyer first. That distinction punishes a technique, not a wrong, and invites platforms to delete first and ask questions later.
[C1.4] Real harms are already addressable without prior restraint. Fraudulent calls, fake endorsements that mislead consumers, and defamatory fabrications can be punished after the fact through fraud, false advertising, and defamation law, with takedown for clear deception. Affirmative consent reverses that logic: block everything lawful to catch the unlawful. The result is censorship by transaction cost, where lawful docudramas, parodies, accessibility dubbing, and low-budget creators die in licensing limbo while scammers — who never sought consent anyway — ignore the rule entirely.
[C1.5] And consent empowers the already powerful. Famous voices gain a tollbooth, estates of the dead gain a veto over history, and obscure private figures become un-clearable risks platforms simply erase. An independent filmmaker recreating a historical speech, a news outlet reconstructing a newsworthy rant for context, a startup helping patients keep communicative ability — all must locate, negotiate, and pay before speaking. Disclosure plus liability for lying protects trust without making imitation itself a trespass.
[C1.6] Reject blanket prior consent; punish deception, not depiction.

[P2.7] Disclosure without consent [C1.6] is a warning label on theft. It does not return agency or prevent the clone from being sold. The burden belongs on those who profit, not on those whose voices are taken.
