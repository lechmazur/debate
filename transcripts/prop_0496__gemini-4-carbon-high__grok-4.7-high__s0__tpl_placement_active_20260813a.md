[P1.1] In a democracy, the power to kill in foreign wars is legitimate only because an unbroken chain of accountability connects voters, elected leaders, uniformed commanders, and the soldier pulling the trigger. Putting private military contractors into combat severs that chain by design. Banning them from combat roles in international conflicts, while leaving non-combat logistics and technical support intact, is essential to keep lethal force under democratic control.
[P1.2] First, combat contractors let executives wage war while evading public and legislative consent. The strongest democratic check on foreign intervention is political friction: legislative authorizations, troop caps, reserve mobilizations, and the political cost of bringing uniformed citizens home in coffins. Combat contractors bypass every one of those checks. Leaders can cap official troop numbers to reassure lawmakers while quietly surging private fighters paid through opaque procurement budgets.
[P1.3] Contractor deaths rarely enter official casualty counts or spark public reckoning. When governments outsource firefights to private payrolls, they sustain foreign wars without sustaining democratic consent.
[P1.4] Second, combat contractors fracture accountability on the battlefield. Uniformed soldiers serve under a binding chain of command and military justice; commanders can compel restraint under fire and court-martial abuses immediately. Contractors answer to corporate managers and contract clauses, not military oaths. When heavily armed Blackwater operatives killed Iraqi civilians in Baghdad’s Nisour Square, they operated outside the military chain of command, leaving field commanders powerless to discipline them and forcing years of tangled civilian litigation at home.
[P1.5] A democracy does not control lethal force when its commanders cannot command the fighters shooting in its name.
[P1.6] Regulation cannot cure this defect, because political insulation is not a side effect of combat contracting; it is the very product governments buy. Even licensed contractors bypass troop caps and voter scrutiny, and no contract officer can govern a firefight through contract law. Democracies may rent trucks and kitchens, but they must never rent out the trigger.
