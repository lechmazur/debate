[P1.1] Stripping individual names off genetic data does not strip away an employer’s financial incentives. When companies use population-level genetic data to shape health insurance and wellness programs, they turn the collective DNA of their workforce into a balance-sheet liability—enabling genetic discrimination in the aggregate that we rightly forbid against the individual.
[P1.2] Start with health-insurance design. Employers are not public health charities; they manage benefit costs in a labor market where the average worker stays only a few years. Imagine an employer discovers through aggregate genetic data that its workforce carries an unusually high predisposition to expensive hereditary cancers or autoimmune conditions. Paying for costly preventative therapies today to avert an illness ten years from now mostly subsidizes a future employer.
[P1.3] The rational cost-control move is the opposite: restructure the insurance plan to shift financial risk onto the workforce. By placing relevant specialty medications on higher copay tiers, narrowing specialist networks, or raising deductibles on complex diagnostics, an employer can penalize workers for their biology without ever knowing which individual holds which mutation.
[P1.4] Nor does genetic data have any legitimate place in workplace wellness programs. Every employer already knows that exercise, nutrition, and smoking cessation improve health; you do not need to analyze a workforce’s genome to justify a gym subsidy or heart-health seminar. Injecting population genetics into wellness programs does something far more insidious: it shifts blame from the workplace to the worker. It allows companies to attribute elevated rates of cardiovascular or metabolic disease to their employees' "genetic baseline" rather than fixing the grueling shift work, chronic stress, or occupational hazards the employer actually controls.
[P1.5] Finally, aggregate data does not fall from the sky. Using it requires coaxing employees into surrendering their DNA to corporate wellness vendors, normalizing genetic harvesting and inviting re-identification in smaller cohorts. A worker’s genome has no place in their boss’s benefits calculus, and only a strict prohibition keeps it out.
