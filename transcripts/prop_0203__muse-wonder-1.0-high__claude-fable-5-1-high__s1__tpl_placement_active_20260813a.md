[P1.1] When a venture firm or pension fund in a democracy finances an advanced chip designer or frontier AI lab in an authoritarian rival, it is not making a neutral market bet. It supplies the scarcest inputs to a strategic competitor: patient capital, technical know-how, managerial expertise, credibility, and access to global networks. In semiconductors and AI, those inputs do not stay commercial. They shorten the path to smarter weapons, pervasive surveillance, cyber operations, and military logistics.
[P1.2] That risk is not reciprocal. An authoritarian state can compel firms to share data, surrender intellectual property, or prioritize military work, with no independent court or press to resist. Civil and military development are deliberately fused, so a facial-recognition model, a vehicle chip, or a factory optimization algorithm can be repurposed for targeting, repression, or battlefield decisions. Exported capital there does not merely earn a return; it can be conscripted.
[P1.3] Existing controls leave this channel open. Export controls can stop a shipment of lithography tools, and inbound screening can stop a rival buying a domestic firm. Neither stops domestic money and know-how from building the rival's capacity abroad. Once a fab is financed, a model trained, or a research team assembled, the transfer is irreversible.
[P1.4] You cannot un-teach expertise or recall tacit knowledge with a later sanction.
[P1.5] Screening answers that gap proportionately. It does not require blanket decoupling or blocking ordinary trade. It means prior review — and prohibition or conditions where necessary — for narrowly defined outbound investments in advanced semiconductors and sensitive AI in authoritarian rivals. Democracies already accept that logic for arms exports: some transactions are too strategically consequential to leave solely to private profit.
[P1.6] Reviewing a narrow set of deals is modest and predictable in cost. Inaction is costlier: private investors pocket short-term gains, the public bears long-term security risk, and the technological lead underwriting deterrence erodes. Countries should not finance the tools of their own coercion.
