[P1.1] The motion before us is deceptively modest. It does not ask whether high-quality internet is nice to have. It does not ask whether the private sector should participate. It asks simply whether access to it should be a legal entitlement, on par with the wires and pipes we have long treated as the basic scaffolding of a modern society.
[P1.2] That question answers itself once we look at what the internet actually does in 2024. A child without broadband cannot complete homework that requires a research database. A patient cannot use a telemedicine portal. A worker cannot file for unemployment, attend a job interview on video, or apply to most openings that are now posted solely online.
[P1.3] A small business cannot accept card payments during an outage. These are not conveniences. They are the ordinary machinery of economic and civic life.
[P1.4] History is on our side. A century ago, electricity was a luxury commodity, and rural electrification required legal obligation to become universal. Water and sanitation followed the same path. Each time, the turning point came when the public recognized that a service had become indispensable and that voluntary provision alone left too many people behind.
[P1.5] We are at that turning point for connectivity.
[P1.6] What the "legally enforceable" language adds is the heart of the motion, and it is what opponents will quietly try to erode. Aspirations, funds, and broadband programs without teeth have a long record of underdelivering, especially for rural, low-income, and disabled users. A legal duty creates a cause of action. It forces regulators to publish standards.
[P1.7] It gives courts and ombudsmen something to enforce when a family in a neglected district is left offline for months.
[P1.8] The choice is not between state-run networks and the free market. It is whether, once connectivity has become as load-bearing as electricity, we codify that status or leave it as a privilege that maps neatly onto income. The proposition is the rational, historically precedented answer.

[C1.1] The proposition's strength rests on an analogy, and the analogy is wrong.
[C1.2] Water and electricity earned universal-service status because the minimum standard is binary and stable. Water must be potable and flow. Electricity must sustain a basic load. A utility can meet that duty for decades without redefining it.
[C1.3] "High-quality internet" is none of those things. Speed thresholds, latency tolerances, and reliability metrics shift every few years as applications evolve. A legal entitlement pegged to today's standard is either obsolete on arrival or requires constant regulatory renegotiation—the opposite of the durable duty that makes water and electricity enforceable in the first place.
[C1.4] That instability creates a second problem the proposition ignores. Enforceability means courts and ombudsmen drawing lines. But connectivity gaps are overwhelmingly a function of infrastructure cost and geography, not provider bad faith. A lawsuit forces a company to show why it didn't meet a standard in a specific location; it does not trench fiber to a remote hamlet or lower the per-customer cost of doing so.
[C1.5] Legal duty sounds like a hammer, but the nail here is capital expenditure and terrain.
[C1.6] Nor is the rural-electrification analogy as clean as PRO suggests. Electrification programs succeeded through targeted public investment and cooperative models, not primarily through individual causes of action. The enforcement infrastructure PRO celebrates was a backstop, not the engine. Treating litigation as the core mechanism misreads what actually closed those gaps.
[C1.7] Finally, there is a crowding-out risk. When a government declares a service legally universal, it commits to subsidizing and defending that status against competing claims on the same budget. A statutory right to high-speed broadband, enforced through courts, will consume resources that could fund libraries, transit, or health programs that serve the same disconnected populations more effectively. The dignity of connectivity matters, but so does the opportunity cost of constitutionalizing one service at the expense of others.
[C1.8] The motion fails because it substitutes a legal ritual for the actual mechanisms that close access gaps.
