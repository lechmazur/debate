[P1.1] Shared algorithmic rent-setting is price coordination with a software alibi. Competing landlords feed private occupancy, lease timing, and asking-rent data into one engine; the engine returns aligned recommendations they reliably follow. They have not discovered the same number independently. They have outsourced the cartel meeting.
[P1.2] Antitrust does not require a handshake. It requires that rivals keep pricing decisions independent. A common algorithm trained on confidential competitor data is not a market report. It is a conduit.
[P1.3] Each participant learns, through the output, what others are charging and what the system will support if they refuse to undercut. Widespread adherence is not conscious parallelism from watching for-rent signs. It is delegated, pooled pricing.
[P1.4] Treat this as if they coordinated directly because that is what the conduct does. Direct talks among rivals would draw per se or quick-look scrutiny. The same treatment belongs here: a shared tool, nonpublic data, and systematic following are the plus factors. Relabeling the output a “recommendation” changes nothing if the design goal is revenue maximization by suppressing discounts and deviation is rare.
[P1.5] The opposite rule is an invitation. Every future cartel hires a vendor and a server, then claims the software acted alone. Law that claims to care about effects cannot treat the algorithm as a consumer. It is the alignment mechanism.
[P1.6] Tenants pay more not because every landlord separately found the same rent, but because they stopped competing on it. Independent pricing is the heart of the market. Shared rent-setting tools empty that heart. Scrutiny as direct coordination is how we put it back.

[C1.1] The motion confuses using a common tool with making a common plan. Antitrust condemns agreements, not shared business inputs. A landlord that buys the same rent-setting software as competitors may act unilaterally: no rival has promised to accept the output, and none can sanction another for deviating. That is not direct price coordination.
[C1.2] It is buying a forecast.
[C1.3] What the motion calls delegated pricing assumes what must be proved. Section 1 requires concerted action—a meeting of minds to suppress competition. Recommendations alone do not create a contract, combination, or conspiracy. If tenants see identical rents after competitors use the same model, that can reflect similar demand, unit mix, vacancy, and financing costs, not reciprocal obligation.
[C1.4] Parallel decisions remain parallel. Antitrust has never made mere use of common information illegal, even when prices rise.
[C1.5] Treating every algorithmic recommendation as a cartel would also criminalize ordinary market intelligence. Landlords have always watched listings, hired local agents, and read market surveys. A tool that packages comparable rents and demand trends is a modern version of that. The fact that some tools use confidential data is a separate evidentiary question; it cannot justify a categorical rule against all shared pricing software.
[C1.6] The proposed rule is dangerously overbroad. It says every landlord using a shared tool should face scrutiny “as if” it engaged in direct price-fixing, flattening the difference between a genuinely rigged exchange and an independent pricing aid. That chills cost-saving, vacancy-reducing tools, especially for smaller owners who lack pricing teams. A true cartel hiding behind a vendor can still be caught through plus factors: mandatory adoption, penalties for undercutting, granular nonpublic data, and evidence of expected adherence.
[C1.7] Courts should not skip that proof by making software use itself the offense. Keep the agreement requirement.
