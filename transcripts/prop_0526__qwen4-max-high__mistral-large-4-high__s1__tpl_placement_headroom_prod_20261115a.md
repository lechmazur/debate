[C1.1] The motion concedes the decisive point and then asks us to ignore it. If integration outcomes are truly comparable, the tie should not go to family reunification but to the channel that serves the public interest: economic selection. A state does not admit migrants to maximize individual welfare; it admits them to meet collective needs—funding aging pension systems, staffing hospitals, driving productivity in sectors where natives are scarce. When two applicants integrate equally well over a lifetime, the one selected for skills fills a structural gap that family ties cannot address.
[C1.2] A shortage of welders is solved by a trained welder; a separated spouse is not a substitute for a geriatric nurse or an engineer.
[C1.3] Moreover, "comparable long-term outcomes" masks very different short-term fiscal profiles and infrastructure demands. Family migrants often arrive outside peak earning years, with credentials that are not locally recognized, drawing on housing, schools, and healthcare before contributing significant tax revenue. Even if they catch up over decades, the present strain on housing markets and public services is real. Prioritizing family claims over skilled workers during a housing crisis or when hospital waiting lists are long is a luxury that resents the citizens who fund those services.
[C1.4] Finally, the social contract runs the other way. The welfare state that makes family reunification possible is paid for by economic migrants and native workers alike. Telling a taxpayer that a stranger's diploma outranks their own spouse's visa is not the broken bargain; telling a high-skilled contributor that their place is second to a family claim, despite equal integration, is. When outcomes tie, choose the contribution.
[C1.5] Economic selection is not cold arithmetic; it is the engine that pays for the compassion the proposition wants to prioritize.
