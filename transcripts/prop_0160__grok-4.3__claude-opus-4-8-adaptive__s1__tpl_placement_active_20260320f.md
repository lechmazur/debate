[P1.1] Brain-computer interfaces that write to neural tissue can reshape mood, attention, and decision-making with effects that outlast any single session. Those changes occur inside an organ whose long-term response to repeated artificial input remains poorly mapped outside controlled trials. Ordinary consumer sales remove the requirement for documented safety data and post-market surveillance that medical regulators demand.
[P1.2] Without those controls, companies face direct pressure to ship devices quickly and minimize warnings. A product sold for productivity or entertainment will be used repeatedly and at higher intensities than any clinical protocol would allow. Users experiencing creeping dependence or personality shifts will have no mandated reporting channel or recall mechanism.
[P1.3] Security exposure compounds the problem. A stimulator that accepts external commands can be hijacked to trigger seizures, erase recent memory, or create artificial reward signals. Medical distribution at least limits the number of units and requires verified firmware; retail channels make every unit a potential attack surface reachable through ordinary apps or updates.
[P1.4] The suggestion that adults can simply weigh these risks for themselves assumes equal information between buyer and seller. Manufacturers know far more about failure modes than any purchaser ever will, and market incentives reward understating subtle harms until they become widespread. Medical restriction keeps the technology inside a system that can detect problems early and remove devices before scale turns individual injuries into population-level damage.
