[P1.1] Mass deportations treat a labor-market failure as a policing problem. Gulf and North African economies need workers in construction, farming, domestic care, hospitality and logistics; families need remittances; employers want flexible labor. If legal entry is scarce, slow, sponsor-tied, or expensive, the journey does not disappear. It moves into smugglers' boats, forged contracts, off-book worksites, and debts that keep workers silent.
[P1.2] The test is not removals but legal, visible work under enforceable terms.
[P1.3] Corridors meet that test. Job-linked visas with origin-state agreements, pre-departure screening, recognized contracts, recruitment-fee caps, portable sponsorship, wage-payment trails, complaint channels, and lawful renewal and return options turn migration into a file the state can see. This is not open borders or amnesty; it is control by design. A ministry that knows who arrived, for whom, at what wage, and for how long has more sovereignty than one counting deportation buses.
[P1.4] If the lawful route is cheaper, faster and safer, it starves smugglers of customers instead of making scarcity their business model.
[P1.5] A deportation-first strategy fails on its own deterrent logic. The removed worker often goes home with debt, the same employer demand remains, and re-entry is still possible. Raids then push migrants away from registration and health checks, raise the smuggler's premium, and push employers further off-book. States pay again for arrest, detention, transport and paperwork while recruitment networks adapt.
[P1.6] Severity produces headlines; corridors reduce the pool.
[P1.7] The rule-of-law objection gets the sequence backwards. Law is not strengthened when the only realistic door is held by a trafficker. Targeted removals for serious crimes or security threats, with due process, remain legitimate; mass deportation as strategy is not. It punishes the visible worker while leaving profitable irregular labor intact.
[P1.8] Prioritizing corridors gives receiving states labor supply, origin states consular oversight, migrants enforceable rights, and police a smaller caseload. The choice is not toughness versus leniency. It is a revolving door versus a gate the state actually controls.

[C1.2] Start with North Africa. The assertion that these economies uniformly suffer from labor shortages [P1.1] ignores reality. States like Tunisia, Egypt, and Morocco face severe domestic youth unemployment and sprawling informal sectors. They are primarily transit states for migrants aiming for Europe, not destinations starving for foreign workers.
[C1.3] You cannot build job-linked visa corridors [P1.3] for jobs that do not exist, nor will a migrant paying smugglers to reach Europe settle for a low-wage North African farm contract. When European border controls trap transit populations in North Africa, host states face acute fiscal and social strain. Without mass deportations, North African countries become permanent holding pens for populations their economies cannot absorb.
[C1.4] Now look at the Gulf. Gulf states already operate some of the largest legal labor corridors in the world. Irregular migration there is not driven by the absence of a legal door; it stems from visa overstayers, illicit trading in sponsorship permits, and porous borders with conflict zones. Expanding legal entry does nothing to resolve the large populations already working off-book.
[C1.5] Worse, the corridor mechanism undercuts itself. Adding wage trails, fee caps, and portable contracts [P1.3] makes legal labor more expensive and burdensome for employers. If states abandon mass removals, why would a construction boss or agricultural broker pay the premium for a regulated corridor? Without the credible threat of mass deportation clearing the informal market, regulated corridors price themselves out, pushing employers directly toward cheaper, undocumented workers.
[C1.6] Limiting removals to serious crimes [P1.7] tells every migrant, smuggler, and employer that bypassing immigration law carries no real consequence. States cannot manage legal gates tomorrow if they refuse to enforce their borders today.
