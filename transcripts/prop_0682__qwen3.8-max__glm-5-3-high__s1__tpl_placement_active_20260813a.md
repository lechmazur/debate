[P1.1] Commercial spyware is not a simple commodity; it is a weaponized capability that spreads, mutates, and turns on its sellers. The central question is not whether one agency can conduct one more intrusion, but whether lax exports create a larger, more hostile surveillance ecosystem that eventually threatens our own officials, allies, and infrastructure. Strict export regulation improves national security because it reduces blowback, abuse, and proliferation faster than it sacrifices marginal intelligence.
[P1.2] First, blowback is predictable. When firms sell exploit chains and remote-access tools abroad, those tools can be turned against our diplomats, military networks, legislators, or companies. Even if the original buyer is friendly, regimes change, contractors leak, and adversaries capture or reverse-engineer capabilities. A tool sold today can become the intrusion vector used against us tomorrow.
[P1.3] Second, abuse is not a moral side issue; it is a national-security harm. Spyware marketed to governments has repeatedly been used against journalists, opposition figures, lawyers, and activists. That repression destabilizes partner states, fuels anti-Western grievance, and damages intelligence cooperation. When allied governments are exposed for using imported spyware to crush dissent, the scandal costs us access, trust, and influence.
[P1.4] Third, loose exports feed the gray market that supplies adversaries. Commercial vendors buy vulnerabilities, recruit exploit developers, and normalize the trade in dangerous cyber capabilities. Tight controls shrink that market, raise prices for bad actors, and reduce the pool of zero-days and malware that can later be used against critical infrastructure.
[P1.5] The CON will say we lose intelligence options. The motion concedes that trade-off. The decisive standard is net security, not maximum options. But strict regulation need not mean total prohibition; it means licensing, end-use checks, allied-only sales, and penalties for abuse.
[P1.6] That preserves deliberate, high-value operations while ending the indiscriminate spread of capabilities. National security is best served by fewer, safer, more accountable tools—not by arming the world with spyware that eventually comes home.
