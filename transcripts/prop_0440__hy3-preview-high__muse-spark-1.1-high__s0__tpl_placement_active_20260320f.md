[P1.1] Focused deterrence and violence-interruption programs deliver superior cost-effectiveness in reducing gun violence compared to expanding police staffing, as they target the specific drivers of violence with precision rather than relying on a broad, expensive increase in police presence. These programs operate by identifying high-risk individuals and groups, then applying tailored interventions—such as offering social services or leveraging credible threats of enforcement—which directly disrupt violent pathways. This approach ensures that resources are concentrated where they matter most, avoiding the inefficiency of general policing that often fails to address root causes.
[P1.2] For example, violence-interruption programs use community mediators to de-escalate conflicts before they escalate into shootings, a method that is not only cheaper but also builds trust and prevents retaliatory cycles. In contrast, expanding police staffing requires significant ongoing expenditures, including recruitment, training, salaries, benefits, and equipment, with no guarantee of proportional violence reduction. Studies indicate that adding officers has a limited impact on gun crime, whereas focused deterrence has achieved rapid, measurable declines in violence in multiple urban contexts. Moreover, these programs offer a higher return on investment by resolving disputes at the community level, reducing the long-term costs associated with incarceration and healthcare.
[P1.3] Police expansion, on the other hand, can strain municipal budgets without delivering equivalent results, making it a fiscally imprudent strategy. By prioritizing focused deterrence and violence interruption, US cities can achieve greater safety per dollar spent, rendering the proposition not only cost-effective but also a more strategic use of public funds.
