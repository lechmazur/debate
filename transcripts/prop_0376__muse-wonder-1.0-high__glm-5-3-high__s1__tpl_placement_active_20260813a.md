[P1.1] A credit card charging 28 or 32 percent is not a bridge to opportunity. Put $5,000 on it, pay close to the minimum, and you can spend years watching the balance barely move while paying thousands extra in interest. That is not pricing risk. It is monetizing slow failure — borrowers who struggle and keep revolving are often more profitable than those who pay in full.
[P1.2] The market will not discipline this because the price is designed to be ignored. People choose cards for cash back, airline miles, and sign-up bonuses, confident they will never revolve — until a job loss, car repair, or medical bill makes them do so. Issuers compete loudly on rewards and quietly on the APR buried in the terms. When overconfidence is the sales model, consent to 30 percent is not meaningful freedom.
[P1.3] A cap is therefore justified even if lenders approve fewer high-risk applicants. If the only profitable way to lend to someone is at a rate they predictably cannot sustain, that credit is not helping them. It postpones exclusion while extracting money first: missed payments, penalty fees, damaged credit, collections, and sometimes bankruptcy. Being denied the card avoids that sequence.
[P1.4] Losing access to a harmful product is protection, not a deprivation on par with losing a home loan or an education.
[P1.5] A cap also forces better lending. Instead of approving nearly everyone and sorting them by punitive prices, issuers must actually underwrite, start with smaller limits, offer secured or low-limit starter cards, and design repayment that can succeed. Existing usury limits and special protections for service members have not ended card lending; firms can profit without unlimited APRs when they manage risk upfront.
[P1.6] Inclusion into a debt trap is not inclusion. Real inclusion is a bank account, a debit card, affordable installment credit, and a path to build a record without a balance that snowballs faster than wages. The law already refuses to treat every profitable contract as legitimate. Credit cards should be no exception.

[C1.1] The proposition concedes its own cost: people the mainstream won't profitably lend to at the capped rate will lose access. That concession is fatal, because it swaps an expensive regulated product for something worse.
[C1.2] When legal credit disappears, demand doesn't. The single mother whose car breaks down doesn't stop needing $800 because a card issuer declined her. She goes to a payday lender charging effective rates that make 30% APR look generous, to a pawnshop, to an unlicensed lender with no disclosures, no grace period, and no regulator. We have run this experiment before: rate caps push marginal borrowers toward products that are worse in every dimension the PRO side claims to care about.
[C1.3] A capped credit card that never arrives protects no one from the payday loan they take instead.
[C1.4] Second, the "debt trap" framing ignores who actually uses these cards. A subprime card with a $500 limit is often a lifeline — it smooths income gaps, covers emergencies without eviction, and builds a credit file that later unlocks a car loan or mortgage. Cut that off, and the borrower isn't excluded from a trap; they're excluded from the credit system entirely, locked out of the very "real inclusion" PRO invokes.
[C1.5] Third, the cap won't behave as advertised. Issuers recoup revenue elsewhere: annual fees, lower limits, killed grace periods, slashed rewards. The 48% of cardholders who pay in full each month — disproportionately lower-income households that use cards as free short-term liquidity — lose that tool so the state can feel decisive.
[C1.6] Finally, PRO's behavioral story proves too much. If borrowers irrationally ignore APRs, they also irrationally need credit when emergencies hit. You cannot build policy on consumer irrationality and then assume those same consumers are better off with zero options and a payday storefront.
[C1.7] Punitive-sounding rates reflect real default losses, not pure greed. The cure — exclusion, fee migration, and gray-market substitution — is worse than the disease.

[P2.1] Cutting a 30% card does not automatically manufacture a payday loan. Spending demand is more elastic than my opponent assumes.
[P2.2] Even where the emergency is real, the comparison is rigged. A payday loan is terrible, but it is a short, visible crisis; a revolving card can turn one $800 repair into years of minimum payments, penalty fees, and a damaged score that raises the price of everything else. If payday lending is the danger, regulate it too — do not hold card borrowers hostage to it.
[P2.3] The lifeline claim makes the same mistake. A $500-limit card only builds toward a car loan or mortgage if payments succeed. At punitive APRs, plus the annual and monthly fees common on subprime cards, one missed paycheck can produce the late payments and maxed-out balance that wreck the credit file it was supposed to create. A cap does not ban starter credit; it shifts it toward secured deposits, credit-builder loans, and low limits that can actually be repaid.
[P2.4] Fee migration is not a refutation; it is an improvement in honesty. An upfront fee can be seen, compared, and declined before borrowing. A buried APR is charged after optimism fails. If issuers must compete on prices consumers notice, that is the market working.
[P2.5] And those who pay in full are not funding their own rewards — revolvers paying 30% heavily subsidize them. Protecting that subsidy is not protecting the poor.
[P2.6] Finally, default risk explains a price only if the loan should be made at all. If losses are so high that 30% barely works, the answer is a smaller line or no line — not approving the borrower and profiting as they slowly sink.
