[P1.1] Widening an urban freeway is a promise that cities cannot keep. When you add lanes, you don't permanently relieve congestion—you induce demand. Drivers who previously took transit, biked, or avoided the trip switch to driving, and within a few years the expanded freeway is just as full as before. The city is left with a wider scar through its neighborhoods, more noise and pollution, and the same traffic.
[P1.2] Widening treats the symptom while deepening the disease.
[P1.3] Removal works differently. When Seoul demolished an elevated expressway to restore the Cheonggyecheon stream, it created a public corridor that now serves as a backbone of civic life. When San Francisco tore down the Embarcadero Freeway after the 1989 earthquake, it replaced a concrete barrier with a waterfront boulevard that reconnected the city to its bay. These projects show that freeways don't just move traffic—they suppress the economic and social life of the districts they cross.
[P1.4] Remove them, and land values recover, streets become walkable, pollution drops, and businesses that had struggled behind the shadow of the concrete see renewed foot traffic.
[P1.5] The question isn't whether removal is ever appropriate; it's which action should be the default. Many urban freeways are now reaching the end of their design life, forcing billion-dollar rebuild decisions. Spending that money to widen asphalt contradicts climate goals and entrenches car dependency. Because widening reliably fails to deliver lasting relief while imposing permanent costs on adjacent communities—often the same neighborhoods displaced when the freeway was first built—and because removal consistently returns valuable urban land to housing, transit, or parks, cities should choose removal far more often than expansion.
[P1.6] Every widening proposal should face a heavy burden of proof that removal could not serve the city better, and most will fail that test.

[C1.4] Congestion disappearing because access became too costly mainly burdens people with rigid schedules and few alternatives.
[C1.5] Seoul and San Francisco show possibility, not a rule. [P1.3]'s cases had dense transit, strong grids, iconic waterfront or stream settings, and enough redundancy to reroute traffic. Removal fits redundant spurs, not an entire network default.
[C1.6] The choice as written is removal versus widening, not removal versus utopia. Demolition offers a visible ribbon of land but an irreversible capacity loss, pushing delay and pollution toward commuters and peripheral communities.
[C1.7] So [P1.5]'s heavy burden belongs on demolition. Before removing a freeway, a city should prove the corridor is redundant, transit already adequate, freight and emergency routes intact, and surface streets able to absorb the traffic.
