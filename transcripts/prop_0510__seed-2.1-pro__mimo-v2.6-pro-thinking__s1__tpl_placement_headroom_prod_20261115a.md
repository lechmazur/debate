[P1.1] Automatic fiscal stabilizers—progressive tax offsets, unemployment insurance, basic household assistance, and formula-driven aid to state and local governments—should expand in downturns even when public debt ratios are high, because restricting them does far more to damage long-run debt sustainability than the temporary support itself. When private demand collapses, these stabilizers deploy instantly, no legislative gridlock required, to prevent cascading layoffs, small business failures, and permanent labor force exit that creates long-term economic scarring. That scarring is the single biggest threat to long-run debt sustainability: when workers lose skills and drop out of the workforce, or viable firms collapse for lack of short-term cash, future tax revenue shrinks permanently, and mandatory social costs stay elevated for years, pushing debt ratios higher than temporary expanded support ever could.
[P1.2] Spending from these stabilizers carries an unusually high multiplier in downturns, because cash flows directly to households that spend every dollar immediately rather than save it, so each dollar delivers outsized GDP gains that offset much of its upfront cost. Critics who point to high debt as a reason to hold back confuse one-time, cyclical spending with permanent structural deficits. Expanded automatic stabilizers wind down on their own as employment and incomes recover, with no built-in ratchet for permanent spending.
[P1.3] Worse, austerity for stabilizers during high-debt downturns is self-defeating: the sharp GDP contraction triggered by pulled support raises the debt-to-GDP ratio faster than modest spending cuts can lower it, as seen across multiple eurozone economies in the 2010s, where cuts to automatic support extended recessions and left debt ratios higher a decade later. In most downturns, sovereign borrowing costs fall as investors seek safe assets, making temporary support cheap to finance even for high-debt states. Waiting for debt ratios to fall before deploying stabilizers traps economies in weak growth, high unemployment, and perpetually elevated debt.
