[P1.1] A price that isn't the price isn't a price. When a delivery app shows you $14.99, adds a "service fee," a "small order fee," and a "regulatory response fee" only on the payment screen, it hasn't quoted you a price — it has quoted you bait, and then asked you to pay something else.
[P1.2] Three reasons this proposition should carry.
[P1.3] First, markets run on comparability. The whole justification for letting private firms set prices is that buyers can weigh one against another. Mandatory charges the seller already knows about, but conceals until the last click, destroy exactly that function. Worse, they punish the honest operator: the restaurant that folds its 18% "service charge" into menu prices looks expensive next to the one that hides it, so the market rewards concealment.
[P1.4] Disclosure doesn't restrain competition here; it restores it.
[P1.5] Second, the payment screen is the one moment when your leverage is gone. You've spent twenty minutes picking seats, or your cart is built, or a countdown timer says your tickets release in four minutes. Revealing a mandatory fee there isn't informing a decision — it's collecting on a decision already made. Consent extracted under a clock, after sunk effort, is the thinnest kind of consent there is.
[P1.6] Move the number three screens earlier and it becomes a real choice.
[P1.7] Third, the cost of compliance is close to zero, because the seller is not discovering these fees at checkout. A mandatory service charge is a fixed, known, seller-set number. The math has already been done; the only decision is whether to share it. Any complaint about burden collapses into a complaint about losing the concealment.
[P1.8] Note what this does not require. It doesn't ban service charges, cap them, or force anyone to predict variable taxes or your voluntary tip. Charge 20% if you like — just say so before the customer is committed. If a fee is genuinely justified, early disclosure costs the business nothing.
[P1.9] If it can only be collected by surprise, that is the strongest possible argument for disclosing it.

[C1.1] Checkout is not a trap. It is the first moment the seller actually knows what you owe.
[C1.2] A delivery app browsing a menu cannot truthfully quote your service fee before it knows your address, cart size, whether your order can be batched, or how far the driver must travel. A restaurant cannot quote a large-party service charge before it knows your party size. A ticketing site cannot give an exact order-processing fee before it knows how many seats, which tier, and whether you pay by credit or wallet. The proposition demands early disclosure of numbers that do not exist yet.
[C1.3] The result is not transparency but fiction: estimated totals that will be wrong, then revised at payment anyway, leaving shoppers more confused and distrustful.
[C1.4] Second, itemization at checkout aids comparison, it does not defeat it. A $15 dish with a 4% kitchen health surcharge listed separately tells a diner something different from a $15.60 dish with higher base pay built in. One reveals how the business funds wages; the other buries it. Forcing everything into the headline price destroys that information and punishes operators trying to be open about why prices rose.
[C1.5] Third, early sticker shock hurts the very workers this idea claims to protect. Many restaurants added service charges precisely to replace volatile tips with stable wages and benefits. Plaster that surcharge across the front window and browsing screen, stripped of context, and price-sensitive customers walk away before a server can explain it. Owners then face pressure to scrap the model and cut guaranteed pay.
[C1.6] And no one is paying blind. The final screen still shows the total before money changes hands, and the customer can still walk away. Regulating the sequence of truthful screens adds clutter, cost for small menus and apps, and false precision — while calling it consumer protection.
