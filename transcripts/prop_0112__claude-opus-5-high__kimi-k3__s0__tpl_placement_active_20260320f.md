[P1.1] Start with the design flaw at the heart of employer-based insurance: it withdraws coverage at precisely the moment people need it most. Getting sick is one of the most common reasons people stop being able to work. A system that ties the insurance to the job is therefore a system that cancels the policy when the claim arrives. Picture a warehouse worker with a cancer diagnosis: the chemotherapy that saves her life also costs her the shifts that pay for it.
[P1.2] No insurance product designed on purpose would work that way. It survives only as an accident of history — a wartime wage-control workaround that hardened into a national institution.
[P1.3] The same flaw scales up. Employment collapses in recessions, which is exactly when household savings are thinnest and when public health needs peak. In a pandemic or a downturn, an employer-linked system sheds millions of people from coverage in the same quarter it should be expanding it. Single-payer financing is countercyclical by construction: eligibility is residency, not payroll.
[P1.4] Second, notice who actually pays now. Employer premiums are not employer generosity; they are compensation, taken out of wages before workers ever see them. So the honest comparison is not "free coverage versus taxes," it is one form of deduction against another — except the employer version hides the cost, gives the worker no bargaining power over the plan, and locks him to a firm he would otherwise leave. That is job lock, and it suppresses exactly the risk-taking and entrepreneurship the market case is supposed to prize.
[P1.5] Third, the motion concerns *basic* coverage. That matters. A single-payer floor can coexist with private supplemental insurance, private providers, and paid upgrades — so arguments about wait times for elective procedures or luxury choice do not defeat it. They argue about the layers above the floor.
[P1.6] The question is simply whether the floor should have a hole in it shaped like your employment status. It should not.
