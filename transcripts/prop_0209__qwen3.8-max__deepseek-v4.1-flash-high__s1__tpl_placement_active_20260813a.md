[P1.1] The proposition asks whether the EU should replace a system in which accidents of geography determine responsibility with one in which all member states share responsibility. It should, because asylum policy cannot be fair, lawful, or effective if the first country a person reaches is expected to absorb the consequences for everyone else.
[P1.2] Frontline states such as Greece, Italy, and Spain do not merely face more arrivals; they face arrivals that are predictable products of Mediterranean and Aegean routes. When responsibility is tied to first entry, the same few states are pushed into perpetual emergency. Overcrowded reception centers, slow procedures, and pressure to deter new crossings are not incidental failures; they are the structural result of asking border states to act as Europe’s gatekeepers without Europe’s support. That environment encourages rights violations, improvised camps, and political resentment, while leaving other member states free to opt out.
[P1.3] Burden-sharing quotas change those incentives. A predictable allocation mechanism would move people from overwhelmed islands and coastal towns into systems with real capacity, shorten reception bottlenecks, and make asylum processing more credible. It would also reduce the destructive competition in which frontline states are tempted to make arrival as miserable as possible while interior states pretend the problem will disappear. Solidarity becomes operational rather than rhetorical.
[P1.4] Quotas need not be rigid or blind. They can account for population, wealth, existing reception capacity, and local integration conditions, and can be paired with financial and administrative contributions for states temporarily unable to receive large numbers. The key principle is that responsibility is common and mandatory, not voluntary and selective.
[P1.5] The alternative leaves the EU with permanent crisis bargaining: emergency summits, ad hoc ship disembarkation disputes, and frontline states bearing costs that belong to the Union as a whole. If the EU wants a common asylum system, it must accept common responsibility.

[C1.1] Quotas sound like solidarity, but they are a redistribution mechanism aimed at the wrong part of the problem. The frontline burden is not mainly where a recognized refugee eventually settles; it is the immediate, unavoidable work of rescue, screening, reception, legal appeals, and returns. A quota can relocate some asylum seekers after status is decided, often slowly. It does not absorb the initial arrivals.
[C1.2] If a state receives a thousand people and a quota later moves a fraction onward, that state still manages the crisis at the point of entry. So the proposition’s central promise—that quotas end the gatekeeper role—does not follow.
[C1.3] Mandatory quotas are also politically brittle. They require member states to accept people they may not want, in places where language, housing, and labor markets may not fit. When governments refuse, the EU must either fine them, sue them, or dilute the quota until it means little. That is not durable burden-sharing; it is a recurring constitutional fight.
[C1.4] If quotas are flexible enough to win consent, they are no longer binding; if binding, they provoke the resistance that makes them hollow. A voluntary scheme with serious funding, joint processing, and flexible relocation can actually move people and resources.
[C1.5] Worse, quotas invite disputes over who counts, where people are sent, and which state is responsible, while encouraging secondary movement toward preferred countries. They also give populists a ready grievance: Brussels is forcing migrants on your town. That backlash shrinks the political space for any humane policy.
[C1.6] The real alternative is not abandonment. It is operational solidarity: money for reception, EU asylum support teams, joint return arrangements, and voluntary relocation from overwhelmed states. That targets the bottleneck directly while preserving consent. The motion asks us to choose a rigid allocation formula over practical support.
[C1.7] Reject it.

[P2.1] My opponent says quotas are “aimed at the wrong part of the problem,” but their case depends on a narrow version of quotas the motion does not require. Burden-sharing can begin at registration, not after years of status determination. If applicants are quickly allocated to states with reception capacity, frontline countries no longer bear the full weight of rescue, housing, interviews, appeals, and, where claims fail, returns. Some initial processing will always occur where people arrive; the question is whether that burden remains trapped there.
[P2.2] Quotas break that trap by making transfer a normal administrative step rather than an emergency negotiation.
[P2.3] Their voluntary alternative repeats the failure we already know. Funding and support teams help, but they do not decide who must host. If relocation is optional, frontline states remain supplicants at every surge, waiting for coalitions of the willing while camps overflow. That is not operational solidarity; it is discretion dressed as solidarity.
[P2.4] The Union’s core problem is not lack of money in the abstract. It is that responsibility is legally concentrated where geography places it. Only a mandatory allocation rule converts common responsibility into shared practice.
[P2.5] They warn quotas are politically brittle. But the present system is already brittle: it produces visible disorder, mutual blame, and populist claims that borders are abandoned. A binding quota, adjusted for population, wealth, and capacity, is not rigid central planning; it is the minimum condition for common responsibility. It does not create backlash out of nothing; it removes the chaos that fuels it.
[P2.6] It also reduces secondary movement, because people have a lawful assigned destination instead of an incentive to vanish toward preferred states. If some states resist, the answer is enforceable EU law, not surrendering fairness to veto politics. The EU cannot claim a common asylum system while leaving the hardest duties to the few states nearest the sea.

People will abscond or move on; states will respond with detention and coercion.
[C2.5] Voluntary operational solidarity is not mere discretion if funding and processing support are conditioned on measurable reception and fair procedures. It can surge resources to the bottleneck without forcing every state into a hosting mandate it may sabotage. The motion asks for quotas instead; that trade is bad.
[C2.6] Decide on this: mandatory quotas may satisfy a principle of fairness among states, but they do not lift the frontline's immediate burden and they poison the cooperation needed to do so. A practical system should move capacity to the crisis, not just numbers around a map.
