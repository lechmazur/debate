[P1.1] Exporting raw cocoa and importing expensive chocolate, exporting crude oil and importing petrol, exporting cobalt and importing batteries is not a trade strategy. It is a leak. Value, jobs, and technical knowledge are created elsewhere, while African economies keep the riskiest, lowest-paid part of the chain.
[P1.2] That dependence is structurally punishing. Raw commodity prices swing with distant recessions, speculation, and decisions in foreign capitals, wrecking budgets and currencies overnight. Former colonial powers and other rich buyers also set the terms through standards, subsidies, and tariffs that often rise precisely when goods are processed — welcoming beans, penalizing chocolate. A country that only digs and ships has little bargaining power and learns little it can reuse.
[P1.3] Prioritizing intra-African trade and manufacturing reverses that logic. When Ghanaian cocoa is processed for West African consumers, when Moroccan fertilizer feeds Senegalese farms, when Kenyan, Ethiopian, and Egyptian firms build regional chains in textiles, cement, pharmaceuticals, and processed food, more of each dollar stays on the continent. Factories create the dense employment Africa’s young population actually needs — not a handful of enclave mining jobs, but work in logistics, engineering, packaging, maintenance, marketing, and supply firms around the plant.
[P1.4] Regional demand also makes industrialization possible. Many African economies are too small alone to sustain efficient factories, but together they form a market of well over a billion people with rising needs for exactly the goods neighbors can make: food staples, medicines, building materials, clothing, simple machinery. Trade within Africa is naturally more diversified toward manufactured and processed goods than exports overseas, which remain dominated by unprocessed commodities. Building roads, power pools, payment systems, and harmonized rules for that market — the promise of continental free trade — does more for long-run sovereignty than another decade chasing distant preferences.
[P1.5] Prioritization does not mean refusing to sell to the world. It means stopping the organization of ports, credit, and infrastructure around extraction first. Sell abroad from strength, after adding value at home and next door, rather than begging abroad from weakness.
