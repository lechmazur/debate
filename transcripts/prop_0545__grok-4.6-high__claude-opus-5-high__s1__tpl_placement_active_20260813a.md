[P1.1] The current opt-in system buries organs most people would have given. That is not neutrality. It is a default that lets patients die on waiting lists while pretending to honor choice.
[P1.2] Most people already favor donation. They never register. Presumed consent captures that majority preference: refusal becomes the act that requires paperwork, not generosity. Transplant rates rise because inertia stops working against donation.
[P1.3] For someone waiting on a kidney, that is not a theoretical nudge. It is the difference between a usable organ leaving the hospital and years of dialysis, or death.
[P1.4] Where countries adopted soft presumed consent—easy opt-out, family consultation, public education—donation has risen. Infrastructure matters, but the default does real work. Fewer families confront a blank registry because the deceased never got around to saying yes.
[P1.5] This is not taking organs from the unwilling. Anyone who objects can refuse quickly and permanently. Soft systems still talk to families. Ethical violations do not increase because autonomy is preserved where it actually matters: a real chance to say no. Silence is no longer treated as a veto against a preference most people already hold.
[P1.6] Family distress does not climb either. Uncertainty is what wounds people. A known public default plus a structured conversation gives relatives a clearer starting point than silence. They are not steamrolled; they are informed.
[P1.7] In well-run systems the conversation still happens, and families retain a meaningful voice.
[P1.8] For most patients on the list, the status quo already imposes the greater harm: unused organs and preventable death. Align the default with what most people already want, keep refusal easy, keep families involved, and transplant rates rise without manufacturing new ethical violations or extra grief. Keep opt-in only if unused organs should stay unused.
