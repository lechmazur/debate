In each case, the severing of family memory was no accident of history — it was an act of government, executed through paperwork.
[P1.2] The question is who owes the repair. Our answer: the institution that did the severing.
[P1.3] First, the principle. These records are not hobbyist trivia; they are the state's own documentation of its own conduct. Slave schedules, adoption court files, indigenous enrollment and mission records, displaced-person registries — governments created them to administer dispossession. Charging descendants a subscription fee to buy back evidence of their own family's dispossession compounds the original wrong.
[P1.4] The duty of repair follows the deed.
[P1.5] Second, the market fails exactly where the need is greatest. Private genealogy firms optimize for paying hobbyists with clean paper trails. But the descendants of enslaved people, closed adoptions, and the Stolen Generations face fragmentary, scattered, expensive-to-reconstruct archives — and often the least disposable income to chase them. Profit-seeking rationally under-serves them.
[P1.6] That is textbook market failure: a non-rivalrous public good with high fixed digitization costs and enormous social returns no subscription captures.
[P1.7] Third, the stakes are material, not sentimental. These records ground citizenship and tribal enrollment claims, land and reparations cases, medical histories, and the basic human interest in knowing who you are. When access depends on ability to pay, justice itself is rationed by income.

[P1.9] The state wrote these families out of the record. The least it can do is fund writing them back in — publicly, and for everyone.

[C1.1] The proposition confuses publication with repair. Sealed adoption files, slave ledgers, residential-school registers, and wartime displacement lists are not one public dataset. [C1.2] It is a second transfer of power over other people's kin.
[C1.3] Start with consent. These files do not speak with one voice. Living birth parents were promised confidentiality. Tribal nations claim authority over membership rolls the colonial state has no right to republish.
[C1.4] Displacement records name victims, informants, and bystanders who never opted into a searchable government portal. Publicly funded access flattens those conflicts into a bureaucratic product. Repair that overrides the people named is just another administration of their lives.
[C1.5] The duty-of-repair slogan points the wrong way. If the state wrote families out of the record, it is the last institution that should monopolize writing them back in. State digitization means political redaction, delay, and a permanent identity file sitting beside tax, police, and welfare systems that already reuse data. Markets and descendant-led archives are imperfect.
[C1.6] A politically managed mega-archive is worse.
[C1.7] The market-failure story invents a cartoon alternative. Recovery is not mostly a subscription wall. Courthouses, national archives, churches, tribes, volunteer indexers, and nonprofits already open records. Genealogy firms compete on search tools; they do not own the underlying public files.
[C1.8] Where access is wrongly sealed, give targeted legal rights to the people named—not a free family-history utility for everyone.
[C1.9] Citizenship, enrollment, and land claims need authenticated documents inside legal processes, not a public browse of everyone else's kin. Ability to pay is a real barrier in some cases; the answer is fee waivers and legal discovery, not a state-run genealogy commons. Digitizing scattered, handwritten, privacy-laden files is neither one-time nor modest. The least the state can do is stop becoming the publisher of other people's families.
