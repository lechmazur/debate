[P1.1] A recommendation system is not a neutral shelf of videos or games. It decides what appears next, how quickly, and in what order, learning which rewards keep a particular child engaged. Yet the adult responsible for that child’s sleep, schooling, and wellbeing often receives only blunt tools—time limits or content filters—while the engagement machinery remains under the app maker’s control. That allocation of power is backwards.
[P1.2] Parents should be able to disable personalized feeds, autoplay, and similar recommendation features, or delay them until a child is ready. A ten-year-old may be perfectly capable of watching a chosen video but poorly equipped to resist an endless sequence engineered to make stopping feel like missing out. Children’s self-regulation is still developing, and the system can experiment at a scale and speed no tired parent standing in a kitchen can match. Giving parents an off switch changes that contest: the child can still access age-appropriate material, but must choose rather than be continuously pulled.
[P1.3] This is also a question of responsibility. App makers generally profit when sessions lengthen; parents deal with the bedtime conflict, lost attention, and distress that can follow. The party insulated from those costs should not possess the final say over whether a family may avoid the feature producing them. Nor does an app’s average benefit answer the objection.
[P1.4] A recommendation system that is harmless or useful for many children may still be wrong for a particular child after bullying, compulsive use, or sleep problems. Parents need discretion precisely because children are not interchangeable.
[P1.5] The motion is deliberately proportionate. It does not ban recommendation technology, prescribe one age for everyone, or let parents dictate what other families see. Blocking and delaying are reversible, feature-specific choices. CON must defend a much stranger principle: that parents may set bedtimes, buy the device, and remain accountable for the child, but a company’s engagement design may override their judgment.
[P1.6] Where a child’s developmental risk meets a reversible control, the presumption should favor the caregiver—not the recommender.

[C1.1] The motion mistakes a product feature for a parental control, and the cure it prescribes would damage children more than the engagement loops it targets. Recommendation systems are not optional modules that app makers reluctantly attach; they are the infrastructure that makes modern platforms functional. A video service without personalization is not a safer YouTube—it is a broken search engine forcing children to hunt through irrelevant content, often pushing them toward riskier workarounds like unmoderated sites or fake accounts to find what they want.
[C1.2] The "off switch" is largely a technical fiction, and "delaying" features until a child is "ready" simply drives teenagers to register as adults, destroying the very age-verification safeguards the motion claims to value.
[C1.3] More troubling is what parental blocking power actually enables. When parents can veto algorithmic feeds, they gain editorial control over what ideas their children encounter. A parent can already limit screen time; the proposed power goes further, allowing ideological filtering of discovery. For a teenager questioning their identity in an unsupportive household, algorithmic recommendations are often the only safe route to community and accurate health information.
[C1.4] A parental kill-switch does not protect that child—it isolates them. The motion assumes parents always exercise this power benevolently, ignoring that the same tool that blocks gambling ads can block suicide prevention resources or diverse viewpoints.
[C1.5] Existing tools already solve the stated problem. Screen-time limits, restricted modes, and content filters give parents real authority over usage without requiring platforms to dismantle their core functionality. The proposition demands a veto over design, not just discipline over devices. That is not parental empowerment—it is a license to curate reality, and children bear the cost of the resulting blind spots.
