[P1.1] In the era of physical media and broadcast syndication, a studio giving up on a film or series meant it moved to bargain bins or late-night reruns. In the streaming era, when a platform purges one of its own originals to harvest a tax write-off or dodge residual payments, the work vanishes from legal existence. That is not normal catalog management; it is state-subsidized cultural erasure, and a minimum availability requirement is the simplest way to stop it.
[P1.2] Consider what makes streaming originals uniquely vulnerable to this abuse. Streaming giants act simultaneously as studio, distributor, and exclusive exhibitor. When a service removes an exclusive series like *Willow* just months after its finale, or purges completed shows like *Infinity Train*, there is no physical Blu-ray or rival network waiting in the wings. Because the platform holds a monopoly over the title's distribution, early removal turns finished art into instant lost media.
[P1.3] Requiring a minimum window of public availability, whether kept on the host service or licensed elsewhere, ensures that exclusive control does not become a license to destroy.
[P1.4] Second, early removals weaponize accounting against creative labor. Hosting a digital file costs virtually nothing. Platforms purge originals to sever residual obligations, avoid ongoing licensing fees, and book immediate write-downs during corporate restructurings. Writers, actors, and crews routinely accept lower upfront pay in streaming on the promise of long-tail availability and performance royalties.
[P1.5] Letting executives pocket the subscriber surge of a premiere and then delete the show to stiff the workers who made it is a blatant breach of good faith.
[P1.6] Finally, this rule protects consumers and taxpayers. Subscribers buy into ecosystems marketed on permanent flagship libraries, not vanishing acts. Meanwhile, the public should never subsidize corporate tax deductions for locking finished art in a vault.

[C1.1] Notice the contradiction at the centre of the case for this mandate. We are told "hosting a digital file costs virtually nothing" [P1.4], and in the same breath that platforms remove shows to escape "residual obligations" and "ongoing licensing fees." Both cannot be true. The server is not the cost of keeping a title up.
[C1.2] The cost is the residuals, music clearances and third-party licence payments that fall due as long as it stays public. This motion is therefore a legal duty to keep paying for a product the publisher has judged a failure.
[C1.3] That duty changes behaviour long before any show is removed. If every greenlight carries a compulsory multi-year liability, commissioning becomes more conservative. The cult animation, the odd mid-budget drama and the niche experiment become the riskiest bets on the slate, and those are exactly the shows this mandate claims to defend. Platforms would order fewer of them, or buy out creators upfront and strip the back-end entirely.
[C1.4] A rule written to protect *Infinity Train* makes the next one less likely to be made.
[C1.5] The mandate also misfires on its own terms. Many "originals" are licensed from outside studios for fixed terms, and the platform cannot lawfully keep what it no longer has rights to show. A struggling or collapsing service cannot be ordered to keep exhibiting. And "vanishes from legal existence" [P1.1] overstates the facts, since a number of purged titles later resurfaced on ad-supported channels or digital storefronts.
[C1.6] That is the market doing what the motion would do by force.
[C1.7] Each grievance raised has a narrower remedy:
[C1.8] - **Tax abuse:** fix the tax code by conditioning write-downs.
[C1.9] - **Broken promises to workers:** enforce them through contracts and collective bargaining, where residual terms are actually set.
[C1.10] - **Preservation:** require archival deposit, or let rights revert to creators when a platform walks away.
[C1.11] None of these needs the state to compel a company to keep publishing. So the question is which works better, targeted fixes or a blunt mandate that taxes creative risk. [clipped]
