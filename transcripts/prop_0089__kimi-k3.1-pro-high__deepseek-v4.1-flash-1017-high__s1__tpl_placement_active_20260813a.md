[C1.1] The proposition does not ask whether some preschool can help some children. It asks whether universal programs produce lasting educational and social gains large enough to justify their public cost. That is a much heavier burden, and it is where this case fails.
[C1.2] The strongest pre-K evidence comes from small, intensive, often targeted programs with well-trained staff, low child-adult ratios, and wrap-around family support. They are not proof that a statewide entitlement—delivered through uneven providers, variable quality, and rushed expansion—will reproduce those results. When programs scale, quality is the first thing to bend. A classroom with an overwhelmed teacher and too many toddlers is not “infrastructure”; it is expensive custodial care with an education label.
[C1.3] Nor can the defense wave away fadeout. Early test-score gains often shrink, and later effects on graduation, behavior, or earnings are inconsistent across large public programs. PRO says we should measure life outcomes instead. Fine—but then PRO must show those durable outcomes at universal scale, not infer them from boutique programs or from the mere plausibility of brain development. “Advantages compound” is a hypothesis, not a fiscal justification.
[C1.4] Universality makes the cost problem worse. A large share of the subsidy flows to families who already enroll their children in preschool or would pay privately. That is not serving the disadvantaged; it is replacing private spending with public spending while adding a new middle-class entitlement. The money has an opportunity cost: smaller classes, better pay for effective teachers, targeted help for children who need it most, or direct family support.
[C1.5] So the central question is not whether early childhood matters. It is whether this particular universal promise delivers enough durable benefit per dollar. PRO must prove that; aspiration is not evidence.
