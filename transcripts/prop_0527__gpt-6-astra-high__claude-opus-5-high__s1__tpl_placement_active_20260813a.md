[P1.1] Countries should permit advance market commitments for new antibiotics because the cheapest dose is worthless when it cannot treat the infection. The relevant question is whether a commitment buys enough clinical protection to justify its cost—not whether every successful antibiotic eventually has a low unit price.
[P1.2] Antibiotics face a structural market failure: we want companies to develop them, then want clinicians to use them sparingly. Research is expensive and uncertain, while stewardship deliberately limits sales. A drug held in reserve against resistant infections can therefore be socially valuable but commercially unattractive. An advance market commitment addresses that mismatch: before development, purchasers credibly promise a defined market for an antibiotic that meets specified safety, effectiveness, and unmet-need requirements.
[P1.3] That makes future demand more predictable without requiring mass prescribing.
[P1.4] High unit prices do not automatically mean poor value. Imagine a reserve antibiotic used for a small number of otherwise difficult-to-treat infections. Its acquisition cost must be compared with the consequences of ineffective treatment: prolonged hospitalization, additional procedures, and avoidable deaths. A substantial price per course can coexist with limited total expenditure and meaningful health gains.
[P1.5] Rejecting the commitment simply because the price remains high confuses the cost of a product with the value of having an effective treatment available.
[P1.6] Permission should come with disciplined contracting, not blank checks. Countries can cap total commitments, make purchases conditional on demonstrated clinical usefulness and reliable supply, and direct guaranteed procurement into controlled reserves rather than prescribing quotas. Public purchasing can keep a high manufacturer price from becoming a prohibitive bedside charge. Eligibility should reward meaningful advances against priority resistance problems, not merely another interchangeable product.
[P1.7] Other tools, including research grants and stewardship, remain useful, but neither alone guarantees a market for a successful reserve drug. Prohibiting commitments unless unit prices fall would remove a tool precisely where low sales make development hardest. Preserve the option, scrutinize each contract, and judge the expenditure against the harm of having no effective antibiotic.
