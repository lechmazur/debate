[C1.1] The proposition's core error is conflating data collection with harm. Most personal data harvesting for advertising is benign—it helps users find relevant products, discover local services, and avoid irrelevant noise. When someone searches for hiking boots, seeing hiking boot ads rather than random cosmetics is not a violation; it is a convenience. The regulatory presumption should match reality: advertising data flows are generally harmless until proven otherwise.
[C1.2] The current ecosystem funds the free internet. Search engines, email services, news sites, and social platforms serve billions at zero direct cost because advertisers pay to reach relevant audiences. An opt-in default would collapse this model. When GDPR-style consent requirements were implemented, opt-in rates for tracking typically ranged from 10-30%—insufficient to sustain current advertising economics.
[C1.3] The predictable result would be subscription walls, degraded services, and reduced innovation, disproportionately harming lower-income users who cannot pay for what was previously free.
[C1.4] The horror stories PRO cites—location tracking of clinic visitors, predatory lending ads—describe conduct that is already illegal or regulable through targeted rules. Stalking, harassment, and discriminatory lending are unlawful regardless of how data was collected. The solution is enforcing those laws, not presuming all data collection unlawful. A blanket presumption sweeps in the local bakery advertising to nearby customers, the job board matching seekers with openings, and fraud detection systems flagging suspicious transactions—all without addressing actual wrongdoing.
[C1.5] Opt-in consent creates consent fatigue, not informed choice. Users already click through countless prompts without reading; adding more barriers trains people to ignore them while degrading their experience. The superior approach regulates specific harmful uses—prohibiting sale of sensitive location data, restricting targeting based on financial vulnerability—while permitting ordinary advertising flows. Harm, not collection, should trigger legal intervention.
