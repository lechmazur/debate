[P1.1] Most sports fans care about one team, maybe two. Yet the dominant streaming model forces a fan of a single club to pay for every team in the league, every competition on the platform, and often an entire catalog of content they will never open. That structure is not a natural feature of sports broadcasting. It is a legacy of the cable bundle, preserved because it extracts maximum revenue from the largest possible audience.
[P1.2] Streaming has removed the technical justification for that model. What remains is a business choice—one that overcharges fans, prices out the next generation, and stifles the growth of fandom itself.
[P1.3] The case for team-specific and competition-specific passes rests on three realities. First, the marginal fan is already walking away. A supporter who wants to follow one club through a season is asked to pay the same price as a neutral who watches five matches a week. That is not sustainable.
[P1.4] Younger fans, lower-income fans, and casual fans are not buying in—they are opting out entirely, turning to highlights, piracy, or simply losing the habit. Cheaper, targeted passes would bring them back.
[P1.5] Second, the technology is already here. Streaming platforms can gate content by team, competition, or date with trivial ease. The infrastructure exists; the pricing models do not. Broadcasters are choosing not to offer granular options because the bundle cross-subsidizes less popular content and protects margins.
[P1.6] But protecting margins by forcing unwilling customers to subsidize products they do not want is not a defense—it is the problem.
[P1.7] Third, offering targeted passes does not require abolishing bundles. Fans who want everything can still buy everything. The proposition is simply that broadcasters should also offer a cheaper, narrower option. Opposing that means defending a world where a fan of one team has no legitimate path to follow that team at a fair price.
[P1.8] That position is untenable. The future of sports viewership depends on meeting fans where they are—not where the bundle demands they be.
