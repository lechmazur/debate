[P1.1] When a parent dies, their bookshelf doesn't evaporate. But today, their Kindle library, iTunes collection, and family photo cloud often do—locked behind passwords and Terms of Service that treat death as a breach of contract. The law should guarantee that purchased digital libraries and non-transferable assets pass to next of kin because digital property deserves the same inheritance protection as physical property, and because the current default is the systematic destruction of irreplaceable personal and cultural records.
[P1.2] Consumers pay full price for digital books, music, games, and films under the reasonable expectation that they own something durable. A paperback novel passes to heirs; an ebook bought for the same price currently vanishes into a corporate account. The average household stores thousands of dollars in licensed media and irreplaceable photos in the cloud, yet most people do not realize these assets have a shorter legal lifespan than the cardboard box they replaced. When a grandmother's decades of digitized family photographs become inaccessible because her account is memorialized or deleted, the loss isn't merely economic—it's the erasure of family history that no probate court can restore once the data is purged.
[P1.3] The "non-transferable" label should not mean "non-inheritable." Licenses restrict resale to commercial strangers, but next of kin occupy a unique legal and moral position: they are successors to the deceased's personhood, not third-party buyers. Statutes granting fiduciaries lawful access to digital estates recognize this distinction, yet without a guarantee, access remains subject to the unilateral discretion of platforms that routinely deny it. The technical mechanisms already exist—legacy contacts, encrypted digital vaults, court-appointed custodians—but only a legal mandate ensures they function for every family, not just those wealthy enough to hire lawyers.
[P1.4] Guaranteeing inheritance does not force platforms to enable piracy; it ensures that what was purchased can be preserved by those who survive. A digital library is a repository of memory and culture that belongs to the family, not the server.

[C1.2] First, mandating inheritable licenses punishes living consumers. A paperback [P1.2] wears out; a digital file never degrades. Authors, studios, and indie developers price one-time digital purchases, often through deep discounts and bundles, around a single user’s lifetime. If the law converts personal licenses into multi-generational entitlements, every sale carries perpetual servicing costs and cannibalizes future demand.
[C1.3] Rational rights-holders will respond either by raising upfront prices for living buyers or by retiring "buy" buttons altogether in favor of pure monthly subscriptions, where nothing is owned at all. Guaranteeing inheritance does not save digital ownership; it hastens its extinction.
[C1.4] Second, the law cannot guarantee what platforms neither wholly own nor cleanly divide. Storefronts operate under bounded distribution contracts with publishers, studios, and music labels; issuing a fresh license to an heir triggers upstream copyright restrictions and expiring third-party rights. Moreover, digital libraries are integrated accounts, not shelves of standalone objects. Guaranteeing transfer forces platforms, including small indie storefronts, to adjudicate probate filings, unbundle intertwined software or game entitlements across multiple heirs, and maintain legacy authentication servers indefinitely.
[C1.5] Finally, digital libraries are intensely private. Unlike a living-room bookshelf curated for guests, personal devices hold unfiltered inner lives: therapy or addiction workbooks, dissident politics, and intimate fiction bought on the promise of a non-transferable personal account. Because most people die unexpectedly or without itemized digital wills, sweeping libraries into inheritable estates hands executors and next of kin, even estranged relatives, a complete ledger of private choices never meant to outlive the buyer.
