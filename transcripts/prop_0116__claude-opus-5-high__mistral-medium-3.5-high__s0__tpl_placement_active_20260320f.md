[P1.1] A university can charge $60,000 for a two-year master's degree, market it aggressively, admit anyone who can sign a federal loan form, and then walk away with the tuition banked while the taxpayer and the student split the wreckage. That is the arrangement we currently defend. The school captures every dollar of the upside and bears zero cents of the downside. No other industry gets that deal.
[P1.2] The proposition simply reintroduces skin in the game. Universities are not passive bystanders to student debt outcomes. They set the price. They design the curriculum.
[P1.3] They decide how many people to enroll, and they write the brochures promising career placement. When a program repeatedly produces graduates whose earnings cannot service the debt the school priced into it, that is not bad luck. That is a business model.
[P1.4] Notice the two qualifiers doing the work here. *Repeatedly* — not one unlucky cohort, not a recession year, but a persistent pattern across classes. And *earnings relative to debt* — not a snobbish ranking of majors. A modest-tuition social work program passes easily. A $90,000 certificate feeding into a $34,000 job does not.
[P1.5] The metric punishes overpricing, not idealism. A philosophy department that charges reasonably is safe; a for-profit-style cash cow charging luxury prices for a dead-end credential is not.
[P1.6] And the mechanism is the point. Lenders who retain risk underwrite carefully; lenders who offload it entirely originate garbage. Right now every incentive at the margin pushes universities toward the cheapest-to-run, highest-tuition, weakest-return programs, because enrollment is revenue and default is somebody else's problem. Put even partial default liability on the institution and the internal conversation changes overnight: cut the tuition, shrink the cohort to what the labor market absorbs, or improve the placement pipeline.
[P1.7] Any of those three outcomes is better than what we have.
[P1.8] The alternative on offer from the other side is a system where the only two parties with no financial stake in whether a degree pays off are the ones who priced it.
