[C1.2] First, public safety is not a luxury; it is a constitutional obligation. Employers, landlords, and licensing boards already rely on background checks; a sealed record does not erase the information, it merely hides it from the people who need it most.
[C1.3] Victims, in particular, lose a tool for monitoring recidivism and for seeking restitution. The presumptive seal would force them to chase courts for an exception every time, a burden the proposition itself claims to relieve.
[C1.4] Second, the “presumptive” standard is a blunt instrument that ignores the wide spectrum of juvenile conduct. A 13‑year‑old who posted a harmless meme is lumped together with a 16‑year‑old who distributed illicit images. The law already distinguishes between categories through sentencing and confinement; sealing erases that nuance and treats all digital footprints as equally damning. This over‑sealing discourages honest rehabilitation because the incentive to stay clean disappears when the record can be wiped automatically.
[C1.5] Third, digital records are already effectively public. Search engines cache pages, third‑party sites repost docket entries, and data brokers sell “juvenile‑offense” tags. A court order to seal a file does not guarantee invisibility; it merely creates a legal fiction that can be challenged at great cost. The real solution is better data‑management—expungement, de‑indexing, limited‑access portals—not a blanket presumption that hides everything.
[C1.6] In short, presumptive sealing sacrifices accountability, victim rights, and realistic safety for an idealized notion of forgiveness that the digital age cannot support. Courts should keep the default open and let sealing be the exception, not the rule.
