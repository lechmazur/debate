[P1.1] When a doctor commits malpractice, their insurance premiums rise. When a lawyer is sued for negligence, they carry that financial scar. When a police officer violates someone's constitutional rights and a city writes a check for millions, the officer feels nothing in their wallet. That gap is precisely the problem this proposal fixes.
[P1.2] The core mechanism is simple and proven across professions: if you make people bear the financial consequences of their own risk, they behave more carefully. Economists call it internalizing externalities. Right now, police misconduct costs are externalized onto taxpayers. Cities pay settlements from general funds—money that was supposed to fund schools, infrastructure, and public services.
[P1.3] Taxpayers who had no role in an officer's decision to use excessive force absorb the bill. That is not accountability; it is a subsidy for bad policing.
[P1.4] Personal professional liability insurance would change incentives at every level. Officers with clean records would pay modest premiums—rewarding good behavior with low costs. Officers who accumulate complaints and settlements would face rising premiums, creating a direct, personal financial signal that current disciplinary systems often fail to deliver. Persistently risky officers would eventually become uninsurable, creating a market-based mechanism that removes dangerous individuals from the force far more reliably than internal review boards, which studies have repeatedly shown are reluctant to discipline their own.
[P1.5] Critics may worry this would discourage people from becoming officers. But doctors carry malpractice insurance and still practice medicine. Lawyers carry professional liability coverage and still practice law. These professions have not collapsed—they have professionalized.
[P1.6] The same would happen here. The vast majority of officers, who do their jobs with integrity, would pay reasonable premiums and benefit from a system that finally distinguishes them from the small minority who cost cities millions.
[P1.7] This proposal does not replace criminal accountability, civil rights lawsuits, or internal discipline. It adds a missing layer: continuous, personalized financial feedback that makes every officer understand—viscerally—that misconduct has a personal price. Right now it does not, and taxpayers are paying for that gap.

Standard liability policies exclude intentional conduct. The officer who strikes a handcuffed suspect, who shoots someone complying with orders — those are intentional torts. [C1.2] In precisely the cases that produce the largest verdicts, the city still writes the check. The scheme does not move the cost it claims to move.
[C1.3] Second, it misplaces the locus of responsibility. Employers answer for employees because employers control selection, training, supervision, and discipline. That is not a "subsidy for bad policing"; it is where the levers of prevention actually sit. The department that ignored a decade of complaints deserves the bill, because the department could have stopped it.
[C1.4] Mandatory personal insurance hands those institutions an exit: blame the now-uninsurable individual, keep the broken supervision intact. PRO argues that review boards are reluctant to police their own. Then why gift them a private-market alibi?
[C1.5] Third, the incentive is not free. Officers make split-second decisions against resisting and armed people, and thousands of discretionary ones — stop, search, arrest, use force. Personal financial exposure at each of those moments buys under-enforcement: hesitated responses to dangerous calls, avoided contacts, fewer interventions in the hardest neighborhoods. Premiums would be priced off complaints and force incidents, so the officer working the most dangerous beats pays the most regardless of fault.
[C1.6] That is a tax on answering the calls nobody else wants.
[C1.7] Fourth, recruitment is already strained in many departments. Adding open-ended personal financial exposure discourages candidates — especially younger ones without assets or actuarial histories — and pushes experienced officers toward desk work or retirement.
[C1.8] PRO calls this accountability. It is cost-shifting: the institutions that manufacture misconduct escape cheaply while individual officers absorb risk they cannot price and cannot fully insure. Wrong target, wrong incentive, and the taxpayers still get billed for the worst cases.
