[P1.1] The cheapest shirt on the rack is not cheap. It is a bill deferred onto people who can least afford it: garment workers paid poverty wages, neighborhoods near landfills and incinerators, and families hit first by heat, floods, and plastic pollution. Fast fashion’s low price is manufactured by ignoring carbon, water, toxic dye, textile waste, and labor. The proposition stops pretending those costs do not exist.
[P1.2] A heavy tax on fast-fashion production is the sharpest lever because it attacks the business model at its source: endless volume, disposable quality, synthetic fibers, rapid turnover. The revenue then subsidizes local, sustainable textile manufacturing—cleaner mills, recycled fibers, repair and remanufacturing, fair-wage cut-and-sew—so the alternative is not a boutique fantasy but real capacity. That is how an economy of disposable shirts becomes an economy of durable clothing.
[P1.3] Yes, prices may rise. But lower-income consumers already pay a hidden tax: polluted air and water, precarious work, and clothes that lose shape after a few washes. A slightly more expensive shirt that lasts three times as long can be the better household bargain. Local manufacturing also creates jobs in the same communities squeezed by higher prices, and it reduces dependence on fragile global supply chains.
[P1.4] Opponents will call this regressive. Regressive compared with what? A system where the savings go to shareholders and the cleanup bill goes to the public. If the state wants to cushion the transition, it can pair this policy with targeted support, but it should not preserve artificially cheap clothing by keeping the damage off the books.
[P1.5] The decisive question is whether clothing should be cheap because it is efficient or cheap because someone else absorbs the cost. Taxing fast fashion and building local sustainable production answers that in favor of real prices, real jobs, and a livable future. A policy that makes the polluter pay and the producer rebuild is worth a transition cost. The alternative keeps buying disposable clothes while the planet and the poor pay the invoice.

A small local mill still uses vast water, chemicals and energy, often less efficiently and on grids no cleaner, while cotton, wool and synthetics must still be sourced globally. Add a leaky global tax easily dodged through imports, relabeling and e-commerce parcels, and you get the worst of both: higher prices for the poor, lost work for the vulnerable, green branding for the lucky few.
