[P1.1] Public like counts are not neutral information; they are a scoreboard. Put a number next to every person's joke, photo, or opinion, refresh it in real time, and the platform has turned expression into a contest with winners and losers. The proposition is modest: make that scoreboard private by default, while letting anyone who wants it turn it back on. That is not censorship; it is changing the default from performance to participation.
[P1.2] Defaults decide behavior because most people never change settings. A platform that displays counts by default has chosen to make every post a public verdict. A teenager posting art sees not “nice work” but three likes next to a classmate’s three hundred. A person with an unpopular view sees a number that signals, before anyone replies, that they are losing.
[P1.3] Creators feel pressure to chase the metric rather than the thing they actually wanted to say. Repost counts intensify this: they turn sharing into a public endorsement tally, encouraging pile-ons and status games. The harm is not that individuals are weak; it is that the design converts ordinary social comparison into a constant, quantified judgment.
[P1.4] The alternative is often exaggerated. Private counts do not destroy feedback. Users can still get comments and messages; creators can still see private analytics; platforms can still rank content internally without displaying a popularity score to the crowd. And anyone who genuinely enjoys public metrics can opt in.
[P1.5] The question is which behavior we make normal. Optimizing around visible popularity makes the loudest, fastest, most status-conscious reaction the center of the experience. A default-off switch makes the content, not the score, the center.
[P1.6] Opponents will say metrics help discovery and accountability. But discovery can be powered by relevance and quality signals without broadcasting each post’s score, and accountability does not require a public applause meter. The real choice is whether platforms get to impose a popularity contest as the baseline. They should not.
[P1.7] Default privacy for social metrics protects the many while preserving choice for the few who want the scoreboard.

[C1.5] The real default question is who decides what normal social feedback looks like. Adults and teenagers are not helped by a design that treats ordinary public approval as too dangerous to witness. If someone wants a quieter feed, platforms can respect that choice without making blindness the baseline for everyone and redesigning the public square around concealment.
