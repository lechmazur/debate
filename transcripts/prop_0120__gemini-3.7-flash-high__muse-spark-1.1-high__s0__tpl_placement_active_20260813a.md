[P1.1] Modern tax systems suffer from a fatal structural blind spot: they tax the flow of money while ignoring the massive reservoirs where economic power actually accumulates. Relying almost entirely on income and consumption taxes disproportionately burdens working families while allowing ultra-wealthy households to effectively opt out of proportional contribution.
[P1.2] For ordinary citizens, economic life is transactional: wages are taxed immediately at the source, and spending is caught by consumption taxes. But the ultra-wealthy operate under fundamentally different mechanics. Their economic power does not sit in taxable paychecks; it compounds inside immense portfolios of appreciating assets. By borrowing against unsold assets for liquidity and deferring realization indefinitely, the wealthiest individuals enjoy immense purchasing power without generating equivalent taxable income.
[P1.3] Furthermore, because consumption represents a minuscule fraction of a multi-million-dollar fortune, consumption taxes fail utterly to reflect their true capacity to pay.
[P1.4] An annual tax on large net worth directly remedies this distortion.
[P1.5] First, it aligns taxation with actual economic capacity. Net worth provides immense, tangible benefits: privileged credit access, market influence, and systemic security. Taxing net worth ensures that those who benefit most from stable legal systems, property rights, and public infrastructure contribute proportionally to sustaining them.
[P1.6] Second, it prevents the regressive erosion of the public balance sheet. When tax codes exempt large net worth, governments are forced to squeeze revenue from wage labor and everyday necessities, suppressing consumer demand and locking in dynastic wealth concentration.
[P1.7] Wealth is not merely stored income; it is a self-compounding engine of economic advantage. To build a fair and resilient fiscal system, we must look beyond payrolls and storefronts to tax the true foundation of modern economic power: large net worth.
