[P1.1] Every donor-conceived person begins life with a piece of their basic identity locked in someone else's filing cabinet. That is the injustice this debate is about, and no supply-side worry can justify keeping the lock on once that person is an adult asking to know who they came from.
[P1.2] Start with what identifying information actually is: it's not property, it's not a favor, it's a fact about a person's own body and history. Half of a donor-conceived adult's genetic ancestry, medical predispositions, and biological relatives are withheld from them by a contract they never signed. Compare this to adoption, where most legal systems have moved decisively toward opening records once the adoptee reaches adulthood, precisely because we recognized that the adoptee's interest in self-knowledge outweighs the adults' preference for privacy negotiated decades earlier.
[P1.3] A sperm or egg donor made a choice as an adult; the resulting person never consented to anonymity on their own behalf, and it is their identity being restricted.
[P1.4] The medical stakes are concrete. A donor-conceived adult who develops a heritable condition, or who is trying to have children of their own, may need to know about genetic risks - and increasingly needs to know simply to avoid accidentally forming a relationship with a half-sibling, which is no longer hypothetical given how many offspring a single donor can produce and how common consumer DNA testing has become. Anonymity was never actually permanent; it was a promise institutions couldn't keep, and pretending otherwise just delays the reckoning to a moment when it's more painful, not less.
[P1.5] Notice too what the "reduced supply" objection really concedes: it accepts the right is legitimate and only argues we shouldn't grant it because donors might disappear. That is a policy tradeoff, not a rebuttal of the right itself, and my opponent will need to show that a smaller, better-informed donor pool who fully understand what they're agreeing to is worse than a larger pool built on a promise of secrecy we cannot actually guarantee.

[C1.1] The proposition treats a profound interest in origins as if it were a simple trump card. But an enforceable legal right to identify an unwilling genetic donor is not just recognition of identity; it is state-compelled disclosure that overrides a third party’s privacy and the terms under which donation occurred. That kind of right has to be justified against competing autonomy interests and predictable harms, and here it fails.
[C1.2] First, the proposition ignores reliance and consent. Many donors gave under a legal regime that promised confidentiality, often decades ago, and organized lives, marriages, and families around that expectation. Retroactively converting a private medical arrangement into a public identity record does not simply update a file; it imposes new obligations on people who never agreed to them. If we care about consent, we should not use the donor-conceived person's lack of consent to excuse overriding the donor's explicit, lawful reliance on privacy.
[C1.3] Second, the adoption analogy overreaches. Adoption law balances the state's custody over severed parental relationships and the child's legal parentage. Donor conception was designed precisely to separate genetic connection from legal parenthood. A donor is not a parent seeking to hide from parental duties; the promise of anonymity was often the condition under which private clinics and recipients could build families at all.
[C1.4] Third, the motion concedes the supply risk and asks us to ignore it. We should not. If identifiable donation shrinks the donor pool, the costs fall on people trying to have children: longer waits, higher prices, more informal or unregulated donation, and fewer safe options. That is not a bookkeeping footnote; it is a real welfare loss.
[C1.5] The better route is prospective: non-identifying medical information, voluntary registries, genetic-risk screening, and clear open-donation rules for future donors. Those measures serve truth and health without forcing disclosure or shrinking access.
