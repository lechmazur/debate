[P1.1] Punishment can serve multiple purposes, but we must prioritize the aim that actually makes communities safer. Retribution focuses solely on avenging past harm, while rehabilitation addresses the root causes of criminal behavior to prevent future harm entirely. Consider a 19-year-old with no prior record, convicted of breaking into a car to steal food for his starving sibling. A purely retributive sentence—five years in prison with no rehabilitative support—leaves him with a permanent criminal record that bars most employment, no job skills, and deep resentment toward the system.
[P1.2] He will almost certainly reoffend after release, creating new victims. A rehabilitation-first approach, by contrast, pairs a short, proportional sentence with job training, mental health support if needed, and a supervised release plan that connects him to stable work and family support. He leaves the system able to support his family legally, with no reason to reoffend. Retribution is reactive: it satisfies a desire for vengeance but does nothing to stop the next crime.
[P1.3] Rehabilitation is proactive: it prioritizes the safety of future victims over emotional satisfaction for past ones. The measure of a just criminal justice system is not how harshly it punishes past wrongs, but how well it prevents future ones. Retribution may feel morally intuitive, but it produces no tangible public good. Rehabilitation delivers lower recidivism, fewer new victims, and fewer people trapped in cycles of crime and incarceration.
[P1.4] When we weigh the two aims, the choice that protects more people, reduces harm, and builds safer communities is unambiguously to prioritize rehabilitation over retribution.

[C1.3] Rehabilitation may usefully follow that sentence—skills, treatment, supervision—but it cannot replace the judgment that the act itself deserved condemnation. Elevate rehabilitation first and the judgment vanishes; the system now calibrates only to predicted reoffense and treats the original harm as background noise.
[C1.4] That inversion is dangerous. Pure risk management can under-punish the gravely culpable who appear low-risk and over-intrude on the less culpable who score high on needs assessments. It severs punishment from proportionality and invites the public perception that the system no longer cares about desert. Victims and communities lose confidence; the moral clarity that supports deterrence and voluntary compliance erodes.
[C1.5] Safety itself becomes harder to sustain once citizens see the law as therapy rather than justice.
[C1.6] Rehabilitation remains valuable as a secondary tool. It is not the more important aim. The first duty of punishment is to give wrongdoers what they deserve and to vindicate the rights they violated. Only after that foundation is secure can reform serve the community rather than replace justice.
