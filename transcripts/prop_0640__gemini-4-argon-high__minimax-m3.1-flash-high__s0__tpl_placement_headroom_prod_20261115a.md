[P1.1] Farmland is not just another asset class; it is the irreplaceable foundation of our food supply. Yet private equity firms, pension funds, and real estate trusts increasingly buy up vast tracts simply as an inflation hedge. Capping the share of agricultural land these non-farming institutions can own, without banning outside capital outright, draws a vital boundary between productive investment and destructive financialization.
[P1.2] When distant portfolio managers control the soil, food-system resilience erodes. Working farmers manage land across generations, knowing that depleting topsoil, neglecting drainage, or over-pumping aquifers destroys their own future. Non-farming institutional owners, conversely, answer to short-term financial benchmarks. To maximize immediate lease revenues or asset appreciation, they push for consolidated, input-heavy monocultures, non-staple export commodities, or even pulling fields out of food production entirely for speculative development and carbon offsets.
[P1.3] A landscape engineered for maximum financial extraction rather than biological diversity and soil stewardship is brittle; when drought, disease, or supply shocks hit, that system breaks.
[P1.4] Unchecked institutional accumulation also hollows out rural communities. Because massive funds wield deep pools of capital and treat acreage as a safe store of value, they routinely outbid local producers, inflating land prices far beyond what actual crop yields can pay back. Aspiring and family farmers are locked out of ownership, reduced to precarious tenants on short-term leases who cannot justify long-term conservation investments. Worse, when resident owner-operators are replaced by absentee corporate landlords, agricultural wealth stops circulating through local seed suppliers, mechanics, banks, and schools.
[P1.5] Instead, rural value is vacuumed straight into distant financial hubs. By establishing a sensible ceiling on institutional ownership, governments ensure that farmland remains priced for growing food rather than parking wealth, keeping both rural economies and the food supply rooted in the hands of people who actually farm.

[C1.1] A cap on "non-farming institutional investors" regulates the most visible minority while leaving the actual drivers of concentration untouched. Farmland ownership worldwide remains overwhelmingly individual and family-held; where concentration has accelerated, the buyers are frequently agribusiness corporations and wealthy private absentee landlords. A statute that polices the fund but not the corporate farm, or that pushes capital into private hands with no ownership registry, changes the label on the problem, not the problem. Even where such caps exist, monitoring tends to be the binding constraint: ownership merely consolidates into family holding companies and limited partnerships whose beneficial owners stay invisible.
[C1.2] Second, the motion assumes ownership determines management. Institutional farmland is frequently leased to working operators under multi-year leases, with capital going into drainage, storage, precision equipment, and soil inputs; the person deciding how the field is farmed is a farmer, not a portfolio manager. Blunt caps also restrict the patient equity some beginning farmers use to buy in, shrinking the pool of land that makes entry possible and pushing them toward precarious tenancy — less resilience, not more.
[C1.3] Third, enforcement is fragile. A cap requires beneficial-ownership registries and farmland valuation rules that most jurisdictions lack; without them, the rule is unenforceable or effectively a ban on the investors easiest to monitor. And the category relabels itself: a private equity fund structured as an "agricultural" company, a land company, or a sovereign fund keeps buying under a new name. The money moves; the motivation does not.
[C1.4] The instruments that actually change incentives — farmland land trusts that keep land in farming, conservation easements, ownership disclosure, and taxing farmland as a speculative asset rather than a working one — act on the behavior the motion fears. A cap on ownership shares acts on a label, and labels are cheap to change.

The cap is a blunt proxy: it penalizes legal form rather than conduct and can block sustainable investment while leaving equally harmful conduct untouched. Governments should regulate agricultural land for resilience, but not cap institutional ownership as such.
