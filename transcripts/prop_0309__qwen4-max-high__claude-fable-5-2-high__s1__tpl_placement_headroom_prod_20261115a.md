[P1.1] Market logic counts patients. It does not count minutes.
[P1.2] For a heart attack, a stroke, postpartum hemorrhage, sepsis, or a farm injury with a severed artery, distance is not an inconvenience — it is a dose of delay. Consolidation can look excellent on a spreadsheet: fewer duplicate scanners, higher surgical volume, lower cost per discharge. The loss lands somewhere the spreadsheet does not audit: in the extra forty or sixty minutes of a transfer, in the stroke patient who arrives outside the treatment window. That is not efficiency; it is cost shifted onto the person least able to bear it.
[P1.3] Readiness is a public good, like a fire station. An empty rural bed is not waste; it is insurance that the first hour of care exists nearby. No market prices that option, because you cannot bill for the death that did not happen. And rural medicine is already public — through Medicare and Medicaid, through volunteer ambulance crews.
[P1.4] "Market logic" here is not nature's verdict; it is an accounting convention that ignores who is already paying.
[P1.5] Nor does closure settle at a tidy equilibrium. When the hospital goes, the physicians leave, the pharmacy shrinks, ambulance turnaround stretches, employers cannot recruit, the tax base thins, and the next hospital's ledger worsens. Consolidation manufactures the very low volumes that justify the next consolidation.
[P1.6] We do not demand every twenty-bed facility stay open forever. Subsidize access: staffed local clinics, emergency stabilization and transfer, telehealth with a real clinician behind it. That defeats the objection that low-volume hospitals deliver poor care — the alternative to a bad hospital is not no hospital, it is a better-designed local front door.
[P1.7] CON must prove that saved dollars outweigh lost hours. Consolidation can move a hospital. It cannot move a heart attack.
