[P1.1] When a consumer buys a smart thermostat, television, or phone, they are not just purchasing physical silicon and glass. They are buying an ongoing tether to manufacturer software, cloud servers, and security patches. Yet today, that digital lifeline can be unilaterally cut after eighteen months, leaving fully functional hardware exposed to cyberattacks or entirely bricked.
[P1.2] The fundamental failure of the smart device market is information asymmetry. Hardware durability is easy to see; software longevity is deliberately concealed. By requiring manufacturers to state a guaranteed support lifespan directly on the physical packaging, this policy treats software support for what it actually is: an essential, non-negotiable product specification. Just as consumers expect food packaging to state an expiration date and appliances to disclose energy consumption, buyers deserve to know the minimum operational lifespan of the software that makes their devices function.
[P1.3] Placing this guarantee on the physical box is essential for true accountability. Online terms of service are easily altered, buried in hyperlinked legalese, or hidden behind setup screens long after purchase. A printed commitment on the package binds the manufacturer at the point of sale under basic consumer protection law. It levels the retail playing field: a cheap camera supported for one year will no longer masquerade as equivalent to a device supported for seven.
[P1.4] When support lifespans are visible on retail shelves, manufacturers must compete on durability rather than planned obsolescence.
[P1.5] The consequences of silence are severe. Abandoned devices do not vanish; they remain plugged into home networks as unpatched vectors for botnets, or they end up in landfills while their microchips still work. We cannot solve premature electronic waste or digital security risks while consumers are kept blind to how long their purchases will last. Demanding a clear, printed support guarantee restores transparency, enforces corporate accountability, and ensures consumers get what they pay for.

So every box will say roughly the same thing: the statutory floor. The printed number does not become a competitive differentiator; it becomes an industry-wide ceiling with official blessing.
[C1.3] Today, many phones and routers receive patches for years past any promise a lawyer would sign. Under this policy, the printed date becomes the socially sanctioned moment to stop. Consumers read "supported until 2027" and hear "dies in 2027," and manufacturers happily oblige. The label manufactures the obsolescence PRO claims to fight.
[C1.4] Second, the physical box is the worst possible medium for a promise about the future. It is printed months before sale and sits on shelves for years. It cannot be corrected when a cloud partner folds, when a regulator mandates a change, or when a company extends support. [C1.5] The box goes in the recycling the same afternoon; the device lives in the wall for a decade.
[C1.6] Third, "every smart device" sweeps in an eight-dollar sensor whose "support" depends on Amazon's or Google's APIs, which its maker does not control. Forcing small firms to personally guarantee the behavior of trillion-dollar platforms doesn't protect consumers; it clears small firms out of the market and hands it to the incumbents who can absorb the legal risk.
[C1.7] PRO wants transparency. What this motion delivers is a single number, narrowly defined by the party being regulated, frozen on cardboard, that invites consumers to trust it and manufacturers to hide behind it. False confidence is not the cure for information asymmetry; it is a more durable form of it.
