[P1.1] Workplace AI is not a tool workers use. It is a system that uses workers. When an algorithm scores your customer calls for "empathy," monitors your keystrokes, decides you get four hours instead of eight, or flags you for termination, that's not IT procurement. That's management.
[P1.2] And management decisions that control pay, privacy, and employment should never be unilateral.
[P1.3] Large employers should be required to bargain with elected worker councils because three realities make anything less a sham.
[P1.4] First, power. A single employee cannot meaningfully consent to or contest an opaque scoring model when quitting means losing health insurance. Collective, elected representation is the only way to give workers actual leverage. We require bargaining over wages and hours for this exact reason; algorithmic control is wages and hours encoded in software.
[P1.5] Second, knowledge. Engineers in Seattle don't know how a fulfillment center actually moves. Workers do. When they can negotiate limits — no facial recognition in bathrooms, human review before firing, access to the data collected on them — implementation gets better and less litigious.
[P1.6] Co-determination in Germany hasn't killed productivity; it has avoided disasters.
[P1.7] Third, harm scale. At a 20-person shop a bad boss affects 20 people. At a 10,000-person firm an un-audited scheduling model can cut pay for thousands overnight, entrench bias at scale, and create black boxes no one can appeal. The threshold focuses the law where the externality is largest and where firms can afford a process.
[P1.8] Talk of innovation costs is misplaced. We already require large firms to bargain over safety, to audit for financial discrimination, to get environmental permits. Those delays didn't end industry. They made it sustainable.
[P1.9] Negotiation is not a veto; it is a requirement to explain, to listen, and to find guardrails.
[P1.10] This doesn't ban AI. It says: if you want the power of an algorithmic manager, you accept the responsibility of negotiating with the people it manages.

[C1.6] Scale cuts both ways. [C1.7] In practice “negotiate” is leverage for side demands and foot-dragging, not pure explanation.
