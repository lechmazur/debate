[P1.2] That is the practice the proposition targets—not all price variation, but algorithmic personalization that hides the basis of the price.
[P1.3] Price is a material term. Consent to a deal you cannot see is not meaningful. When a seller says “the price is $35,” you can accept, negotiate, or leave. When an algorithm silently charges you $35 because your battery is low, your neighborhood is affluent, or you have searched three times, you cannot do any of those things rationally.
[P1.4] Disclosure restores the missing information. A simple notice—“this price was personalized for you”—is not burdensome. It lets the buyer compare, and comparison is what disciplines markets.
[P1.5] Opponents will defend price discrimination as efficiency: discounts to price-sensitive buyers, higher prices to those willing to pay. Fine—disclose it. [P1.6] The only business model threatened by disclosure is one that depends on the customer not knowing.
[P1.7] Algorithmic scale changes the stakes. Old haggling was episodic, face-to-face, and negotiable. A ban is the backstop where disclosure cannot cure: hidden manipulation, invidious traits, or prices so personalized that no meaningful comparison remains.
[P1.8] The motion says “usually” precisely because disclosure is the default remedy and prohibition is for what transparency cannot fix. Secret personalized pricing is not a free market; it is a rigged one.
