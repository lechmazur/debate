[P1.1] A 9:47 p.m. message from your manager rarely demands an answer tonight. It doesn't have to. Its arrival tells you the workday never really closed, and that is the harm these laws target.
[P1.2] Start with what knowledge work runs on: attention, judgment, and memory. Those recover only when people genuinely detach. Recovery research points consistently one way: it is not just hours answered but the expectation of availability that keeps stress elevated, erodes sleep, and bleeds into family life. A worker who checks her phone forty times across an evening has done perhaps ten minutes of work and lost the whole evening.
[P1.3] Why doesn't the market fix it? Because it is an arms race. No individual can go dark when responsiveness is read as commitment; the person who logs off looks less dedicated than the one who replies at midnight. Everyone would prefer the truce, nobody can declare it alone.
[P1.4] A legal default is exactly the tool that solves collective-action problems: it makes disconnecting normal rather than a career risk.
[P1.5] Now the cost side. The laws enacted — in France, Australia, Ireland and elsewhere — are modest by design. They generally protect a right not to answer unreasonable contact, or require employers to negotiate a policy; they carve out emergencies, on-call roles, and cross-time-zone arrangements. What they remove is the low-value layer: the reflexive evening email that could wait until nine.
[P1.6] And that layer isn't productive anyway. Output in knowledge work does not scale with hours of availability; tired minds make worse decisions, write worse code, and quit sooner, taking expensive expertise with them.
[P1.7] So the comparison is lopsided: a large, dependable welfare gain against a small, speculative productivity cost. To win, the other side must show the evening ping is where the value lives. It isn't.
