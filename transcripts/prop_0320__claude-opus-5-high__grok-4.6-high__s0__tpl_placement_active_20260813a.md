[P1.1] The decisive fact about nightlife is that the danger isn't spread evenly across the night — it clusters in a twenty-minute window when everyone hits the street at once. An earlier closing time doesn't remove that window; it just moves it earlier and makes it sharper. Ten thousand people are ejected simultaneously into a district with no trains, no staffed presence, and a taxi queue that turns into a shoving match. That is the moment fights start, and it is the moment someone with a car keys decides they're "fine to drive."
[P1.2] So the right test isn't whether a policy makes one street quieter on one measured night. It's whether it reduces total harm per unit of nightlife. On that test, restrictions do badly, because demand for a night out is stubborn. Close venues at 1am and the party migrates — to house parties with no bouncers, no cameras, no trained staff, no one calling an ambulance; to unlicensed spaces; or into cars driving home from the suburb that still serves.
[P1.3] Sydney's lockout experience is the cautionary tale: the targeted precinct got quieter largely by getting emptier, while drinking and its risks relocated. You can hollow out a district and call the vital signs an improvement.
[P1.4] The alternative actually works on the mechanism. Running trains and buses through the small hours staggers dispersal instead of compressing it, and it gives the sober option a real chance — you cannot ask people not to drive home if you've given them no way home. Trained, visible street presence — outreach teams, first-aid points, licensed marshals — intervenes before an argument becomes an assault, and prevents the ordinary tragedy of a lone woman deciding a dark forty-minute walk is her only route.
[P1.5] There's also a fairness point restrictions never answer. The cleaner, cook, nurse and security guard finishing at 2am are not out partying; they are the collateral damage of a city that shuts its transit and calls it a safety policy.
[P1.6] Serve the night. Don't just switch off the lights and hope.

[C1.2] Earlier closing times and venue restrictions cut that growth at the source. Extending the night merely staffs it.
[C1.3] PRO's twenty-minute surge is real, but it is not the main wound. The main wound is the hours after midnight, when blood alcohol peaks and the person who should have taken a cab decides they are fine. Last call and last-entry rules shrink that window. They do not just move a spike earlier; they stop a portion of the drinking from occurring at all.
[C1.4] A night that ends is a night with fewer impaired people in public. Emergency rooms see the pattern every weekend: later service, worse injuries.
[C1.5] The migration story is oversold. Some people will look for a house party. Most will not. An after-hours room or willing host has cost and risk, and that friction cuts consumption.
[C1.6] A leaky one-precinct rule can empty a street; coordinated citywide closing times close the hop to the next postcode. The remedy for a porous lockout is broader rules, not abandoning rules.
[C1.7] Late transit does not dissolve the surge. It relocates the last-train crush and exports drunk conflict onto platforms. Street teams treat injuries they cannot prevent. Both are expensive at 3am, and they subsidize staying out longer — so the unit of nightlife gets larger and drunker.
[C1.8] Those officers and medics are then missing from streets that never asked for a dawn economy.
[C1.9] Night-shift workers are not a nightlife crowd. Limited scheduled routes can serve them. That is not a reason to keep an entertainment district open until dawn.
[C1.10] Close the tap. Do not hire more people to mop.
