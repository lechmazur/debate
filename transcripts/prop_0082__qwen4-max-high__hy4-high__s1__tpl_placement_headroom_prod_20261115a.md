[P1.1] Dating apps sell two incompatible products: a good match, and one more evening on the app. A successful match deletes two paying customers. Endless swiping and boost purchases keep them. That is the conflict of interest at the center of this motion, and it is why self-correction cannot be assumed.
[P1.2] Consider the mechanism. An infinite deck plus unpredictable intermittent reward — a like, a match, then silence — is the same schedule that keeps people at slot machines. It trains volume, not judgment. Someone who would thoughtfully read three profiles instead flicks through three hundred.
[P1.3] Attention is the scarce input in matching. Mechanics that inflate swipe counts necessarily thin the care each profile receives, so worse matches are not a bug; they are the output the design optimizes for.
[P1.4] Paid visibility corrupts the signal itself. When the face at the top of your stack bought its way there, you cannot tell whether the app understands you or is selling to you. Blurred "see who liked you" walls go further: they manufacture ambiguity and then charge to remove it — a problem the product created on purpose. Trust is the actual infrastructure of online dating.
[P1.5] You must believe the profile is real and the interest sincere. Monetized ambiguity taxes precisely that belief.
[P1.6] The obvious escape — delete it, choose a better app — fails on contact with reality. Network effects mean the people are where the people already are. And the major apps converge on the same mechanics because those mechanics work financially, not because users demanded them.
[P1.7] Competition disciplines price far better than it disciplines deception.
[P1.8] Read the motion's limits. It says *limit*, and only *when* match quality and trust are undermined. Cap the deck. Label paid placement.
[P1.9] Stop paywalling sincerity. Nobody loses the freedom to subscribe. If CON refuses even that, they are defending the harm, not the liberty.
