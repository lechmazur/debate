Districts that genuinely lengthen the four remaining days and hold total hours constant show no consistent losses in math and reading, and some show gains. A policy with a known failure mode is not a harmful policy; it is a policy with a design requirement.
[C1.3] We do not condemn kindergarten because half-day programs underperform; we fund full-day programs. Condemn the districts that cut hours, not the calendar.
[C1.4] The attention-decay story also proves too much. If a seventh hour does not stick, then every five-day high school already running seven periods is broken the same way. The fix is scheduling within days, not the number of days.
[C1.5] Now the help, which PRO undersells. Retention is not a soft plank, because staffing stability is itself a learning variable. Churn concentrates in rural districts that cannot raise salaries, and a revolving door of novices depresses achievement more reliably than almost anything else a school controls. That is why hundreds of districts in Colorado, Missouri, and Texas use the schedule — it is the one recruitment tool they can afford.
[C1.6] Keeping an experienced teacher in that classroom five more years helps children more than twenty extra Fridays. PRO weighs learning against retention as if they compete. They compound.
[C1.7] And the savings, though modest, are recurring and redirectable: buses and utilities fund tutoring, aides, and the fifth-day enrichment programs many districts run for exactly the students PRO worries about. The Friday childcare scramble is real, but it is a logistics problem communities solve, not evidence children learn less.
[C1.8] The bar is comparative. The harm is avoidable by design; the help produces learning of its own. PRO cannot clear it.
