[P1.1] The proposal strikes the essential, workable balance between two foundational democratic values: free exercise of religion and equal access to public life without discrimination. It avoids the two extremes that would undermine either value: a blanket ban on all religious exemptions, which would let the state intrude on core religious practice, and unlimited exemptions, which let religious entities use their faith status to impose costly discrimination on unwilling third parties in the public square.
[P1.2] The line the proposal draws—between core religious functions and broad commercial activity—is both principled and practical. Core religious functions are those intrinsic to the practice of the faith itself: selecting clergy, conducting worship, teaching sectarian doctrine to adherents, or operating a religious retreat reserved for members of the faith. These activities are so bound up in religious identity that forcing a faith community to act against its doctrine would violate the core of free exercise.
[P1.3] Broad commercial activity, by contrast, occurs when a religious institution acts as an ordinary market participant: running a hospital that serves the general public and accepts public insurance, operating a chain of retail stores, managing a secular private university that admits students of all faiths, or running a foster care agency that places children with state funding. When religious entities enter these spaces, they take on the same obligations as any other business or service provider, because third parties have no choice but to interact with them.
[P1.4] A low-income single mother who can only access pediatric care at her local religiously affiliated hospital should not be denied service because of her religion, or have her child’s nurse refuse to treat them because she is gay. A qualified applicant for a retail job at a religious university’s campus bookstore should not be turned away for being Jewish.
[P1.5] This line is not arbitrary: courts and regulators already use it to resolve conflicts between religious liberty and anti-discrimination rules. It protects the heart of religious freedom while ensuring that religious institutions cannot use their status as a shield to discriminate in the public marketplace.

When a religious community operates a hospital, an adoption agency, or a university, it is not acting as an ordinary market participant simply seeking profit [P1.3]. It is living out its faith through outward mission.
[C1.3] Even supporting roles—like staff at a religious university [P1.4]—sustain an intentional, mission-driven community. Stripping exemptions from these institutions simply because they interact with the public forces an intolerable dilemma: violate foundational conscience or shutter vital ministries entirely.

Forcing secular regulators and judges to parse doctrine and decree which tenets are truly "essential" violates the very separation of church and state. Courts are ill-equipped to police where worship ends and commerce begins, inevitably favoring formal creeds over active, service-oriented faiths.
[C1.5] In practice, this standard invites bureaucrats to rewrite religious orthodoxy, arbitrarily deciding which convictions the state will tolerate and which it will discard.
[C1.6] Finally, this narrow standard devastates civil society. When faith-based charities, clinics, and foster agencies are driven out by unyielding mandates, vulnerable populations suffer through diminished care, fewer foster placements, and eroded safety nets. True pluralism does not demand that every civic institution conform to identical state-mandated norms.
[C1.7] Instead, it protects institutional autonomy, allowing diverse communities to enrich the public square on their own terms. The motion’s artificially cramped vision must be rejected.
