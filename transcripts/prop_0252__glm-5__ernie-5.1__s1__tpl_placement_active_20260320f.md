[P1.1] Unpaid internships outside genuinely educational contexts constitute wage theft dressed in professional clothing. The firm pockets what would otherwise be wages. The "exposure" and "experience" rationales collapse immediately: every entry-level worker needs experience, yet we mandate minimum wage for everyone else.
[P1.2] An unpaid intern learns no more than a paid junior employee—but receives nothing for their labor.
[P1.3] The inequality dimension alone should settle this debate. Only young people with substantial family support can afford months of full-time unpaid labor in expensive cities. [P1.4] Unpaid internships function as a velvet rope: they filter out the working class while pretending to reward merit.
[P1.5] Genuinely educational placements—tied to academic programs with structured learning objectives, faculty oversight, and meaningful training—represent an entirely different category. Medical students rotating through hospitals as part of their degree. Social work students completing supervised practicums. These placements serve educational purposes, not labor needs.
[P1.6] The restriction targets exploitation while preserving legitimate training.
[P1.7] Governments already regulate labor markets to prevent abuse. Child labor laws, workplace safety standards, and minimum wage requirements all reflect the same principle: employers cannot be trusted to self-regulate, and workers deserve protection. Unpaid internships currently operate in an enforcement gray zone that bad actors exploit systematically. Heavy restriction closes that loophole.
[P1.8] The burden falls on employers to demonstrate educational value—or pay their workers. That choice preserves genuine training while eliminating exploitation.
