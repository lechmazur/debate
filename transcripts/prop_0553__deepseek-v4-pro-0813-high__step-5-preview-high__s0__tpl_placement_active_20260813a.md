[P1.1] A device is not truly owned if the manufacturer controls whether and how it can be repaired. India should adopt a national right-to-repair law because repair monopolies over parts, tools, and documentation now impose a regressive cost on consumers, suppress competition, and shorten device life.
[P1.2] The central harm is artificial repair scarcity. Brands serialize screens and batteries, withhold service manuals, and gate diagnostics behind authorized accounts. A small fault then becomes an expensive replacement: a charging port issue can lead to a motherboard swap, and a cracked screen can be priced near a new budget phone. Outside big cities, where independent shops are often the only realistic option, these barriers push people toward debt, downtime, or e-waste.
[P1.3] A statutory duty changes the market. It should require non-discriminatory access to genuine parts, service schematics, diagnostic tools, and calibration software for independent technicians. That is not a technical giveaway: service manuals are not trade secrets, and repair access can be provided without unlocking encrypted user data or disabling anti-theft features. The objection that this threatens security confuses repairability with bypassing security.
[P1.4] The objection that safety is at risk confuses regulation with prohibition; repair can be done to standards while preserving access.
[P1.5] National law also beats voluntary programs, which are narrow, temporary, and brand-controlled. By contrast, a right to repair would formalize India's large repair economy, create local jobs, cut e-waste, and make device ownership meaningful for millions. The core choice is whether repair is a buyer's right or a brand's privilege. It should be a right.
