[P1.1] Every month, millions of consumers pay for services they forgot they had or can’t figure out how to stop. Subscription fatigue is real, but the deeper problem is the dark pattern industry: links hidden in account settings, multi-step processes with deceptive "Are you sure?" prompts, and interfaces that make cancellation harder than sign-up. This asymmetry isn’t accidental—it’s a calculated business model that extracts billions from consumer inertia and confusion.
[P1.2] The harm is measurable. Behavioral research shows that adding friction to cancellation can reduce churn by 30-40%, but this isn’t customer satisfaction—it’s hostage-taking. The welfare costs accumulate: direct financial loss, especially for those on tight budgets; the time tax of navigating these mazes; and the broader erosion of trust that makes consumers wary of all digital transactions. When users expect to be trapped, even legitimate businesses suffer from the stigma.
[P1.3] Regulators must impose symmetry. If sign-up is a single click, cancellation must be too. Same button size, same placement, same number of steps. Early movers like the UK’s CMA have targeted these practices, and the results are clear: complaint volumes drop sharply when dark patterns are banned.
[P1.4] This isn’t about limiting business freedom; it’s about ensuring fair competition where firms win on value, not trickery. The standard is simple and fair: the door out must be as wide as the door in.

[C1.1] Paying for a service you forgot for a month is annoying. It is not a serious consumer-welfare crisis, and it does not justify regulators redesigning every account page in the economy.
[C1.2] First, the scale is inflated. Most consumer subscriptions are low-value, month-to-month, and terminable through app-store hubs, bank controls, account dashboards, or a chargeback if a firm truly stonewalls. The typical harm is brief and reversible: one unwanted renewal before a reminder, email receipt, or bank statement prompts action. Subscription fatigue is largely a budgeting problem created by choosing many cheap trials — not evidence of captivity.
[C1.3] When a service is expensive or essential, people notice and cancel; when it is trivial, delayed cancellation tells us the loss was trivial too.
[C1.4] Second, sign-up and cancellation are not the same act, so symmetry is a false standard. Signing up adds a service. Cancelling can delete data, end access for a whole household, forfeit prepaid discounts, break connected devices, or be triggered accidentally or by someone else. A short confirmation, identity check, pause offer, or warning about what will be lost is not a dark pattern — it is useful information.
[C1.5] Some who click cancel actually want to pause, downgrade, or fix billing, and surfacing that choice saves money without forcing a resubscribe later.
[C1.6] Third, requiring cancellation be 'as easy as' sign-up is vague and will backfire. Same steps? Same button size? For annual contracts sold at a discount, gyms with fixed costs, software bundled with onboarding, and free trials?
[C1.7] Firms will respond predictably: fewer trials, higher upfront prices, tougher sign-up, and less generous discounts. Large platforms can absorb design lawyers; small firms cannot.
[C1.8] Real deception — hidden terms, fake cancel buttons, charges after clear cancellation — is already actionable as fraud or unfair trading. Target that. Do not criminalize friction, confuse regret with captivity, or raise prices for everyone to solve forgetfulness.
