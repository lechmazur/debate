[P1.1] Orbital space is a shared commons we are degrading faster than we can govern it. Mega-constellations are being licensed and launched at a pace that treats orbit as an empty frontier when it is actually a crowded, fragile system with a dangerous failure mode: collisions beget debris, debris begets collisions, and the resulting cascade could render low Earth orbit unusable for generations. That risk is not speculative physics; it is the straightforward consequence of putting thousands of large objects into intersecting shells without binding rules for how they share altitude, avoid each other, and deorbit reliably.
[P1.2] Once a cascade begins, no amount of money reverses it. A moratorium is justified precisely when harms are irreversible and current rules are inadequate — and both conditions hold.
[P1.3] The astronomy harm compounds the case. Each new constellation adds thousands of bright moving objects that streak across survey images, blind sensitive instruments at dawn and dusk, and degrade the sky as a scientific and cultural resource that belongs to everyone. Radio astronomers face interference in protected bands. These costs are imposed globally by a handful of operators and states, without consent or compensation.
[P1.4] That is a textbook market failure.
[P1.5] Third, the governance gap is glaring. There is no binding international rule requiring operators to share collision-avoidance data, no agreed standard for deorbit reliability, no meaningful cap on debris generation, and no enforcement mechanism with teeth. National licensing races to the bottom, because each state benefits from its own constellations while bearing only a fraction of the orbital costs. Only a coordinated pause breaks that race.
[P1.6] The motion is careful: temporary, and conditional on rules getting materially stronger. That structure answers the obvious objection. This is not a ban on satellites or innovation — it is a timeout while we write traffic rules for a highway we are already crashing on. Industry will survive a pause; orbit may not survive its absence.
[P1.7] The question is who bears the risk of waiting — and right now, the answer is everyone who needs space.
