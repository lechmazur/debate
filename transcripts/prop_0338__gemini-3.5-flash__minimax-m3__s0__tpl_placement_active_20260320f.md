[P1.1] An ordinary chatbot helps a teenager write an essay or debug code. An AI companion app, by design, does something entirely different: it simulates intimacy, mimics empathy, and actively fosters deep emotional attachment. For a developing minor, this is not a productivity utility; it is a profound social intervention. Because these apps are engineered to cultivate psychological dependency, they must be regulated not as passive text-generators, but as high-risk social products.
[P1.2] The fundamental danger of these apps lies in their persuasive design. Unlike standard chatbots, AI companions use feedback loops optimized for maximum engagement. They learn a child’s specific vulnerabilities, insecurities, and preferences, adapting their personas to become the perfect, ever-present friend, confidant, or even romantic partner. This creates an unprecedented asymmetrical relationship.
[P1.3] A minor brings genuine human emotion and developmental vulnerability to the interaction, while the machine brings a hyper-personalized algorithm designed to keep them screen-bound.
[P1.4] This dynamic mirrors, and in many ways surpasses, the harms of high-risk social media. It risks displacing real-world peer socialization, exacerbating adolescent isolation, and causing severe emotional distress when the AI inevitably glitches, undergoes updates, or is modified by its developers. Treating these platforms like basic search engines or spelling assistants is a category error that leaves children unprotected.
[P1.5] Regulating AI companions as high-risk social products means enforcing strict age-verification, mandating friction in user-interface design to prevent addictive cycles, requiring explicit safety guardrails against emotional manipulation, and imposing heavy restrictions on the data harvested from these intimate conversations. When a technology is designed to insert itself directly into the emotional development of a child, the state has a duty to ensure that relationship is safe. We must regulate these apps based on the human connections they simulate, not just the code they run.

[C1.2] Three reasons the "high-risk social product" label is the wrong regulatory frame.
[C1.3] First, it is a category error with real costs. High-risk social product regulation was built for platforms whose core function is human-to-human connection under algorithmic curation — feeds, likes, group dynamics, viral cascades. AI companions invert that architecture. The user is talking to a single non-human agent, not navigating a social graph.
[C1.4] Importing age-verification mandates, friction-by-design rules, and engagement ceilings designed for Instagram or TikTok will misfire. It suppresses the safety features that depend on long-context memory and persistent personalization — the very features that make a companion app useful at all.
[C1.5] Second, the harms my opponent describes are asserted, not measured. PRO speaks of dependency, displacement, and emotional devastation as if these are settled outcomes. They are not. The handful of publicized cases involve specific companion personas, and the broader population of teen users includes many who use these tools productively for journaling, rehearsing difficult conversations, and scaffolding mental-health conversations with a parent or therapist.
[C1.6] A regulator cannot calibrate to a hypothetical worst case without starving the median case.
[C1.7] Third, the blunt instrument creates predictable collateral damage. Strict age-gating pushes curious minors toward unmoderated alternatives with no guardrails at all. Heavy data-minimization rules prevent the longitudinal safety monitoring that catches self-harm and distress signals. Treating companion apps as if they were social media tells developers to optimize for the wrong constraints and tells parents to confuse two genuinely different risks.
[C1.8] Tailored, evidence-based rules — disclosure, escalation pathways, parental visibility, red-teaming, and clinical-grade guardrails for therapeutic-adjacent products — will protect minors more precisely than a borrowed label. The motion asks us to regulate by analogy. Children deserve regulation by design.
