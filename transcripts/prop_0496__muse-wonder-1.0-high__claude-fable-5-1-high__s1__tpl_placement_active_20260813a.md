[P1.1] When a democracy goes to war, its restraint is political. Voters must see the cost, legislators must authorize and fund it, and soldiers must answer through a public chain of command. Private military contractors in combat break all three links, and that is why they should be banned from combat roles.
[P1.2] First, they let governments wage war off the books. A cap on deployed troops means little if a government can quietly add thousands of armed contractors. Their injuries and deaths are not reported in the same official casualty counts, do not trigger the same public rituals, and rarely create the same political pressure at home. The executive gets the battlefield capacity; the public loses the signal that would let it judge, protest, or vote to end the mission.
[P1.3] A war that looks smaller and cheaper than it is is a war the electorate cannot control.
[P1.4] Second, they blur who answers when force goes wrong. A regular soldier operates under a clear military code, a known commander, and courts built to try abuses. A contractor sits between systems: employed by a company, contracted by a ministry, operating in another country whose courts may be weak or excluded by agreement. When civilians are killed, each actor can point elsewhere — company to contract, government to company, host state to immunity.
[P1.5] That diffusion is the business model of plausible deniability.
[P1.6] Third, profit changes the mission. Armies serve a publicly debated objective and go home when it ends. Combat contractors earn more when deployment expands, threat persists, and contracts renew. Even without assuming bad faith in every individual, a system that rewards prolongation with revenue cannot be trusted with escalation.
[P1.7] Logistics, training, or maintenance raise different questions. Lethal combat is the core sovereign act democracies reserve for accountable public force. If citizens cannot see it, stop it, or punish its abuse, it is not democratically controlled war — it is outsourced violence.
