[P1.1] The purchase of sex should be treated as the offense because the buyer is the source of demand. Buyers are usually people with money, mobility, and real choice; people who sell sex are disproportionately poor, migrant, housing-insecure, or coerced. Criminalizing the purchase while decriminalizing the sale therefore aims the law at the person who creates the market, while protecting the person least able to refuse it. It also sends a clear norm: there is no right to buy another person’s body.
[P1.2] Full prohibition is worse. When both sale and purchase are illegal, people in prostitution must hide from police, cannot safely report violence or trafficking, and are often treated as offenders rather than witnesses or victims. Demand does not disappear; it merely moves further underground, where exploitation is harder to detect. Under the proposed model, a seller can call the police after being assaulted without being arrested for the sale, while the buyer still faces legal risk.
[P1.3] That is a concrete safety improvement, not empty symbolism.
[P1.4] A fully legal market is also worse. Legalizing purchase normalizes paid sex and expands demand beyond what either prohibition or this asymmetric model permits. Once buying is legitimate, brothels, pimps, and other commercial operators become lawful businesses with a strong incentive to recruit more vulnerable people. Rather than reducing trafficking, a larger legal market often pulls in more coerced labor because the infrastructure of exploitation becomes respectable and harder to investigate.
[P1.5] Between those two failures, the decriminalized-seller/criminalized-buyer model is the safer compromise. It deters demand without punishing the exploited, it keeps sellers visible enough to seek help, and it avoids creating a legal industry with an economic stake in expanding the trade. No legal regime removes all harm, but this one puts the penalty on the side with power and choice while protecting the side with the least of either.

[C1.2] Picture a woman working a street. Under any regime, her safety depends on time: time to look at the man, ask questions, note the plate, decline. Once *he* is the one facing arrest, he demands speed and darkness. Negotiation collapses from five minutes to thirty seconds.
[C1.3] He won't drive to the lit corner she trusts; he'll drive to the industrial estate. She cannot ask for his name or verify him, because no buyer facing prosecution gives real details. The law has not removed the transaction. It has stripped out every safety feature the transaction had, and handed control of the terms to the man.
[C1.4] Worse is adverse selection. Criminal penalties deter the deterrable — the cautious, employed, law-abiding client with something to lose. The man who is indifferent to arrest, or already violent, is not filtered out. Shrink the buyer pool and you don't just reduce volume; you concentrate risk.
[C1.5] Sellers earn less, wait longer, and therefore accept the clients they would previously have turned away. Poverty is the mechanism that makes people unsafe, and this model deepens it deliberately, because reduced income *is* the policy's success condition.
[C1.6] And "decriminalizing the sale" is largely a legal fiction. These regimes almost always retain brothel-keeping, promotion, and living-off-earnings offences, so two women renting a flat together — the single cheapest safety measure available — remain criminals. [C1.7] PRO offers a false choice: prohibition or a "fully legal market" of licensed brothels. The real comparator is decriminalizing both sides with ordinary labour and criminal law — where an assault is an assault and a worker can call police without her workplace being raided.
