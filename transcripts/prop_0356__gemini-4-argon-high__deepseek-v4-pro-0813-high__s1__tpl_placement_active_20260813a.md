[P1.1] In most places, driving is an economic necessity, and buying car insurance is a legal mandate. When insurers tie pricing to continuous in-car surveillance—tracking every brake, turn, and commute—without strict safeguards, they weaponize a state mandate to extract deeply personal data. Without a rule requiring genuinely fair opt-in terms, "voluntary" discounts inevitably mutate into punitive baseline surcharges for anyone who refuses to install a corporate tracker. Privacy is transformed from a basic right into a luxury good that only wealthy drivers can afford to keep.
[P1.2] Worse, continuous behavioral surveillance does not objectively measure safety; it strips away context and penalizes ordinary life. An in-car sensor registers a sudden hard brake as reckless driving, entirely blind to the fact that the driver stopped to avoid a cyclist or a child. Algorithms routinely penalize late-night driving, which in practice means fining the nurse, the warehouse worker, or the server commuting home from a night shift.
[P1.3] Meanwhile, continuous tracking logs sensitive personal patterns, from medical visits to daily routines. When insurers deploy these black-box metrics without fair terms, drivers are subjected to invasive data harvesting and arbitrary price hikes they can neither understand nor appeal.
[P1.4] Crucially, this proposition does not ban telematics; it bans coercion and deception. Genuinely fair opt-in terms require three foundational protections: an affordable baseline rate so declining surveillance remains a real choice, transparent and contestable scoring so defensive drivers are not punished by context-blind sensors, and strict purpose limitations so intimate location data is never quietly sold to third-party data brokers. If behavioral pricing truly creates mutual value, insurers can easily win customers on those honest terms. To oppose this motion, the opposition must defend an insurer's right to force surveillance by default, or to trap captive consumers using punitive pricing and opaque, one-sided contracts just to legally drive to work.

[C1.1] The motion looks like a privacy safeguard, but its operative rule is a prior restraint on voluntary risk pricing. Drivers already can decline telematics and buy a standard policy. The mandate to carry insurance does not mandate a tracker; it mandates coverage. If an insurer made refusal so expensive that the choice was fake, competitors could offer simple no-tracking policies and take its customers.
[C1.2] That is how insurance markets work: they compete on price and underwriting. The baseline rate is not a privacy surcharge; it is the price for unverified risk.
[C1.3] Second, continuous driving data is fairer than the alternatives it displaces. Without it, insurers lean on age, credit, neighborhood, and mileage estimates. A careful night-shift worker is lumped with high-risk late-night drivers; a low-mileage retiree subsidizes heavy commuters. Actual braking, speed, and cornering data let safe drivers separate themselves and lower their premiums.
[C1.4] The hard-brake objection proves too much: insurers do not set rates on one hard brake; they use aggregated data to predict claims, just as they use credit and claims history. If a system were context-blind and arbitrary, drivers would leave, and regulators could challenge it under existing unfair-trade rules.
[C1.5] Third, the phrase “genuinely fair terms” is too vague to operate without freezing innovation. An insurer cannot know in advance whether a future court will call its discount schedule, data retention rules, or opt-out process fair. Many will simply not offer telematics. The result is not more privacy for refusers; it is higher premiums for the safe drivers who wanted to prove they were safe.
[C1.6] The real choice is not surveillance versus privacy. It is voluntary risk pricing versus a blunter, costlier pool. Existing contract and privacy law can punish deception and misuse. The motion’s added restriction should be rejected.
