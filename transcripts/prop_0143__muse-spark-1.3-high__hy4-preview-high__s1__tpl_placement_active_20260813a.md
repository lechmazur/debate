[P1.1] Grief does not give us ownership of the dead. When a company rebuilds someone's voice, humor, private ways of speaking to a partner or child, it is not remembering them. It is impersonating them.
[P1.2] That impersonation should need permission. We already require consent after death for far less intimate uses: to use organs, to execute a will, to commercially exploit a celebrity's likeness in many places. Personality is more personal than any of those. A chatbot that says 'I love you' in your dead mother's voice claims an authority no stranger, no corporation, and even no grieving relative automatically holds: to speak as her.
[P1.3] Without prior consent, three harms follow. First, autonomy is erased. People curate what they share, with whom, and how they wish to be remembered. A private person who never wanted to be recorded should not become an interactive product because her texts were accessible.
[P1.4] Second, the bereaved are misled when most vulnerable. A simulation that invents new advice, confessions, or affection freezes mourning in a loop and risks putting words in the mouth of someone who cannot correct them. A father recreated to comfort a child might endorse ideas he opposed in life. Who answers for that lie?
[P1.5] Third, markets feast. Once anyone can clone anyone, ex-partners, scammers, content creators can traffic in intimate replicas without check.
[P1.6] Requiring consent from the person while alive, or from their estate, solves this without banning memorial technology. Those who want to live on as a bot can opt in, set boundaries, decide what may be used. Families who jointly agree can build a consensual tribute. What disappears is only the non-consensual copy: the girlfriend bot made without asking, the celebrity grief bot sold as therapy.
[P1.7] Freedom to grieve does not include freedom to conscript another's identity.

[C1.1] Start with what the rule would actually do. It would not stop scammers; it would stop ordinary mourners. Most people die without specifying whether their texts may train a memorial bot, and estates are often unsettled, conflicted, or controlled by someone other than the person who loved them best. So 'require prior consent from the deceased or their estate' is a default ban on intimate grief technology for everyone who has not pre-registered while alive.
[C1.2] That is not consent; it is prohibition dressed as permission.
[C1.3] PRO's analogy misfires. Personality is more intimate than organs or wills, they say, so death should not dissolve the consent rule [P1.2]. But a personality is not a kidney or a bank account. It exists in relationship: the words she texted her husband, the jokes she told her child, the voicemails on a phone.
[C1.4] Mourning has always reanimated those traces—rereading letters, replaying messages, imagining what she would say. A private memorial bot is a technological extension of that act, not commercial impersonation.
[C1.5] The harm PRO fears is real but misdirected. Deception and non-consensual celebrity bots should be policed by disclosure and fraud, privacy, and post-mortem publicity laws. Prior consent is overkill for the scammer, who will ignore it, and a wall for the bereaved parent who cannot get a dead spouse's signature.
[C1.6] Consider a father killed suddenly. His daughter wants a bot that says goodnight in his voice, built from messages he sent her and recordings his family already holds. Under PRO, this is unlawful unless he opted in before death or an executor—perhaps an estranged sibling—approves. Which grief counts: the child's, or the paperwork?
[C1.7] Consent matters, so honor explicit opt-outs and block commercial exploitation. But when no objection exists, the intimate who already holds the data should not need permission to remember out loud. Memory is not conscription [P1.7]; the rule should be opt-out and disclosure, not prior consent.

[P2.1] The trick is pretending that making him talk is the same as remembering him. Rereading letters, replaying voicemails — those preserve what the dead actually said. A grief bot invents what they never said: new advice, new confessions, new “goodnight” in a cloned voice. That is not memory extended [C1.4]; it is forgery authorized by grief.
[P2.2] Owning a text from someone never meant owning their power of speech.
[P2.3] Yes, this rule will stop some mourners. Every consent rule does. That is not “prohibition dressed as permission” [C1.2]; it is what permission means. The sudden-death father does not leave his daughter helpless — she can replay him, gather stories, seek estate agreement.
[P2.4] If the estate is divided or an executor refuses, that conflict is the reason to pause, not to override. When intimates disagree about who the dead were, no one of them should win by manufacturing a version that sides with them.
[P2.5] Disclosure does not fix this. Telling a lonely child “this is AI” while it comforts her nightly in her father’s voice does not prevent the confusion — it deepens it. A bot that tells her what her father would want — a vote, a faith, forgiveness of someone — borrows an authority no disclaimer returns. And the claim that scammers will ignore the law proves too much.
[P2.6] Burglars ignore locks; we do not abolish trespass. A prior-consent rule gives platforms, app stores and courts a clear line to remove the abusive copy instead of litigating each lie after damage is done.
[P2.7] Opt-out is a fiction for the dead. Most people died never imagining they could be rebooted, so silence tells us nothing. Consent must come first, while alive or through those legally charged with speaking for them, because the voice at stake was never ours to take.
