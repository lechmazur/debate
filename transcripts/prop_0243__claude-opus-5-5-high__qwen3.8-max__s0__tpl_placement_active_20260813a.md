The strongest reason to reject the motion is that ride-hail competes with the most congesting alternative: the privately owned car used for a single errand, then parked or circling for a space. In dense cities, a household that can rely on ride-hail, carshare, and transit may drop a second car or avoid buying one at all.
[C1.2] Fewer owned cars mean fewer vehicles sitting on streets, fewer cold-start trips, and less search for parking. Even if a ride-hail trip has empty approach miles, it can still reduce total car presence over the day.
[C1.3] Second, PRO conflates vehicle miles with congestion. Congestion is not simply the number of miles driven; it is how vehicles interact with scarce curb and road capacity. Much private-car congestion comes from low-occupancy trips, school runs, and drivers circling blocks. Ride-hail can intensify downtown cores, but it also substitutes for those inefficient private trips and for taxi cruising.
[C1.4] It also gives people an on-demand option that makes transit viable without owning a car, especially for late-night or low-frequency routes where buses are empty.
[C1.5] Third, the cities PRO cites do not establish a net, overall effect. San Francisco and New York acted because specific curbs and corridors were strained, not because the net citywide balance was clearly worse. Rising congestion in those years also reflected population growth, job growth, construction, freight delivery, and underpriced road space. If congestion was going to worsen anyway, ride-hail becomes a convenient scapegoat, not the cause.
[C1.6] The right question is not whether ride-hail adds some empty miles; it is whether major cities would have less total car dependence without it. The answer is likely no. Ride-hail is part of a mixed urban mobility system, and the proposition cannot prove it made congestion worse overall.
