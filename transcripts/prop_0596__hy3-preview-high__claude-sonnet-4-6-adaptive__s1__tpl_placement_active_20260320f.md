[C1.2] That is not a safeguard; it is a structural disincentive at the precise moment the industry is most fragile.
[C1.3] The innovation argument is not merely overstated risk, as PRO suggests. It is the central one. Gene-editing platforms—unlike small-molecule drugs—require continuous reinvestment to refine delivery vectors, reduce off-target effects, and develop tissue-specific applications. Investors funding these programs are already absorbing enormous uncertainty.
[C1.4] A credible compulsory licensing pathway changes the calculus before a single therapy reaches market. It does not wait for abuse; it announces in advance that governments may seize returns if a therapy is deemed sufficiently important. The predictable result is capital flight to lower-risk categories and reduced pipeline depth for the patients PRO claims to help.
[C1.5] The "essential" qualifier, far from being reassuring, is the proposal's sharpest flaw. Who determines essentiality? Governments under electoral pressure, lobbied by payers seeking cost reduction, face enormous temptation to classify broadly. Yesterday's orphan disease cure is tomorrow's national health burden.
[C1.6] Once the category is stretched, every breakthrough with a large patient population becomes a candidate. The pathway PRO describes is not a narrow valve—it is a gate that widens with political convenience.
[C1.7] There are better instruments. Tiered pricing agreements, publicly funded basic research with open licensing conditions attached at the grant stage, advance purchase commitments, and voluntary access initiatives address affordability without announcing to innovators that their highest-value assets are subject to compelled transfer. The debate is not access versus profit. It is whether this mechanism, as written, produces more access or less innovation—and the evidence favors the latter.

[P2.2] The argument that this will stifle innovation ignores that a cure no patient can afford is not meaningful innovation. Investors favor predictable rules expanding demand, not unregulated monopolies that price out 99% of patients, creating fragile revenue. A seven-figure gene therapy for a common genetic disorder that only a tiny pool of wealthy patients can access generates less total revenue than a mid-five-figure version available to thousands. This pathway expands the addressable market, creating more stable, scalable revenue for developers than the current model that relies on price-gouging niche patient pools.
[P2.3] Critics prefer tiered pricing or voluntary access programs, but those rely on corporate goodwill that has repeatedly failed. Past health crises prove voluntary measures fail marginalized populations, while binding mechanisms work. When companies can set prices untethered to production costs, they have no incentive to lower them for marginalized populations. This pathway creates a binding backstop that actually makes voluntary cost-sharing agreements more likely, as companies will prefer negotiating terms to facing compelled licensing.
[P2.4] Warnings about the "essential" qualifier being stretched ignore that the term is tied to objective medical criteria, not electoral whims. Companies already freely define "essential" markets to maximize profit—this pathway simply gives governments the same discretion to prioritize citizen welfare. Even the remote risk of overclassification pales against the current certainty that millions are denied life-saving cures because patent monopolies treat survival as a luxury good. This pathway fixes that imbalance, prioritizing public welfare over unlimited private profit from essential medicines.
