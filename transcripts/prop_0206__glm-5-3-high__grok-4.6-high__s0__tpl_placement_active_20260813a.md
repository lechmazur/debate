[P1.1] Life expectancy in most developed countries has risen by roughly a decade since the 1970s, yet many public pension systems still operate on retirement ages designed for that era. This is the core problem: we have built a fixed-age system on top of a moving-target demographic. The proposition says that when lifespans extend, the retirement age should follow. That is not austerity ideology—it is basic arithmetic and fairness across generations.
[P1.2] Start with the arithmetic. A pay-as-you-go pension depends on workers supporting retirees. The ratio between them is driven by two numbers: how long people work and how long they draw benefits. When lifespans rise and retirement ages do not, the dependency ratio deteriorates automatically—more years of benefits per career, and relatively fewer workers per retiree.
[P1.3] Indexing the retirement age to life expectancy is the only lever that corrects the imbalance at its structural source. Payroll tax increases, by contrast, treat the symptom: they pour more money into a formula that is still misaligned with reality, and they must be raised again and again with each decade of added longevity.
[P1.4] Second, consider who actually pays those taxes. Payroll taxes fall on labor income—and regressively, since they are typically capped and hit ordinary wages hardest. Raising them means young workers, who already face strained housing costs and precarious employment, transfer an ever-larger share of their income to retirees who, on average, now hold more wealth than the young. Indexing retirement age distributes the adjustment across the lifecycle instead of across generations: everyone works somewhat longer, rather than one cohort paying for another indefinitely.
[P1.5] Third, work capacity has changed. The extra years of life expectancy gained have largely been healthy years. Jobs today are far less physically punishing than in 1970, and policies can accommodate genuine hardship through disability provisions, early-retirement exceptions for arduous occupations, and gradual phased retirement. The existence of hard cases argues for well-designed indexing, not for abandoning it.

[C1.4] Payroll taxes are not a mere symptom treatment. They are how a pay-as-you-go system shares a longer-lived, more productive society. Indexing age does not spread adjustment fairly across a lifecycle.
[C1.5] It lengthens work for the already worn down and shortens the only healthy leisure many will ever get.
[C1.6] Labor markets refuse the neat model. Older workers who lose a job rarely find equivalent work. Age discrimination is common. A higher statutory age produces hidden unemployment, more disability claims, and old-age poverty.

[C1.9] Age indexing has no progressive dial: the same extra years for everyone, whatever their remaining health.
