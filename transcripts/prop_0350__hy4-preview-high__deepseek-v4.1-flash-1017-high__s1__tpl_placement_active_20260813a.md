[P1.1] Obesity is not mainly a puzzle of ignorance that a slogan can solve. People already know vegetables beat doughnuts and soda is sugary. The preventable driver is an environment engineered for overconsumption: cheap, hyper-palatable food is everywhere, portions are inflated, and marketing saturates daily life, especially children’s. When policy answers with another individual-behavior campaign—“choose water,” “move more”—it treats a structural problem as personal failure and asks people to defeat industry-designed cues with willpower alone.
[P1.2] Environmental and marketing restrictions change defaults instead of moralizing. A sugary-drink levy, limits on junk-food ads aimed at children, healthier school meals, and zoning that supports grocery access do not require anyone to read a poster or summon discipline at every meal; they reshape what is affordable, visible, and convenient. Because choice follows the path of least resistance, these measures move whole populations. If the corner shop stocks fresh food near the counter and cereal ads no longer use cartoon mascots on children’s programming, consumption shifts broadly, not only among the motivated.
[P1.3] Individual campaigns underperform exactly where obesity is worst. Their effects depend on attention, time, and cognitive bandwidth—resources strained by low income and food deserts—so they widen inequality because the already advantaged act on advice. They also leave the obesogenic environment intact. Telling a parent to cook from scratch while fast-food outlets cluster near schools, dollar menus undercut groceries, and targeted ads shape a child’s craving is not empowerment; it is blame.
[P1.4] Preferences are formed by exposure long before informed choice is possible.
[P1.5] The decisive question is which lever produces durable, fair population change. Structural restrictions protect real autonomy by reducing manipulation, shield children from marketing they cannot rationally resist, and alter defaults for everyone. Individual campaigns ask people to swim upstream against a current policy could shut off. Obesity policy should therefore focus more on food environment and marketing restrictions than on individual consumer behavior campaigns.

[C1.6] The strongest policy is not environment versus behavior. It is environment plus capability. Restrictions change the current; behavior support teaches people to swim and helps them navigate the current that remains. The proposition would starve the demand side and call it structural progress.
