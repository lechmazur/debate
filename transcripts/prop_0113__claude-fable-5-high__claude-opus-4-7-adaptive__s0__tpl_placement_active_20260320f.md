[P1.1] A bottle of soda costs a company a few cents to package. When that bottle ends up in a gutter, a landfill, or a municipal recycling truck, the cost of dealing with it lands on someone who never made a dime from its sale: the taxpayer. That arrangement is not natural law. It is a subsidy — a quiet, multibillion-dollar transfer from the public to some of the most profitable corporations on earth — and this debate is about whether it should continue.
[P1.2] The case rests on three pillars.
[P1.3] First, fairness. The polluter-pays principle is the bedrock of how we handle every other industrial byproduct. A chemical plant cannot dump effluent in a river and send the city the cleanup bill. Yet beverage and packaging giants design products whose end-of-life cost they knowingly externalize.
[P1.4] Packaging waste is not an accident; it is a predictable, engineered feature of their business model. The party that designs the waste, profits from the waste, and controls the waste stream should pay for the waste.
[P1.5] Second, incentives. Taxpayers cannot redesign a bottle; producers can. When companies internalize disposal costs, they suddenly discover lighter packaging, refillable systems, and genuinely recyclable materials. Where producers bear real responsibility — deposit-return systems in places like Norway and Germany — return rates climb toward ninety percent.
[P1.6] Where taxpayers foot the bill, recycling stagnates, because the one actor with design power has no skin in the game.
[P1.7] Third, equity. Municipal waste costs fall on everyone, including people who buy little packaging at all. A regressive system makes a retiree on a fixed income subsidize the disposal costs of a global cola brand.
[P1.8] Note what the motion asks: not that producers pay everything, but that they pay much more than they do now — shifting the burden off taxpayers. To defeat it, my opponent must defend the status quo: privatized profits, socialized cleanup. That position should be as hard to defend as it sounds.

[C1.2] So the real question is not "corporations versus taxpayers." It is: should disposal be funded through municipal budgets — which in most countries draw heavily on income and property tax — or through a flat surcharge on packaged goods that hits hardest the very retiree PRO claims to defend? [C1.3] Second, look at what "producer pays" actually becomes in practice. Recycling rates in such systems often plateau, and the cost per tonne can rise, because the entity collecting the fee is the same entity lobbying to keep obligations soft.
[C1.4] Third, PRO's strongest evidence — Norway and Germany hitting ninety percent return rates — is a deposit-return system on drink containers. Fine. But that is a narrow, targeted policy already compatible with the status quo in many places. The motion is far broader: shift "much more" of the cost of all packaging waste collection onto producers.
[C1.5] Conflating a working niche scheme with a sweeping cost transfer is the central bait-and-switch of the PRO case.
[C1.6] Finally, producers do not litter; people do. They do not run trucks or sorting plants; cities do, at scale. Breaking that public infrastructure into thousands of producer-funded contracts trades a system with economies of scale for fragmented bureaucracy.
[C1.7] The honest choice here is between a transparent public service and a hidden, regressive grocery tax. Pick honesty.
