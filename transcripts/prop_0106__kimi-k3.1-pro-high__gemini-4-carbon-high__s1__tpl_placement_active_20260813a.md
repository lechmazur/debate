[P1.1] This motion does not ask whether every distressed teenager should receive medication. It asks whether a diagnosed minor may receive puberty blockers or hormone therapy when parents and qualified clinicians agree treatment is appropriate. Access means a safeguarded clinical option, not treatment on demand.
[P1.2] The comparison CON cannot avoid is that treatment has risks, but so does refusing it. Gender dysphoria can make puberty feel like an accelerating loss of control over one’s body. Some adolescents experience persistent distress, impaired schooling, social withdrawal, and self-harm. For a carefully assessed patient whose symptoms endure, blockers can suppress further pubertal changes and create time for continued evaluation without forcing the body further in a direction causing distress.
[P1.3] Hormone therapy, generally a later and more consequential step, can align physical development more closely with the patient’s experienced identity. The aim is relieving suffering and restoring function, not ideological conformity.
[P1.4] CON will likely invoke uncertainty, bone health, fertility, regret, and adolescent judgment. Those concerns justify safeguards: thorough assessment, qualified clinical judgment, parental agreement, developmentally appropriate assent, honest counseling about unknowns, and monitoring. They do not justify ruling out every minor in advance. Blockers are not risk-free, and some hormone effects can be lasting.
[P1.5] But medicine routinely makes individualized decisions under uncertainty, especially when waiting itself carries consequences.
[P1.6] Imagine a fifteen-year-old with years of persistent dysphoria, assessed over time, whose distress intensifies with each bodily change. CON must say that even if the family and treating team judge a staged intervention best, the answer remains no because another adolescent might regret treatment. That protects a hypothetical future patient by sacrificing the actual one.
[P1.7] A debate about stricter screening or better follow-up is compatible with this motion; only categorical denial supports CON. The correct standard is not “prove every outcome before offering care,” which would paralyze pediatric medicine. It is whether a regulated option can serve a particular minor’s best interests. Where rigorous assessment and shared decision-making say yes, access should remain.

Halting it compromises bone density accrual, interrupts psychosexual maturation, and routinely propels youths onto cross-sex hormones rather than neutral reflection.

[C2.2] Blockers, by contrast, arrest bone accrual and psychosexual maturation, while hormones add irreversible cross-sex traits, lifelong medicalization, impaired sexual response, and, after early blockade, potential permanent infertility. Waiting with support imposes a difficult delay; early medicalization inflicts losses no adult can undo.
[C2.3] That asymmetry exposes the flaw in [P2.1]. You cannot determine a child’s "present best interests" by severing them from the adult who must inhabit that body for decades. Nor does [P2.3] rescue staged care by calling post-blocker hormone use "persistent need."
[C2.4] Because puberty itself helps many youths reconcile with their sexed bodies, halting it removes the very developmental process that could resolve dysphoria, turning blockade into a self-fulfilling track.
[C2.5] "Shared decision-making" [P2.4] cannot carry these stakes either. Because diagnosis rests on reported identity, not an objective physical test, distressed parents and clinicians are still building permanent medicine on an unfinished adolescent self-concept. [C2.6] Contrary to [P2.5] and [P2.6], reserving medical transition for adulthood weighs both present and future harms. Intensive mental-health care treats acute distress and self-harm risk today without destroying healthy capacities. Because no team can tell beforehand which minor will regret irreversible bodily loss, an age boundary is the only safeguard that protects both the hurting teenager and the adult they will become.
