[P1.1] Every ride-hail trip involves three parties: a rider paying a fare, a driver doing the work, and a platform setting both prices while showing each side only what it wants them to see. The platform knows the exact split; the rider and driver do not. That asymmetry is the whole problem, and transparency by default is the obvious fix.
[P1.2] First, drivers are running businesses on incomplete information. A driver deciding whether to accept a trip is effectively asked to bid on a sealed price. If the rider pays $30 and the driver receives $18, the driver needs to know that ratio to decide whether the trip is worth the fuel, the dead miles home, and the depreciation. Platforms already show drivers an upfront fare in many markets — but not what the rider paid, so drivers cannot tell whether a low offer reflects genuine trip economics or a quiet squeeze on their share.
[P1.3] Mandatory full-split disclosure turns every accept decision from a guess into a calculation.
[P1.4] Second, riders are being asked to trust a black box. Surge pricing, service fees, booking fees, and driver incentives are blended into one number with no itemization. When a rider pays $40 and later learns the driver received $22, the resentment lands on the driver — the tip screen implies the driver is underpaid by the rider's stinginess, when the gap was designed by the platform. Disclosure would correctly locate responsibility.
[P1.5] Third, opacity survives only because it benefits one party. Every other major marketplace that splits money between an intermediary and a provider discloses the split: app stores publish commission rates, credit card networks publish interchange, restaurants disclose card surcharges in many jurisdictions. Ride-hail is the outlier because drivers and riders have never had the leverage to demand it.
[P1.6] The privacy objection is weak — a split needs two numbers and a percentage, no personal data. The trade-secret objection is weaker still — commission rates are not algorithms; Uber already publishes take rates for investors.
[P1.7] Transparency, information symmetry, and market efficiency all point one [clipped]

[C1.1] Requiring a full fare split sounds like sunlight, but it discloses the wrong number and poisons the decisions around it.
[C1.2] Rider fares and driver pay are not two slices of one pie. Platforms price them separately: what a rider will pay to get a car in the rain, and what a driver needs to leave home at midnight. Sometimes that means the platform takes a large cut; sometimes it subsidizes the ride and takes a loss. Freezing that fluid balancing act into a single take-rate percentage on every screen pretends every trip is zero-sum profiteering.
[C1.3] Riders will read normal cross-subsidization as theft.
[C1.4] That misunderstanding actively makes drivers poorer. What determines whether a trip is worth it is dollars per minute, miles, and deadhead back — not what the stranger in the back seat paid. Tell a driver 'rider pays $30, you get $18' and many will reject a perfectly profitable $18 trip out of insult, waiting instead for a 'fair' $14 trip. More cherry-picking means longer waits, more cancellations, lower utilization, and lower total earnings.
[C1.5] Advocates call this calculation; it is resentment masquerading as information.
[C1.6] Riders misuse it too. Put 'driver gets $22 of your $40' in front of every passenger and you invite haggling, confrontation, cancelled rides, and off-app cash deals that strip away insurance, tracking, and safety protections. Or the opposite perversity: when riders see a driver got a 'generous' share, they skip the tip. Either way the driver loses.
[C1.7] The mandate's endpoint is worse: political pressure for a fixed commission cap. That would kill the loss-leader trips that keep short, suburban, and low-demand rides served at all. Keep pricing logic private and flexible, and make platforms compete on what actually matters — reliable pickups for riders and profitable hourly earnings for drivers.
