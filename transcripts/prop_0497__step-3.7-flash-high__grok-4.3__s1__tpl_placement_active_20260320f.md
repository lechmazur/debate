[P1.1] Cultural heritage is not confined to paper and celluloid, yet our national archives still exclude the defining creative works of the 21st century: games, apps, digital art, and culturally significant software. This gap is not a minor oversight—it is active, ongoing erasure of modern cultural history, and mandatory legal deposit, matching the structure of existing book and film requirements, is the only scalable, equitable fix. Consider the 2020 death of Adobe Flash: thousands of independent games, experimental digital art pieces, and interactive educational tools built on the platform vanished overnight when support was discontinued, with no formal legal pathway for national archives to preserve them.
[P1.2] Only ad-hoc, legally precarious hobbyist efforts saved a fraction of these works, many of which were cultural touchstones for a generation of internet users. Flash is not an outlier: when servers for beloved MMOs shut down, when app developers pull culturally significant apps due to platform policy changes, when digital art platforms go dark, those works disappear entirely unless archives have a mandate to collect them. Critics will claim software is too hard to preserve, or that deposit imposes unfair burdens on creators.
[P1.3] But film archives already store original reels that require specialized, expensive playback equipment; book archives maintain climate-controlled storage for fragile physical media. Mandated deposit for digital works would require submission of source code, emulation context, and documentation, with tiered requirements tied to clear cultural significance thresholds (e.g., user base size, critical acclaim) to avoid burdening small independent creators. Most critically, a legal deposit requirement would eliminate the current copyright barrier that stops archives from preserving works without explicit creator permission—the single biggest obstacle to saving our digital cultural heritage today.

[C1.1] Mandating legal deposit for software would fail because digital works cannot be preserved by static handover the way books or films can. A game or app depends on specific operating systems, hardware drivers, network services, and external libraries that themselves disappear. Depositing source code and emulation notes does not keep the title runnable once the surrounding infrastructure moves on; it merely creates an inert file collection that requires continuous, expensive engineering to remain functional. National archives lack the resources or mandate to perform that ongoing maintenance for every deposited title.
[C1.2] The selection problem compounds the issue. Any workable mandate needs clear criteria for “culturally significant” software, yet governments will inevitably apply political, bureaucratic, or budgetary filters. Smaller independent creators whose work falls below arbitrary thresholds receive no protection, while well-connected studios game the system or face sudden compliance costs that deter new development. The Flash example illustrates the opposite problem: even when material is recognized as worth saving, the legal and technical obstacles are not solved by a deposit rule alone; they require active reverse-engineering and rights negotiation that archives already struggle to conduct at scale.
[C1.3] Finally, the copyright barrier is overstated. Existing exceptions for preservation, research, and private copying already allow targeted acquisition without blanket compulsion. A mandatory system replaces targeted effort with indiscriminate collection, flooding repositories with unmaintainable code while doing nothing to guarantee public access or long-term usability. The result is symbolic paperwork rather than genuine cultural continuity.

Emulation does not erase this gap—it merely shifts the maintenance burden onto archives that already lack funding for perpetual reverse-engineering across thousands of titles.

[C2.3] Existing copyright exceptions already permit targeted preservation where genuine public interest can be shown. A blanket deposit mandate replaces that discretion with indiscriminate collection, guaranteeing repositories filled with code that cannot be kept executable. The result is not systematic rescue of digital heritage but an administrative record of submissions whose long-term usability depends on resources archives have never demonstrated they possess.
