[P1.1] A recession is a risk. Housing inflation is a bill, and it arrives every month, compounds every year, and never gets refunded.
[P1.2] That asymmetry decides this debate. The motion asks central banks to accept a higher *probability* of a downturn in exchange for attacking the largest, stickiest cost in ordinary life. That trade is worth making, for three reasons.
[P1.3] First, housing is the inflation people cannot escape. When beef gets expensive, you buy chicken, but there is no substitute for a roof. Shelter is the largest single line in most household budgets and one of the heaviest components of the price indexes central banks are mandated to control. A central bank that is soft on housing inflation is simply not doing its job, because the core of the problem is still burning.
[P1.4] Second, the harms are not symmetrical. Recessions are painful, but they are temporary and they can be cushioned: unemployment insurance, fiscal stimulus and rate cuts all exist for exactly that purpose. Housing inflation has no cushion and no recovery phase. Rents that jump rarely fall back.
[P1.5] A young worker priced out of ownership at thirty does not get that decade returned when growth resumes. Meanwhile every year of tolerated housing inflation transfers wealth from renters and the young to those who already own assets. [P1.6] Third, tolerating housing inflation does not avoid recession. It postpones it and makes it larger. Housing prices are driven by credit, and credit is precisely what central banks control. [C1.1] Housing inflation is a supply problem, and central banks do not control supply. That fact destroys the motion. The proposition asks monetary authorities to raise recession risk to fight a cost driven largely by land-use regulation, permitting delays, and construction capacity—factors entirely outside their remit. When rates rise, developers do not suddenly break ground on more apartments; they shelve projects because financing costs spike, shrinking future supply and entrenching the very shortage that made rents soar.
[C1.2] The tool cannot reach the target, but it can still break the economy.
[C1.3] Pro frames recessions as temporary and cushioned, and housing costs as permanent and uncushioned. That asymmetry is overstated. Recessions leave deep scars: workers displaced in a downturn rarely recover lost earnings; small firms shuttered by tight credit do not reopen; and households entering a weak labor market delay family formation and buy less housing, not more. The 2008 crash was supposed to deliver affordable homes; it delivered foreclosures, frozen credit, and a lost decade of new renters.
[C1.4] Sacrificing growth to force down rents often destroys the very income growth that would make housing affordable.
[C1.5] Nor is this the 1980s. Volcker fought broad, wage-driven inflation. Today’s housing inflation is geographically concentrated and structurally constrained. Raising rates to cool rents in supply-starved cities is like using a sledgehammer to perform surgery—it shatters surrounding tissue without touching the clot.
[C1.6] If housing costs are the enemy, the weapons are zoning reform, social housing, and construction subsidies. Central banks are guardians of aggregate stability and employment. They should refuse a trade that sacrifices certain jobs and output for an uncertain, likely unattainable rent reduction.
