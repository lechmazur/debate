[P1.1] High-harassment transit systems should be allowed to offer women-only cars or sections. Allowed is the key word. That modesty is a strength, not a weakness. This is not a mandate, not a claim that one car solves transit violence, and not an excuse to abandon staffing, lighting, enforcement, reporting, and design.
[P1.2] It is permission to add one immediate safeguard while the deeper failures are still being repaired.
[P1.3] Harassment changes freedom of movement: the late shift, the evening class, the visit across town become calculations of risk. In a packed train, a woman may be unable to identify the hand, move away, or prove a threat before it is dismissed as crowding. A designated car changes the practical geometry: fewer possible aggressors, clearer signs of intrusion, easier staff intervention, and a safer place chosen before the crush begins. Using it is not a judgment on all men; it is a rational response to a known pattern of abuse.
[P1.4] The “only partial” objection proves too much. Locks do not end burglary, cameras do not end assault, brighter platforms do not end stalking, yet we do not ban them because other harms remain. A perfect-safety-or-nothing rule sounds principled only if one ignores who bears the waiting: the rider told that, because the whole network is unsafe, she may not have any safer option inside it.
[P1.5] An optional car need not be “separate but unequal.” It can sit on the same train, at the same fare and quality, be voluntary for women, use clear, respectful enforcement, and leave the duty to police the rest of the system intact. Other riders lose one choice of where to stand; women gain a meaningful chance to avoid sexualized intimidation. When a system has already failed, banning this tool preserves the failure in its cleanest legal form. The better rule is harm reduction now, with full reform still owed.

[C1.2] When a transit authority has already failed basic safety, allowing it to rebrand one car hands officials a visible, low-cost alibi that drains political pressure for expensive essentials: more frequent trains to relieve crush loads, platform staffing, and real accountability.
[C1.3] Worse, segregation is not merely partial; it actively endangers the majority of women left outside that car. One section cannot absorb half a rush-hour crowd, nor does it protect platforms, stairs, or bus transfers. Women riding with male relatives or teenage sons, boarding near a needed exit, or squeezed out once the designated car fills must ride in general cars. [C1.4] Harassers, bystanders, and indifferent staff treat a woman groped in a mixed car as partly at fault for skipping the women's section. Brighter lights do not license assault next door; segregated cars teach that general public space belongs to men by default.

Without dedicated guards, crushes breach the boundary or passengers turn vigilante; with guards, thirty-second stops force appearance-based gender policing that humiliates transgender women, masculine-presenting women, and mothers with boys. And any agency with staff to guard car doors [P1.3] could instead deploy them to patrol crowding and eject harassers, delivering immediate relief without state-sanctioned segregation. Public transit owes women equal safety across every car, not containment inside one.
