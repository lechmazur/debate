[P1.5] This moratorium does not shut down existing services. It does not block upgrades to already launched constellations. It simply presses pause on launching hundreds of thousands more craft, until we have enforceable rules that protect this shared, irreplaceable resource. That is not radical.

A pause on new constellations leaves every one of those objects exactly where it is.
[C1.2] Meanwhile the satellites it stops are the ones with electric thrusters, automated conjunction screening, and orbits low enough that atmospheric drag drags failures down within a few years. You would be freezing the maneuverable population to protect the unmaneuverable one.
[C1.3] Second, ask who actually obeys. There is no world regulator to impose this; there are national licensing authorities. So the operators who stand down are the ones inside transparent legal systems, and the ones who fill the vacated shells and spectrum are those with the least interest in deorbit reliability or brightness mitigation. Orbital slots and frequencies are use-it-or-lose-it.
[C1.4] A moratorium doesn't create an empty sky waiting politely for rules; it reallocates the sky to whoever ignores the pause.
[C1.5] Third, the trigger is a trap. "Until rules are materially stronger" hands an indefinite veto to anyone who judges the rules insufficient — and rules get stronger precisely through operational pressure. Brightness-reduction coatings, sunshades, satellite-avoidance windows for observatories, autonomous collision avoidance, published ephemerides: these emerged from friction between astronomers and active operators, not from a decade of quiet treaty drafting with nobody launching. Remove the deployments and you remove the funding, the data, and the political urgency that make binding standards happen.
[C1.6] And the costs land on real people. A fishing vessel, a wildfire crew, a village clinic, a war-zone hospital: their alternative to satellite broadband is not slightly worse broadband, it's none.
[C1.7] The right instrument is conditional licensing — mandate deorbit performance, insurance, brightness limits, data sharing, and enforce them on each new tranche. That gets stronger rules without buying them with a self-imposed blackout.
