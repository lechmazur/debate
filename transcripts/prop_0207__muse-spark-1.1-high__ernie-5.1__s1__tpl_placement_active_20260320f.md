[P1.1] Universal fuel subsidies are sold as pro-poor protection but are profoundly anti-poor in practice. Fuel consumption rises steeply with income. The household with two cars, an air conditioner and a diesel generator captures many times more subsidy than a farmer who walks or takes a minibus. The result is a massive transfer to the upper quintiles, financed by a budget hole that directly crowds out spending on clinics, schools, buses and roads the poor actually use.
[P1.2] Holding pump prices below market also destroys the signal prices are supposed to send. When gasoline costs less than bottled water, people drive farther, idle longer, and neglect efficiency. Smugglers truck cheap fuel across borders to sell at market rates. State oil companies bleed losses, underinvest, and shortages follow - leading to the absurdity of cheap official prices and long queues, as Nigeria has seen for years.
[P1.3] The subsidy is burned twice: once in the treasury, once in wasted fuel, traffic and polluted air. It also locks in fossil dependence at exactly the moment governments claim to want transition.
[P1.4] The real problem is not the price of fuel, but low incomes. If a family cannot afford cooking energy or transport, give it purchasing power, not a distorted pump price. A targeted cash transfer lets a low-income household decide: spend on bus fare if it must commute, on food if that is more urgent, or on a more efficient stove. The price remains true, so conservation and investment in alternatives are not punished and the fiscal cost is capped.
[P1.5] Cash is simply better targeted. Even imperfect registries miss far fewer poor people than universal subsidies that by design include everyone — including foreign truckers and wealthy industry. Cash is transparent, adjustable with inflation, and can move through bank accounts and mobile money most states now have. Indonesia's BLT and Iran's 2010 reform show the politics: people will accept real prices when compensation arrives first.
[P1.6] Phase out the price control, keep the protection.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds elegant on paper: cut the waste, give cash to the poor, everyone wins. In practice, it collapses under the weight of what targeting actually requires — and what it destroys on the way out.
[C1.2] Targeted cash transfers demand something governments rarely possess: a reliable, up-to-date registry of who is poor, where they live, and how to reach them. In countries where fuel subsidies matter most, that infrastructure is weak or absent. India's experience with direct benefit transfers shows how exclusion errors persist even with massive biometric investment — eligible households fall through cracks, ineligible ones slip in, and the political cost of every mistake is explosive. When you strip away a universal benefit and replace it with a means-tested one, you create a class of visible losers who had the subsidy yesterday and don't have it today.
[C1.3] That is not a technical glitch; it is a recipe for unrest.
[C1.4] Consider the transition itself. Removing price controls overnight spikes transport costs, which ripples through food prices, wages, and business viability before any cash arrives. Egypt, Ecuador, and Kazakhstan all learned this the hard way — protests, not efficiency gains. The proposition assumes a clean handoff that real bureaucracies cannot execute at speed or scale.
[C1.5] Universal subsidies also do more than PRO admits. They keep rural transport networks alive, subsidize agricultural inputs and fishing fleets, and maintain industrial competitiveness in economies where energy costs determine survival. A cash transfer to a household does not substitute for cheap diesel keeping a supply chain functional.
[C1.6] Most fundamentally, the proposition treats targeting as a solved problem. It is not. The administrative apparatus needed to identify, verify, and disburse to the genuinely poor is itself expensive, corruptible, and slow — often costing a meaningful share of the savings the reform was supposed to generate. Before we dismantle a blunt instrument, we should be honest about whether the scalpel actually works.

[C2.1] PRO's case rests on a sleight of hand: it treats the reform as if it happens in a vacuum, cash appearing magically before prices move. In reality, the sequencing problem is not theoretical — it is the central failure point. PRO points to Indonesia and Iran as proof that cash-first works, but both cases involved massive political capital, external fiscal support, and years of preparation that most fuel-subsidy countries simply do not have. Egypt did not explode because it removed subsidies; it exploded because it removed them without the capacity PRO is now pretending is routine.
[C2.2] PRO also waves away the political economy with a buffet analogy — "you don't keep a free buffet because you can't find the hungry." But a universal subsidy is not a buffet; it is a shared public good. When you strip it away, you do not create grateful recipients of cash — you create millions of visible losers who had something yesterday and have nothing today. That is not a targeting error; it is a political detonator.
[C2.3] PRO's own claim that exclusion errors cause unrest conveniently ignores that removal errors cause far worse unrest.
[C2.4] On supply chains, PRO says cheap diesel doesn't keep rural buses running — bankrupt importers and smuggling do. But who creates the bankrupt importers? The subsidy itself, when prices are held below cost and the state oil company bleeds. PRO wants to phase out the distortion while pretending the distortion is separate from the system it lives in.
[C2.5] You cannot cleanly extract the subsidy without first wrecking the import finances that keep fuel flowing at all.
[C2.6] The deeper problem PRO never confronts: cash transfers assume functioning markets where money buys goods. In remote areas with thin markets, a household with cash and no fuel faces the same immobility it faced before — except now it has no price signal to encourage efficiency or alternative energy. Targeting solves the distribution question. It does not solve the delivery question, the inflation question, or the political survival question.
