[P1.1] The question is not whether nuclear is low carbon. It is whether it is necessary as the centerpiece. The last ten years of energy economics answered that.
[P1.2] Solar and wind are no longer alternative. They are the cheapest new electricity in history for most of the world. Nuclear has moved in the opposite direction. A new reactor in the West now costs many times its initial budget and takes a decade or more after permitting.
[P1.3] In that same decade a country can permit, build and connect gigawatts of renewables in 12 to 18 month cycles, learning and driving costs down each cycle. If the goal is to cut carbon fast before 2035, betting the center of the plan on the slowest, most expensive technology is a strategic failure.
[P1.4] Reliability does not save the case. The old idea that grids need inflexible baseload to survive intermittency has been retired by operators themselves. Modern systems manage intermittency with a portfolio that nuclear cannot provide: cheap overbuilding of renewables, batteries that now handle evening peaks cost-effectively, existing hydropower, interconnectors, and flexible demand. Nuclear is a poor complement - it cannot ramp cheaply, it demands high constant prices to pay off, and it crowds out the flexible resources that make high-renewable grids work.
[P1.5] Finally, "most countries" matters. Some 30 countries run nuclear plants. Nearly 200 do not. For them to build a central nuclear strategy would require creating a regulator, a waste repository, a security apparatus, and a financing structure for a 10-plus billion dollar asset from scratch.
[P1.6] Denmark, Portugal, Kenya, Vietnam, Chile - they are decarbonizing power precisely because they can scale wind, solar and storage without that burden.
[P1.7] Nuclear may remain for a few rich, large-grid states with existing fleets. For most, it is no longer necessary, and not central. The future is renewable-led; nuclear at best is a costly side investment, not the core.
