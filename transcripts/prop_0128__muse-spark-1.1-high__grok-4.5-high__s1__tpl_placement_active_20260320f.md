[P1.1] An employer who admits a college degree isn't genuinely necessary for performance, but requires it anyway, is confessing to a bad hiring practice. That's what this proposition ends.
[P1.2] For decades American employers engaged in degree inflation. Jobs that had been done for years by high school graduates - executive assistants, customer service leads, IT help desk, bank branch managers, sales reps - suddenly listed a bachelor's as the minimum. Not because the work changed, but because the degree was a cheap sorting device when applicants were plentiful.
[P1.3] The cost is enormous, and it falls on everyone.
[P1.4] First, it shrinks talent for no gain. If a four-year credential isn't needed to do the job well, filtering on it discards capable workers, lengthens vacancies, and forces you to pay a premium for a signal that doesn't predict success. That's why companies like IBM, Google, Delta and Bank of America stripped degrees from large swaths of roles - not as charity, but to fill jobs faster with better-matched people. Skills-tested hires stay longer because they were judged on fit, not paper.
[P1.5] Second, it turns inequality into company policy. College access tracks wealth, zip code, and race. Only about a third of American adults holds a bachelor's. Requiring it where unnecessary tells a veteran with ten years of logistics experience, or a community-college-trained coder with a real portfolio, that proven ability matters less than a proxy they never had the chance to buy.
[P1.6] That is not meritocracy. It is class gatekeeping.
[P1.7] We have better tools now - work samples, industry certifications, paid trials, structured interviews - that predict performance far better than a diploma in an unrelated field.
[P1.8] This proposition doesn't lower standards. It raises them, from counting credentials to proving competence. If the degree is genuinely necessary, keep it. If it isn't, stop requiring it.

[C1.2] Degree inflation was a rational response to larger applicant pools and the steep expense of bad hires. A bachelor’s degree offers a cheap, scalable first cut that works at volume. Work samples, certifications, and paid trials can be useful, yet they are costly to administer widely, susceptible to gaming, and still require an initial screen. Dropping the degree simply shifts selection to polished interviewing or personal connections—advantages that frequently track wealth and networks more tightly than a public college pathway open through community colleges and financial aid.
[C1.3] Even firms that stripped requirements from some postings continue to recruit heavily from colleges and keep the degree filter for higher-volume or higher-stakes roles. [C1.4] Treating the requirement as pure class gatekeeping misunderstands the trade-off. Veterans and portfolio-strong applicants already get through via experience, yet that fact does not force every employer to rebuild their entire process. Employers absorb the cost of mismatch; they are entitled to retain an imperfect but transparent proxy that has reduced risk for decades. The proposition demands a purity test around “genuine necessity” that no real hiring system can cleanly meet, and markets already punish pure excess.
[C1.5] Necessity is not the sole rational criterion. Keep requiring degrees where the signal continues to help.
