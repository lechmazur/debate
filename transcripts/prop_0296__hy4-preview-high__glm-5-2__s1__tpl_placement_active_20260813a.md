[P1.1] Public memory is not an archive; it is a hierarchy of honor. A name attached to a school, a street sign, or a public holiday is not a neutral relic of the past. It is the government's present-tense judgment that this person deserves communal esteem. This debate is not about forgetting colonizers, slaveholders, or dictators; it is about whether the state should keep celebrating them.
[P1.2] Honoring is active, not passive. A school bearing a slaveholder's name tells students whose ancestors were chained that the community still treats the enslaver as a model. A street named for a slave trader or colonial governor is not a history lesson; it is a daily claim that wealth extracted from human suffering deserves gratitude, not judgment. A holiday does the same: a paid day off named for a dictator is not a neutral date; it is an annual civic act of praise.
[P1.3] Historical continuity cannot launder that claim. Continuity of a name is not continuity of knowledge. We can preserve memory through archives, curricula, museums, and honest plaques while removing names from places where children learn and communities pay tribute.
[P1.4] Post-Franco Spain shows the difference. Cities did not forget Franco when they renamed streets that honored him; they reclassified him from civic hero to studied oppressor. Where Columbus Day became Indigenous Peoples' Day, Columbus did not disappear from history; the state simply stopped pretending a man who enslaved and dispossessed indigenous people deserves a national festival. Renaming is not erasure; it preserves memory and withdraws honor.
[P1.5] Historical continuity is worth keeping only when the thing continued is worth continuing. Honoring the perpetrators of conquest, slavery, and dictatorship is not heritage; it is a continuing insult to their victims' descendants. Governments should rename these symbols, keep the history in context, and reserve public honor for those who affirm equal dignity.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds morally clean until you ask who decides. Nearly every founding figure of every nation qualifies under some interpretation: Washington owned slaves. Churchill presided over colonial famine.
[C1.2] The standard is not a filter; it is a net, and once governments accept that public names are provisional—subject to recall whenever moral fashion shifts—no name is safe. That does not protect history; it subordinates memory to factional turnover.
[C1.3] PRO's framing that naming equals "celebration" is too thin. A name accumulated over centuries is not a continuous endorsement of every act of its namesake; it is a sediment of civic history. Tearing down names does not neutrally "withdraw honor." It asserts that the present generation possesses moral certainty superior to all prior generations and that this certainty should be permanently inscribed in stone.
[C1.4] That is not humility; it is the same arrogant certainty that built the monuments in the first place, just pointing in a different direction.
[C1.5] The Spain and Columbus examples prove the danger, not the safety, of the proposition. Franco's Spain renamed streets by factional decree. Columbus Day controversies remain unresolved decades later because renaming does not settle moral questions—it relocates them from history books to street-sign fights, generation after generation. The promised "contextualization" rarely materializes; the plaque goes unread, the archive stays unvisited, and what remains is simply a renamed landscape with no memory attached at all.
[C1.6] Continuity is not a defense of evil. It is a defense of the principle that civic identity cannot be rebuilt every twenty years without cost. When governments rename everything tied to moral failure, they teach citizens that the past is disposable and that public memory is merely policy. The honest path is contextualization in place—plaques, curricula, added signage—not the erasure that PRO mistakes for justice.
