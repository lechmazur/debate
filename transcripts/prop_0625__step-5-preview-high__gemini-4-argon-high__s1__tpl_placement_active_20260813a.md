[P1.1] Solar geoengineering field experiments are not ordinary field science. Even a limited stratospheric particle release is an intervention in a global commons. Particles, changed sunlight, and side effects do not respect borders. A test intended by one government as “small” can alter rainfall, temperature, or atmospheric circulation patterns that affect agriculture, water supplies, ecosystems, and public health across many countries.
[P1.2] The people bearing those consequences have no vote in the experiment’s design. That makes national permitting alone an inadequate safeguard, because the object being changed is not a contained apparatus but the shared atmosphere.
[P1.3] The question is therefore sequencing, not whether solar geoengineering should ever be studied. A temporary moratorium stops outdoor trials while allowing modeling, laboratory research, environmental monitoring, and governance design to continue. It forces states to answer the questions that must precede intervention: who may authorize a trial, what transboundary harms trigger liability, what monitoring and shutdown duties apply, and how compensation is enforced. Those rules cannot be improvised after material is already aloft.
[P1.4] A pause is modest compared with the cost of discovering the answers only after a contested release.
[P1.5] CON must show that voluntary guidelines, national permits, or scientific self-restraint are enough. But that is precisely the failure mode. One state’s “limited” test can set precedent, invite imitation, reduce incentives to cut emissions, or be perceived as hostile climate manipulation. Waiting costs time; proceeding without binding governance risks irreversible effects, geopolitical escalation, and a world in which the right to alter the atmosphere belongs to whoever moves first.
[P1.6] The moratorium should begin now, so that field experiments become a decision of the international community rather than an accident of national ambition.
