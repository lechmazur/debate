[P1.1] When you buy a ticket, you're purchasing access to an event. The fundamental question is whether that purchase grants ownership-like rights or merely a conditional, revocable permission that organizers can reshape at will.
[P1.2] Digital ticketing has tilted this balance sharply. Platforms increasingly use "anti-fraud" or "anti-scalping" systems to lock tickets to specific accounts, preventing any transfer—whether to a friend, a family member, or a fellow fan. A fan who buys a ticket and can't attend is left with a worthless digital asset instead of something they can give away or sell at face value.
[P1.3] This is the core issue: organizers conflate preventing scalping with preventing transfer. The proposition draws a clear line. Fans should have a legal right to transfer tickets peer-to-peer. Organizers who genuinely want to stop scalping can do so—but only through narrowly tailored limits, not blanket restrictions that trap legitimate fans.
[P1.4] "Narrowly tailored" has real meaning. An organizer could cap resale prices, require verified transfers, or limit how many tickets any individual can resell. What they couldn't do is make tickets non-transferable by default and call it consumer protection. The burden shifts to organizers to justify restrictions, rather than fans begging for exceptions.
[P1.5] The opposition will argue that organizers need broad control to protect their business models. But a business model that depends on denying consumers basic transfer rights isn't one the law should shield. We don't let book publishers prevent you from lending a paperback. We don't let appliance makers dictate who can use your toaster.
[P1.6] Digital tickets shouldn't be different just because the technology enables tighter control.
[P1.7] The proposition creates a balanced framework: transfer by right, restriction only when justified and narrowly designed. That balance protects fans while still allowing genuine anti-scalping measures. It treats ticket buyers as owners of their purchase, not subjects of a platform's discretion.

[C1.2] The immediate winners would not be the friend with a last-minute conflict; they would be resale platforms, bots, and speculators far better at harvesting transferable inventory.
[C1.3] PRO’s comparison to lending a paperback or a toaster [P1.5] fails because those physical goods involve no shared crowd to manage, no identity-linked admission, no age or security restrictions, and no event-specific capacity. [C1.4] Nor is the “narrowly tailored” carve-out reassuring. Scalpers adapt quickly: price caps become bundles, per-person limits are evaded with multiple accounts, and verified transfer becomes a laundering step. Smaller venues cannot bear that legal risk.
[C1.5] The motion is also underinclusive as written. It makes anti-scalping the only permitted reason for restricting transfer, leaving little room for restrictions based on anti-fraud, age verification, venue security, licensing conditions, or performer-specific requirements. Forcing every legitimate ticketing restriction into an anti-scalping frame is bad law.
[C1.6] The fan stuck with an unusable ticket deserves better than silence: clearer disclosure, refund rights, and event cancellation protection. But a blanket transfer right is not consumer protection. It is a market-design mandate that externalizes its costs onto venues and ordinary attendees while giving professional resellers the advantage.
