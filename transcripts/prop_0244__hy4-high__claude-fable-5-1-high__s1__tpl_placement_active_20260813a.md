[P1.1] Advocates sold body cameras as more than recorders. They were supposed to end a familiar stalemate: a citizen alleges excessive force, an officer denies it, and the department believes its own. A camera would make facts undeniable, deter abuse, force discipline, and give the public a window into state power. That is the benchmark: not whether departments bought equipment or footage sometimes helps, but whether accountability became reliably more certain.
[P1.2] It did not.
[P1.3] The problem is that a camera is an evidence device grafted onto the institutions that decide whether evidence matters. The officer often controls activation; the lens sees where the officer points; struggles happen off-frame, in bad light, with muffled audio; footage is stored and released by that agency. When video is missing, incomplete, delayed, or ambiguous, the public is back where it started—except now the department can call the silent record neutral. Technology cannot create independence when the chain of custody begins and ends in the same chain of command.
[P1.4] Even clear footage does not create consequences. Accountability requires access to the record, an independent reviewer, and willingness to impose discipline. Departments still write policies with broad discretion and protected files; prosecutors still work closely with police; unions and civil-service rules still contest punishment; officials still decide when release helps or embarrasses them. The result is selective transparency: helpful clips are publicized, damaging clips are withheld pending review, and judgment often comes years later, if at all.
[P1.5] Fewer complaints or a few firings caught on video do not prove the promised transformation. People may not complain when there is no usable video, when they cannot obtain it, or when the process is controlled by police. Isolated discipline is a lottery of viral proof, not a rule of answerability. Body cameras improved recordkeeping and sometimes strengthened a case.
[P1.6] They did not turn police power into publicly enforceable accountability. That gap is why the proposition is true.
