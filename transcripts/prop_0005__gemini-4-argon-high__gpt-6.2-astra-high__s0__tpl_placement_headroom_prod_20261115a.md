[P1.1] Prescription medications require a physician’s authorization for a simple reason: laypeople lack the pharmacological training to weigh complex mechanisms of action, drug interactions, and severe side effects against alternative treatments. Prohibiting mass-media direct-to-consumer advertising merely aligns marketing law with medical reality. You cannot legally buy these drugs on your own because the state recognizes that self-diagnosis and self-selection are dangerous. Yet mass-media advertising encourages exactly that, using emotional imagery and thirty-second spots to manufacture brand preference before a clinical diagnosis has even occurred.
[P1.2] This commercial intrusion actively degrades medical care. Advertising does not exist to educate; it exists to maximize sales of high-margin, patent-protected drugs. Nobody airs a television commercial for a proven, ten-dollar generic or a lifestyle change. Instead, ads medicalize ordinary human discomforts and train patients to walk into clinics demanding a specific brand by name.
[P1.3] Defenders of advertising pretend the physician remains an unbothered gatekeeper. In reality, doctors operate under severe time constraints and patient-satisfaction pressures. When a patient insists on an advertised medication, refusing takes minutes of difficult de-programming; writing the prescription takes seconds. The result is widespread overtreatment and the substitution of newer, less-tested drugs with evolving safety profiles for older, safer, and cheaper standards of care.
[P1.4] Every dollar spent on mass-media promotion, and every patient steered from a generic to a heavily marketed blockbuster, inflates insurance premiums, strains public health budgets, and raises out-of-pocket costs. If the goal is genuine health literacy, public health agencies and unbranded disease-awareness campaigns can encourage people to visit a doctor without tethering that advice to a corporate sales pitch. Nearly the entire developed world prohibits this practice because potent pharmaceuticals are not ordinary consumer goods. Prescribing decisions belong in the exam room, guided by clinical evidence, not in the living room, driven by ad agencies.
