[P1.1] A safe-third-country rule does more than coordinate asylum processing. It allows one state to refuse examination of a protection claim and compel the claimant to seek safety elsewhere. That transfer is justified only if the destination offers protection in substance, not merely the formal promise that a person will not immediately be deported.
[P1.2] The three rights in the motion are essential to that substance. Appeal is the safeguard against wrongful rejection, mistaken identity, procedural failure, and return to persecution. It must include meaningful, independent review with enough time and access to assistance to challenge an adverse decision. Without enforceability, “safety” rests on an administrative label that the affected person cannot contest.
[P1.3] The right to work prevents the asylum system itself from creating destitution. Claims may take months or years. If people are legally barred from supporting themselves, they become dependent on inadequate state aid, vulnerable to exploitation, or pressured into “voluntary” return despite real danger. A country is not a safe destination when survival depends on illegal labor or indefinite charity.
[P1.4] Family unity is equally fundamental. Governments should not manufacture separation between spouses, parents, and children as the price of receiving protection. Effective procedures to keep immediate families together or reunite them are not optional comforts; they prevent predictable harm and make settlement, caregiving, and integration possible.
[P1.5] Most importantly, these rights must be enforceable. Statutes without remedies, inaccessible tribunals, or discretionary promises cannot justify forced transfer. The transferring state is deliberately substituting another country’s system for its own, so it bears the burden of ensuring that system offers real recourse.
[P1.6] This standard does not abolish migration management. States may still cooperate, share responsibility, and transfer claims where these minimum conditions exist. But administrative convenience and burden shifting cannot turn mere physical presence into genuine safety. If the proposed destination cannot guarantee work, appeal, and family unity, the originating country must examine the claim itself rather than export the human consequences of its decision.
