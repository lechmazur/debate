[P1.1] Dignity is a mirror; bodily autonomy is the hand that holds it. In personal medical choices, we should trust the hand. Autonomy grounds medical ethics in an irreducible fact: this is your body. It yields a clear rule—no invasion, no coercion, no override.
[P1.2] Dignity, by contrast, is too often a borrowed language used to seize that decision from the person living inside the body.
[P1.3] Consider end-of-life care. A patient with a terminal illness seeks a hastened death to avoid prolonged suffering. The autonomy argument is simple and bounded: it is her life, her pain, her exit. Dignity-based objections, however, fracture into competing definitions.
[P1.4] One observer calls a natural death dignified; another calls refusing a medically assisted exit dignified. Because dignity is culturally and socially constructed, it becomes a vehicle for projection. Doctors, judges, and strangers can invoke dignity to veto the patient’s own calculus, effectively saying, "We know better than you what your life means." That is not respect; it is paternalism wearing a moral costume.
[P1.5] Autonomy has a further virtue: it scales across disagreement. We live in pluralistic societies with incompatible visions of the good life. Bodily autonomy provides a common currency—consent—that does not require us to resolve metaphysical questions about what makes a life dignified. It asks only that the individual’s bodily borders remain sovereign.
[P1.6] Dignity appeals demand consensus on contested values before the patient gets to choose. That priority is backwards.
[P1.7] In reproductive care, too, autonomy protects the pregnant person as a whole being, not merely a gestational vessel. Dignity rhetoric can be weaponized by those who claim childbirth is inherently dignified, or that abortion violates fetal dignity. But once we make bodily autonomy the heavier weight, the calculus is disciplined: interventions require permission from the person whose body is at stake.
[P1.8] Medical ethics works when it limits power. Autonomy limits power. Dignity, unbounded, too often extends it.

[P2.1] The claim that dignity is the foundation, and autonomy therefore derivative and lighter, gets the relationship backwards. Dignity is a reason to respect persons, but autonomy is the only reliable rule that stops respect from turning into control. If dignity outweighed autonomy, the state could declare that some dignified-sounding life is worth more than the patient’s own refusal. That is not protection; it is paternalism with better manners.
[P2.2] The claim that autonomy vanishes when patients cannot speak is also false. For the patient with advanced dementia or the sedated patient, autonomy still governs through advance directives and substituted judgment—ask what the patient would want. Dignity alone supplies no decision procedure. Relatives can invoke dignity to demand every possible intervention, or doctors can invoke it to withdraw care, and the patient has no voice either way.
[P2.3] Autonomy is the tie-breaker that keeps the patient’s perspective at the center even when the patient is silent. A principle that works through proxies is stronger than one that dissolves into whoever claims to know what dignity requires.
[P2.4] Nor does desperation prove that dignity must outrank autonomy. Exploitation is a failure of consent conditions, not of autonomy itself. The debt-ridden kidney seller is wronged because coercion invalidates his choice—an autonomy verdict. If dignity were the heavier weight, a court could simply declare the sale undignified and seize his kidney for his own good.
[P2.5] We do not need dignity to override his body; we need to remove the coercion so his autonomy is real. Autonomy is the tool that diagnoses exploitation; dignity too often becomes the excuse for overriding it.
[P2.6] Finally, the terminal patient is not asking strangers to certify her life as dignified. She is asking that no one veto her exit on the basis of someone else’s definition of a dignified life. Autonomy secures that. Dignity, unbounded, invites the veto.
