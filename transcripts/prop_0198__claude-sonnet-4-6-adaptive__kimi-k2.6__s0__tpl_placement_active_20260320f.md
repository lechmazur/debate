[P1.1] The Constitution assigns Congress the power to lay duties and regulate foreign commerce. That is not a procedural footnote—it is a deliberate choice by the framers to place the most economically consequential trade decisions in the hands of the branch closest to the people paying the price. Tariffs are taxes. Every consumer, farmer, and manufacturer who absorbs higher costs deserves to have that burden authorized by representatives who answer to them at the ballot box.
[P1.2] What happened instead is a slow-motion abdication. Through statutes like the International Emergency Economic Powers Act and sections of the Trade Expansion Act, Congress handed the executive a virtually unchecked ability to impose sweeping tariffs whenever a president declares an "emergency"—a term elastic enough to cover almost any trade dispute a White House wants to win. The word "emergency" was meant for genuine national security crises: asset freezes, sanctions against hostile states. It was not designed to give a single official the power to unilaterally restructure the trade relationships of a twenty-trillion-dollar economy.
[P1.3] The consequences are not hypothetical. Presidents of both parties have used these authorities to impose broad tariffs on steel, aluminum, and consumer goods—sometimes on close allies—triggering retaliatory measures that hurt American exporters and farmers without any meaningful congressional vote. These cycles of escalation and retaliation happen faster than democratic accountability can function.
[P1.4] The CON side will argue that speed and flexibility require executive authority. That argument proves far too much. By the same logic, presidents could set tax rates unilaterally because tax legislation is slow. The answer to congressional sluggishness is congressional reform, not permanent surrender of the legislative power.
[P1.5] The central question here is simple: who should be taxing Americans on imported goods? The answer the Constitution gives, and the answer democratic accountability demands, is the legislature. Reclaiming that authority is not anti-trade—it is pro-governance.
