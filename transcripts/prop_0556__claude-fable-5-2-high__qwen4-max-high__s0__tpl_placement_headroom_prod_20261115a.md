[P1.1] Your phone knows where you sleep, where you worship, which clinic you visited on Tuesday, and whose house you were at until 2 a.m. Right now, companies you have never heard of can sell that trail to nearly anyone who pays. [P1.2] Three claims will decide this debate.
[P1.3] First, precise location is not ordinary data. It cannot be meaningfully anonymized, because the place a device rests every night and the place it sits every weekday identify one person. [P1.4] Second, the current "consent" is a fiction. People tap "allow" so a weather app can show the local forecast. They do not agree to an indefinite resale chain ending with unknown buyers. [P1.5] Third, the harms are real, not speculative. Commercially purchased location data has reportedly been used to out a clergy member, to track visitors to reproductive health clinics, and to trace the movements of military personnel. [P1.6] Notice how modest the remedy is. Nothing here bans location data. Navigation works. Aggregated, coarse data for city planning survives.
[P1.7] Any genuinely valuable use remains available: just ask. If a business model collapses the moment people are honestly asked, that is not an argument against asking. It is the proof that consent was never there.
