None of them collapsed into permanent holdup. They produced impasse procedures: bargain in good faith, and if you reach genuine deadlock, the employer can implement.
[P2.2] That is not a veto. CON needs you to believe "negotiate" secretly means "obtain permission," and it doesn't - not in German law, not in US labor law, not in this proposal.
[P2.3] The vendor-churn point actually cuts against CON. That's a fixed-cost institution, not a recurring veto point, and it's precisely the German pattern CON tries to wave away. CON says Germany "doesn't scale" because it covers a narrower class of tools - but that's an argument for calibrating scope, which any statute can do.
[P2.4] It is not an argument against the mechanism.
[P2.5] The "incumbents versus applicants" point proves too much. Every negotiated workplace protection - safety standards, seniority rules, grievance procedures - is bargained by current employees and still benefits future ones, because councils are elected by and accountable to the whole workforce under the same duty-of-fair-representation principles that already govern unions. If that logic invalidated worker voice, it would invalidate collective bargaining itself, which CON hasn't actually attacked.
[P2.6] Strip away the dilemma and the scaling objection, and what's left of CON's case is speculation about calendars - not a reason to leave AI deployment entirely inside management's discretion.
