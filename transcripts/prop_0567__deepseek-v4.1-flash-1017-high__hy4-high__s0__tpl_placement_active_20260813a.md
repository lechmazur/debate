[P1.1] Open justice is not a ceremonial slogan; it is the mechanism that keeps courts honest. A trial is already a public act, but in practice “public” often means a handful of people who can take time off work, travel downtown, and squeeze into a gallery. Cameras extend that gallery to the community whose laws are being enforced. The proposition does not demand broadcasting every sensitive moment; it demands a presumption in favor of cameras in most proceedings, with narrow, case-specific exceptions.
[P1.2] That is the correct default because the courtroom’s legitimacy depends on people being able to see what is done in their name.
[P1.3] Cameras improve conduct. Judges know their rulings will be reviewed by the public as well as appellate courts. Lawyers know theatrics and misstatements will be preserved. Witnesses know their accounts cannot be quietly reshaped later.
[P1.4] A recording also settles disputes about what was said and done in court—an accurate record is not a threat to justice; it is a foundation of it. When communities can watch a trial rather than absorb secondhand spin, verdicts are easier to understand and harder to demagogue.
[P1.5] The predictable objection is privacy and witness chilling. Those are real concerns, but they are reasons for tailored safeguards—excluding minors, sexual-assault complainants, jurors, confidential informants, and especially vulnerable witnesses; restricting where and how footage is used; giving the judge discretion to order no recording of particular testimony. They are not reasons for a blanket presumption against public scrutiny. A witness who genuinely needs protection can testify with the camera turned off or the courtroom closed; that does not justify sealing every routine trial from the public eye.
[P1.6] So the central choice is this: do we trust the public with a window into the justice system, or do we insist that justice remain visible mainly to the few who happen to be in the room? The burden should be on those who want secrecy in any given case. That is why cameras should be permitted in most trial proceedings.
