[P1.1] Expert panels and internal forecasts fail for a structural reason, not a personnel reason: the people making the predictions face no cost for being wrong and often face career costs for being right when "right" is unpopular. A prediction market fixes the incentive problem directly. Put real money on the line, let anyone with relevant information trade, and you get a price that aggregates dispersed knowledge no single panel can match — because the panel only pools the people in the room, while the market pools everyone willing to bet against a bad forecast.
[P1.2] Consider the corporate world's own revealed preference: firms like Google ran internal prediction markets for years specifically because their expert forecasters and internal planning teams kept missing things that traders caught early. Policy has the same disease. Committees producing GDP forecasts, pandemic projections, or war-risk assessments are vulnerable to groupthink, deference to seniority, and the incentive to avoid being the lone dissenter who's later proven wrong in a memo trail. A market has no seniority.
[P1.3] A junior epidemiologist with better data than the panel chair can simply bet and move the price — no committee vote required.
[P1.4] This doesn't mean markets replace expert judgment. It means they discipline it. When Metaculus and similar public forecasting platforms outperformed many pundit predictions on election outcomes and pandemic trajectories, that wasn't luck — aggregation of incentivized, falsifiable predictions beats a handful of credentialed guesses precisely because it punishes overconfidence in real time and rewards updating on evidence.
[P1.5] The proposition asks for prediction markets as a formal input, not a sole decision-maker. That's a modest, well-hedged claim: governments already use flawed inputs like committee consensus and agency modeling that are opaque and unaccountable. Layering in a market-generated probability — transparent, continuously updated, costly to game at scale — gives decision-makers an additional, independently-verifiable signal that current processes structurally cannot produce. The burden on CON is to explain why we should trust unaccountable expert consensus over a mechanism that has, again and again, out-forecast exactly those experts when tested head-to-head.
