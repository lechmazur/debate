[P1.1] A restaurant meal is a priced service, not a favor settled by conscience. Labor is part of producing that service; it belongs in payroll and in the price the customer sees before ordering. Tipping converts a wage obligation into a private moral test. The tablet, preset percentages, a waiting server, and fear of looking cheap turn an “optional” tip into a hidden charge enforced by guilt.
[P1.2] That is a bad way to fund skilled work.
[P1.3] For workers, that means rent depends on table assignment, weather, bias, and a stranger's mood. Careful service can still earn less on a slow night. Because customers control so much of the paycheck, servers have incentives to tolerate harassment, flirtation, and unreasonable demands instead of enforcing normal boundaries. Cooks, dishwashers, hosts, and runners help create the meal but often stand outside the tip stream.
[P1.4] Tipping does not simply reward good service; it rewards vulnerability, luck, and visibility.
[P1.5] Consumers are misled too. An eighteen-dollar entrée with a socially expected twenty percent addition is not really eighteen dollars; it is a price with a mystery surcharge at the end. Two diners can pay different amounts for identical service because of generosity, confusion, or fatigue. A disclosed service charge and higher base pay do not deny that service costs money.
[P1.6] They put that cost in the open before the customer chooses, where comparison shopping and competition can work.
[P1.7] The objection that prices will rise concedes the point: wages were never free; tipping just hid them in guilt. Some menu prices may rise, some margins may tighten, but a restaurant that needs strangers to complete its payroll is externalizing labor costs. Higher base pay plus a clearly included service charge puts responsibility with the business: quote the real price, pay the team predictably, and make service a profession rather than a negotiation with a stranger's conscience.
