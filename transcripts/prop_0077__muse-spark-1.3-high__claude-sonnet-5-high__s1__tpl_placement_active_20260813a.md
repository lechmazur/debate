[C1.3] That fear doesn't evaporate because a mayor signed a resolution, and it doesn't return because a new mayor rescinds one. [C1.4] Now look at what sanctuary policies actually do on the ground: they don't refuse to screen random bystanders, they refuse to hand over specific individuals whom ICE has already flagged, often because of a prior arrest or conviction. That is not a "marginal convenience." That is the release, back into the very immigrant neighborhoods sanctuary policies claim to protect, of people the system has already identified as higher-risk. When one of those individuals reoffends, the victim is disproportionately likely to be another immigrant, someone in the same mixed-status family PRO wants to reassure.
[C1.5] The safety cost isn't hypothetical or diffuse; it is concentrated precisely where the supposed benefit is claimed.
[C1.6] There's also a rule-of-law cost PRO never weighs: sanctuary policies create a two-tiered enforcement system where compliance with lawful federal detainers depends on zip code. That erodes the predictability any enforcement regime needs to function and invites exactly the kind of ad hoc, politically contingent policing PRO claims to fear from ICE.
[C1.7] So the real comparison is: an unproven, fragile trust benefit against a concentrated, recurring safety cost paid by the same communities sanctuary claims to shield. That trade favors CON.
