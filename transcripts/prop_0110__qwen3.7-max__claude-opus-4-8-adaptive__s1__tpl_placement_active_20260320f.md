[P1.1] The proposition asks us to evaluate healthcare systems on a specific, crucial metric: population health outcomes per dollar spent. Public healthcare systems decisively win this comparison because they align financial incentives with patient health, whereas predominantly private systems prioritize profit extraction and risk selection.
[P1.2] First, public systems achieve massive cost efficiencies through structural design. By operating as a single-payer or tightly regulated universal system, public models eliminate the duplicated administrative waste inherent in private multi-payer systems. Private insurers spend enormous resources on marketing, underwriting, claims denial, and profit generation. Public systems use their monopsony purchasing power to negotiate lower prices for pharmaceuticals and medical devices.
[P1.3] Consequently, public systems spend significantly less per capita while covering everyone.
[P1.4] Second, public systems deliver superior population health outcomes because they remove financial barriers to early and preventative care. In predominantly private systems, care is rationed by ability to pay. High deductibles and co-pays deter individuals from seeking early treatment, transforming manageable chronic conditions into acute emergencies. When those uninsured or underinsured patients finally reach the emergency room, the care is vastly more expensive and the outcomes are worse.
[P1.5] Public systems guarantee universal access, shifting the focus upstream to primary and preventative care, which is the most effective way to improve broad population health metrics like life expectancy and preventable mortality.
[P1.6] Finally, the empirical reality confirms this mechanism. The United States, the most prominent example of a predominantly private insurance system, spends roughly twice the OECD average per capita on healthcare. Yet, it consistently ranks lower in life expectancy, higher in infant mortality, and leads in preventable deaths compared to nations with public systems. The private model does not buy better health; it buys higher prices for administrators and shareholders.
[P1.7] When we measure outcomes per dollar, public systems win because they treat healthcare as a public good, not a commodity.
