[P1.1] An actor’s face and voice are not raw clay a studio buys forever with a single paycheck; they are the performer’s ongoing livelihood, reputation, and identity. When a studio uses AI to de-age a living actor or synthesize their voice to materially alter a performance, it is no longer merely editing footage; it is generating a new performance out of the actor’s biometric data. Doing so requires explicit consent and compensation.
[P1.2] First, explicit consent is essential to protect artistic and reputational autonomy. Notice the threshold: "materially alter." This is not about removing background noise, fixing a minor audio glitch, or routine color grading. Material alteration means fundamentally changing an actor’s physical presentation across time or fabricating vocal deliveries they never gave.
[P1.3] Imagine a studio using a synthetic voice clone in post-production to rewrite a character’s dialogue into something crude or artistically humiliating, or deploying a botched AI de-aging pass that turns a respected actor into an uncanny digital puppet. Audiences cannot tell where the human ends and the algorithm begins. Because the living actor bears the career and reputational risk of whatever their face and voice are made to do on screen, they must have the right to say no.
[P1.4] Second, compensation is basic economic fairness. Historically, changing lines in post-production meant paying the actor to return for Automated Dialogue Replacement sessions. Similarly, de-aging an actor to carry flashbacks or entire storylines extracts massive commercial value by leveraging their current star power alongside their youthful likeness. Using AI to synthesize new dialogue or de-age a performer without extra pay directly replaces paid human labor with unpaid biometric extraction.
[P1.5] Owning a recorded take does not mean owning the human being who performed it. Vague, boilerplate contract clauses cannot justify turning living performers into uncompensated digital puppets. Requiring explicit consent and compensation does not ban creative innovation; it simply ensures studios cannot hijack an actor’s identity and labor without asking and paying for it.
