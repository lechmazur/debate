[P1.1] Brain-signal data should not become a raw material for targeting, steering, or charging people. The issue is not whether a company can place an “I agree” button on a screen; it is whether click-through consent can legitimize a relationship in which one party can commercially exploit reactions the other may not even recognize in themselves.
[P1.2] Standard consent is structurally inadequate. A disclosure can describe collection, but not the future inferences, model updates, data combinations, and commercial experiments that give neural data its value. Even imperfect signals can be useful: a company need not read thoughts to correlate fatigue, attention, or arousal with purchasing behavior. Users cannot realistically anticipate those uses, compare their consequences across products, or negotiate separate terms.
[P1.3] Consent given to obtain a headset, wellness service, or application is therefore not meaningful authorization for an open-ended advertising and pricing laboratory.
[P1.4] The resulting power imbalance threatens both autonomy and fair dealing. A firm could test which presentation captures attention or weakens resistance, personalize subsequent appeals accordingly, and adjust prices using inferred willingness to pay—all while the user sees only an ordinary advertisement or price. Calling this “personalization” does not make it beneficial: personalization can mean adapting exploitation as readily as adapting service.
[P1.5] The user’s own neural responses become leverage against the user.
[P1.6] A prohibition is also clearer and more protective than CON’s likely regime of disclosures and opt-outs. Those approaches preserve the incentive to make refusal costly, bury uses in bundled consent, and reidentify or recombine data later. A purpose-based ban instead prevents the harmful market from forming.
[P1.7] The motion does not ban neurotechnology, medical or safety functions, or advertising based on non-neural information. Companies can still sell useful devices and services; they simply cannot monetize brain signals through manipulation and discriminatory pricing. [P2.5] Most misleading is the claim that the motion denies choice to adults “no matter how well informed.” That replaces standard click-through consent—the actual issue—with an idealized consent process CON has not defended.
