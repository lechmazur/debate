[P1.1] The cloud has a physical footprint, and taxpayers are currently subsidizing it. Hyperscale data centers consume electricity on the scale of small cities and draw millions of gallons of water daily for cooling. When governments offer uncapped tax incentives to attract them, they are not just reducing corporate tax bills. They are masking a massive transfer of infrastructure costs onto local ratepayers.
[P1.2] Grid upgrades, peak-load strain, and aquifer depletion do not vanish because a developer gets a tax holiday. They simply appear as higher utility rates and strained municipal budgets.
[P1.3] Capping incentives unless grid and water costs are fully internalized fixes this market failure. Internalization means operators cover the actual marginal cost of their consumption, including necessary infrastructure expansion. Without it, uncapped breaks act as a double subsidy: one through the treasury, another through the public utility system. A cap forces rational siting and innovation.
[P1.4] Developers will adopt closed-loop cooling, invest in waterless technologies, or build where local systems can actually sustain the load. Uncapped incentives distort location decisions, pushing development into water-stressed or grid-constrained regions simply because the tax math works. The cap restores price signals, ensuring expansion follows physical capacity rather than accounting loopholes. This does not kill the industry.
[P1.5] It stops rewarding inefficiency and shifts financial risk back to the private actors capturing the profits.
[P1.6] The alternative is fiscal negligence. Municipalities are trapped in a bidding war, trading long-term revenue for highly automated facilities that create few permanent jobs while locking in decades of infrastructure liability. When droughts hit or heatwaves strain the grid, residents face restrictions while subsidized data centers draw resources at bargain rates. This motion does not block technological growth.
[P1.7] It draws a necessary line: public funds will no longer underwrite private resource extraction. If the digital economy wants to expand, it must pay for the pipes and wires that keep it running.

[C1.1] Capping tax incentives for hyperscale data centers is a blunt instrument that would cripple the very economic engine the proposition claims to protect. Tax incentives are not subsidies for water or electricity; they are foregone revenue that spurs private capital to build infrastructure that generates jobs, expands the tax base, and accelerates digital connectivity. When governments slash these incentives, they do not force operators to “pay for the pipes and wires”; they simply push investment elsewhere—often to jurisdictions with weaker environmental oversight—leaving the original community poorer and the externalities unchanged.
[C1.2] The proposition assumes that only a cap can internalize grid and water costs. In reality, utilities already price marginal consumption, and targeted surcharges or water‑use fees can capture those externalities without strangling investment. A cap treats all data centers alike, ignoring that many operators already adopt closed‑loop cooling, on‑site renewables, and water‑recycling systems precisely because market signals—not tax breaks—drive efficiency. By imposing an arbitrary ceiling, we punish the innovators and reward the laggards, distorting location decisions away from regions that can sustainably host load and toward those that merely offer the cheapest tax deal.
[C1.3] Consider the rapid growth of cloud hubs in places like Northern Virginia and the Dallas‑Fort Worth metroplex. Tax incentives attracted billions in private spending, created high‑skill jobs, and generated subsequent tax revenues that far exceeded the foregone incentives. Those same regions have since invested heavily in renewable energy and water‑reuse projects, not because a cap forced them, but because competitive markets and smart utility pricing made it profitable. Removing incentives would have delayed that growth, reduced local employment, and left municipalities with a smaller fiscal cushion to manage any real grid strain.
[C1.4] The real market failure is not the existence of tax incentives but the absence of transparent, cost‑reflective pricing for water and electricity. Addressing that failure with precise fees, not with a sweeping cap, preserves growth, protects ratepayers, and still holds operators accountable for their resource use. The proposition’s cap is a politically convenient shortcut that sacrifices economic vitality for an illusory fix.
