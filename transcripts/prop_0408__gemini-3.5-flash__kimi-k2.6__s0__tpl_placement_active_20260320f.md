[P1.1] Entering adulthood without basic financial literacy is like being handed the keys to a car without ever taking a driving lesson. Yet, our current educational system routinely allows young people to graduate into a complex, high-stakes financial landscape—facing student loans, credit cards, taxes, and predatory lending—with zero formal preparation. Leaving financial education to elective exposure or family background is a recipe for systemic inequality and preventable financial ruin. A serious, mandatory financial-literacy course for graduation is not a luxury; it is an essential public utility.
[P1.2] First, relying on family background entrenches economic inequality. Parents cannot teach what they were never taught themselves. When schools default to "family background" as the primary source of financial education, they ensure that generational poverty and financial insecurity are passed down alongside wealth. A mandatory curriculum levels the playing field, ensuring every student, regardless of their zip code or parental income, understands the compounding nature of debt and the basics of budgeting.
[P1.3] Second, electives fail because of self-selection bias. The students who opt into financial literacy electives are often those who already have some domestic exposure to these concepts or have plans for business school. The students most vulnerable to predatory financial products or cycles of debt are the least likely to choose these courses voluntarily. Only a graduation requirement guarantees that the safety net of knowledge reaches everyone.
[P1.4] Finally, the timing is critical. At age eighteen, young people are legally permitted to sign contracts for life-altering sums of student debt before they even understand how interest accrues or how a credit score works. We do not let people drive without a test because the risks to themselves and others are too high. We must apply the same duty of care to the financial highway.
[P1.5] Requiring financial literacy for graduation is the single most effective way to protect our youth and build a resilient economy.

[C1.3] A mandated, uniform curriculum cannot possibly be "serious" for all of them; it inevitably flattens complexity into a forgettable checkbox exercise that students treat like compliance theater.

[C1.6] Finally, a hard graduation requirement punishes the vulnerable students it claims to rescue. Real equity comes from flexibility and integration, not from another high-stakes hurdle standing between teenagers and their futures.
