[P1.1] Curb space is public operating space, not a warehouse for private cars. This is not about abolishing every parking space; it is about whether cities should keep protecting most of the curb for parked cars or devote more of it to moving people and goods. By throughput, safety, and access, the default is backwards.
[P1.2] One parking bay stores one car for hours. The same strip as a bus lane carries a steady line of riders through congestion; as a loading zone, it lets shops receive goods without vans blocking a travel lane; as a protected bike lane, it creates a safe, compact route that needs no car-sized storage at the destination. Protecting parking is therefore not preserving access. It is reserving scarce public land for its least productive use.
[P1.3] The costs ripple outward. Buses forced into general traffic while curb lanes hold parked cars become unreliable, punishing riders with the least flexibility. Without legal loading space, delivery drivers double-park, slowing traffic and forcing cyclists into dangerous swerves. Without protected bike space, riders mix with opening doors and fast cars.
[P1.4] Cheap curb parking also invites circling, adding congestion and pollution on commercial streets it is supposed to help. Reliable buses, legal deliveries, and safe cycling make a block easier to reach, shop on, and move through.
[P1.5] The familiar objection is the driver who loses a convenient space. But a stored car’s convenience is not a neighborhood’s access. Customers, workers, students, seniors, and people with disabilities arrive by many modes; a curb built around one mode fails the rest. Accessible spaces and short-stay bays can remain.
[P1.6] What should not remain is the assumption that most public curb belongs to private vehicles by default.
[P1.7] Allocate the curb by public value. Buses move many people, deliveries keep commerce working, bike lanes save space and reduce danger. Most on-street parking protects cheap storage. Cities should stop treating that as the highest use of the street.

[C1.1] PRO calls a parking bay the curb’s “least productive use.” That is true only if productivity means motion at a single instant. It ignores what the stored vehicle is often doing: completing a trip. A nurse arriving after shift, a plumber with tools, a family with a car seat, and an older resident who cannot count on a transfer all need a place to stop. Remove most on-street parking and those trips do not vanish.
[C1.2] They circle, park farther out, spill onto residential streets, or go to destinations that still offer easy access. The motion would not delete car demand; it would export it to the blocks least able to absorb it.
[C1.3] The promised gains are not automatic. A bus lane pays off only if service is frequent and the lane remains continuous through intersections and loading zones. A bike lane works only when it is connected and maintained, not when one protected block ends beside a chaotic delivery curb. A loading zone helps only if it is sized, timed, and enforced, rather than carved from parking and contested by vans.
[C1.4] PRO’s bay-versus-bus comparison assumes the network already exists and treats local conditions as irrelevant.
[C1.5] Neighborhood commerce is also not a downtown throughput problem. On an ordinary shopping street, a short-stay space can be the difference between a customer entering a shop and driving past to a strip mall. In residential areas, evening parking is not a luxury; it is how people with tools, instruments, childcare, or mobility limits get home.
[C1.6] The motion is not asking for priced short-stay parking, resident permits, shared loading, or selective transit lanes, all defensible measures. It asks cities to reserve more curb for buses, deliveries, and bikes rather than protect most parking for private cars. That is a blanket reversal, not a management plan. Curb space should be judged by whole-trip access, local demand, and reliable alternatives—not by an ideological preference against stored cars.
