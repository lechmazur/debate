[P1.1] In almost every major market, buyers and sellers see clear prices before committing. Yet in the labor market, where people invest years of their lives, large employers hold exact data on pay and career progression while forcing workers to negotiate blind. Requiring large firms to disclose both pay bands and promotion rates by role corrects this rigged asymmetry.
[P1.2] Pairing both metrics is vital. Pay bands alone show what a job pays today, not whether it is a launchpad or a dead end. Companies routinely lure applicants with vague promises of rapid growth into bottlenecked roles where almost nobody advances. Picture an analyst or retail lead comparing two corporate offers with identical starting ranges.
[P1.3] Knowing that one firm steadily promotes from within while the other leaves staff stranded turns a blind gamble into a real market choice. Employers that genuinely develop people win talent; firms peddling hollow career paths lose their cover.
[P1.4] Transparency also dismantles hidden bias and the loyalty penalty. Secrecy rarely protects merit; it protects inertia and uneven leverage. Behind closed doors, dependable workers who quietly deliver results routinely earn less than external hires or louder negotiators doing the exact same job, while demographic gaps compound out of sight. Because large employers already maintain structured HR data and broad role categories, publishing these figures costs little while compelling firms to anchor pay to actual work.
[P1.5] Even where disclosure reduces managerial flexibility, the trade is overwhelmingly worth making. Bands are ranges, not straitjackets: managers can still place top performers near the ceiling or promote stars early. What sunlight actually eliminates is unaccountable discretion, like the quiet side deal a boss cuts to retain one squeaky wheel, knowing team morale would crater if peers found out. If a pay or promotion decision cannot survive daylight, managers should not have the flexibility to make it.
