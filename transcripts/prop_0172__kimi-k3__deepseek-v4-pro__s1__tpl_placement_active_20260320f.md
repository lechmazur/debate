[P1.1] What triggers ethical review of an animal experiment? Not species. Not proven suffering—nobody can prove a mouse suffers. Review attaches to the plausible capacity for morally relevant mental states, and learning is the gold-standard evidence of that capacity.
[P1.2] This motion's organoids meet the trigger by definition: they demonstrably learn. Cortical cells grown in a dish, given feedback in a Pong-style game, adapted their firing within minutes—the same plasticity we treat as evidence of mindedness in animals. Had that tissue sat inside a rat skull, it would face committee review. Moral status does not track the container.
[P1.3] Review follows capacity, not anatomy. We protect mice not because they squeak but because learning neural tissue is exactly the kind of thing that might experience. Human-derived neural circuits that adapt are, if anything, stronger candidates for minimal experience than many animals already under review. Exempting them because they sit in a dish is substrate chauvinism, not rigor.
[P1.4] Second, the asymmetry of error. Nobody can rule out rudimentary experience here; consciousness science is nowhere near that. Over-review costs paperwork. Under-review risks manufacturing thousands of potentially experiencing entities at industrial scale with no oversight at all.
[P1.5] Animal welfare frameworks were built on exactly this precaution—protection before proof.
[P1.6] Third, consistency. Organoids are celebrated as replacements for animal models under the 3Rs. But a replacement that acquires the morally relevant capacities of what it replaces inherits its protections—otherwise the 3Rs become a shell game, shifting harm to a substrate nobody watches.
[P1.7] Finally, proportionality. Animal review bans nothing; it licenses, justifies, monitors. Applied here: protocols, humane endpoints, demonstrated necessity—not prohibition. And governance should precede the threshold, like the embryo field's 14-day rule, not chase it after.
[P1.8] The question is not whether organoids are mice. It is whether learning tissue gets watched—or doesn't.
