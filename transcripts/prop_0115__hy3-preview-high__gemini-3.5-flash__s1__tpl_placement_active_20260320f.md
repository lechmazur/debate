[P1.1] BNPL products are functionally indistinguishable from short-term consumer credit, yet they evade nearly every regulation that applies to credit cards. When a shopper splits a $200 purchase into four interest-free installments, they are receiving a credit extension identical to charging that $200 to a Visa or Mastercard. Credit card issuers are required to run hard affordability checks, disclose total borrowing costs in standardized terms, cap penalty fees, and report all balances to credit bureaus. BNPL providers are exempt from nearly all these rules.
[P1.2] They can deny a loan with no explanation, resell debt to collectors without notifying borrowers, and avoid requirements to verify a user's ability to repay before extending credit.
[P1.3] This regulatory gap fuels hidden debt spirals. Consumers can stack BNPL loans across a dozen providers, with no single lender or credit bureau aware of their total outstanding obligations. A shopper might carry $2,000 in unpaid BNPL splits across six apps, while their credit report shows zero debt, letting them take on even more risky borrowing. Credit card regulations prevent this: every balance is reported, every new application triggers a check that accounts for existing debt, and penalty fees are capped to avoid compounding harm.
[P1.4] Credit cards also require clear opt-in for automatic payments, and mandatory 21-day grace periods before late fees apply—protections BNPL users almost never receive.
[P1.5] Opponents will claim BNPL is only a checkout feature, not credit, because most plans charge no interest. This is a false distinction. BNPL late fees often far exceed the penalty caps that apply to credit cards, and the lack of standardized disclosures means shoppers rarely recognize they are taking on debt until they miss a payment. Treating BNPL as a harmless checkout add-on instead of regulated credit lets providers profit from consumer confusion.
[P1.6] Aligning BNPL rules with credit card standards would close this loophole, protect vulnerable borrowers, and preserve the core convenience of split payments.

[C1.1] Imposing credit card regulations on Buy-Now-Pay-Later (BNPL) is a regulatory category error that would destroy a highly effective, low-risk consumer budgeting tool. Traditional credit regulations were explicitly designed to govern revolving, high-interest, long-term debt. Applying these heavy-handed rules to small-dollar, short-term, interest-free transactions would eliminate the exact features that make BNPL a consumer-friendly alternative to traditional credit.
[C1.2] First, BNPL is structurally different from revolving credit. Unlike credit cards, which profit from compounding interest and keeping users in perpetual debt, BNPL is transactional and self-limiting. If a consumer misses a $50 installment, the account is immediately frozen; they cannot accumulate more debt. There is no revolving balance, no compound interest, and no lifetime debt trap.
[C1.3] Forcing BNPL providers to implement credit card-style underwriting—which requires complex "ability-to-repay" calculations designed for multi-thousand-dollar credit lines—would make small-value, zero-interest transactions economically impossible to offer.
[C1.4] Second, forcing BNPL to use credit card-style hard credit checks would actively harm the very consumers it helps. Millions of "thin-file" or underbanked consumers lack the pristine credit history required to qualify for traditional credit cards. BNPL offers them a safe pathway to purchase essential goods without facing high interest rates. Requiring hard credit pulls for every split payment would damage these consumers' credit scores and lock them out of the financial system, driving them back to high-interest payday lenders or predatory subprime credit cards.
[C1.5] The "debt spiral" narrative is highly exaggerated. The average BNPL purchase is under $150 and paid off in weeks. Regulating BNPL like a credit card does not protect consumers; it protects the legacy credit card duopoly from healthy competition. We must regulate BNPL based on its actual risks, rather than forcing it into an ill-fitting framework designed for an entirely different and more predatory financial product.
