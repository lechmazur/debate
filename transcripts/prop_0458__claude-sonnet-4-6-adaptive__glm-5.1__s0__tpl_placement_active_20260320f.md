[P1.1] The climate math on liquefied natural gas exports is damning, and it runs in two directions at once.
[P1.2] First, methane. LNG is not simply "cleaner than coal" once you account for the full supply chain. [P1.3] Research examining upstream leakage rates consistently finds that when those losses exceed roughly 3 percent, the climate benefit of gas over coal disappears entirely. That is not a clean energy success story; it is a methane pipeline pointed at the atmosphere.
[P1.4] Second, domestic lock-in. Export demand does not just ship gas overseas—it sustains and expands the entire upstream infrastructure that makes domestic decarbonization harder. Every new terminal approved is a multi-decade commitment to fossil fuel infrastructure financed partly by the expectation of foreign buyers.
[P1.5] Restricting exports removes that financial floor beneath fossil gas, letting renewable alternatives compete on their actual merits rather than against a system propped up by export revenues.
[P1.6] The counterargument will be that American LNG displaces coal in importing nations. That argument collapses under scrutiny: it relies on optimistic leakage assumptions, ignores that export demand can crowd out renewable investment in recipient countries by locking them into regasification infrastructure, and asks Americans to subsidize other countries' emissions management rather than leading the transition at home.
[P1.7] Restricting LNG exports is not isolationism—it is recognizing that the fastest path to lower global methane is for the world's largest exporter to stop expanding the supply. That is the question this debate turns on.
