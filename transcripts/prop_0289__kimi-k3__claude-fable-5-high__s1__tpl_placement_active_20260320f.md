[P1.1] A robotaxi does not operate in a market vacuum. It runs on publicly built roads, under publicly granted permits, picking up passengers at publicly maintained curbs. When a company receives that license, it receives something of enormous value — and the public is entitled to attach conditions. The oldest condition in for-hire transport is simple: you serve the public, not just the profitable slice of it.
[P1.2] Left alone, fleets will concentrate in high-margin districts — not from malice, but from math. Dispatch algorithms chase utilization: dense, wealthy, high-turnover areas mean short deadhead trips and constant fares. Low-income and low-demand neighborhoods mean empty miles and idle cars. The predictable result is algorithmic redlining: long waits, cancelled rides, or quiet geofencing that leaves entire districts off the service map.
[P1.3] Traditional cab drivers did this informally, which is exactly why cities passed anti-refusal rules. Robotaxis automate the same bias at scale, invisibly, with no driver to complain to.
[P1.4] The people stranded are those with the fewest alternatives: households without cars, night-shift workers commuting after transit stops, elderly and disabled riders. A technology that could close those gaps instead deepens them — while competing for affluent riders who already have options.
[P1.5] The remedy is neither novel nor heavy. Universal service obligations are how we have always run essential networked services: electric and telephone utilities must connect unprofitable customers; paratransit must serve riders regular transit cannot. Nobody calls it radical when the power company wires a farmhouse at the end of a dirt road. Cross-subsidy from dense routes to sparse ones is simply the normal economics of a licensed network.
[P1.6] And note the motion's modesty: "reasonably similar reliability," not identical fleets, not charity. A floor on wait times and coverage, enforced as a permit condition.
[P1.7] If robotaxi economics only work by writing off the neighborhoods that need them most, then it is the license — not the neighborhood — that should be reconsidered.
