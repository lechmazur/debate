[P1.1] The issue is not whether a spreadsheet names employees. It is whether an employer may turn hereditary risk into a management input. Anonymization solves a data-security problem, not a power problem. Population-level genetic health data still describes a cohort, office, age band, or ancestry-linked community.
[P1.2] No names are needed to act on that group. The employer reshapes the rules under which that group gets care. That use should be barred outright.
[P1.3] Workplace wellness and health-insurance design are not decoration: they set premiums, cost-sharing, incentives, screenings, networks, and coverage. If a vendor says one cohort has elevated inherited risk for an expensive condition, the employer can, without row-level names, steer that cohort into monitoring, make relevant care less attractive, or justify higher plan-wide cost-sharing. Workers meet the result as a price, hurdle, or label attached to the body. "Aggregate" describes an input; it does not sanitize an output.
[P1.4] Genetic data is unlike step counts. It is inherited, partly immutable, probabilistic, and meaningful for relatives and communities; it invites managing people before illness and treating tendency as destiny. Consent cannot cure this at work: the employer controls wages, benefits, and culture, so "optional" programs can become the safe choice. Workers then avoid counseling, family-history disclosure, or challenges to benefit design.
[P1.5] The "no one is identified" defense defines harm too narrowly. The harm is collective classification by an institution that is both a health promoter and a cost-cutter, able to dress cost-cutting as care. Case-by-case rules cannot work: no worker can tell whether a diabetes module came from claims, employee requests, or a genetic report; vendors can relabel analysis as "wellness priorities"; audits reopen the sensitive inquiry. A categorical ban is the only administrable line.
[P1.6] Employers can offer voluntary programs using non-genetic information and employee-chosen clinical advice. What they must not do is become genetic actuaries over their workforce. The strict prohibition protects equality, trust, and the boundary between assistance and employer control.

[C1.1] The motion bans the wrong thing. It does not prohibit discrimination. It prohibits knowledge. "Anonymized population-level genetic health data" is simply medical epidemiology: what fraction of people carry a risk variant, which hereditary conditions are underdiagnosed, which screenings catch them early.
[C1.2] A strict prohibition means an employer designing a health plan must pretend that science does not exist.
[C1.3] Picture what that forbids. A company with a large workforce learns, from published population data, that familial hypercholesterolemia is common, inherited, and usually missed until a first heart attack. It decides to fully cover lipid testing for employees' relatives and drop the copay on genetic counseling. Under PRO's rule, that is illegal, because a genetic population insight shaped the design.
[C1.4] The same goes for covering BRCA counseling for communities with higher carrier rates, or building sickle cell support into benefits for a workforce where the trait is common. The ban hits exactly the workers PRO claims to protect: groups with elevated inherited risk lose the tailored coverage that serves them best.
[C1.5] PRO's harms are real, but they are harms of output, not input. Charging a cohort more, making relevant care "less attractive," steering people by ancestry [P1.3]: these should be prohibited directly, and much of that is already restricted by genetic nondiscrimination law and by rules limiting health-status-based premium differences within group plans. Ban the harmful outcome and you catch it whether it came from genetic data, claims data, or prejudice. Ban only the genetic input and the identical harm walks through the door labeled "claims analysis."
[C1.6] That is why PRO's administrability argument [P1.5] collapses on its own terms. PRO admits no one can tell whether a program came from a genetic report or from claims. An input ban is therefore unenforceable against bad actors and binding only on good-faith employers who document their reasoning. An outcome rule is checkable: look at the premiums, cost-sharing, and coverage.
[C1.7] So the clash is simple. PRO blocks preventive design to stop discrimination it cannot detect. We stop the discrimination directly and keep the prevention.
