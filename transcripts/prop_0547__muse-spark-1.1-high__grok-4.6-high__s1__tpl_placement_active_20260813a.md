[P1.1] An engagement-optimized feed is not a neutral mirror of what teens want; it is a machine for keeping them watching. Compared to a chronological feed that simply shows what friends posted in order, it learns what holds attention and then feeds more of it without end.
[P1.2] That design is uniquely harmful to adolescents. Teen brains are hypersensitive to social feedback and appearance comparison, while impulse control is still developing. The algorithm discovers this quickly: content that triggers self-evaluation — flawless bodies, luxury lifestyles, dramatic social rejection, fear-based news — holds gaze longer than ordinary life updates. So one video about feeling left out becomes ten, then fifty.
[P1.3] A chronological feed might show one friend's makeup tutorial; the optimized feed builds a tunnel of idealized beauty you cannot easily exit.
[P1.4] Second, optimization creates compulsive use that directly fuels anxiety and depression. Short-form video is built on variable rewards, endless scroll, and autoplay. Teens report intentions to watch for five minutes and losing an hour. That loss of agency is itself anxiety-inducing, plus it displaces sleep, homework, and in-person interaction — the strongest buffers against depression.
[P1.5] It also creates a nightly rumination loop: lying in bed, algorithm serving increasingly emotionally charged content when self-regulation is lowest.
[P1.6] Finally, chronological feeds break both mechanisms. They have natural stopping cues — you reach the end of what was posted. Personalization is limited; you are not trapped in a sensitivity-matched vortex. Early platform tests and user reports of switching to following-only feeds consistently show less time spent and relief from feeling "stuck."
[P1.7] The content pool is the same, but the dose and delivery change.
[P1.8] Same kid, same interests, different feed: one shows life as it happens; the other learns how to make her anxious enough to keep watching.
