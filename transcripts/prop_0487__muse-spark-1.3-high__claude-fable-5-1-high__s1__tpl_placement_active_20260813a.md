[P1.1] An asteroid impact is the only preventable mass disaster. We cannot stop earthquakes or hurricanes, but a threatening rock can be nudged aside if we reach it early. That option disappears if we wait until after detection to start building.
[P1.2] In 2013 a relatively small object exploded over Chelyabinsk, injuring over a thousand people and shattering windows across a modern city with no warning. In 1908 Tunguska flattened a vast stretch of Siberian forest. Neither ended civilization, yet both prove the threat is real. Larger city-killers remain unmapped, dark, and silent, and physics grants no extensions.
[P1.3] Deflection is a timing game. A kinetic strike has already been shown in space to shift an asteroid's path, but such missions take years to build, launch, cruise, and then more time for the nudge to accumulate. Papers and prototypes cannot meet that clock. Only permanent infrastructure can: survey telescopes to complete the catalog, pre-built impactors in storage, reserved launch capacity, and practiced teams able to fly in months rather than a decade.
[P1.4] The cost objection collapses on comparison. States spend vastly more on weapons, highways, and disaster recovery than a ready defense would require. A few telescopes, stored spacecraft, and rehearsal flights are budget dust next to armies. Against a vaporized port, a dead downtown, or ruined harvests, the return is enormous.
[P1.5] Insurance is not waste because the house has not yet burned.
[P1.6] Only governments can provide permanence. No firm profits from deflecting a rock that might strike in forty years, and no lab can keep rockets ready and crews authorized to launch. Leaving survival to grants guarantees fragmentation and delay. Deploying operational hardware turns knowledge into protection and fulfills government's most basic duty: shield its people from foreseeable catastrophe.
