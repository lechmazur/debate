[P1.1] When a company both controls the marketplace and sells its own products inside it, the incentive to tilt the playing field is not a theoretical risk—it is a structural certainty. Apple’s App Store and Google Play are the clearest illustrations. Apple’s own services—Apple Music, iCloud, Apple TV+—consistently receive premium placement, prominent recommendation slots, and reduced commission rates that rival developers cannot obtain. When Spotify complained that Apple’s 30 % cut was a tax on competition, Apple responded by promoting its own streaming service and, in some cases, restricting Spotify’s ability to inform users about cheaper alternatives.
[P1.2] Google similarly pre‑installs its own suite of apps on Android devices and gives them priority in search results within the Play Store, crowding out independent developers who must pay the same fees for a less favorable position.
[P1.3] These patterns are not anomalies; they are the predictable outcome of a single firm owning both the gate and the goods. Self‑regulation has repeatedly failed: the EU’s “fair‑play” guidelines and Apple’s own “App Store Review” policies have been shown to be applied unevenly, because the regulator and the regulated are the same entity. The only remedy that aligns incentives is structural separation—splitting the platform operation from the first‑party development business, just as we separate utility generation from transmission to prevent discrimination.
[P1.4] Separation does not diminish the platform’s value. It preserves the curation, security, and distribution efficiencies that users expect while eliminating the conflict that lets the owner silently boost its own products at the expense of innovation and consumer choice. Without it, the market remains a closed garden where the gardener also sells the fruit, and the garden’s health is sacrificed for the gardener’s profit. Structural separation is the clearest, most enforceable way to guarantee a level playing field.
