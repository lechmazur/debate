[P1.1] A minimum wage is one number doing one job: it lifts the bottom of the pay scale and nothing else. In fragmented service industries—cleaning, care work, fast food, security—the problems workers actually face are not just an hourly figure. They are unpredictable schedules, no sick pay, no path from entry-level to skilled, safety corners cut by the lowest bidder. A wage floor cannot reach any of that.
[P1.2] Sectoral bargaining can, because it sets multidimensional standards across an entire sector at once.
[P1.3] That is my central claim, and here is the mechanism. These industries are fragmented precisely because work is outsourced, franchised, and subcontracted to whoever bids cheapest. When standards are set firm-by-firm, the responsible employer who pays decently gets undercut by the one who doesn't. A minimum-wage hike doesn't fix that race; it just resets the floor everyone competes below.
[P1.4] Sectoral bargaining binds every firm in the sector to the same negotiated terms, so competition shifts from squeezing workers to delivering service. It takes labor costs out of the cutthroat bidding war.
[P1.5] Second, consider how each tool adapts. Minimum-wage hikes are episodic and political—they happen when legislatures find the votes, often years apart, lagging inflation and ignoring the specific economics of, say, home care versus hospitality. A standing sectoral body, with workers and employers at the table, adjusts continuously and tailors standards to what a sector can bear. One blunt national number cannot be right for every industry at once; a sector-specific table can be.
[P1.6] Third, legitimacy. A wage hike is something done to employers by statute. A negotiated standard is something employers help write—which means better compliance and buy-in, not just enforcement after the fact.
[P1.7] So the question is not "should workers be paid more"—both routes raise pay. It is which route reaches the full set of problems and holds across a fragmented market. Only sectoral bargaining does both.
