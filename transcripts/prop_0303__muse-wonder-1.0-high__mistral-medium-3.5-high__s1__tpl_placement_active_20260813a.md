[P1.1] A child can work hard, be talented, and still lose to a less talented child who was bought a better version of merit. That is the core deception of meritocracy: it does not eliminate class privilege, it launders it into credentials that look earned.
[P1.2] Start before any fair contest begins. Affluent families buy stable housing in strong school districts, tutoring, exam preparation, music, travel, and the time to build an extracurricular record. Poorer children balance unstable housing, under-resourced schools, part-time work, and caring duties. When both sit the same exam or submit the same application, the test measures ability plus years of purchased preparation.
[P1.3] Calling the result pure merit mistakes the receipt for the race.
[P1.4] Elite universities certify that advantage. Legacy preferences, donor networks, private-school pipelines, polished personal statements, and familiarity with unwritten admissions rules all reward insider knowledge. The degree feels like proof of individual brilliance, but access to the institution that grants the proof was class-filtered.
[P1.5] The same conversion happens at work. Prestigious firms recruit from a narrow set of universities, prize unpaid internships that only the subsidised can afford, and judge 'fit' by accent, confidence, hobbies, and social ease learned in privileged settings. Referrals travel through family and alumni networks. A working-class candidate may be qualified, but she is less likely to know someone inside, to survive months without pay in an expensive city, or to sound like the interviewer.
[P1.6] None of this denies effort. The successful did study, compete, and sacrifice. That is precisely why the advantage is under-admitted: because the effort was real, the head start becomes invisible. It is easier to say 'I earned this' than 'I earned this from a starting line far ahead, on a track my parents helped pave.'
[P1.7] A system that reliably turns parental wealth into children's credentials, contacts, and confidence is not primarily sorting by merit. It is reproducing privilege — efficiently, politely, and in merit's name.

[C1.1] Modern meritocracy is not overstated—it is the dominant force breaking the chains of class determinism. The PRO paints a static picture of entrenched advantage, but ignores the broader trend: social mobility has increased dramatically over the past century. Today, more individuals from modest backgrounds attend elite universities, earn professional degrees, and reach leadership positions than ever before. Standardized assessments, for all their flaws, consistently correlate with future performance, demonstrating that credentials reflect genuine ability.
[C1.2] Furthermore, the specific mechanisms PRO highlights—legacy preferences, unpaid internships—are actively being dismantled. Top universities now meet full demonstrated financial need, and first-generation student representation has grown significantly.
[C1.3] The core flaw in PRO’s argument is its deterministic view: if class privilege were the primary driver, every wealthy child would succeed and every poor child would fail. Reality disproves this—many from privileged backgrounds underperform, while many from disadvantaged ones excel, proving merit remains the decisive factor. Preparation helps, but it is itself a form of merit that can be developed. Dismissing the entire system as "laundering" denies individual agency and the clear, measurable progress toward fairness.
[C1.4] The alternative—a return to explicit nepotism—would be far worse. Meritocracy’s imperfections are not evidence of failure, but of a system still improving. Rather than overstated, its successes are underappreciated.

[P2.1] More people from modest backgrounds at elite universities is not proof that class has loosened its grip. Universities and populations got bigger, so absolute numbers can rise while relative odds stay lopsided. The decisive question is: in the same cohort, who is vastly more likely to arrive, apply, and be admitted? When affluent children dominate elite intakes out of proportion to their share of the population, that is access at the margins, not reproduction overturned.
[P2.2] Full-need aid has the same flaw. Paying the bill for disadvantaged students who survive eighteen years of unequal schooling, learn the admissions game, and clear the bar does not equalize the years before the offer. It does not buy back the quiet home, tutored childhood, unpaid time for a dazzling application, or parent who knew which deadlines mattered.
[P2.3] Nor does test prediction rescue pure merit. A test can predict who will thrive in the next selective institution while still measuring who was trained longest for tests like it. Prediction is not innocence. If privileged preparation raises both the entrance score and the later polished performance, correlation shows advantage travelling forward under a new label.
[P2.4] That privilege must be destiny to count — “every wealthy child would succeed and every poor child would fail” — is a straw standard. Loaded dice do not roll sixes every time, yet they are still loaded. Wealthy children can squander cushions; poor children can beat terrible odds. Those exceptions sustain belief: visible escapees prove the door exists, while traffic through it remains class-patterned.
[P2.5] Finally, beating open nepotism sets the bar in the cellar. A system can be fairer than inherited titles and still be overstated, still convert parental money into credentials, networks, and confidence more reliably than winners admit. Better than the worst past is not the same as merit today.

[P4.3] Those two answers expose CON's central move throughout: treat any mobility as parity, and any predictive value as purity. But the proposition is that meritocracy is overstated — not that merit is fictional. If a poor applicant needs exceptional talent plus resilience to reach a place a rich applicant reaches with good talent plus coaching, contacts, and confidence, merit is operating, but class is setting the price of entry. That unequal price, repeated at school, university, internship, and hiring, is reproduction.
[P4.4] CON has still not explained the persistent pattern: why the wealthiest remain vastly overrepresented at the institutions that certify success. Exceptions and reforms show the system is permeable; they do not show it is primarily merit-sorted. Winners experienced real effort, so they naturally overweight that effort and underweight the purchased conditions that made it count. The filter rewards merit — after privilege has decided who gets to develop, display, and convert it.
