[P1.1] Conservation divides a shrinking pie. Desalination bakes a new one. That is the whole debate.
[P1.2] Conservation and rationing manage demand; they cannot create a single litre. A genuinely water-scarce region — falling aquifers, over-allocated rivers, unreliable rain — can cut waste only until it hits the floor of what people, farms, and industry actually need. Below that floor, "conservation" is just a polite word for deprivation. And every efficiency gain is spent once: fix the leaks, and the next drought finds you with no slack left to cut.
[P1.3] A strategy built mainly on rationing gets more brittle precisely as it succeeds.
[P1.4] Desalination is the only major supply that does not depend on rain. The ocean does not have dry years. The places that escaped scarcity did it this way. Israel went from chronic crisis to water security, even surplus, by building large coastal plants that now supply a large share of its household water.
[P1.5] Gulf states sustain entire cities in deserts on it. Perth turned to seawater when its rainfall-fed reservoirs stopped filling. Compare Cape Town, which leaned on restrictions and came within weeks of shutting off its taps.
[P1.6] The standard objections are aging badly. Reverse-osmosis costs have fallen dramatically over recent decades; large plants now deliver water at prices ordinary households can afford. Energy demand is real but can be met with solar, which water-scarce regions tend to have in abundance. Brine is a managed engineering problem, not a veto.
[P1.7] Nothing here means wasting water. Efficiency stays as good housekeeping. But the question is where the primary investment goes: into reliable new supply, or into ever-tighter management of decline. Scarce regions should buy certainty.
[P1.8] Rationing is what you do when you failed to plan; desalination is the plan.

[C1.1] Cape Town is the best advertisement conservation ever got. When Day Zero loomed, the city roughly halved its consumption within months through pricing, pressure management, and restrictions — and the taps stayed on. No desalination plant on earth can be permitted, financed, and built in that time. The crisis PRO cites as a failure of rationing was in fact rationing's finest hour, and the emergency desalination units Cape Town did rush out delivered a trickle at eye-watering cost.
[C1.2] That reveals the real question: where should the first dollar go? Water from fixing leaks, drip irrigation, tiered tariffs, and wastewater reuse typically costs a fraction of desalinated water per cubic metre. A region that invests *primarily* in desalination is choosing to buy its most expensive water while leaving its cheapest water on the table. That is not planning; it is skipping the cheap options to reach the glamorous one.
[C1.3] Then look at who actually uses the water. In most scarce regions, agriculture takes the great majority. Desalinated seawater is far too expensive to irrigate wheat, and it arrives at sea level, often hundreds of kilometres and hundreds of metres of pumping from inland farms and cities. Israel, PRO's showpiece, did it the other way round: decades of drip irrigation, aggressive pricing, and reusing most of its wastewater came first; desalination was layered onto a system already squeezed efficient.
[C1.4] Finally, consider the plants built on PRO's logic. Several Australian cities commissioned large desalination plants during drought, then watched the rain return and the plants sit idle for years while ratepayers kept paying under take-or-pay contracts. Expensive supply also invites demand to grow into it, leaving a region just as exposed, only poorer and more energy-hungry.
[C1.5] Desalination belongs at the margin, for coastal cities, after demand is disciplined. Make it primary, and you have purchased a bigger pie you cannot afford to eat.
