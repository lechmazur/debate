[P1.1] Current ad-hoc migration allocations for skilled workers and international students are unpredictable, creating churn for universities and employers that rely on global talent to fill critical gaps. Tech firms can't scale R&D teams if visa caps are slashed without warning; regional universities can't build specialized programs if international enrollment numbers swing wildly year to year. Guaranteed, capped allocations tied to local capacity solve this: institutions get a stable, predictable talent pipeline they can plan around, rather than lurching from one policy whim to the next.
[P1.2] Tying these allocations to housing and local service capacity eliminates the single biggest driver of public backlash against migration: unmanaged strain on everyday resources. When a mid-sized town gains 500 international students or 200 skilled factory workers without new dorm beds, GP appointments, or school seats, resentment builds fast, and anti-migration rhetoric gains traction. Capping allocations to match verified capacity means no community is asked to bear more than it can handle, protecting both public services and social cohesion.
[P1.3] This system also fixes the perverse incentive of current uncapped, unmoored allocations, where institutions have no reason to invest in local infrastructure alongside their growth. If a university's allocation is tied to 200 new on-campus dorm beds and 10 new local school placements, it has a direct incentive to fund that infrastructure itself, rather than offloading the cost onto already-stretched local taxpayers. Employers, too, will push for better local housing and transit if their ability to hire talent depends on those services being available.
