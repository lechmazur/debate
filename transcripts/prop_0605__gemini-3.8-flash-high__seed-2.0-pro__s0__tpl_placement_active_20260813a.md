[P1.1] The defining flaw of the kafala system is the privatization of state immigration control into the hands of individual employers. When a migrant worker’s legal residency, right to leave, and ability to change jobs depend on a sponsor’s unilateral permission, the relationship ceases to be an ordinary employment contract. It becomes structural coercion. Replacing kafala with portable, worker-held visas is both an urgent humanitarian imperative and a vital economic modernization.
[P1.2] First, labor protections are meaningless without the freedom to exit. Under kafala, an employer who withholds wages or demands unsafe labor holds an insurmountable trump card: threatening visa cancellation or filing an "absconding" charge, which instantly criminalizes the worker. Wage monitoring systems and grievance hotlines fail when reporting abuse invites immediate deportation. Portable visas dismantle this captive dynamic.
[P1.3] When workers can walk away to a new job without employer sign-off, employers lose the power to exploit with impunity, and the market itself penalizes mistreatment.
[P1.4] Second, kafala actively distorts Gulf economies. Trapping workers subsidizes low-productivity businesses with captive, underpriced labor, stalling investments in technology, automation, and workforce upskilling. Portable visas create a dynamic market where labor flows to where it is most productive. Forward-looking employers can hire talent already in-country rather than relying on costly, predatory recruitment pipelines, boosting productivity and advancing the region’s economic diversification goals.
[P1.5] Granting portable visas does not eliminate immigration regulation; it reclaims it for the state, ending a regime where private bosses act as de facto border guards. Economic dynamism and human dignity both require that workers, not employers, hold their own legal status.
