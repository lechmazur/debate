[P1.1] Your phone is not really yours. Two firms control what software you may install and how you may pay for it, taking up to a 30% cut of digital sales simply for standing at the gate. No grocer, landlord, or credit-card network enjoys that unchallengeable power over an entire economy. That is why government action is justified: private gatekeeping over a general-purpose platform is regulation already, just unaccountable regulation for profit.
[P1.2] That toll harms everyone downstream. Consumers pay higher prices for subscriptions, e-books, music and dating apps because developers must bake the commission into the price or forgo features to survive. Developers, especially small ones, face take-it-or-leave-it rules, delayed approvals, and bans on telling users cheaper options exist. Innovation suffocates when a platform owner can tax, copy, or block rivals to its own services.
[P1.3] Requiring third-party payments and sideloading under baseline security standards breaks the bottleneck without breaking safety. No one is demanding anarchy; notarization, malware scanning, permission disclosure and revocation of malicious certificates preserve protection while ending compulsion. Your laptop already works this way: you can install from the official store or directly, with warnings and safeguards, and civilization has not collapsed.
[P1.4] With choice, competition disciplines fees. If a store charges too much or reviews too slowly, developers can bill directly or distribute elsewhere, forcing incumbents to earn their cut through better discovery, security and tools. That rivalry has already lowered costs where openness was forced, and passed savings to users. Payment processors already compete fiercely on fraud prevention and price; there is no technical reason only the platform owner may compete.
[P1.5] Baseline standards prevent a race to the bottom while ending a monopoly at the top.
[P1.6] Freedom to install legal software and choose how to pay is the baseline of ownership in a digital age. Security should protect that freedom, not serve as its pretext for monopoly rent.

PRO’s laptop analogy in [P1.3] proves too much—open PCs are malware magnets, and corporate laptops are locked down for good reason.
[C1.5] Phones avoided that fate through gatekeeping.
[C1.6] Are app store commissions sometimes excessive? Yes. But the remedy is targeted: ban anti-steering, require fee transparency, police self-preferencing, allow enterprise and developer distribution with clear liability. [C1.7] The real choice is not monopoly versus freedom. It is accountable gatekeeping versus fragmented risk. Government should not force the second and call it baseline safety. If a platform abuses its power, regulators can target that abuse; a blanket command to open every phone is a permanent cure with a permanent side effect.
