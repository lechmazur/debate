[P1.1] A decade ago, Europe built its energy system on cheap Russian gas because the economics looked irresistible. We know how that ended. The same logic now governs Chinese EVs, batteries, and solar: cheap, abundant, green — what could go wrong? Everything that went wrong last time, plus something worse: this dependency eats not just energy security but Europe's industrial base itself.
[P1.2] Start with concentration. China dominates solar wafer production, battery cells, and critical-mineral processing like graphite and rare earths. Beijing has already weaponized exactly this leverage — export controls on gallium, germanium, and graphite are fact, not hypothesis. A decarbonization strategy whose every panel and cell depends on a strategic rival's goodwill is not a strategy; it is a hostage arrangement with green branding.
[P1.3] Second, these exports are not market magic. They ride on state subsidies, cheap policy-bank credit, and deliberate overcapacity built to flood foreign markets and clear out domestic rivals. Europe's solar manufacturing led the world twenty years ago; it was wiped out in a few years by precisely this dynamic. The car industry — millions of jobs, the industrial core of Germany, France, Italy, and Central Europe — is next in line.
[P1.4] Third — and this is where the decarbonization framing collapses — destroying that base destroys the transition itself. Net zero survives politically only if Europeans see jobs and prosperity in it. Hollow out the factories and you harvest backlash, not emissions cuts; a transition imposed on deindustrialized regions will be voted out long before 2050. And you lose the engineering ecosystems that invent the next generation of clean technology.
[P1.5] Treating Chinese clean-tech primarily as an industrial threat does not mean autarky. It means tariffs that price in subsidies, screened investment, local-content rules, and real reindustrialization — buying decarbonization that lasts rather than renting dependency. Cheap imports today, coercion tomorrow. That trade is not green; it is merely short-sighted.
