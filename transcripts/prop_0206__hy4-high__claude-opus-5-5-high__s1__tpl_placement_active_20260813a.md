[P1.1] Public pensions are a promise about time: years of contributions on one side, years of benefits on the other. That promise was built when retirement was shorter. Longer lives are a triumph, but if the eligibility age stays fixed while lifespan rises, each added retirement year is funded by someone else's paycheck. Keeping the old age is not generosity; it is a quiet transfer from younger workers to longer retirements.
[P1.2] Indexing the retirement age to life expectancy preserves the original bargain. The aim is not work until frailty, but a roughly stable division of adult life between work and retirement. Some extra healthy years should enlarge working lives as well as leisure. A pension designed for a short retirement cannot absorb one twice as long with no change in the contribution boundary.
[P1.3] Predictable adjustment avoids repeated emergencies: people know the rule decades ahead and can plan, save, and retrain.
[P1.4] The tax-first alternative puts the entire cost of longevity on labor. Higher payroll taxes cut take-home pay, raise hiring costs, and make formal work less attractive at the margin. They are also unfair to younger and middle-income workers, who have less wealth and little confidence in receiving the same deal, yet pay more now for a growing retired population. As workers per retiree fall, this becomes a loop: higher rates, weaker wages, another shortfall, another increase.
[P1.5] It hides demography behind a contribution rate.
[P1.6] Targeted protections fit the reform: disability pathways, hardship rules for arduous jobs, minimum benefits, long phase-ins. But exceptions should not freeze the normal age for everyone, especially those with longer, healthier lives. The decisive comparison is simple. Linking retirement age to longevity shares the gains of longer life across work and retirement; relying mainly on payroll taxes loads those gains onto a shrinking workforce.
[P1.7] A pension younger workers can trust needs the first rule, not the second.
