[P1.1] Post-authoritarian transitions fail when they mistake silence for peace. Broad amnesties offer a seductive bargain: perpetrators walk free in exchange for quiet streets. But that bargain is built on denial, and denial is a poor foundation for a democratic state. The proposition asks societies to prefer the harder road—public truth over enforced forgetting—because that road produces stability that lasts.
[P1.2] A truth commission with public testimony does three things an amnesty cannot. First, it creates an authoritative record. When citizens see perpetrators named and victims heard, the state acknowledges what happened, replacing rumor and propaganda with a shared, if painful, history. Second, it dignifies victims.
[P1.3] Public testimony transforms private suffering into public recognition, which is essential for rebuilding trust in institutions that either committed or enabled atrocity. Third, it constrains future power. Once a record exists, later governments cannot easily rewrite the past or recycle old abuses.
[P1.4] The stability purchased by amnesties is usually temporary. In Argentina, military self-amnesties did not secure the transition; they triggered decades of legal struggle and eventual repeal, as courts and civil society refused to treat state terror as a closed chapter. Chile’s blanket amnesty similarly failed to end demands for accountability, forcing later truth-seeking anyway. These cases show that burying the past does not neutralize it—it weaponizes it, leaving grievances to resurface with interest.
[P1.5] Objections about reigniting conflict assume truth commissions are prosecutions. They are not. They offer conditional space for acknowledgment, not punishment, and can be designed with clear mandates. The real risk is not that societies will investigate too much; it is that they will build democracies on foundations of lies.
[P1.6] A state that cannot face its own crimes cannot earn the loyalty of its citizens. Truth is not the enemy of stability; it is its precondition.
