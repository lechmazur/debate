[P1.1] The core issue is who should bear the cost of airline-controlled failures. If a carrier delays or cancels a flight because of maintenance, crew scheduling, or overbooking, it has already broken its basic promise. A refund only returns part of the fare; it does nothing for the stranded family’s hotel, the missed job interview, or the lost day of a prepaid trip. Forcing passengers into complaint-heavy processes makes them pay again with time, stress, and often forfeited claims.
[P1.2] Automatic compensation is administratively realistic because airlines already possess the relevant data. They know whether a delay was controllable, whether a connection was missed because of their own schedule, and which passengers were affected. Requiring manual claims pretends travelers can prove what the airline knows best. It converts a clear service failure into a negotiation where the firm with superior information and resources wins by exhaustion.
[P1.3] Many passengers will not claim at all, especially those with language barriers, tight onward travel, or small losses that exceed the hassle of pursuing them.
[P1.4] This requirement also corrects incentives. If controllable disruptions are cheap to impose, airlines will underinvest in spare aircraft, staffing, realistic connection times, and schedule buffers. Automatic payments put the cost where the decision is made, making unreliable operations financially visible. A passenger who misses a connection because an airline sold an impossible 35-minute transfer should not need to beg for a voucher; compensation should trigger as automatically as the ticket charge.
[P1.5] Opponents may point to weather or air-traffic control, but the proposition is limited to controllable delays and missed connections caused by the airline’s own operation. It does not require perfection; it requires responsibility. The status quo treats compensation as a reward for persistence rather than a remedy for harm. That is unfair to passengers, inefficient for regulators and airlines, and it subsidizes avoidable disruption.
[P1.6] Airlines should compensate automatically because they caused the harm, know the facts, and can reduce it.
