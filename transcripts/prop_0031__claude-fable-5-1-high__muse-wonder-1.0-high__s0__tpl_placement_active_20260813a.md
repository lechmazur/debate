[P1.1] Single-family-only zoning does one thing: it makes it illegal to build a duplex, a triplex, or a backyard cottage on a lot where a larger single house would be perfectly legal. That prohibition is the whole policy. Nobody is debating whether people may build detached houses — ending the district type leaves that right fully intact. The only question is whether cities should keep manufacturing new neighborhoods where the cheapest forms of housing are crimes.
[P1.2] Density bonuses don't answer that question; they dodge it. A bonus leaves the prohibition as the baseline and sells exceptions back to whoever can afford the price of admission — affordability set-asides, impact fees, parking requirements, design review, and a public hearing where neighbors get to say no. Those conditions only pencil out for large developers on large parcels in hot markets. The small builder who wants to split a house into two units, or the homeowner who wants to add a flat for a parent, never clears the bar.
[P1.3] So bonuses produce occasional mid-rise projects on arterials while the vast interior of the city — in many American cities, roughly three-quarters of residential land — stays frozen. Incremental tools cannot move a problem that size.
[P1.4] The banning approach has a track record. Minneapolis and the state of Oregon ended single-family-only zoning; what followed was not towers but a modest, steady trickle of duplexes and triplexes that look like houses from the street, and rent growth that stayed flatter than in comparable regions. The feared destruction of neighborhoods never arrived, because removing a prohibition is not the same as mandating anything.
[P1.5] And the honest history matters. Single-family-only districts spread after courts struck down explicit racial zoning; minimum lot sizes and single-unit rules did the same sorting by price. A bonus system that preserves that baseline and lets neighbors veto exceptions preserves the sorting too.
[P1.6] The choice is simple: a clear, uniform floor that applies to every new district, or a permanent negotiation that only the well-resourced ever win.

[C1.1] A ban on new single-family-only districts sounds decisive, but it misdiagnoses where housing is blocked and surrenders the tool that can trade density for public benefits. Most exclusion sits in districts already platted and built; forbidding a label on future maps does little for a renter priced out today. Bonuses can work now, inside existing neighborhoods, adding cottages and affordable units where people already live. Cities need flexibility to grow, not a prohibition on planning.
[C1.2] That distinction matters because new development is where context varies most. A subdivision at a city's edge may lack sewers, transit, or roads for doubled traffic; a site near a station can support far more. A categorical ban prevents planners from sequencing growth, forcing costly infrastructure overbuilding or pushing developers to leapfrog to the next county and build the same houses farther out, with longer commutes. Bonuses let a city allow more where capacity exists, rather than everywhere at once or nowhere at all.
[C1.3] Bonuses also buy what blanket upzoning gives away free. Extra density is valuable, and cities can exchange it for deed-restricted affordable homes, sewer extensions, parks, or designs that fit a block. Under a ban, two expensive duplex units replacing one modest house owe the public nothing. Incentive zoning turns added density into a negotiated return instead of a windfall.
[C1.4] That leverage disappears once density is an entitlement.
[C1.5] Durability matters too. Many households want detached homes and yards, and developers need predictable rules to finance streets and utilities at scale. Eliminating an entire district type invites ballot fights and lawsuits that freeze projects, while bonuses create visible wins, enlist homeowners as small builders, and build support for further reform. A surviving policy that produces affordable units beats a pure ban that changes a map label, pushes growth outward, and delays supply.
