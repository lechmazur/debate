[P1.1] New multi-unit buildings last for generations. Every unit built without basic accessibility is not "neutral"; it locks in a design that will exclude aging residents, people with disabilities, and even temporarily injured occupants for decades. The motion already concedes the added construction cost is modest. That concession decides the debate, because the true comparison is not "pay a little now or pay nothing."
[P1.2] It is "pay a little now, or pay far more later through disruptive retrofits, higher turnover, early move-outs, and avoidable institutional care."
[P1.3] Universal design is inexpensive when it is designed in from the start. A no-step entrance, slightly wider doorways, reinforced bathroom walls for future grab bars, lever handles, and reachable switches add little to a project already dominated by land, structure, and finishing costs. They become expensive only as retrofits, when walls are opened, bathrooms are rebuilt, and common areas are torn up. The CON side's cost objection therefore mistakes timing for magnitude.
[P1.4] It highlights a small upfront line item while ignoring the heavier bill that inaccessible housing pushes onto future tenants, landlords, and public programs.
[P1.5] This is also not a subsidy for a small group. Disability and reduced mobility are ordinary life risks, not rare identities. Most people will at some point use a stroller, a walker, a cane, or a wheelchair after surgery, and the population is aging. Universal design simply lets a home keep working for the people who already live there.
[P1.6] That reduces costly moves, preserves independence, and keeps people out of institutions that are far more expensive than a wider doorway.
[P1.7] Finally, private markets under-supply this value. Developers capture the upfront cost but may not capture the long-term benefit to tenants or society, and individual renters cannot retrofit common entrances or shared bathrooms. A stronger standard corrects that split incentive. When the cost is modest, inclusion is not a luxury; it is basic competence in building homes meant to last.

[C1.1] The most important accessibility feature a home can have is existence. A wheelchair user priced out of the city entirely gains nothing from wider doorways in an apartment that was never built. That is the tradeoff this motion refuses to face: in the middle of a housing shortage, layering stronger mandates onto new construction shrinks supply at exactly the margin where projects live or die.
[C1.2] Notice what "modest" conceals. The cheap items—lever handles, reachable switches—are already common or already required. What "stronger" standards actually demand is space: larger bathrooms, wider corridors, turning radii, elevators in small buildings. Space is the single most expensive commodity in multi-unit housing.
[C1.3] A bigger bathroom in every unit means fewer units per floor, or higher rent per unit, on every project, forever. On small infill lots, an elevator requirement alone can kill the three-story walk-up—the classic affordable "missing middle" building—outright. Call that modest to a developer's spreadsheet and watch the project quietly not happen.
[C1.4] And this is not one mandate; it is one more mandate. Energy codes, parking rules, fire standards, impact fees—each defended as modest, together consuming a substantial share of construction cost. Every marginal project killed pushes rents up citywide, and rising rents fall hardest on the very populations PRO claims to champion: seniors and disabled people on fixed incomes.
[C1.5] The honest alternative is targeting, not universality. Existing fair-housing and building codes already require meaningful accessibility in new multifamily construction. Beyond that, require or subsidize a proportion of fully accessible units matched to actual demand, and fund retrofits for people who develop needs in place. That delivers accessibility to those who need it without taxing every studio apartment for a wheelchair turning radius its occupant may never use.
[C1.6] PRO must show the benefits of universal mandates outweigh the units they prevent. Until then, the compassionate-sounding policy is the one that leaves more people without a home at all.
