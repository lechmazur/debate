[P1.1] The United States, the predominantly private-insurance system, spends roughly twice as much per person on healthcare as peer countries with public systems — and gets less life for it. Life expectancy is lower, infant and maternal mortality are higher, and deaths from treatable conditions like diabetes, hypertension, and asthma are more common. Per dollar, it buys less health.
[P1.2] Public systems win that conversion for three structural reasons.
[P1.3] First, they eliminate friction. A patchwork of private insurers means thousands of different billing rules, eligibility checks, prior authorizations, marketing budgets, shareholder returns, and armies of staff on both sides arguing over payment. Hospitals hire billers; insurers hire deniers. None of that treats a patient.
[P1.4] A public system with one set of rules and no profit extraction can run administration for a fraction of that cost, freeing money for nurses, clinics, and medicine.
[P1.5] Second, public systems can buy healthcare at sane prices. A national payer negotiates drug prices, hospital fees, and physician payments as a bulk buyer. Fragmented private insurers cannot — or will not — match that leverage, so Americans pay multiples more for the same insulin, the same scan, the same hip replacement. When identical inputs cost three times as much, efficiency is impossible no matter how good the doctor is.
[P1.6] Third, universality is a health technology. When coverage is automatic, people see a primary-care doctor early, get vaccinated, control blood pressure, and catch cancer while it is still cheap and curable. In a private system, the uninsured and underinsured delay care until a manageable condition becomes an emergency-room catastrophe — worse for the patient, vastly more expensive for everyone. Prevention only works at population scale if nobody is priced out at the door.
[P1.7] Private insurance optimizes for selecting profitable customers and profitable procedures. Public healthcare optimizes for covering everybody with the basics that move population health. Per dollar, that is why it delivers more.

That is confirmation of the mechanism, not a refutation.
[P2.2] And on the motion's actual metric — outcomes per dollar — they lose to public systems anyway. Swiss spending is famously the highest in Europe and second only to the United States, for life expectancy broadly similar to Britain's. The Netherlands also spends well above the British level. Matching an outcome while spending substantially more is not efficiency; it is a more expensive way to buy the same health.
[P2.3] Singapore makes the same point from the other direction: dominant public hospitals, heavy state price control, and compulsory savings directed by government. Calling that predominantly private confuses the label on the payment with who actually sets the rules and prices.
[P2.4] The waiting-list argument also proves too little. Private systems ration too — by price, by network, by prior authorization and denial — except their queue is invisible because patients who cannot pay never join it. Public waits for elective procedures are real, but urgent care is prioritized, and crucially any harm from delay is already captured in life expectancy and treatable mortality. Those final outcomes are exactly where public systems beat the United States and match high-spending private hybrids for less money.
[P2.5] Finally, obesity and violence do not explain away deaths from conditions healthcare is supposed to manage. Treatable mortality isolates precisely that question: deaths from diabetes, hypertension, appendicitis, and similar conditions that timely care should avert. America lags there too. A system that prices people out of primary care turns lifestyle risk into unmanaged disease; universal public coverage interrupts that chain earlier and cheaper.
