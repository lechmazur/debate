[P1.1] Infertility is a medical condition that deprives millions of the ability to have children, causing measurable physical, psychological, and social harm. When a nation classifies a service as basic healthcare, it commits to making that service universally accessible, affordable, and integral to its health system. Treating infertility treatment, including IVF, as basic healthcare fulfills that commitment for three decisive reasons.
[P1.2] First, infertility meets the clinical definition of a disease. The World Health Organization estimates that roughly one in six couples worldwide experience difficulty conceiving, a prevalence comparable to diabetes or hypertension. Ignoring it while we fund treatment for those conditions is inconsistent. If a nation provides dialysis for kidney failure, it must also provide reproductive care for a condition that robs families of the same fundamental life function.
[P1.3] Second, the economic argument collapses under scrutiny. A single IVF cycle costs a few thousand dollars, yet the long‑term savings from reduced mental‑health crises, lower divorce rates, and increased labor‑force participation of parents outweigh the expense. Countries that already cover IVF—such as the United Kingdom, Australia, and several Nordic states—demonstrate that budgets can absorb the cost without sacrificing other services. Their experience shows the fiscal impact is modest when spread across the whole population.
[P1.4] Third, equity demands inclusion. Without universal coverage, infertility treatment becomes a privilege of the wealthy, deepening existing health disparities. Basic healthcare is precisely the tool that levels the playing field, ensuring that a person’s chance to start a family does not depend on bank balance. When governments fund cancer treatment, they must also fund a condition that determines whether families can exist.
[P1.5] Denying it entrenches inequality and violates the basic human right to form a family.
[P1.6] These three pillars—clinical legitimacy, fiscal feasibility, and social justice—form the core of the case. Any opponent who argues otherwise must confront the fact that we already treat comparable conditions as basic care, and we already have real‑world models proving that covering IVF works.
