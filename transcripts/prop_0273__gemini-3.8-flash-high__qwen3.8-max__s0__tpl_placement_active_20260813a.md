[P1.1] Modern airfare shopping is engineered around an illusion. A search engine displays a tantalizing $180 flight, but for a family traveling with children, that number is fictitious. By the time parents navigate mandatory adjacent seating fees and basic luggage charges, that $180 ticket balloons to $350. Revealing these costs only at the final checkout screen is not market freedom; it is deceptive drip pricing designed to exploit sunk-cost fatigue.
[P1.2] The reality of family travel is fundamentally distinct from solo travel. An unbundled base fare works for a solo traveler with a laptop bag who does not care where they sit. But a parent traveling with a young child cannot physically or safely leave that toddler seated five rows away with a stranger, nor can a family of four travel for a week using only coats and pockets. For families, seating together and bringing luggage are not discretionary luxuries.
[P1.3] They are non-negotiable practical necessities.
[P1.4] When airlines and booking platforms hide these costs during initial comparison, they destroy transparent competition. An honest carrier that includes seat assignments and bags for $300 appears artificially expensive and gets buried below a carrier showing a phantom $180 fare that later totals $360. This dynamic rewards obfuscation and penalizes upfront pricing, creating an anti-competitive race to the bottom. It forces parents into an exhausting chore: navigating multiple multi-step checkout funnels just to calculate which flight is genuinely affordable.
[P1.5] Requiring platforms to display the full practical cost upfront restores price discovery. Search engines already ask how many adults and children are flying. Factoring in baseline seating together and baggage needs at the initial query is technically trivial and economically essential. It strips away the smoke and mirrors, respects consumers' time, and ensures that when a family sorts by price, the lowest price displayed is actually the price they pay.

[C1.3] That error harms the very consumers PRO claims to protect. Budget fares exist because airlines unbundle services. [C1.5] The stronger principle is disclosure, not presumed bundling. Platforms should be required to show clear fee schedules, seat-selection terms, and baggage rules before checkout, so users can add what they actually need. But the motion as written demands a pre-computed total for a “practical” trip that varies from household to household. That is too vague, too intrusive, and too likely to distort comparison.
