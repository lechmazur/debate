[P1.1] Start with what flaring and leakage actually are: the deliberate destruction and release of the very commodity we're told we can't afford to lose. A flare stack is a chimney burning money. A leaking valve is a pipeline that vents its cargo into the sky. So when the motion concedes a possible hit to "short-term domestic energy supply," notice how strange the trade really is: we are being asked to protect the supply we already throw away.
[P1.2] That is the core of my case. Every year the world burns off and vents gas on the order of the annual consumption of a mid-sized industrialized nation. Capturing it does not shrink energy supply; it converts waste into fuel. The only reason it isn't captured already is that the flare is free to the operator and the atmosphere pays.
[P1.3] That is a textbook externality, and externalities are precisely what regulation exists to price.
[P1.4] Second, the climate arithmetic is unusually favorable. Methane traps far more heat than CO₂ over the decades that matter most for tipping points, but it also leaves the atmosphere within roughly a couple of decades. That combination is rare: cutting methane buys near-term temperature relief in a way that no other single intervention does. If you want to slow warming this decade rather than next century, this is the lever.
[P1.5] Third, the abatement is cheap and known. Leak detection and repair, replacing pneumatic devices, vapour recovery, gathering lines to avoid stranded associated gas — these are plumbing problems, not moonshots. A large share pays for itself once the operator can sell the gas instead of torching it. Which is exactly why voluntary pledges have underdelivered for thirty years: if it were self-executing, it would already be done.
[P1.6] Strict thresholds with real penalties are what turn "we intend to" into "we did."
[P1.7] Finally, the supply cost falls almost entirely on the worst-run wells — the ones flaring because they built oil capacity without gas infrastructure. Making that unprofitable is the point, not a side effect.

[C1.2] The motion's distinctive commitment is to the other cases: the wells where capture doesn't pay, where no gathering line exists, where compliance means shutting in production. That is where I'll fight, because that is where the ban does its real work.
[C1.3] First, the atmosphere has no borders. When domestic supply falls, demand doesn't vanish. It gets filled by imported LNG, often from producers with worse flaring and weaker oversight, or by coal. Europe's scramble after 2022 showed the pattern: tight gas meant coal plants restarted.
[C1.4] A ban that shuts in a moderately leaky domestic well and replaces its output with a flaring-heavy import or a coal plant has made the climate worse while calling it a victory. PRO's near-term temperature argument actually cuts against a supply-sacrificing rule: leakage abroad erases the near-term gain.
[C1.5] Second, PRO correctly calls this an externality. The textbook answer to an externality is a price, not a prohibition. A steep, rising charge per tonne of methane makes the cheap fixes happen immediately and forces the expensive ones to compete honestly, without a cliff where production simply stops. Norway has long taxed offshore flaring and emissions and sustains some of the lowest flaring intensity anywhere, without banning its way there.
[C1.6] Third, bans on measured thresholds invite perverse outcomes. Leakage is intermittent and hard to measure; a binary ban rewards gaming the reading and punishes whoever gets caught. Worse, operators facing shut-in often abandon marginal wells, and abandoned, unplugged wells are themselves chronic methane leakers.
[C1.7] So the choice is not climate versus energy. It is a blunt ban that exports emissions versus a price that cuts them where they actually happen.
