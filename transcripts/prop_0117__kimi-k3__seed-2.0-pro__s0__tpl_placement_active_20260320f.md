[P1.1] The case for keeping executions for terrorism and mass murder rests on three hidden premises: that the state will get it right, that killing achieves what prison cannot, and that justice demands it. All three fail.
[P1.2] First, fallibility. Courts convict the innocent through coerced confessions, flawed forensics, and mistaken identity — and the pressure to convict is most intense precisely in terrorism and atrocity cases, when the public demands a culprit. A life sentence can be revisited; an execution cannot. Abolition does not require proving every condemned person innocent — only that the system is human, which it demonstrably is.
[P1.3] An irreversible punishment demands an infallibility no institution possesses.
[P1.4] Second, deterrence. Terrorists frequently plan to die; martyrdom is the point, and a state execution completes their script and hands their movement a recruiting poster. Mass killers are often suicidal at the scene. Promising death to people who seek it deters nothing.
[P1.5] No consistent evidence shows executions protect the public better than permanent imprisonment — comparisons across jurisdictions have never produced the safety dividend defenders promise.
[P1.6] Third, everything legitimate the death penalty claims to deliver, life without parole already provides: the offender is neutralized, society is protected, punishment is severe — arguably more severe, since many prisoners would choose death over fifty years in a cell. What execution adds is not safety but spectacle: decades of appeals that retraumatize victims' families, vast cost, arbitrary outcomes shaped by geography and counsel, and botched killings performed in the public's name.
[P1.7] The real question is not whether the worst offenders deserve to die. Perhaps some do. It is whether we should grant the state the power to kill prisoners in cold blood, knowing it will sometimes be wrong and gains nothing a cell cannot provide. A society proves its character by the restraint it shows toward those who showed none.
[P1.8] Abolish the death penalty — above all for the crimes that test that principle hardest.
