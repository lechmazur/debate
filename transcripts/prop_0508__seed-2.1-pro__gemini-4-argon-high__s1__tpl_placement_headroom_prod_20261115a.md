[P1.1] Most trade blocs have already eliminated the vast majority of goods tariffs between their members, leaving average applied rates on intra-bloc trade in the low single digits. New tariff cuts now deliver diminishing, narrow gains: they shave fractions of a percent off costs for large, established goods producers that already have the supply chain teams and legal resources to navigate remaining border frictions, while leaving the fastest-growing segments of cross-border exchange almost entirely untouched. Digital services and licensed professional work now make up the majority of economic output across nearly every trade bloc member, but they face effectively no relief from traditional tariff cuts, because tariffs apply to physical goods crossing a border, not to a software subscription, a telehealth consultation, an engineer’s stamped design, or a freelance creative contract delivered remotely.
[P1.2] The real barriers to that trade are redundant, behind-the-border requirements: demands to re-take professional licensing exams even after qualifying in a partner country, or to rebuild entire data compliance and digital security regimes from scratch even when a firm already meets equivalent rules in its home market—frictions that routinely add 20 to 30 percent to service delivery costs with zero corresponding gain in genuine consumer protection. Mutual recognition deals cut those frictions at a stroke, without forcing members to surrender regulatory sovereignty by harmonizing rules top-down.
[P1.3] They unlock disproportionate gains for small businesses and independent workers who cannot afford to navigate a dozen duplicative credentialing and compliance regimes across a bloc, delivering far broader, more inclusive growth that reaches people locked out of traditional goods export networks, compared to the marginal tariff cuts still left on the table. [C1.2] Worse, PRO treats mutual recognition as a painless shortcut that preserves regulatory sovereignty [P1.2]. In reality, it surrenders sovereignty more recklessly than harmonization. If one member state has lax data-privacy oversight or lower clinical training requirements for doctors and engineers, mutual recognition compels every other member to let those under-vetted practitioners and data brokers operate domestically.

Tariff cuts, by contrast, are transparent, self-executing, and immediately enforceable at the customs line. Trade blocs should not burn scarce political capital chasing unworkable regulatory shortcuts when slashing remaining tariff peaks delivers certain, economy-wide gains for consumers and producers alike.
