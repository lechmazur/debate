[P1.1] Aging should not mean forfeiting autonomy at the door of a facility. No one denies that acute, round-the-clock medical needs sometimes require institutional settings; the question is how we organize care when a choice is possible. For the vast majority of older adults, the need is not intensive clinical machinery, but help with daily living—bathing, cooking, or managing medications. Organizing elder care around the home preserves the familiar routines, neighborhood ties, and personal agency that keep people mentally and physically anchored.
[P1.2] Uprooting someone from a home they have known for decades into a regimented facility severs their social fabric and accelerates cognitive decline, especially for those with early memory loss who rely on familiar surroundings to stay oriented.
[P1.3] Beyond dignity, home-centered care protects physical health. Institutions concentrate frail, immunocompromised people under one roof, turning routine respiratory or stomach viruses into lethal outbreaks. Furthermore, institutional care is governed by rigid staffing ratios: an overworked aide splitting attention across a dozen residents down a hallway. Under that pressure, even well-meaning facilities fall back on practices that harm residents—delayed assistance that triggers falls, unaddressed immobility, or sedatives used to manage wandering.
[P1.4] Supporting care at home through visiting nurses, personal aides, and family respite replaces assembly-line supervision with tailored attention in a safer environment.
[P1.5] Finally, defaulting to institutions squanders scarce resources. When families or public budgets pay for a residential facility, a massive share of that funding goes toward brick-and-mortar overhead, administrative layers, and real estate—duplicating housing costs for seniors who already have a place to live. Redirecting that money toward home modifications like grab bars and ramps, visiting health workers, and direct compensation for family caregivers buys actual human care rather than facility overhead. When possible, a humane system brings support to where older adults live, rather than dismantling their lives to fit where the bureaucracy keeps its beds.

[C1.6] The proposition assumes institutions are merely warehouses and homes are sanctuaries. But for the most vulnerable elders, the institution is not the enemy of dignity—it is the only place where their medical and safety needs can actually be met. The PRO dismisses advanced dementia as an "extreme," but cognitive decline is progressive. The elder who needs a stove shutoff today needs someone awake at 3 a.m. to prevent wandering next month.

"Bringing professionals into the living room" describes hours of presence, not the twenty-three other hours of solitude that actually endanger lives.
[C2.5] Defaulting to institutions isn't about warehousing; it's about ensuring that when vulnerability peaks, the safety net is continuous, not scheduled.
