[P1.1] A stock exchange is not an ordinary business. It is where the public price of a company is set, and every pension fund, retail saver, and corporate treasurer is forced to trade against that price. The question is simple: should the operator of that public utility be allowed to sell a handful of firms a faster seat at the table? We say no.
[P1.2] Start with what co-location actually buys. When a price moves, someone who sees it microseconds earlier can trade against orders that have not yet updated. That is not insight about a company's value; it is a head start on information everyone else will receive moments later. The firm paying for the rack beside the matching engine hits the stale quote, and the losers are whoever posted it: the mutual fund rebalancing your retirement account, the market maker without the premium cable.
[P1.3] Economists call this latency arbitrage. A plainer name is a toll, collected by the fastest firms from everyone slower, with the exchange selling the tollbooth.
[P1.4] Second, the exchange has a structural conflict of interest. Connectivity, data feeds, and co-location have become a major revenue line for exchange operators. That gives the referee a direct financial stake in keeping speed advantages valuable, and in inventing ever more expensive tiers to sell. A venue that profits from inequality of access cannot be trusted to design fair access.
[P1.5] Third, the arms race is wasteful. Billions have gone into shaving microseconds: microwave towers, custom chips, cable routes. None of it makes a single company better managed or a price more accurate in any sense investors care about. It is pure rent-seeking, and it entrenches a few incumbents who can afford the entry fee.
[P1.6] CON will say co-located firms supply liquidity and tightened spreads. But spreads narrowed for many reasons, from decimalization to electronic order books, and speed-purchased liquidity tends to evaporate exactly when markets are stressed, as the flash-crash episodes showed.
