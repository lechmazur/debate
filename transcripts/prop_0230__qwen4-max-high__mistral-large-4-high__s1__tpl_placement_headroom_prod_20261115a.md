[P1.1] Readiness is not a shopping list. Europe can order shells and air defence this year; it cannot order a trained reserve. When a professional brigade rotates or takes losses, the question is who comes next, and in most European countries the honest answer is nobody. Mandatory service is the only mechanism that creates a large pool of people who have already trained, already know their units, and can be recalled in weeks instead of built from zero over a year.
[P1.2] Finland kept that pool; the Baltic states rebuilt theirs after 2014. That is arithmetic, not nostalgia.
[P1.3] The objection that modern war needs specialists, not masses, actually argues for my model. Ukraine has shown that high-technology war still consumes people at scale. Norway and Sweden conscript selectively: they screen for aptitude and motivation, take a fraction of each cohort, and train them properly. Expand that across Europe and you get quality and depth together, with a civil-service track for those who cannot or will not bear arms.
[P1.4] Cohesion is the second product, and it is not sentimental. Shared service puts the banker's son and the delivery driver's daughter in the same tent, dependent on each other for something that matters — and almost nothing else in modern European life does that. Today the burden of defence falls on whoever needs the paycheck; universal service turns it into a claim every family holds. Societies do not stay solidaristic by consuming separately and voting angrily.
[P1.5] They stay solidaristic when they owe each other something and have visibly paid it.
[P1.6] Judge this by two questions: in ten years, can Europe field replacements, and do its citizens share a stake in defending it? The status quo answers no to both, and no recruitment budget has yet answered them either. Cost and inconvenience are real objections; being unready and unbound is worse.

[C1.1] Modern European defense does not fail for lack of bodies; it fails for lack of precision. The proposition mistakes mass for readiness, but high-intensity conflict today is won by specialists—drone operators, cyber analysts, logistics officers—whose skills take years to build, not six to twelve months a conscript typically serves. A cohort called up at eighteen, given basic training, then released does not constitute a reserve; it constitutes a demographic exercise. By crisis time, those skills have atrophied, unit cohesion has dissolved, and the state is left carrying veterans' costs for personnel who never reached operational depth.
[C1.2] Even in Ukraine, the decisive units are motivated volunteers and long-trained reservists, not raw conscripts rushed to the front. Professional forces backed by paid, voluntary reserve systems deliver deployable capability at a fraction of the administrative burden.
[C1.3] The cohesion argument is equally fragile. Forced cohabitation in barracks does not manufacture solidarity; it manufactures resentment, particularly when the wealthy secure deferments or civil-service posts while others bear the burden. Genuine social cohesion is built through shared civic institutions and voluntary endeavor, not state coercion at the threshold of adulthood. Universal conscription deepens divisions it claims to heal by turning service into a lottery that falls hardest on those with fewest means to avoid it.
[C1.4] Finally, opportunity cost is decisive. Every euro and bureaucratic hour spent drafting hundreds of thousands of unwilling teenagers is diverted from investments that deter aggression: air defense, ammunition stockpiles, cyber resilience, and professional training that turns a small army into a credible force. Estonia demonstrates that readiness comes from smart, repeated reserve training and whole-of-society resilience, not from inducting entire cohorts into disruptive stints. Europe needs capable, funded defense institutions, not a twentieth-century ritual that taxes youth for symbolic unity while hollowing out the professional core that does the fighting.
