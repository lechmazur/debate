[P1.1] Serious cheating and harassment are ways a player treats other people, not quirks of one game. A player who runs cheats in one ranked ladder will not suddenly respect competitive integrity elsewhere; a person banned for stalking or hate campaigns in one major ecosystem does not become safe on another. Yet current bans mostly stay where they were issued, punishing accounts while the worst actors simply move.
[P1.2] The core case is protective, not punitive. Major game ecosystems already share players, cross-play, linked accounts, and communities. When a serious ban is local only, the risk follows the actor while the record stays behind. Serial cheaters rotate to the next major title, and serial harassers continue from a new account on another service.
[P1.3] A ban should remove serious harm from the broader space, not relocate it.
[P1.4] This does not require automatic lifetime exclusion for every offense. It means serious, evidence-based bans carry a strong cross-ecosystem presumption: at minimum a visible flag, queuing restriction, or high entry bar until appeal. Minor or weak moderation decisions need not travel. The proposition asks for more than now, not maximum portability for every infraction.
[P1.5] Due-process concerns are real, but they cut both ways. A known harasser receiving a clean slate on the next platform gets no hearing; the people they target get no protection. False positives can be handled through appeals, shared evidence, and tiered or time-limited actions. Requiring each ecosystem to independently rediscover the same serious offender is not due process; it is failure to act on information already gathered.
[P1.6] The default should favor protecting players, with correction available to the accused.
[P1.7] When a serious ban stays in one place, it protects no one except the person seeking a fresh start. That is the wrong default.
