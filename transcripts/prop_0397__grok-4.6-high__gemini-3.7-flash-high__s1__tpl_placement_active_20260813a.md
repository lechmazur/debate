[C1.1] Bankruptcy is designed for debts backed by tangible collateral or rigorous underwriting. Student debt is neither. When a borrower buys a home, the bank can foreclose; when a business takes a loan, the lender rigorously screens credit. Student loans, by contrast, are granted universally to young adults with no credit history, no collateral, and no track record, financing intangible human capital that can never be repossessed.
[C1.2] The heightened standard for student loan discharge is not an arbitrary penalty; it is the structural pillar that makes broad access to higher education possible. Because lenders cannot seize a degree, making discharge "much easier" invites acute moral hazard. Borrowers at the threshold of their working lives could wipe away substantial debts during their lowest-earning years, retaining the lifetime appreciation of their credentials while shifting the financial loss directly onto taxpayers. If discharge becomes routine, the inevitable result is tighter underwriting, higher borrowing costs, and the exclusion of the very disadvantaged students who need unsecured financing most.
[C1.3] Moreover, bankruptcy is the wrong tool for addressing long-term inability to repay. We already have a tailored, non-adversarial safety valve: Income-Driven Repayment. Under income-driven frameworks, borrowers facing chronic underemployment or low wages pay only an affordable fraction of discretionary income—often zero dollars—while progressing toward complete administrative forgiveness. This directly alleviates insolvency without forcing vulnerable people through costly, adversarial litigation that destroys their credit profile for a decade.
[C1.4] Weakening bankruptcy protections does not create a compassionate fresh start; it destabilizes the federal loan system and crowds out administrative solutions that already protect struggling borrowers. To preserve universal educational opportunity and fiscal stability, the bar for discharge must remain exceptionally high.
