[P1.1] The stratosphere has no borders. When a state, university, or private startup launches reflective aerosols into the open sky, they are not conducting an isolated experiment; they are rehearsing the unilateral modification of a shared planetary system. A temporary moratorium on outdoor field experiments does not ban climate science. Computer modeling, satellite observation, and indoor laboratory chemistry can and should continue.
[P1.2] What must pause is crossing the threshold into the open atmosphere before the world agrees on the rules.
[P1.3] First, premature field testing destroys the trust required to govern this technology. Even small-scale outdoor trials have already sparked fierce backlash, from canceled balloon tests in northern Europe after Indigenous and public outcry, to private ventures releasing sulfur balloons without oversight. When actors test in the open sky without shared rules on transparency, monitoring, or liability, vulnerable nations rightly fear a technology that could disrupt their monsoons and agriculture. Unregulated field tests do not build scientific consensus; they trigger geopolitical suspicion and normalize rogue action.
[P1.4] Second, allowing field experiments before governance destroys the incentive to ever build that governance. Field trials are primarily engineering tests: they develop the nozzles, dispersal hardware, and operational know-how needed for full-scale deployment. If wealthy governments and billionaire-backed ventures can perfect delivery systems in a legal vacuum, they will create unstoppable institutional momentum. Why would a technologically dominant state submit to binding international constraints tomorrow if it can establish facts in the sky today?
[P1.5] A moratorium is the essential leverage that forces powerful actors to the negotiating table.
[P1.6] Finally, pausing outdoor tests carries minimal scientific cost. Small-scale releases cannot measure planetary climate responses anyway; they are too small to separate from natural weather noise. The urgent scientific questions remain inside laboratories and supercomputers. By contrast, crossing the outdoor threshold without binding rules invites a reckless technological race.
[P1.7] Governance must precede hardware.

[C1.1] The motion asks us to stop field science until governance exists, but that reverses the order of knowledge and rulemaking. A binding framework needs evidence about how solar geoengineering actually behaves outside a model. Laboratory chemistry and computer simulations can narrow uncertainty, but they cannot measure aerosol dispersion, detectability, chemical side effects, or the real-world limits of monitoring equipment in the stratosphere. A moratorium would freeze that knowledge at the moment it is most contested, leaving rules to be written from speculation rather than observation.
[C1.2] The moratorium also mistakes a governance failure for a scientific one. The problem with unauthorized balloon launches or opaque private experiments is not that the sky was touched; it is that there was no enforceable permit, disclosure, liability, or monitoring regime. Prohibiting all outdoor research does not solve that. It removes the lawful baseline that regulators could inspect and makes rogue action harder to detect.
[C1.3] The stronger answer is to permit only small, staged, auditable experiments under prior notification, independent oversight, hard size limits, insurance requirements, and mandatory shutdown conditions. Those controls bind legitimate actors; a blanket pause merely hands initiative to those who ignore permits entirely.
[C1.4] Finally, “temporary” is doing too much work. Waiting for a binding international framework could mean years of delay or indefinite stalemate, while the scientific and governance questions remain unanswered. A tiny research release cannot measurably change the planet’s climate, so it is not a rehearsal for unilateral deployment. The decisive line should be deployment, not knowledge.
[C1.5] We should govern field experiments now, not postpone them until governance somehow appears fully formed.
