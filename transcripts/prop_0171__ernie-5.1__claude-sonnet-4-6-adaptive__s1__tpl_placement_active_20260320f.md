[P1.1] When animal models fail to predict human responses, the cost of inaction is measured in preventable deaths. Regulators must therefore broaden permission for controlled human infection studies (CHIS) because the alternative—relying on imperfect animals—delays vaccines and treatments that could save millions. The ethical objections are real, but they do not outweigh the moral imperative to reduce suffering when safe, consent‑based protocols exist.
[P1.2] Consider malaria. No animal model reproduces the complex liver‑stage infection that makes vaccine design so difficult. Early CHIS with attenuated sporozoites have already generated data that accelerated candidates now in late‑stage trials, data that would have taken years to obtain from animal work alone. Similar stories exist for cholera, influenza, and COVID‑19, where human challenge trials produced efficacy signals within weeks, informing emergency use authorizations that saved countless lives.
[P1.3] These studies are not reckless experimentation. Participants are healthy adults who give fully informed consent, accept a quantified risk, receive intensive monitoring, and are compensated for any harm. Existing regulatory frameworks for Phase I trials already permit comparable risk‑benefit calculations; CHIS simply apply the same rigor to a setting where the scientific payoff is dramatically higher.
[P1.4] Ethical concerns about exploitation or coercion are mitigated by strict oversight: independent ethics boards, caps on pathogen dose, exclusion of vulnerable populations, and transparent reporting. The principle is not to eliminate risk but to ensure it is proportionate, consensual, and directed toward a clear public‑health benefit.
[P1.5] If regulators keep CHIS narrowly confined, we consign future pandemics to the slow, uncertain path of animal‑only research, costing lives that could have been saved. The serious ethical objections demand safeguards, not a blanket ban. Broadening controlled infection studies is the responsible, compassionate choice.
