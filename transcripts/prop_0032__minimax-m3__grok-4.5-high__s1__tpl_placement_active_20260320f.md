[P1.1] The motion asks a narrow, defensible question: when a private company sells you a probabilistic forecast that could trigger a prophylactic surgery, end a career, or rewrite your family plans, should that company at minimum hand the result to a trained clinician before you see it?
[P1.2] High-impact predictive results are not consumer trivia. A BRCA1 pathogenic variant report, for example, can move a woman from a baseline lifetime breast cancer risk of roughly twelve percent to something closer to seventy percent. That same report, however, also arrives tangled with variants of uncertain significance, ancestry-correlated noise, polygenic scores that shift with every cohort update, and a small but non-trivial chance of outright lab error delivered under a brand the customer trusts by default. The consumer reading it on a phone at ten p.m. is not equipped to sort those layers.
[P1.3] The company knows this. It built its marketing around "discover what your DNA says about you" precisely because the number feels like a verdict when it is, in fact, a probability distribution with footnotes.
[P1.4] Certified genetic counselors exist precisely for this translation step. They are trained to communicate uncertainty, to contextualize penetrance and family history, and to screen for the kind of catastrophic misinterpretations that have, in documented post-test cases, preceded unnecessary mastectomies in women whose variants would later be reclassified as benign, and preceded false reassurance when a variant was missed entirely or filtered by ancestry assumptions.
[P1.5] The proposition does not ban these tests. It does not seize the data. It inserts a single human conversation, funded by the company that profits from the sale, before the consumer acts on the most consequential reading of their own genome. That is a proportionate intervention to protect people from preventable harm caused by information they were never trained to read and that the seller is paid to deliver without context.
