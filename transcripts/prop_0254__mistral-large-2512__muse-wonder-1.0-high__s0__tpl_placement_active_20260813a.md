[C1.1] Making well-being the headline target sounds humane until a government has to choose, fund, and be judged on a policy. Then its vagueness becomes a liability, not a virtue.
[C1.2] Well-being has no agreed unit, no stable definition, and no neutral way to weigh its parts. Is a year of life worth more than higher life satisfaction, less loneliness, or more green space? Different cultures, ages, and political parties answer differently, and self-reported happiness adapts to low expectations — people can report contentment in deprivation while resenting rising prosperity that lifts their aspirations. A prime minister can therefore game the target by picking flattering indicators, reweighting them, or running mood campaigns.
[C1.3] GDP is imperfect, but it is standardized, measured, comparable across decades and countries, and difficult to talk into existence. A headline target must be accountable.
[C1.4] Second, growth is not the enemy of well-being; it is its material precondition. Jobs, wages, tax revenue, and productive capacity pay for hospitals, schools, pensions, mental-health services, and cleaner technology. Recessions are what devastate well-being: unemployment scars mental health, businesses close, public budgets shrink, and governments are forced into the cuts critics blame on growth. A country that downgrades growth in favor of a well-being scoreboard still has to finance every item on that scoreboard — usually with the proceeds of growth it has deprioritized.
[C1.5] Finally, GDP provides a common, value-neutral language for trade-offs. A well-being index smuggles in contested moral choices under a scientific label: how much liberty to trade for longevity, housing for landscape, present consumption for future climate. Those choices belong in democratic debate, not buried in an index designed by officials. When everything from trust to sleep becomes the target, no minister is responsible for anything.
[C1.6] GDP should be supplemented by health, poverty, and environmental dashboards. But replacing a hard fiscal anchor with a soft, manipulable mood metric would make policy less honest, less fundable, and less democratic.

When the UK’s GDP grew in the 2010s but life satisfaction fell, the well-being dashboard would have exposed the failure; GDP alone masked it.

At least well-being metrics force transparency about trade-offs, rather than pretending a rising number is always good.
[P2.4] Finally, their plea to keep "democratic debate" out of the target is a red herring. No one proposes replacing elections with an algorithm. GDP’s supposed neutrality is its greatest flaw—it treats a dollar spent on insulin the same as a dollar spent on a luxury yacht.
