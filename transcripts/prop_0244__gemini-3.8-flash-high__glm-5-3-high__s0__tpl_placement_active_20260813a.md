[P1.1] A decade ago, body-worn cameras were heralded as a transformative silver bullet for police accountability. In the wake of nationwide protests over police violence, advocates and policymakers promised that strapped-on lenses would deter excessive force, eliminate evidentiary disputes, and pierce the veil of departmental secrecy. The expectation was nothing short of a systemic overhaul: sunlight acting as the ultimate disinfectant.
[P1.2] The reality has fallen dramatically short of that promise.
[P1.3] First, the behavioral deterrent failed to materialize. Rigorous, large-scale field evaluations in major metropolitan departments have consistently revealed that body cameras produce negligible, often statistically indistinguishable changes in officers' use of force. Ingrained street-level culture and split-second institutional habits did not magically yield to the mere presence of a lens.
[P1.4] Second, accountability was subverted by institutional capture. A tool meant to oversee the police was placed entirely under the control of the police. Officers retain functional discretion over when to activate or mute devices, and footage frequently goes missing behind convenient technical errors. More critically, police departments maintain exclusive custody of the data.
[P1.5] They routinely withhold, heavily redact, or selectively release footage to protect their own narratives, while forcing victims of abuse into protracted public records battles just to view the tape. What was designed as a civilian oversight mechanism quickly morphed into an instrument of departmental public relations.
[P1.6] Finally, video evidence did not dismantle legal impunity. Advocates assumed that capturing misconduct on tape would guarantee legal consequences. Yet the judicial system filters video through doctrines like qualified immunity and the deeply deferential "reasonable officer" standard. Video has rarely translated into indictments or convictions because the barrier to accountability was never merely evidentiary; it was structural.
[P1.7] Advocates expected body cameras to fundamentally rebalance power between the state and the public. Instead, police departments absorbed the technology, co-opted its deployment, and left the architecture of impunity intact.

[C1.5] Third, on deterrence: even the studies PRO leans on tell a messier story. Complaints against officers fell measurably in several deployments, and citizens' complaint processes accelerated everywhere. A tool that changes outcomes after an incident was never going to rewrite split-second street behavior—and advocates never promised it would.
[C1.6] Finally, the structural argument proves too much. Qualified immunity and deferential standards predate cameras by decades and filter every accountability mechanism—civilian review, journalists, lawsuits. Blaming the camera for the doctrine is blaming the witness for the verdict.
[C1.7] Cameras didn't rebuild the architecture; they installed the windows. Before them, we were arguing in the dark.
