[P1.1] Schools should make students accountable for what they learn and submit, not pretend that banning a readily available writing tool makes that accountability easier. The right default is permission with disclosure: identify the AI used, cite its contribution, and show how the student checked, revised, or rejected its output.
[P1.2] Permission is not automatic credit. A student who submits generated claims they cannot explain has not demonstrated understanding, however neatly they cite the tool. Teachers should assess reasoning, revisions, and command of the material, using brief explanations or periodic in-class checks where appropriate. They can also require unaided work when the learning objective demands it—for example, practicing foundational writing or testing independent algebra skills.
[P1.3] The presumption favors permitted use; restrictions need an educational purpose, not a blanket suspicion of technology.
[P1.4] That policy improves academic integrity because it gives students a legitimate route to honesty. Under a broad ban, students who use AI have a reason to conceal precisely the information teachers need: what assistance they received and where their own understanding begins. Disclosure does not eliminate deception, but prohibition does not either. Both systems must verify learning.
[P1.5] Only one makes ordinary, truthful disclosure compatible with completing the assignment.
[P1.6] It also turns AI’s weaknesses into teachable material. Consider an essay assignment: a student asks AI for a counterargument, discovers that it misrepresents the text, and explains the correction using passages from the reading. That is substantive intellectual work. A broad ban excludes that exercise merely because the assistance came from AI.
[P1.7] Transparent permission lets the teacher distinguish critical engagement from unthinking outsourcing.
[P1.8] The decisive question is not whether AI can undermine learning; it can. It is which default better addresses that danger. Broad bans regulate access to a tool while leaving understanding uncertain. Permission with citation and process transparency puts the focus where schools need it: on what students contributed, what they can defend, and what they actually learned.

[C1.1] “Permissible by default” concedes too much. It makes availability, not educational purpose, the starting point. Schools grade evidence of a student’s own reading, reasoning, practice, and judgment, not mere text. A no-AI default with explicit exceptions protects that evidence; a permissive default asks teachers to reconstruct it from disclaimers and logs.
[C1.2] PRO says disclosure shifts attention to “what students can defend” [P1.8]. But disclosure is itself a text AI can help write. “I used it for counterarguments, found an error, revised my thesis” is easy to produce without understanding the counterargument. A chat log shows prompts, not cognition: who noticed the mistake, chose the revision, or can reproduce the reasoning tomorrow. Citation proves an instrument was present, not whose mind did the work.
[C1.3] Generative AI is not spellcheck at the margin. It can supply the thesis, structure, examples, objections, transitions, and post-hoc explanation. So it can replace nearly every observable stage teachers use to infer learning. When both the paragraph and the reflection about it may be AI-assisted, process transparency is another artifact to be gamed, not an audit.
[C1.4] Broad bans are not “blanket suspicion” [P1.3]. Schools ban translation apps on vocabulary tests, answer keys during practice, and calculators while teaching arithmetic facts because valid assessment needs controlled conditions. Those limits are not accusations; they are design. A student who skips paragraph practice has lost something even if the cited AI output is polished.
[C1.5] PRO’s honesty claim [P1.4] confuses disclosure with enforceability. Under a ban, concealed use violates a clear line. Under permissive default, disclosed use gets legitimacy, and the teacher must prove the student’s contribution was inadequate across fluent papers—a crushing classroom burden. Explicit teacher-designed exceptions remain possible.
[C1.6] The default should be: no generative AI unless permission is explicit. Citation does not make learning transparent; it makes outsourcing easier to normalize.
