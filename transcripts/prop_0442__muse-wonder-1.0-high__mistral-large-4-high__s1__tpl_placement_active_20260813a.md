[P1.1] International shipping is a climate orphan, and that is exactly why it needs a binding rule. Ships carry the majority of world trade by volume, burn some of the dirtiest fuel, and operate outside any country's carbon budget. A vessel can be owned in one state, flagged in another, crewed from a third, and emit in international waters. When responsibility is that diffuse, voluntary pledges and national encouragement will always arrive too late.
[P1.2] A binding IMO timetable for low- or zero-carbon fuels breaks the deadlock that has stalled decarbonisation. Shipowners will not order green methanol or ammonia-capable vessels without guaranteed fuel supply and a level playing field. Fuel producers will not build production, bunkering, and safety systems without guaranteed demand. Each side waits for the other, while conventional ships ordered today lock in emissions for decades.
[P1.3] A phase-in date, rising over time, turns a gamble into an investment case and stops responsible operators being undercut by competitors who keep burning cheap, polluting oil.
[P1.4] The objection is cost, but cost is already being paid — not by polluters. It is paid in coastal flooding, port disruption, crop losses, and health damage from air pollution around shipping routes. Freight is also a small share of the final price of most goods: even a substantial rise in shipping costs translates into cents, not dollars, on a pair of shoes or an appliance. A gradual, predictable timetable spreads adjustment, allows fleet renewal to align with replacement cycles, and is cheaper than a late transition or the climate damage of doing nothing.
[P1.5] Only the IMO can set one standard for a global industry and prevent a patchwork of loopholes and carbon leakage. Revenues or flexibility mechanisms within that system can support small island and developing states, fund fuel infrastructure, and protect exposed routes. Waiting for perfect fuels or perfect fairness means entrenching the dirtiest status quo.

[C1.1] A binding fuel mandate is a prescription written before the medicine exists. Green methanol, ammonia, and hydrogen remain boutique fuels: global production is a rounding error against the roughly 300 million tonnes of bunker fuel ships burn annually. There is no bunkering infrastructure at most ports, no finalized safety codes for ammonia's extreme toxicity, and no engine supply chain capable of retrofitting the 60,000-vessel global fleet on any timetable the IMO could credibly set. Mandating a fuel that cannot be supplied at scale is not decarbonisation; it is scarcity by regulatory fiat, and scarcity in shipping means empty shelves.
[C1.2] The cost burden the proposition waves away falls hardest on those least able to bear it. Yes, a container of sneakers can absorb higher freight rates. But the vessels that matter most for human welfare carry grain, fertilizer, and fuel to import-dependent nations. When freight rates spike on Capesize bulk carriers, wheat prices follow within weeks in Cairo, Dhaka, and Lagos.
[C1.3] A binding timetable does not primarily raise the price of consumer electronics; it raises the price of bread in countries that contributed least to historical emissions and can least afford the transition premium.
[C1.4] Nor can the IMO enforce such a rule. The industry operates through flags of convenience, layered shell ownership, and opaque beneficial-ownership structures designed to evade oversight. A non-compliant operator can reflag, reroute, or delay while competitors absorb the cost. The IMO's own record shows systematic under-enforcement of existing sulphur caps and safety regulations.
[C1.5] A binding fuel mandate would not create a level playing field; it would accelerate regulatory arbitrage and push tonnage toward the least scrupulous registries.
[C1.6] The alternative is not inaction. It is massive investment in fuel production, port infrastructure, and engine R&D without locking the industry into specific unproven technologies on a political timetable. Carbon pricing and innovation funding move capital faster than mandates that outpace physics. The proposition asks the global poor to subsidise a technological gamble enforced by an institution that cannot police its own existing rules.

[P4.1] A1: Neither. A binding timetable is phased — rising low- or zero-carbon shares, pooling, banking and penalties — not a single morning when 60,000 ships must be green or stranded. If a port genuinely lacks fuel, a narrow, time-limited availability exemption lets a vessel sail while paying into compliance and booking future abatement; the obligation survives, so the investment signal does not collapse. Priced, expiring relief is not nullification; it is how phased standards avoid cliff edges.
[P4.2] A2: In the interim, operators bear the compliance cost first and pass only part through, spread across cargo. Protection for essential bulk need not wait for revenue to accumulate: transitional rebates for grain and fertilizer routes to highly import-dependent states can be committed at adoption, financed by non-compliance penalties, advance transition funds and the phase-in itself. Because shares rise gradually, there is no immediate spike to absorb — unlike the drought, flooding and heat damage already raising food costs without any compensation at all.
[P4.3] Those answers reveal why the alternative fails its own tests. If supply scales only when buyers must appear, investment without a mandate leaves producers waiting; a carbon price that can simply be paid leaves the same chicken-and-egg deadlock, and the same flag-hopping predicted for a mandate. The choice is not mandate versus cost-free transition. It is a phased duty that builds fuel, engines and bunkering together, versus a price that raises freight while still hoping someone builds them.
[P4.4] The sulphur point cuts the same way: compliant fuel reached scale because regulators signalled the switch years ahead and ports could verify what was burned. Concentrated bunkering hubs and port-state access make fuel use checkable where it matters — at delivery into major markets. A two-tier fleet cannot win by choosing weaker ports if its cargo must reach buyers in enforcing ones. Even accepting higher freight, a binding, phased timetable is the only credible route from boutique fuels to a working global system.

Moreover, pricing does not create the scarcity premium that makes black markets and regulatory arbitrage profitable; it applies uniformly at major ports, closing the evasion gap without creating physical shortages or a two-tier fleet.
