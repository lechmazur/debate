[P1.1] A car is not a streaming account. When you buy it, you own the physical machine, the right to use it, maintain it, and sell it. Software has not erased that bargain—it has only given manufacturers a new way to quietly rewrite it. The proposition draws one narrow line: when a connectivity subscription lapses, the car must remain repairable.
[P1.2] Automakers can still charge for live traffic, remote start, concierge data, and cloud features. They just cannot turn a lapsed data plan into a repair lock.
[P1.3] That distinction matters because repair is where ownership becomes real. If a module fails and the replacement must be paired through a manufacturer server, if diagnostics are gated behind a subscription, if an independent shop cannot calibrate a battery or reset a system without a paid account, then the owner's use of the car depends on permission from the seller. That is not a service model. It is a ransom note attached to the title.
[P1.4] The harm falls hardest on ordinary people: used-car buyers, rural drivers, small shops, and anyone keeping a vehicle past warranty. A subscription can expire because of a budget crunch, a sold car, or a dead cellular network. None of those events should disable basic repair. And this rule is not anti-innovation.
[P1.5] Automakers can secure systems locally, provide authenticated repair access, and keep selling genuine connected services. They simply may not make repairability conditional on a recurring payment.
[P1.6] Opponents will call this interference with contract and design. But the law already sets ownership defaults: you cannot sell a locked product and call the key a subscription. If a manufacturer can degrade repair capability after sale, the customer never truly bought the car—they bought a license that can be narrowed remotely. The requirement is modest, targeted, and pro-ownership.
[P1.7] Subscriptions may expire; ownership should not.

The proposition demands something else: that manufacturers keep “core repair functionality” alive indefinitely, even after the subscription—and sometimes the network—is gone.
[C1.2] That sounds narrow until you ask what “core” includes. Module pairing, immobilizer programming, battery calibration, driver-assistance alignment, diagnostic clearance: each can be branded repair. Once statute, not engineering, draws the line, every security check looks like a lock and every sunset looks like a lawsuit.
[C1.3] Ownership of the machine has never meant a perpetual claim on someone else’s servers, keys, or liability. You can own a house without owning the utility grid, and a phone without a lifetime right to the carrier’s provisioning system. Authenticated repair exists because vehicles are theft targets and safety systems. A module that pairs with no manufacturer check is convenient for a shop and convenient for a thief.
[C1.4] Forcing that pathway to outlive a canceled plan does not restore a bargain. It rewrites the price into free lifetime infrastructure.
[C1.5] Those costs land on every buyer, including people who never need an independent flash. Secure offline fallbacks and tool support across fifteen or twenty years must be designed in and paid for up front. Firms answer vague mandates with higher prices, fewer software features, or repair channels so cramped that compliance is theater. Safety rules still expect traceable calibration.
[C1.6] Strip the control that makes traceability possible, and the public keeps the risk while the slogan keeps the victory.
[C1.7] Warranty and competition law already punish a car sold and then disabled. They do not require government to freeze one service model and call it ownership. A subscription can end without handing every future owner a permanent key to the manufacturer’s vault.
