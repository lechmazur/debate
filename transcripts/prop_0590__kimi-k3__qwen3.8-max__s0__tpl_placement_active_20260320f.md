The rejected candidate in Milan suffers the identical harm either way. [P1.3] That points to the fatal flaw in headquarters-based rules: they are an engraved invitation to arbitrage. The EU need not govern foreign soil; it need only govern its own marketplace, which it unquestionably may.
[P1.6] Finally, the fairness point. HQ-based rules would burden European firms with compliance while foreign competitors selling identical systems to the same Europeans skate free—a subsidy to regulatory havens and a penalty on European industry.

[C1.1] The proposition confuses where harm is felt with who can prevent it. High-risk AI obligations—risk management, conformity assessment, technical documentation, human oversight—must attach to the actor that designs and controls the system. That is the provider, normally anchored by headquarters or establishment, not an infinite set of places where downstream effects ripple.
[C1.2] Impact-based jurisdiction is unworkable. A speech model, hiring tool, or diagnostic aid can produce outputs across borders instantly, often after being adapted by deployers. If liability follows impacts, a provider in Amsterdam could face high-risk duties in dozens of countries because a customer, integrator, or user chose a regulated application. The provider cannot redesign for every jurisdiction’s interpretation of “impact,” nor can it know which outputs will matter where.
[C1.3] The result is not protection; it is paralysis: overbroad compliance, withdrawal from markets, or design stagnation.
[C1.4] The motion also undermines enforcement. Regulators need one accountable economic operator, a technical file, an authorized representative, and a clear conformity procedure. Impact triggers scatter responsibility among every place an output is felt, inviting duplicative audits, conflicting orders, and forum shopping. The EU’s strength is coordinated market supervision, not a global tort-style free-for-all.
[C1.5] The toy analogy defeats the proposition. Cars and toys are regulated when placed on a market, through importers, distributors, and sellers—not because a regulator chases every injury location. The same logic works for AI: if a foreign provider wants EU customers, it must comply before placing the system on the EU market. That condition can be enforced through importers, app stores, procurement rules, and authorized representatives.
[C1.6] Headquarters matters not as a magic shield, but as the identifiable point of control.
[C1.7] Finally, impact jurisdiction boomerangs. If the EU claims authority because impacts occur here, other states will do the same to European providers whose systems affect their citizens. The safest rule is simple: regulate the provider who places the system into commerce, and bar noncompliant systems from the market.
