[C1.1] Legal deposit worked for books because a book is finished. You hand over a bound object, and five centuries later it still does everything it ever did. Software is not an object; it's a running relationship between code, an operating system, a chip, a server, and a dozen licences. Mail the disc to a national archive and you have preserved a coffin, not a game.
[C1.2] That is the fatal gap in the motion. It mandates delivery, which is the cheap and easy part, while leaving untouched the expensive part: emulation, migration, documentation, reverse-engineering, and rights clearance. Unfunded deposit mandates produce dark warehouses of bits nobody can boot. We have already seen this with web archiving — collection outran the capacity to make anything usable.
[C1.3] Second, publishers frequently cannot legally deposit what you're asking for. A modern game is licensed music, licensed middleware, licensed fonts, third-party engine code, contracted voice performances. A studio has the right to ship that bundle under specific terms; it does not have the right to hand a DRM-stripped master to a state institution in perpetuity. Mandating it doesn't dissolve those contracts — it just makes compliance impossible and criminalises the studio for it.
[C1.4] Third, "culturally significant" is doing enormous silent work. Significance is visible only in hindsight; nobody in 1993 nominated *Doom*'s shareware as heritage. So the mandate either sweeps in everything — millions of apps, hobbyist itch.io releases, one-person art projects, each now facing legal duties and penalties — or it appoints officials to decide in advance which digital art matters. State pre-selection of art is a strange way to fight amnesia.
[C1.5] And notice what actually blocks preservation today: my opponent named it. Archivists "cannot legally break copy protection." That's a copyright problem, solved by exceptions and safe harbours — permission plus funding, not compulsion. Give libraries the legal right to crack, copy and emulate, and fund them to do it, and you get preservation without the contract collisions, the arbitrary significance tests, and the chilling effect on the smallest creators.
