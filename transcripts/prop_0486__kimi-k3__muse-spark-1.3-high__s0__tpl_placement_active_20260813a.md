[P1.1] A permanent burial plot is a promise a land-scarce city cannot honestly keep: one person, roughly ten square feet, forever. Multiply that by every death, every year, in a city where a parking-space-sized lot sells for a fortune, and the policy stops looking like dignity and starts looking like arithmetic failure.
[P1.2] Cities are already hitting the wall. London has had to reuse "full" cemeteries by deepening graves. Singapore, Greece, and much of Germany simply lease plots for a fixed term and then reclaim them. New York's major cemeteries are effectively closed to new buyers.
[P1.3] These are not edge cases — they are the future of every dense city, because deaths recur and land does not.
[P1.4] Meanwhile, the alternative exists and works. Natural organic reduction transforms a body into roughly a cubic yard of usable soil in a matter of weeks and has been legalized in a growing list of U.S. states, starting with Washington. Alkaline hydrolysis is already used by medical schools and funeral homes; it returns sterile remains to the family just as cremation does, while using far less energy, with none of flame cremation's emissions, no mercury from dental work, no embalming formaldehyde leaching toward groundwater, and no concrete vaults buried by the acre.
[P1.5] The opposition will talk about dignity. But dignity attaches to ritual, memory, and the family's wishes — not to a land deed. Families still receive remains: soil to plant a tree in, or remains for an urn. Public cemeteries can become memorial groves instead of fenced-off stone lots that hold prime land in perpetuity while the living are priced out of housing nearby.
[P1.6] Cremation itself was taboo a century ago; norms follow practice, and practice follows necessity.
[P1.7] The weighing question is simple: does one generation's preference for permanent plots outweigh every future generation's need for land, housing, and clean water? Public land policy must serve the living. Composting and alkaline hydrolysis honor the dead without mortgaging the city to do it.

[C1.1] Public cemeteries are not housing lots that happen to contain bodies. They are a promise that the city will bury its own, equally, without making grief pass an ideological test.
[C1.2] That arithmetic treats that promise as waste. One person, ten square feet, forever. But forever was never literal; cities have always managed burial space with depth, family plots, re-use after long rest, modest markers, and better design. Land scarcity demands smarter burial, not compulsory disappearance of the body.
[C1.3] Because composting and alkaline hydrolysis are not just different paperwork for the same goodbye. One reduces a mother or son to soil in a warehouse vessel in weeks. The other dissolves the body in heated alkali and flushes the liquid down the drain, keeping only bone powder. Call it sterile if you like, but to millions of people that is not ritual evolution, it is desecration.
[C1.4] That matters in a public cemetery, which must serve Catholics who require bodily burial anticipating resurrection, Jews and Muslims who require intact, prompt burial as sacred obligation, and countless secular families who simply need a place to visit. A transition that ends permanent plots tells those citizens their deepest duties are now unsustainable and unwelcome in public ground. Private alternatives will remain for the rich; the poor get told to take the urn and plant a tree.
[C1.5] Nor is the green case decisive. Green burial without vault or embalming already protects groundwater and uses no industrial plant. Hydrolysis requires purpose-built machines, large volumes of water and chemicals, and wastewater handling. Composting requires facilities, energy, trucking, and monitoring.
[C1.6] Putting that industry inside land-scarce cities does not free land, it industrializes mourning.
[C1.7] Offer these methods as options. Do not use land pressure to abolish choice. A city that cannot make room to lay its dead to rest intact has not solved scarcity; it has surrendered humanity to it.
