[P1.1] Social media platforms are not ordinary private forums; they are the gatekeepers of contemporary civic and economic life. A ban can silence a journalist, cut off a small business from customers, or erase someone’s community. When a private institution exercises that kind of power over speech and livelihood, the minimum safeguard against error and favoritism is procedure: tell the user what rule they allegedly broke, give the evidence or at least the specific conduct, and provide a real appeal before the ban becomes final.
[P1.2] Opponents will frame this as courts micromanaging content moderation. It is the opposite. Due process is content-neutral. It does not require platforms to host anyone, nor dictate what speech is allowed.
[P1.3] It requires only that when a platform exercises its own power to exclude, it does so transparently and subject to review. That distinction is the heart of the case.
[P1.4] Without it, mistakes are inevitable and unreviewable. Automated systems and overburdened moderators routinely confuse satire, journalism, organizing, and abuse; users often receive a generic violation notice or none at all. An appeal to the same opaque system is not a remedy. Notice and reasons create a record; independent review within the platform creates a chance to correct bias, mistaken identity, and context-blind enforcement.
[P1.5] That protects users and platforms alike.
[P1.6] Nor is this an impossible burden. Platforms already track moderation decisions, employ review staff, and write internal policies. Requiring them to surface a reason and offer an appeal is an operational change, not a technological miracle. The question is whether immense private power over speech and association should be exercised without answer.
[P1.7] On balance, it should not. A legal floor of notice, reasons, and appeal is the modest, workable response.
