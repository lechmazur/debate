[P1.1] Every city that markets itself as vibrant is trading on something it did not build and rarely protects: the small rooms where music happens. When zoning boards, licensing offices, and police departments make decisions that can kill a 200-capacity venue, the motion asks only that they weigh what they're destroying the way they'd weigh a library or a transit stop — as infrastructure, not as a nuisance with a liquor license.
[P1.2] Three reasons this framing is right.
[P1.3] First, market failure. Small venues generate enormous value they cannot capture. They incubate the artists who fill arenas later; they anchor nightlife districts that raise surrounding property values and restaurant revenue. Then those same rising rents and new condo neighbors — drawn by the vibrancy the venue created — file the noise complaints that shut it down.
[P1.4] A business punished by the prosperity it produces is the textbook case for public-interest treatment. The "agent of change" principle, adopted in cities like London, fixes this: the newcomer developer, not the long-standing venue, bears soundproofing costs.
[P1.5] Second, irreversibility. Infrastructure logic exists for assets that are cheap to preserve and nearly impossible to rebuild. A venue is a web of relationships, reputation, and physical acoustics accumulated over decades. When it converts to condos, no market signal brings it back.
[P1.6] Austin, Nashville, and Berlin learned this the hard way as venue closures hollowed out the very identity their tourism boards sell.
[P1.7] Third, the motion is modest. It doesn't demand subsidies for every bar with an amp, or immunity from safety enforcement. It demands that when a survival-threatening decision is on the table, cultural value enters the calculus — a hearing, a mitigation-first approach, a presumption toward preservation where feasible. Cities already do this for historic buildings and stadiums.
[P1.8] The question, then: should decisions that permanently destroy cultural capacity be made blind to that fact? The status quo says yes. That is the position my opponent must defend.
