[P1.1] Border enforcement fights economic gravity; expanding legal pathways channels it. Most irregular migration is a predictable market collision: aging economies have relentless demand for labor in agriculture, construction, care work, and hospitality, while offering virtually no legal visas for workers without university degrees. When the law forbids a mutually beneficial transaction between a willing employer and a desperate worker, it does not stop the transaction—it moves it underground.
[P1.2] Expanding seasonal and sectoral pathways reduces irregular migration through direct substitution. Consider a migrant worker’s calculus. Crossing irregularly means paying thousands to criminal smugglers, risking death in transit, and enduring exploitation in the shadow economy. If that same worker can pay a modest fee, cross at an official port of entry, earn legal wages, and return home safely after the harvest or building season, the smuggling business model collapses.
[P1.3] Legal pathways also make compliance self-enforcing: when returning home on time guarantees next year’s visa, workers have every incentive to follow the rules. Historically, when destination countries have opened realistic guest-worker visas, irregular crossings from partner nations have dropped sharply because legality is cheaper and safer than smuggling.
[P1.4] Conversely, ramping up border enforcement without legal valves fails and backfires. Walls and patrols do not erase a massive wage gap or harvest rotting crops. They merely raise the price of illicit entry, enriching sophisticated cartels that adapt with tunnels, bribes, and deadlier routes. Worse, heavy enforcement creates a perverse lock-in effect.
[P1.5] Workers who would prefer to migrate seasonally—working for six months and returning home—realize that if they leave, a militarized border might stop them from coming back next season. So they stay permanently and eventually pay smugglers to bring their families too. Enforcement turns temporary circular labor into permanent irregular residency.
[P1.6] You cannot police away a structural labor shortage. Channeling workers into regulated pathways drains the black market and restores order where enforcement alone only breeds evasion.

[C1.1] The proposition is comparative: not whether legal migration is desirable, but whether seasonal and sectoral expansion reduces irregular migration more than stronger border enforcement. PRO assumes one legal visa cancels one irregular journey. That assumption fails at scale.
[C1.2] Places are capped, eligibility restricted, and employers select participants. A program may recruit people who would not otherwise have migrated, while those excluded by nationality, quota, fees, documents, or lack of sponsorship retain the same incentive to use smugglers. More lawful arrivals therefore do not prove fewer unlawful ones. Nor can temporary labor visas channel people driven primarily by family reunion, safety, or permanent settlement.
[C1.3] Even among participants, legality is not self-enforcing. A repeat visa matters only if another season is worth more than remaining for higher cumulative earnings, changing employers, or joining family. Programs can displace risk from illegal entry to overstay rather than shrink the irregular population. The compliant circular worker in PRO’s example is plausible, but cannot stand in for the entire flow.
[C1.4] Border enforcement changes a broader calculation. Patrols, surveillance, port processing, anti-smuggling operations, and prompt return after unlawful entry reduce the chance that paying a smuggler delivers durable access. Smugglers sell expected success, not transport alone. PRO narrows enforcement to walls, then treats adaptation as failure.
[C1.5] Yet costlier routes, failed attempts, and disrupted networks can deter attempts and prevent successful entries. Enforcement need not abolish labor demand to outperform a scheme reaching only a small, preapproved slice of migrants.
[C1.6] Indeed, legal pathways presuppose a credible boundary. If bypassing them is easy, migrants and employers can still choose the cheaper unlawful option, and return rules lose force. Expanded pathways can be a valuable complement where genuine seasonal shortages exist. But complementarity is not comparative superiority.
[C1.7] PRO offers selective diversion and sometimes mere reclassification; the motion requires population-wide reduction. On scale, coverage, and durability, expanded pathways cannot carry that claim.

[C2.1] PRO’s central answer is that a legal worker filling a vacancy makes the “job magnet vanish.” It does not. A farm with twenty legal pickers may not need a twenty-first, but the worker refused a visa can try another farm, another sector, or an employer outside the scheme. Seasonal and sectoral programs cover selected vacancies; irregular migration responds to the wider labor market, as well as family and settlement incentives. Substitution is therefore partial unless legal supply meets demand everywhere—precisely what capped, employer-selected expansion cannot promise.
[C2.2] Employers may prefer legal labor when visas are timely, affordable, and flexible. That does not establish that unlawful hiring exists only because visas are unavailable: cash wages, avoided obligations, and weak oversight still reward cheating. And PRO’s claim that pathways free agents to intercept threats concedes that channels depend on enforcement rather than replace it.
[C2.3] Lock-in likewise supports well-designed circular visas, not their comparative superiority. The promise of next year’s visa disciplines only workers whom employers select again; someone facing family separation or much greater long-term earnings abroad may rationally overstay. Meanwhile, if unlawful entry produces prompt return instead of durable work, PRO’s debt argument reverses: repeated failure makes smuggling a worse investment. Higher costs need not stop everyone to outperform a program reaching only eligible workers.
[C2.4] Finally, rising enforcement budgets alone prove neither failure nor the counterfactual of what would have happened without them. PRO treats smuggler adaptation as proof that deterrence cannot work, yet treats caps, exclusion, and overstay incentives as mere design details. That is asymmetric.
[C2.5] The strongest PRO case is diversion among approved seasonal workers. Enforcement addresses those bypassing quotas, uncovered workplaces, overstays, and people whose migration is not seasonal labor at all. It changes expected success across that wider field; expanded pathways change options for a selected subset. On the motion’s actual comparison—population-wide reduction—the broader mechanism beats the narrower valve.
