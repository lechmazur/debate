[P1.2] Start with speed. Electric buses run on existing roads; a fleet can be procured and operating within two to three years. Santiago already runs roughly two thousand electric buses; Bogotá more than a thousand. Rail tells the opposite story: Bogotá's metro has been debated for generations and still hasn't carried a passenger; Lima's Line 2 has been under construction for a decade.
[P1.3] When the question is what cuts emissions this decade, only one option actually exists this decade.
[P1.4] Then cost. Metros in the region routinely cost on the order of a hundred million dollars per kilometer or more—an entire climate budget sunk into one corridor. [P1.5] Then coverage. Rail serves one corridor; buses serve the whole city. The poorest commuters already ride buses along long peripheral routes no rail line will reach for decades. Electrifying those routes pulls diesel particulates out of the neighborhoods with the worst air immediately—not after ten years of construction dust.
[P1.6] And the carbon math fits: Latin American grids are comparatively clean, hydro-heavy in Brazil and Colombia, so each electric kilometer displaces diesel almost one-for-one. Rail front-loads enormous embodied emissions in concrete and tunneling, repaid only if ridership materializes.
[P1.7] CON will invoke rail's capacity and permanence. Fine—as a long-term complement. But "prioritize" means choosing where the next decade of money and political capital goes.
