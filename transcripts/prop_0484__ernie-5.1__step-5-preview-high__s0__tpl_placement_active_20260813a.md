[P1.1] The climate crisis is not a uniform threat; it is an existential one for a handful of nations that will vanish if global warming continues unchecked. Small island developing states—Tuvalu, Kiribati, the Maldives—face the prospect of total inundation within decades, loss of sovereign territory, and cultural annihilation. Yet under the current “one‑state‑one‑vote” or population‑GDP weighting, these nations wield a fraction of the influence of a single large emitter. That mismatch is both morally indefensible and strategically self‑defeating.
[P1.2] Weighted voting proportional to existential risk corrects the core injustice: those whose survival hangs on the outcome of negotiations receive commensurate voice. Risk can be quantified through projected sea‑level rise, exposure to cyclones, and adaptive capacity—metrics already embedded in climate‑finance frameworks such as loss‑and‑damage assessments. By translating those metrics into voting weight, we align decision‑making power with stakes, ensuring that the most vulnerable are not merely consulted but can shape binding commitments.
[P1.3] The payoff is concrete. When nations that risk disappearance sit at the table with amplified weight, the political calculus shifts toward the ambitious mitigation pathways required to keep warming below 1.5 °C. History shows that marginal voices are ignored; the 2015 Paris Agreement’s “common but differentiated responsibilities” clause was watered down precisely because those most affected lacked leverage. A risk‑weighted system would have forced stronger language on emission cuts and financing, because the parties most likely to disappear could veto insufficient ambition.
[P1.4] Critics will warn of manipulation or inequity, but risk is an observable, science‑based variable, not a subjective claim. Moreover, weighting does not erase other perspectives—it rebalances a system that presently privileges economic might over survival. The principle is simple: in a negotiation about the planet’s future, those whose future is on the line must have a voice that matches the danger they face. That is the moral and pragmatic foundation for risk‑proportional voting.

[C1.1] Weighted voting sounds like justice; in practice it would make climate governance less legitimate and less effective. The proposition asks us to treat existential risk as a measurable constitutional currency. It is not. Sea-level exposure and cyclone frequency are projections, but existential risk also depends on adaptation, engineering, migration, law, and political choices that states can change.
[C1.2] A country can be “existentially threatened” under one scenario and resilient under another. The claim that risk is “observable, science-based” [P1.4] confuses a hazard indicator with a stable voting qualification.
[C1.3] Worse, the motion divorces voice from the actual object of negotiation. International climate talks are not a referendum on suffering; they allocate mitigation duties, finance, technology, and sovereignty costs. Power should track responsibility and capability, not fear alone. A Tuvaluan veto over India’s or Brazil’s development pathway because Tuvalu faces inundation is not fair burden-sharing; it is single-issue hostage-taking of the majority by the most sympathetic minority.
[C1.4] Conversely, large poor countries with tens of millions exposed to lethal heat, drought, and displacement would lose voice precisely because their states are unlikely to disappear.
[C1.5] There is also an institutional precedent problem. Once “existential risk” creates super-votes, every state will claim it. Nuclear-armed states will say deterrence failure is existential; delta states will cite disappearance; food importers will cite collapse of supply. Weighting by risk invites permanent contest over who is most doomed, converting negotiations into a victimhood auction.
[C1.6] SIDS deserve influence—and already have outsized moral authority because their cause is just. Small-island coalitions have kept 1.5 °C on the agenda despite tiny populations and GDPs. But legitimacy comes from rules all parties can accept. Differentiated responsibility based on emissions, capacity, and vulnerability is harder; it is also the only basis that can bind the emitters whose decisions determine whether island nations survive.
[C1.7] Votes do not build sea walls; binding obligations and delivered finance do.
