[P1.1] For decades, employers have engaged in "degree inflation"—slapping bachelor’s degree requirements on job descriptions for roles that do not functionally require them. Using a diploma as a default filter is economically irrational and socially destructive. Employers must abandon this practice because it creates artificial talent shortages, blocks social mobility, and relies on an inaccurate metric for human potential.
[P1.2] First, unnecessary degree requirements artificially choke the talent pool. When a company demands a four-year degree for an IT support specialist, an administrative assistant, or a sales representative, they are not selecting for necessary job skills. They are arbitrarily restricting their applicant pool. This forces employers to leave positions open longer and pay a credential premium that has no bearing on actual productivity or success in that specific role.
[P1.3] Second, this practice erects a "paper ceiling" that severely damages economic equity. Higher education is prohibitively expensive. By making a degree a baseline requirement for middle-class jobs, employers filter candidates by socioeconomic status and privilege rather than competence. This systematically excludes millions of highly capable workers—including veterans, rural populations, and lower-income individuals who built their skills through apprenticeships, bootcamps, or direct experience.
[P1.4] It locks them out of upward mobility and perpetuates generational inequality.
[P1.5] Third, the standard defense of degree inflation—that a diploma proves "trainability" or basic reliability—is entirely outdated. A degree is a lazy, crude proxy for these traits. Employers now have access to direct, skills-based hiring methods, rigorous industry certifications, and targeted aptitude assessments. These modern tools measure exactly what a candidate can do today, rather than how much time and money they spent in a university setting years ago.
[P1.6] A job requirement should reflect the actual demands of the job. By shifting from pedigree-based to skills-based hiring, employers can unlock massive untapped talent pools, improve organizational efficiency, and restore a fundamental pillar of economic fairness.

[C1.1] The motion sounds obvious until you notice its standard. "Genuinely necessary for performance" is a test that no hiring requirement passes. Three years of experience isn't genuinely necessary—someone brilliant could do the job with one. A clean interview isn't necessary.
[C1.2] A reference check isn't necessary. Employers don't hire against necessity; they hire under uncertainty, using imperfect signals to predict who will show up, finish things, and learn. Judged by "necessity," every filter fails. Judged by predictive value per dollar, a completed degree does remarkably well.
[C1.3] Consider what a diploma actually certifies: four years of deadline-driven, largely unsupervised work, sustained through boredom, setbacks, and competing temptations, verified by an institution with no stake in the hire. That is not a "lazy proxy" for conscientiousness. It is one of the cheapest, hardest-to-fake measures of it available. PRO's alternatives—aptitude tests, bootcamps, portfolios—are gameable, costly to build and validate, and legally hazardous, since an unvalidated test that produces disparate outcomes invites exactly the litigation that pushed employers toward degrees in the first place.
[C1.4] And when firms drop the degree line, what replaces it in practice is not rigorous skills assessment. It is referrals, networks, and gut-feel interviews—the most privilege-laden filters in existence. This is where PRO's equity argument inverts. A degree is the one credential a first-generation student from a rural county can earn at a public university with grants and loans and have recognized by any employer in America, without knowing anyone.
[C1.5] Skills-based hiring rewards people who know which bootcamp matters, which portfolio impresses, and which insider will vouch for them. Removing the public ladder while the private ladders stay in place does not open doors; it hands them to the connected.
[C1.6] Finally, employers bear the cost of a bad hire. If degree requirements truly starved firms of talent, competitors ignoring them would already be eating their lunch. That this hasn't happened is evidence the signal earns its keep. The right project is building credible alternative credentials—not commanding employers to discard one that works.
