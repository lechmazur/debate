[P1.1] Major policy disasters rarely happen because nobody saw the truth; they happen because the institutions charged with forecasting punish candor and reward conformity. When governments rely mainly on internal forecasts and expert panels, evidence filters through hierarchy and politics. Agency analysts know that telling a minister his flagship rail project will double in cost is a career hazard. Appointed panels, meanwhile, select for credentialed insiders, polite consensus, and vague verbal hedging rather than calibrated probabilities.
[P1.2] When a committee misjudges an inflation surge, an energy shortfall, or a procurement timeline, its members pay no price—and often chair the next commission.
[P1.3] Subsidized prediction markets fix both the incentive problem and the information bottleneck. By funding a modest liquidity pool on concrete, verifiable outcomes—whether winter grid reserves will drop below safe margins, whether hospital demand will breach capacity, or whether an enacted housing reform will hit its two-year permit target—governments put a standing bounty on truth. The subsidy is crucial: unsubsidized markets mostly draw volume on splashy elections, whereas a government-seeded market pays informed participants to correct bad odds even on technical policy questions.
[P1.4] Regional engineers, supply-chain analysts, independent researchers, and specialists outside the minister's inner circle can price in ground-level reality, while straightforward conflict rules bar officials from betting on outcomes they directly control. Weight goes to accuracy and conviction rather than seniority, and prices update continuously instead of freezing inside a static report.
[P1.5] Making these markets a formal input—ending primary reliance on closed-door briefs and handpicked panels by forcing officials to reckon publicly with market odds—breaks bureaucratic monopoly over reality without surrendering democratic rule. Elected leaders still decide moral priorities; markets forecast measurable consequences, not human values. Even attempted manipulation backfires, because noisy money only fattens the payout for informed traders to push prices back toward reality. Governments should stop anchoring billion-dollar choices on rooms where being agreeable beats being right.

[C1.4] Formal status also kills the correction story. Informed money punishes noise when the wager is the prize and the book is deep. [C1.5] They can spend less than the decision is worth to shift the odds. The subsidy buys a lever, not a self-correcting truth machine.

[C1.7] No one cross-examines 0.62, and officials will hide behind it while the public funds the pool.
[C1.8] Experts who are wrong can be named, compared, and removed. A market price has no author. Track records, published dissents, and red teams can discipline forecasting without handing it to whoever can move a subsidized score.
