[P1.1] Severe treatment-resistant conditions—from chronic PTSD and major depression unresponsive to multiple lines of medication, therapy, and even interventions like ECT, to crippling end-of-life anxiety—leave tens of thousands of people with no meaningful path to relief, facing drastically elevated suicide risk, fractured connection to family, and years of unnecessary, unrelenting suffering. For a large share of these patients, replicated evidence spanning decades of research shows psychedelic-assisted therapy delivers large, durable symptom reductions when paired with trained clinical support, often producing transformative, lasting change after just two or three structured sessions where years of standard care failed.
[P1.2] Restricting this care exclusively to narrow clinical trial settings is both cruel and counterproductive. Trials are designed to answer narrow research questions, not deliver equitable care: they lock out most eligible patients with arbitrary exclusion criteria, geographic barriers, and limited slots, pushing many who cannot wait years for wider approval to seek unregulated, black market substances with no consistent dosing, no pre-screening for contraindications like unmanaged psychosis risk, and no trained guide to navigate intense psychological experiences, creating far more avoidable harm than a structured, supervised access model would.
[P1.3] Careful regulation eliminates those risks without abandoning patients: rules can mandate rigorous, standardized therapist certification, mandatory pre-treatment medical and psychological screening, dedicated clinical administration spaces, required multi-week post-session integration care, and universal adverse event tracking to continuously refine safeguards. This is not untested permissiveness: early regulatory rollouts in Oregon and Australia have already demonstrated these guardrails work to keep care safe while delivering life-changing relief to people who had exhausted every other clinical option, while generating far more robust real-world safety data than small, highly selected trial cohorts ever can.
[P1.4] Blocking that pathway is not cautious public health stewardship; it is condemning patients to preventable despair when a workable, evidence-based path to care already exists.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes research promise for a treatment standard. Conspicuous, emotionally powerful sessions make blinding difficult: participants and therapists often know who received the drug, magnifying expectancy effects. Small, highly selected studies with intensive support cannot establish durable benefit for the broader population labelled “treatment-resistant,” which covers different diagnoses and treatment histories. Promising symptom scores do not justify clinical deployment.
[C1.2] Nor is “carefully regulated” a safety mechanism by itself. Screening can reduce, not eliminate, risks of psychosis, mania, cardiovascular complications, acute panic, or destabilization in people already at high suicide risk. Adverse-event registers record injuries; they do not prevent them. Certification cannot guarantee competent handling of a profoundly suggestible patient, and required “integration” is not a proven antidote to harm.
[C1.3] Moving beyond trials multiplies precisely the variations in therapists, settings, patient selection, and commercial pressure that research protocols control.
[C1.4] The claimed regulatory precedents prove far less than suggested. Oregon’s supervised psilocybin framework is not a demonstration of effective psychiatric treatment for severe treatment-resistant illness. Australia’s early specialist-access arrangements cannot yet establish rare-event safety or lasting population benefit. And uncontrolled real-world outcome data cannot reliably distinguish drug effects from natural fluctuation, concurrent treatment, or expectations.
[C1.5] Trial exclusions, meanwhile, are not automatically arbitrary: some exist precisely because psychosis vulnerability or unstable illness makes exposure dangerous.
[C1.6] Desperation deserves urgent care, not lowered evidentiary standards. An expensive regulated system may also coexist with, rather than displace, cheaper unregulated use. Governments should expand rigorous research and access to proven care, while resisting commercial normalization dressed as compassion. Until reproducible functional benefits and longer-term safety are established in representative patients, releasing these treatments from trial settings asks the most vulnerable patients to become the next experiment without its protections.

[P2.1] The core flaw in the case against regulated access is that it weighs risks against an imaginary zero-risk baseline, not against the actual harms of the status quo patients already face. Critics note trials show expectancy effects, small selected samples, and imperfect blinding, but for patients who have already cycled through every approved, evidence-based treatment—multiple antidepressants, structured psychotherapy, even electroconvulsive therapy—there is no perfectly proven, risk-free option waiting. The choice they face is not between psychedelic therapy and a reliably effective standard cure, but between three options: carefully screened, supervised, regulated access; unregulated black market use with no dosing consistency, no contraindication screening, and no trained support to manage acute distress; or continuing to live with crippling, life-threatening symptoms that carry a well-documented elevated suicide risk.
[P2.2] Trial exclusions are not limited to patients at clear risk of psychosis or unstable mania, as critics claim; they routinely lock out patients with common comorbidities, geographic barriers, or inflexible work and care responsibilities that make rigid trial schedules impossible, pushing exactly those vulnerable patients into unregulated markets. Regulation does not need to eliminate every possible risk to be the far safer choice: adverse event tracking does not just record harm, it lets regulators rapidly update screening rules, remove underqualified providers, and refine protocols far faster than slow, narrow, unrepresentative trials can.
[P2.3] The early regulated rollouts dismissed by critics already show far lower serious adverse event rates than unguided use, because even basic screening and supervision cuts the most severe risks dramatically. Claims that commercial pressure invalidates regulation cut both ways: every controlled psychiatric medication carries commercial risk, which we manage with targeted guardrails, not permanent trial-only lockdowns that deny relief to people who cannot wait a decade for final, perfect data while their lives unravel. Regulated access also cuts demand for unregulated supply.

[C2.2] If efficacy is uncertain and destabilization possible, treatment can add harm to suffering rather than substitute for it.
[C2.3] Nor does desperation reverse the burden of proof. Psychiatric drugs reach routine practice after benefits, dosing, contraindications, and risks are characterized, not simply because commerce exists. Here, expectancy, intensive accompanying care, and selected samples obscure what the drug adds and for whom. “No risk-free option” argues for careful decisions, not for waiving the distinction between investigation and treatment.
[C2.4] The claim in [P2.3] that early rollouts show lower serious adverse-event rates than unguided use asserts a comparison the evidence cannot support. Different populations, inconsistent detection, and uncontrolled denominators make such rates unreliable. A registry improves rules only after injuries are detected and reported; it cannot establish rare psychiatric harms or delayed deterioration before wide exposure. Removing an unsafe provider is remediation, not proof the initial authorization was safe.
[C2.5] Exclusions expose a contradiction. If comorbidities and unstable presentations are common among treatment-resistant patients, favorable trial results tell us less about routine access, while excluded conditions may increase danger. The remedy for rigid locations and schedules is accessible research, not inferring effectiveness from inconvenience.
[C2.6] Finally, regulated access does not eliminate unregulated use. High prices, eligibility limits, or refused screening can leave demand outside the system. PRO has not shown that expansion replaces black-market harm; it may add a second pathway. Governments should reduce suffering urgently, but this motion normalizes an unproven treatment before establishing that its benefits exceed its harms.
