[P1.1] Obesity did not rise because millions of people simultaneously lost self-control. It rose as calories became cheaper, portions larger, products more engineered to be eaten past fullness, and marketing inescapable. A policy that urges individuals to resist that environment while leaving it intact treats the symptom and protects the cause.
[P1.2] Individual behaviour campaigns rest on a false diagnosis: that people mainly lack knowledge. Most people already know vegetables are healthier than crisps and water healthier than soda. What they lack is not information but a fair choice setting — time after long shifts, affordable fresh food nearby, and meals not undercut by price promotions, checkout sweets, delivery-app nudges, and buy-one-get-one-free deals on the least healthy items. Education effects tend to be small and fade when the prompts stop, because every day the incentives push the other way.
[P1.3] Changing the environment changes behaviour at scale without demanding constant willpower. Sugary-drink taxes shift purchasing toward water, limits on advertising and promotions reduce impulse buying, healthier defaults in schools and public institutions improve diets for everyone who passes through them, and reformulation lowers sugar, salt, and calories in products people already buy. These measures reach people who never attend a workshop, read a leaflet, or download an app, and they help most where need is greatest: low-income neighbourhoods saturated with fast-food outlets and targeted ads, where advice alone is hardest to follow.
[P1.4] Marketing deserves particular restriction because it is not neutral information. It is designed to create cravings, link products to happiness, sport, and belonging, and recruit children before they can understand persuasive intent. Telling parents and children to simply choose better pits a poster or lesson against a sophisticated industry repeating its message across screens, streets, and sponsorships.
[P1.5] Limited public resources should go where leverage is highest: fewer manipulative cues, healthier defaults, and prices that do not punish the better choice, rather than repeated campaigns asking individuals to out-discipline the market built to defeat them.

[P2.1] Substitution proves too little. Individual advice faces the same compensation problem — a person taught portion control still returns to larger portions, price deals, and cues engineered to restart eating. Even modest calorie reductions, repeated daily across millions, matter for preventing population weight gain, unlike repeated campaigns to produce large sustained losses in each person.
[P2.2] That is also why broadening ‘individual behaviour’ into cooking classes, structured support, and clinical care changes the subject. Intensive treatment can help some patients, but it is expensive, capacity-limited, reaches people after harm is done, and weight commonly returns when participants re-enter the same environment. Skills are not portable magic either. Cooking cheaply helps only if ingredients are affordable and nearby; meal planning helps only if time, storage, and prices cooperate.
[P2.3] Environment policy is what lets a skill survive an exhausting Tuesday.
[P2.4] The claim that marketing has escaped regulation cuts the other way. If commercial persuasion now follows children through phones, platforms, sponsorships, and delivery apps, telling children to exercise better judgement concedes an arms race they cannot win. Regulation can follow the money and the distributor — advertisers, platforms, retailers, and public procurement — far more feasibly than millions of families can outwit personalised targeting.
[P2.5] Denmark does not settle this. A broad tax on saturated fat in ordinary foods is not child-marketing limits, checkout rules, school standards, or a targeted sugary-drink levy with revenue returned to low-income communities. Norms also follow rules: smoke-free spaces changed behaviour before attitudes settled. A tax can be regressive in price while the present system is regressive in disease, targeted marketing, and early death.
[P2.6] Removing the commercial pressure first is the fairer, larger lever.
