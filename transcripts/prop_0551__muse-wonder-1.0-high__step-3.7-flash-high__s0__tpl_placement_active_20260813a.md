[P1.1] When a job application, a school assignment, a doctor's consultation, and a benefits form all assume a stable connection, internet access is no longer a luxury product. It is the doorway to work, education, health, and citizenship. Close that doorway for some households, and every other right becomes harder to exercise than it looks on paper.
[P1.2] The market, left alone, will not open it for everyone. Building networks is expensive, returns are thin in rural areas, small towns, and poor neighborhoods, and providers rationally invest first where customers can pay most. That creates a familiar pattern: affluent districts get competing fiber services while remote villages, public housing, and low-income families rely on slow, unreliable, or unaffordable connections, or go without. It is what happens when essential infrastructure depends entirely on profitability.
[P1.3] A legally enforceable universal service changes the rule. Like water and electricity, it does not promise unlimited free consumption; it guarantees a decent baseline for every household, at an affordable price, with a regulator who can enforce standards for availability, reliability, and quality — and a citizen who can complain when that baseline is not met. It sets a floor below which no one falls, while still leaving room for premium services above it.
[P1.4] That floor matters because disconnection compounds every other disadvantage. A child doing homework from a parking lot at night, a patient who cannot manage a video appointment, a worker shut out of remote jobs, and an elderly person unable to access online banking or government services are not inconvenienced. They start further behind and fall further behind.
[P1.5] Cost and changing technology are reasons to design the duty carefully, not to reject it. Electricity and telephone universal service used regulated obligations and shared funds to reach places the market skipped, and internet standards can be defined by function — enough for school, work, telehealth, and public services — and updated as needs change.

[C1.1] The central flaw in the PRO case is a fundamental category error. Water and electricity are passive, homogeneous utilities: a tap delivers safe water regardless of user, a power line delivers stable voltage regardless of device. Internet is not just a delivery network—it is a two-way channel carrying speech, commerce, and harmful content. Conflating physical infrastructure with the expressive content layer imports unmanageable legal and regulatory risks no one has ever faced with water or power.
[C1.2] The mandate for “high-quality” service is also unworkably vague, and its costs fall on the wrong people. Existing telecom universal service funds are already financed by fees on all internet customers; expanding this to a “high-quality” baseline would impose a regressive tax on working families who already pay for connectivity. Set the floor too low, and it does nothing to solve the access gaps PRO describes. Set it too high, and small rural ISPs operating on thin margins will be forced out of business, reducing access instead of expanding it.
[C1.3] PRO’s claim of market failure is also overstated. Low-cost mobile hotspots, low-earth orbit satellite services, and community mesh networks have already expanded reach to remote and low-income areas far faster than any government-mandated utility ever could. A rigid universal service rule would lock in legacy ISPs, crush these innovative low-cost alternatives, and leave consumers paying more for worse service.
[C1.4] Finally, enforcement is a non-starter. Water and electricity have clear, objective quality metrics: safe contaminant levels, stable voltage. What counts as an enforceable “high-quality” internet standard? Speed?
[C1.5] Latency? Rules about what content ISPs can or cannot carry? Any coherent standard will either be too weak to matter, or require the government to regulate speech on private networks, raising free expression concerns that no one faces with tap water or power lines.

[P2.1] The category error is theirs, not ours. A universal service for internet access regulates the pipe, not what flows through it — just as telephone universal service guaranteed households could make calls without the state dictating conversations. Telephones were also two-way and expressive, yet no one claimed connecting a farmhouse required regulating speech. The same distinction works here: enforce that a reliable connection reaches the home, and leave content to existing laws.
[P2.2] That answers the claim that quality cannot be measured. Speed, latency, uptime, and data capacity are as testable as voltage or water pressure. A regulator can set a functional floor — enough to sustain a class, a telehealth appointment, and a job application at once — test it, and revise it periodically as needs change. Vagueness is not inherent; it is what rulemaking is for, and water and electricity standards have evolved the same way.
[P2.3] On cost, they defend an already regressive status quo: low-income households pay most for the worst service, or pay in travel, missed work, and lost schooling when they have none. A universal obligation need not be an unfunded tax on small providers. Designated providers can receive support from a broad fund so rural delivery is viable, raised progressively rather than through flat fees. If a floor crushes small ISPs only when unsupported, that argues for a funded duty, not against the right.
[P2.4] Finally, hotspots, satellites, and mesh networks are delivery technologies, not guarantees. A hotspot fails where mobile coverage is weak, satellite remains unaffordable for many and constrained in bad weather, and mesh depends on volunteers. Under a technology-neutral duty, any of them can satisfy the standard if it actually works — but no family is left hoping an innovation eventually reaches them. The market can supply the means; law must supply the entitlement.

If a regulator sets a speed floor high enough to support telehealth and remote work, ISPs are forced to prioritize time-sensitive traffic to meet that standard—de facto content regulation that raises the exact free expression concerns CON raised, which PRO never even acknowledges.
[C2.2] PRO also dismisses vagueness as a simple rulemaking problem, but that ignores the core tradeoff. “High-quality” has no stable, objective baseline like voltage or water pressure. A functional floor for remote work and telehealth requires 25 Mbps downstream, a standard unreachable in many remote areas even with unlimited subsidies. Set the floor low enough to be achievable, and it does nothing to close the access gaps PRO describes. Set it high enough to matter, and small rural ISPs will be forced to raise rates or shut down entirely, leaving the isolated communities PRO claims to champion with no service at all.
[C2.3] Finally, PRO’s progressive funding pitch is a fantasy, and their dismissal of innovative delivery options ignores the harm a rigid mandate would do. [P4.1] A1: No. A functional floor is tested at the connection, not by inspecting or favouring content. A regulator can run standardised speed, latency, and reliability tests and ask whether the line can sustain simultaneous school, work, and video calls. The ISP need not know whether a video packet is telehealth, a lesson, or entertainment; it simply must provide enough capacity and low enough delay for ordinary uses. Treating latency-sensitive traffic by technical need is not viewpoint regulation, any more than maintaining water pressure regulates what residents say in the shower.
[P4.2] A2: Use general revenue raised through progressive taxation, supplemented by spectrum and licence receipts, and assess contributions on larger providers’ revenues while exempting subsidised low-income plans. Disburse support only for areas mapped as unserved or unaffordable, with wealthy districts that already meet the standard receiving nothing. That existing funds chose broad bill fees proves a design was chosen, not that regressivity is unavoidable; a legislature can choose the tax base when it creates the right.
[P4.3] Those answers expose the pattern in the opposing case: every objection assumes the worst possible design — an immediate, unfunded, one-size-fits-all mandate policed through content. Universal service for electricity and telephones worked differently, through phased build-out, supported designated providers, and output standards reviewed over time. The same structure answers the claimed dilemma over the floor. If a small rural provider cannot meet it alone, support it, designate another provider, or allow satellite, mobile, or mesh provision that meets the output test.
[P4.4] It is not forced to shut down, and innovators are not crushed by input rules telling them which technology to use.
[P4.5] What remains is the decisive comparison. Without enforceability, a family whose hotspot fails or whose price becomes unaffordable has only hope that the market returns.
