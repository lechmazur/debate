[P1.2] The only thing we reject is making AI the *main* source of writing feedback for teenagers. That is the ground CON must actually defend: not "AI is useful," but "a machine should be the primary voice telling a fifteen-year-old what her writing is worth."
[P1.3] Why does the line fall there? Mastery practice lives in domains with verifiable answers. AI's virtues — infinite patience, instant checking, adaptive difficulty — map perfectly onto problems where correctness is objective. Writing is the opposite.
[P1.4] Feedback on an essay is a judgment about a mind: whether an argument persuades, whether a voice is emerging, whether this student is dodging the hard idea she raised in paragraph two. That judgment requires knowing the writer over time, knowing what she attempted last month, knowing the classroom conversation the essay grew out of. AI has none of that context, and it shows: language models reliably flatter mediocre prose, sand every essay toward the same bland competence, and confidently praise reasoning that doesn't hold together.
[P1.5] Two further costs. First, students write for the reader who responds. If the primary reader is a machine, students learn to optimize for machine taste — formulaic, hedged, voiceless. Second, reading student writing is how teachers diagnose thinking.
[P1.6] Outsource that, and teachers lose their clearest window into what students actually understand, degrading everything else they do.
[P1.7] So the comparison is simple. Our division captures nearly all of AI's genuine benefits — practice at scale, instant correction — while protecting the one place automation does documented harm. CON must argue teenagers develop as writers primarily under algorithmic judgment. That claim should lose.
