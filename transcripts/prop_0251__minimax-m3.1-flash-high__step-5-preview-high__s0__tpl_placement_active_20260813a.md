[C1.1] The real dispute is not whether heat kills. It does. The dispute is whether a mandatory, government-set stop-work trigger is the right tool—and it is not.
[C1.2] First, heat is not a single threshold. Risk depends on humidity, wind, sun, workload, clothing, and above all acclimatization. A construction crew in Phoenix in July has a different physiological baseline than the same crew in Boston. A fixed heat-index trigger forces either unnecessary shutdowns where workers are adapted or inadequate protection where they are not.
[C1.3] It converts a medical judgment into a bureaucratic line.
[C1.4] Second, PRO’s warehouse example cuts against the mandate, not for it. If a firm must stop at a single temperature, the cheapest compliance is not safer work—it is fewer hours. Piece-rate quotas vanish, seasonal crews shrink, and vulnerable workers absorb the loss in take-home pay. The law protects their kidneys while emptying their paychecks.
[C1.5] Third, PRO’s precedent fails. We regulate silica with respirators and ventilation, not by banning quarrying at a fixed particulate count. Those rules control the hazard at its source. A stop-work trigger does the opposite: it ignores whether the employer has provided shade, hydration, rest, or acclimatization, and simply orders everyone off site.
[C1.6] Fourth, enforcement suffers. A numerical line invites paper compliance and legal disputes over whether the threshold was technically met, while inspectors ignore actual conditions. By contrast, general duty clauses and workers’ compensation already punish negligence and assign the true cost of heat illness to the employer who profits from the labor.
[C1.7] What vulnerable workers need is enforceable, flexible safety standards and real compensation when injured—not a rigid number that shifts risk onto their wages.

[P5.1] CON’s strongest objection is that a numerical trigger may misfit individual physiology and shift income risk to workers. But that answers how to administer protection, not whether workers need enforceable protection before collapse.
[P5.2] The decisive question is simple: who decides that dangerous heat has become safe enough to keep working? CON answers “the employer,” backed by supervision, medical screening, and enforcement after complaints. Yet the employer may prioritize production, and the worker who can least afford to refuse may be least able to make the call. General-duty enforcement and a right to refuse are necessary, but still depend on someone invoking protection.
[P5.3] A mandatory stop-work protection establishes the legal default: when an independently calibrated safety standard is crossed, the exposed activity stops unless effective controls make it safe. Individual assessments can additionally protect new hires or other higher-risk workers; they supplement, rather than replace, that enforceable floor.
[P5.4] That is why heat must be treated as a labor-rights issue. The right to stop cannot depend on the worker’s income, the employer’s generosity, or a complaint filed after symptoms appear. Mandatory cessation places the duty before irreversible harm and on those able to act, rather than on the worker forced to absorb the risk.
