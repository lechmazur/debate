[P1.1] Deleted is supposed to mean deleted. The proposition bars data brokers from keeping and selling deleted or otherwise non-public traces of private citizens—deleted posts, abandoned accounts, browsing logs, location pings, messages, and inferences built from them. That prohibition is necessary because the broker business model depends on denying people an exit.
[P1.2] First, autonomy requires a meaningful right of revocation. When a person deletes a post, closes an account, or uses a service that is not public, they are not donating a permanent dossier to strangers. They grant limited, situational access. If brokers can copy, archive, and resell those traces, consent becomes a trap: one click, one app, one compromised website can create a lifetime shadow record.
[P1.3] A teenager’s deleted mental-health search, a domestic-violence survivor’s location history, or a worker’s union-related browsing should not become inventory. That threat chills expression, association, and help-seeking.
[P1.4] Second, the harms are severe and hard to reverse. These traces are aggregated into intimate profiles sold to advertisers, lenders, landlords, employers, scammers, or anyone with a data-feed subscription. Individuals usually cannot know which brokers hold their data, cannot correct inferences, and cannot retrieve it after resale. Unlike ordinary reputational harm, archived digital traces are searchable, replicable, and permanent.
[P1.5] Notice-and-opt-out regimes fail because the ecosystem is deliberately opaque: hundreds of firms, cascading transfers, and ever-changing names place the burden on the very people who never intended disclosure.
[P1.6] Third, the rule is narrow and administrable. It does not forbid all analytics or all consensual data brokerage. It says that once a citizen deletes data or keeps it non-public, brokers may not transform that choice into a commodity. If companies need data, they can obtain it through clear, ongoing consent.
[P1.7] The decisive question is whether private life can be permanently commercialized after a person has said no. It cannot. Vote PRO.

[C1.2] First, the proposition hands every bad actor a permanent reset button. The exact systems that keep the internet and financial system safe—fraud detection, anti-money-laundering, and identity verification—depend on third-party threat-intelligence and risk brokers. Those firms archive non-public traces like historical device fingerprints, IP logs, and closed or deleted accounts to spot synthetic identities and repeat scammers. If deleting an account or keeping a trace "non-public" makes it illegal to archive and sell, criminals can launder their digital identities on demand.
[C1.3] Meanwhile, ordinary consumers pay the price in higher fraud losses and stricter, exclusionary gatekeeping.
[C1.4] Second, the motion destroys vital tools for accountability and inclusion. Every fraudster, stalker, or extremist starts out legally as a "private citizen." Watchdog journalists, civil litigants, and researchers routinely buy archived posts and non-public connection data from commercial aggregators to prove wrongdoing after perpetrators delete the evidence. Likewise, "non-public" traces include alternative credit data—like utility and rental histories—that brokers aggregate so millions of thin-file consumers can access housing and loans.
[C1.5] Third, contrary to [P1.6], this ban is neither narrow nor administrable. How can an independent broker know a citizen deleted a trace on another platform without continuously monitoring that user's live identity? Enforcing deletion requires more invasive tracking, not less. Worse, banning third-party brokers simply cements the monopoly of giant first-party walled gardens, which can hoard and monetize the exact same non-public traces internally while starving smaller competitors and researchers.
[C1.6] Protect privacy by regulating harmful downstream uses—not by blinding fraud defenses, erasing evidence, and outlawing the plumbing of digital trust. Vote CON.

[P2.5] Finally, CON’s administrability objection fails on its own terms. The real monopoly problem is not caused by banning brokers; it is caused by allowing any firm to hoard and resell private life without exit.
