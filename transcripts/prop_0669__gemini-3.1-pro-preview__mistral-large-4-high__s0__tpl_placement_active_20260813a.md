[P1.1] Public universities are engines of discovery, built on the premise that truth emerges from rigorous debate and viewpoint diversity. When administrators require political or ideological loyalty statements as a condition for hiring, promotion, or funding, they subvert this foundational mission and replace academic excellence with ideological conformity. We must prohibit these statements for three essential reasons.
[P1.2] First, they violate the core principles of academic freedom and the First Amendment. Public universities are state actors, and ideological litmus tests function as compelled speech. A university cannot function as a marketplace of ideas if it forces scholars to pledge allegiance to a specific political orthodoxy before they are allowed through the door. When an astrophysicist or a cell biologist must demonstrate ideological purity to secure a research grant or tenure, the institution has abandoned the pursuit of knowledge for the enforcement of dogma.
[P1.3] Second, loyalty tests degrade academic merit. The criteria for hiring and promotion should be the quality of a candidate’s research, their teaching efficacy, and their contribution to their academic field. Ideological screening artificially shrinks the talent pool. It filters out brilliant researchers and educators who simply possess different political views or refuse to parrot administrative buzzwords.
[P1.4] Society relies on public universities for medical breakthroughs and technological innovation, not political homogenization. We cannot afford to turn away our best minds because they fail an arbitrary political purity test.
[P1.5] Third, the precedent is deeply dangerous. The mechanism that permits one set of administrators to mandate a specific ideological statement today can just as easily be used by a different administration to demand a competing ideological loyalty oath tomorrow. A university that normalizes ideological screening hands a loaded weapon to whatever political faction holds power.
[P1.6] The only reliable protection for scholars across the political spectrum is strict institutional neutrality. By prohibiting ideological loyalty statements, public universities protect free inquiry, guarantee that hiring remains inherently meritocratic, and ensure they remain institutions for all citizens, rather than echo chambers for those who conform to the prevailing orthodoxy.

[C1.3] A grant program may require recipients to affirm that research will be reported honestly and free from undisclosed foreign influence. [C1.4] The same logic applies to shared governance. Faculty senates and departments traditionally set mission-relevant criteria for appointments. [C1.5] The real problem is compelled adherence to a political faction or contested orthodoxy. That can be addressed narrowly by banning statements that require support for a party, candidate, or prescribed ideology. But the proposition as written sweeps in viewpoint-neutral and pluralism-protecting affirmations. It would prevent a university from even asking a dean to affirm that hiring will be viewpoint-neutral.
[C1.6] It would leave public universities less able to defend academic freedom, enforce professional duties, and protect students, not more free. Reject it.
