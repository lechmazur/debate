[P1.1] The question isn't whether apps may use defaults — they already do. It's whose interests those defaults should serve when they quietly pick the most expensive fulfillment path for millions of distracted, hungry users.
[P1.2] Start with how choice architecture actually works here. When an app opens with delivery pre-selected, every downstream screen inherits that choice: fees accumulate, minimums apply, tips get suggested on top of inflated menu prices. The total lands at checkout, after the user has already built a cart and committed psychologically. Nobody "chose" a $14 burrito becoming $28.
[P1.3] They chose a burrito and were routed to the version that extracts the most from them. That is steering, and the default is the steering wheel.
[P1.4] Second, the proposition is modest and the burden is asymmetric. "Default to pickup or lower-fee options more often" doesn't ban delivery, doesn't hide it, doesn't even slow it down — one tap and you have your courier. Compare the costs: on my side, an extra tap for people who genuinely want delivery. On their side, every inattentive or price-insensitive user silently pays premium fees they didn't evaluate.
[P1.5] A nudge that costs a deliberate chooser one second cannot outweigh silent overcharging of thousands who never noticed. And if one extra tap is supposedly too costly to bear, that concession proves the whole point: defaults override real preference.
[P1.6] Third, the default should reflect the honest comparison. Pickup is the genuinely cheaper fulfillment of the same meal; delivery's real cost includes congestion, packaging waste, and courier time that its fee only partly covers. When an app defaults to the expensive path, it isn't being neutral — it's monetizing user inattention. A cheaper default is the more defensible status quo precisely because the user who wants premium fulfillment can say so, whereas the user who wanted the cheap one rarely sees the bill until it's too late.
[P1.7] Change the default, keep the choice. Let the expensive option earn its clicks.

[C1.1] People open a food-delivery app because they want food delivered. That obvious fact is fatal to the push for pickup defaults.
[C1.2] A default should match what most users came to do, not what scolds them cheapest. Someone who installs the app, enters a home address, and browses at 7pm on a rainy night is not idly wondering whether to drive across town. Defaulting that person to pickup does not enlighten them; it obstructs them, forcing extra taps, re-sorts, and failed minimums on every single order to protect a hypothetical distracted user who somehow forgot why they opened the app. If one tap is truly trivial, as its defenders claim, then the pickup seeker can already make that tap today — do not tax the majority to spare the minority a click.
[C1.3] And calling pickup the genuinely cheaper option confuses a lower fee with a lower cost. Pickup charges payment in gas, parking, traffic, waiting in line, and time away from work, kids, or rest. For a single parent without a car, an elderly customer, a disabled user, or a night-shift worker, that "free" pickup is far more expensive than a delivery fee. Steering regular users toward convenience is honesty about value, not extraction.
[C1.4] That steering also keeps the system alive. Delivery orders create courier shifts and give restaurants — especially small kitchens without foot traffic — far more sales than counter pickups ever could. Choking that flow by default to shave fees means fewer jobs, smaller tickets, and ultimately higher menu prices to cover lost volume.
[C1.5] Finally, no one pays silently. The total appears before you pay, and you still have to press order. If drip pricing hides the sting, mandate upfront totals. Do not cure one manipulative default with its mirror image that punishes deliberate choice.
