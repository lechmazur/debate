[P1.1] When a mass grave is opened, the first question is not what science can extract, but whose dead these are. Descendant communities should have substantial control over excavation, display, and explanation because these sites are not abandoned data. They are kin, crimes, and unfinished mourning, and treating them as property owned by experts repeats the original stripping of agency.
[P1.2] Excavation without consent can retraumatize and desecrate. Bodies exhumed for study, photographs circulated, remains arranged in museum cases may satisfy curiosity, but for communities tied by ancestry, culture, or faith, that is a second handling of loved ones without permission. At the African Burial Ground in New York, Black New Yorkers protested building and research plans that treated ancestral remains as an obstacle and a resource; descendant pressure forced respectful treatment and proper memorialization. The result educates without displaying ancestors as specimens.
[P1.3] Control prevents harm from being the price of history.
[P1.4] Control also makes the history better, not narrower. Descendants carry oral histories, languages, burial practices, place names, and knowledge of the missing that archives written by perpetrators often erase. That knowledge can restore names, identify remains, and explain forced labor or killing as the destruction of lived communities, not just abstract casualty counts. It also checks sanitization.
[P1.5] When a state or institution implicated in an atrocity alone curates its site, language drifts toward euphemism — "relocation" and "conflict" instead of murder and coercion. A community with real decision power can insist the explanation name what happened.
[P1.6] Substantial control does not mean descendants dictate lab results or exclude archaeologists and the public. It means consent before digging or displaying remains, a governing voice in what is shown and how, and power to require reburial, restricted access, or ritual care. Experts still test and document; the public still learns. What changes is accountability.
[P1.7] Universal heritage cannot mean universal entitlement to someone else's grave.

[C1.1] Mass graves are crime scenes before they are memorials. An atrocity site holds evidence of murder and system, not only ancestors. Giving descendant communities substantial control turns a duty owed to humanity — to prove, to teach, to prevent denial — into private inheritance, where a subset of the living can forbid digging, hide remains, or edit the explanation.
[C1.2] The owner of that veto is rarely obvious. After mass violence, community fractures across generations, faiths, diasporas, and factions. Distant descendants of a slave labor camp may number in the hundreds of thousands and disagree over exhumation, photography, or reburial rites. Substantial control does not empower kinship; it empowers whoever organizes first to claim it, producing gridlock, rival vetoes, and capture while bones degrade and witnesses die.
[C1.3] That cost is mortal for justice. Naming the missing and proving systematic killing often require disturbing remains some relatives would understandably leave untouched, photographing what some wish unseen, and preserving samples longer. Exhumations after the Balkan wars, the Rwandan genocide, and Spain’s civil war succeeded because forensic teams could proceed on legal and scientific authority despite painful disagreement among families. A right to require immediate reburial or sealed archives would fragment evidence, destroy context, and hand deniers their argument: that nothing was proven.
[C1.4] The same danger corrupts display. Grief seeks closure, dignity, and a heroic narrative; history needs completeness, including horror and inconvenient facts. Communities may want skulls hidden, labor sites softened, or complicity erased. Independent researchers and curators, bound by ethics but not by kin veto, are more likely to resist both state euphemism and family sanitization.
[C1.5] The African Burial Ground needed listening, not surrender; respectful treatment came from professional standards plus protest, not from handing one group authorship.
[C1.6] Consult, honor ritual, rebury when science and law allow. But excavation, preservation, and explanation of atrocity must answer to courts, evidence, and the public’s right to know — not to substantial control by descent.

[P2.1] The claim that mass graves are “crime scenes before they are memorials” [C1.1] proves too much. A crime scene does not belong to the laboratory. Its purpose is justice for victims, and kin are not obstacles to that justice—they are its reason and its source. Forensic teams identify the dead because relatives provide DNA, names, and accounts of who disappeared.
[P2.2] Where families distrust authorities, samples are withheld and identifications stall. Consent is not the enemy of evidence; it is the condition for obtaining it.
[P2.3] That answers the success of proceeding “despite painful disagreement” [C1.3]. In Spain, exhumations of Civil War graves were driven for decades by families and memory associations demanding the digging the state had avoided. In the Balkans and Rwanda, families pressed for recovery and identification, even when disputing methods. The choice was rarely experts versus descendants; it was descendants demanding accountable work versus institutions delaying or politicizing it.
[P2.4] Control channels that demand into protocol: document first, test what is needed, then rebury under agreed rites—preserving context without indefinite possession.
[P2.5] Disagreement does not make authority impossible. Substantial control does not mean every relative holds an individual veto, any more than public control means every citizen votes on each artifact. Communities can act through elected councils, tribal governments, family associations, and mixed boards where they hold decisive weight. Fractures justify careful representation, not transferring power to courts and curators who disagree and can be captured—often by the state whose culpability is at issue.
[P2.6] Finally, grief is accused of sanitizing, while professional distance is treated as neutral. It is not. The African Burial Ground was not corrected by professional standards alone; those standards allowed remains to be treated as a construction problem. It changed when Black New Yorkers gained leverage to stop and reshape the work.
[P2.7] Consultation without power is advice the powerful may discard. Substantial control makes the people to whom the dead belong impossible to discard.

[C2.2] A murder victim's family is the reason for prosecution, but does not own the crime scene, decide what forensic tests run, or edit the trial exhibit. Atrocity evidence belongs to that same public logic, only larger: proof that must convince not relatives, but courts, skeptics, and future deniers.
[C2.3] Consent as the 'condition for obtaining' evidence [P2.2] gets the remedy backwards. When families distrust authorities, the answer is independent warrants, chain of custody, outside observers — guarantees that work precisely when trust is absent. A veto for distrust lets one fearful faction shut down identification for all other families waiting for a name.
[C2.4] Spain proves the point against them [P2.3]. Families had to fight a state that refused to dig. The solution was to break monopoly, not to create a new one in kinship form. What happens when descendants demand digging and others demand leaving the dead in peace?
[C2.5] Or when a forced-labor site holds victims from a dozen nations, three faiths, and eighty years of dispersion? There is no council to elect, only rival associations claiming decisive weight [P2.5]. Substantial control then means first organizer wins, last dissenter loses.
[C2.6] 'Document first, then rebury' [P2.4] sounds responsible until trials come decades later and new methods can identify what old ones could not. A power to require reburial and sealed display makes evidence perish on one group's timetable.
[C2.7] Distance is not neutral, but neither is grief. Families can heroize, hide complicity, or demand horror be covered. The African Burial Ground was saved by protest inside a public process, not by deed of ownership. Voice, yes.
[C2.8] Veto, no.

[P4.1] A1: Descendants are not identified by a blood test or by who claims the microphone first. They are the organized communities tied to the victims by kinship, faith, culture, or targeted group membership: family associations, tribal governments, religious bodies, and survivor-led organizations. At a mixed site, none receives sole control; they share a governing board, with seats reflecting their connection to the dead. Rival claims are settled through a transparent recognition process, just as courts recognize representative plaintiffs without thereby deciding the history themselves.
[P4.2] A2: Substantial control is not an unreviewable power to make evidence vanish. The governing agreement sets a preservation floor before work begins: complete forensic documentation, retained samples under secure and restricted custodianship, and renewed access for a court-ordered investigation or credible new identification, with descendants at the table. Reburial after that record is made does not erase proof; indefinite storage in a laboratory is not the only form of preservation. Dissatisfied families also have recourse inside the structure through minority seats, public reasons, and independent mediation, rather than being silently overruled by a distant institution.
[P4.3] Those answers turn the supposed dilemma against the opposition. Mixed constituencies and future trials are problems every legitimate authority faces, and we solve them with representation, protocols, and appeals. We do not conclude that because a city, a jury pool, or a profession is divided, an outside ministry should own the decision. Difficulty justifies design, not dispossession.
[P4.4] Without that design, the alternative remains consultation that ends exactly when it matters: when a state wants delay called prudence, murder called relocation, or ancestral remains kept as exhibits. Descendants with decisive weight can insist on digging, naming, and honest labels as well as on ritual care. That is why control protects evidence and explanation together, while mere voice leaves both at the mercy of the institutions that may have the most to hide.

[P5.1] The decisive choice is not between dignity and evidence. It is between advice that can be ignored and authority that cannot.
[P5.2] Consultation ends at the exact moment it matters: when an institution wants to delay a dig, keep remains on display, or call murder something gentler. At that moment, being heard changes nothing unless descendants can say no — and can also say dig, name, and tell it plainly. That is substantial control: not each relative ruling alone, not lab results dictated by grief, but a representative body with decisive weight, bound by documentation, retained samples, and court-ordered access.
[P5.3] The opposition trusts courts and curators to stand above the fight. But those are the very institutions that have delayed, euphemized, and treated graves as construction problems until organized descendants forced a reckoning. External safeguards matter, yet they work best when the people to whom the dead belong can enforce them.
[P5.4] A grave is not made public property by the scale of the crime committed against it. Without control, the victims are taken twice: first by the perpetrators, then by whoever claims neutral custody of their bones and story.

[C5.1] The opposition has conceded the deciding point. A board that needs a 'transparent recognition process,' 'retained samples,' 'court-ordered access,' 'independent mediation' has admitted it cannot govern itself. Someone above rival kin must decide who counts, what is kept, and when evidence returns. That someone is law, science, and public accountability — our case: strong voice, not substantial control.
[C5.2] That concession is unavoidable because a veto cannot be split. The power to say dig, name, tell plainly is the same legal power to say never dig, rebury now, show nothing. At a mixed pit with a dozen nations and three faiths there is no natural electorate to wield it fairly, only a race to claim decisive weight while other families wait for a name and deniers wait for a gap.
[C5.3] Mass murder creates a duty owed outward — to prove, to convict decades later, to teach skeptics — not property inherited inward. Give grief enforceable rights, ritual care, a seat that cannot be ignored. But do not give one organized grief the power to bury everyone else's proof. That is why control must lose.
