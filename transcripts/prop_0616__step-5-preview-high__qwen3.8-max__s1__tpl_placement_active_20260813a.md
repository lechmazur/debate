[P1.1] Semiconductors are the ammunition of the AI age. The extreme ultraviolet lithography systems and associated process tools that fabricate advanced chips are not ordinary commercial goods; they are force multipliers that determine whether an adversary can field autonomous weapon swarms, run real-time battlefield intelligence, or harden military command networks against disruption. When governments permit the export of this equipment to rivals, they are not merely forfeiting market share. They are transferring the industrial capacity to automate violence at scale.
[P1.2] The case for restriction rests on a stark asymmetry. Commercial losses are recoverable; a decisive military disadvantage is not. Chip firms can pivot to adjacent markets, absorb slower growth, or accept temporary revenue declines. But once an adversary possesses the fabrication capacity to mass-produce military-grade AI accelerators, the advantage compounds in ways no later trade policy can reverse.
[P1.3] The cost of ceding a technological lead in weapons systems is measured in lives, not quarterly earnings.
[P1.4] Opponents will argue controls are porous and black markets will diffuse the technology anyway. That is an argument for tighter enforcement, not surrender. Precisely because these machines are rare, expensive, and require specialized maintenance, they are easier to track than almost any other strategic commodity. A sanctioned regime cannot simply download a foundry; it needs spare parts, software updates, and human expertise.
[P1.5] Restricting exports creates friction, raises costs, and buys time for democratic militaries to develop countermeasures and defenses.
[P1.6] The commercial pain is real, but it is bounded. Governments have long restricted exports of uranium enrichment technology and precision munitions components when buyers are hostile. Advanced semiconductor manufacturing equipment belongs in the same category. The question is not whether companies maximize short-term profit, but whether we are willing to sell the tools of the next arms race to those who would use them against us.

[C1.2] A determined rival will treat the restriction as a reason to eliminate dependence, not as a permanent barrier. The result is not permanent denial; it is a delay purchased with our own industrial strength.
[C1.3] That trade is strategically self-defeating. The firms that build these machines and advanced chips depend on enormous capital spending and continuous iteration. Major export restrictions shrink the revenue base that funds next-generation research, slows process improvements, and weakens the very ecosystem democratic militaries rely on. If the commercial cost is truly major, it also reduces tax receipts, employment, and the capacity to surge production in a crisis.
[C1.4] Security is not protected by starving the industrial base; it is often hollowed out.
[C1.5] Nor does equipment control map cleanly onto military AI. Modern AI power comes from algorithms, data, software stacks, and access to compute, not only the newest fab. [C1.6] Controls that are unilateral or half-coordinated will also shift sales to less scrupulous suppliers while fracturing alliances.
[C1.7] The better test is not whether restrictions inflict commercial pain but whether they make us safer at acceptable cost. Here they fail: they slow a determined adversary only temporarily, spur self-sufficiency, and weaken the commercial engine of our own defense. The motion should be rejected.

PRO’s uranium analogy fails because enrichment is a narrow, state-scale infrastructure problem; semiconductor capability is a diffuse commercial stack.
[C2.6] If the best case is a costly, temporary delay while our own innovation base weakens and rivals mobilize, the proposition fails the only test that matters: it makes us less safe, not safer.
