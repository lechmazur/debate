[P1.1] Imagine a lucid adult with late-stage ALS, gradually losing the ability to swallow, speak, and breathe, or a cancer patient whose pain breaks through maximal medication. When medicine can no longer cure and hospice cannot relieve agony short of inducing an irreversible coma, the law faces a stark choice: respect that person’s ownership of their own body, or force them to endure every hour of a brutal decline. Allowing medical assistance in dying under strict safeguards chooses mercy and self-determination over state-mandated suffering.
[P1.2] We already recognize that competent adults hold authority over fatal medical choices. Patients may legally disconnect ventilators, refuse artificial nutrition, or choose palliative sedation until death. Banning direct assistance does not preserve life; it merely forces patients into slower, harsher exits like dehydration or suffocation, or strips away consciousness before they are ready.
[P1.3] Crucially, this principle applies equally to unbearable degenerative conditions. A progressive neurological disease that traps a sharp mind inside a paralyzed body for years inflicts torment at least as severe as an illness with a six-month prognosis. Intractable physical ruin, not an arbitrary calendar, is what justifies relief.
[P1.4] Palliative care is vital, which is why strict safeguards require thorough palliative counseling first. Yet even optimal hospice care cannot conquer every refractory pain or the terror of air hunger. Nor do blanket bans protect the desperate; instead, they backfire. Denied a peaceful, regulated option, patients facing degenerative decline frequently end their lives violently, secretively, and prematurely, acting months early while they still retain the physical strength to do so.
[P1.5] Regulation replaces that lonely desperation with transparent oversight: multiple independent medical evaluations, verified competence, waiting periods, and active screening against coercion. Safeguards protect the vulnerable without holding the autonomous hostage to their diseases. When suffering cannot be healed, a humane law lets competent adults die gently, surrounded by loved ones, on their own terms.
