[P1.1] A descendant of enslaved people, an adoptee sealed out of her own birth certificate, a grandchild of a boarding-school survivor, and a refugee's child searching a wartime camp register do not start from the same place as a hobbyist tracing a famous ancestor. For them, the missing name is not trivia. It is where a state, a court, a church, or an army helped sever a family — and too often still holds the only paper trail that can reconnect it.
[P1.2] That is why historical recovery cannot be left mostly to private genealogy markets. Those markets follow paying demand. They digitize what is already plentiful, easy to sell, and attractive to large subscriber bases — census pages, passenger lists, well-preserved parish books. They have far less reason to rescue fragile slave sale receipts scattered across courthouses, indigenous allotment files, untranslated displacement lists, or closed adoption indexes.
[P1.3] When the ancestors were treated as property, orphans, wards, or enemies, their records are the least commercial and the most at risk of loss.
[P1.4] Making families pay again compounds the original harm. A subscription wall may be a minor inconvenience for leisure research; it is a second dispossession when identity, tribal enrollment, citizenship claims, inheritance, land evidence, or basic medical history sits behind it. Public funding treats access as repair and as public infrastructure, like a courthouse record or a national archive reading room: preserve once, index properly, and let people search without renting back their own past month by month.
[P1.5] Public does not have to mean careless. Governments can digitize with privacy tiers, waiting periods for living people, and — crucially — indigenous and adoptee community control over sensitive material. Private firms answer to shareholders and data brokers; a public program can be audited, corrected, and forbidden from selling trauma as a product. The choice is between treating severed histories as a market opportunity and treating reconnection as a public obligation.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds like justice. It reads like repair. But the mechanism it proposes — government digitization and public funding as the primary vehicle — is precisely the kind of slow, politicized, under-resourced approach that has failed to deliver comparable records for decades.
[C1.2] Consider what governments actually do with mass digitization. They produce bloated, over-budget projects with interfaces that trail commercial platforms by years. The U.S. National Archives, despite enormous mandates, has digitized only a fraction of its holdings, and its search tools remain clunky compared to what private firms built on the same raw material.
[C1.3] Shifting the primary role to the state does not accelerate access. It replaces competition with monopoly and accountability with bureaucracy.
[C1.4] More critically, public control of these specific records invites politicization. When a government holds the master index to slavery transactions, indigenous land allotments, and sealed adoptions, it holds the power to emphasize, de-emphasize, or restrict narratives that challenge official history. Private firms, for all their flaws, answer to customers — not to the incumbent political class. A government archive that can be defunded, reorganized, or directed by executive order is not neutral infrastructure.
[C1.5] It is a vulnerability.
[C1.6] The proposition also misidentifies where the real bottleneck lies. The hard work is not scanning pages. It is interpreting fragmented, multilingual, context-dependent records — slave manifests in inconsistent handwriting, indigenous treaties in legal jargon, wartime displacement logs in damaged condition. Private genealogy markets have invested heavily in the human expertise and machine-learning tools that make raw scans searchable.
[C1.7] Public funding rarely sustains that kind of specialized, iterative development. It funds the scan, not the scholarship.
[C1.8] Finally, public money is finite. Every dollar poured into a government digitization platform is a dollar unavailable for direct material reparations, community health programs, or land-back initiatives that actually change living conditions. The proposition trades tangible redress for a database that may never be finished.
