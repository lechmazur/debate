[P1.1] Health systems should not choose between compassion and fiscal stewardship; they should refuse to spend marginal funds on treatments that mostly prolong dying while leaving preventable illness underfunded. The decisive question is not whether end-of-life care is valuable—good palliative care is essential—but whether additional high-cost intervention produces enough health benefit to justify displacing prevention and basic care.
[P1.2] Preventive public health measures have three advantages. First, they reduce disease before expensive treatment begins. Vaccination, tobacco control, sanitation, and maternal health programs can avoid hospitalizations and premature deaths across populations. Second, they distribute benefits more fairly, including among people who never enter the intensive-care unit.
[P1.3] Third, stronger prevention can lower future system costs, making prevention an investment rather than merely another competing expense. By contrast, marginal extensions of intensive treatment at the very end of life often provide brief additional survival, substantial suffering, and little opportunity for rehabilitation or contribution. Their high costs also consume capacity that could shorten waits, improve primary care, and support palliative services.
[P1.4] This is not a claim that every preventive program is cheap or every terminal patient should receive minimal care. The relevant comparison under scarcity is expected health and social value per marginal dollar. Prevention usually offers broader reach and more durable returns than the final increment of costly treatment.
[P1.5] Consider a smoker prevented from lung cancer: one avoided case averts surgery, chemotherapy, disability, and family disruption. The same funds used for a burdensome intervention giving a terminal patient only brief additional life could instead fund screening and tobacco cessation that prevent several cases. Neither example dismisses the patient; both expose the opportunity cost of the spending choice.
[P1.6] Therefore, health systems should preserve appropriate palliative care while prioritizing prevention over low-benefit, high-cost treatment extensions. This honors patients, uses scarce resources well, and produces more population health.
