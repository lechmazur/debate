[P1.1] Today, the only thing standing between an unauthorized worker and a payroll job is a forged document that merely has to "look genuine" to a manager with no training and no way of checking it. The question is whether making that check universal for new hires is worth it, and the proposition sets the standard: a meaningful reduction, without large collateral harm.
[P1.2] First, it works where it's actually required. Arizona mandated E-Verify well over a decade ago, and researchers studying it broadly found the same pattern: the likely-unauthorized population shrank, and formal employment and wages for those workers fell. Other mandate states show similar pressure. The big weakness of today's system is that it is a patchwork.
[P1.3] A worker who is rejected by a participating employer walks across the street, or across the state line, to one that doesn't participate. A nationwide mandate closes that exit. It doesn't need to be airtight to be meaningful, because raising the cost and risk of every formal hire is itself the mechanism.
[P1.4] Second, E-Verify fights discrimination rather than fueling it. Discrimination thrives on discretion, and the current system forces employers to guess who is "really" authorized. [P1.5] The overwhelming majority of authorized workers clear it within seconds, and the small share flagged in error have a process to contest.
[P1.6] Third, the feared labor shock is designed out. The mandate covers new hires only. Nobody's existing workforce disappears overnight, and the adjustment arrives gradually through ordinary turnover. That gives wages, recruitment and legal visa channels time to respond.
