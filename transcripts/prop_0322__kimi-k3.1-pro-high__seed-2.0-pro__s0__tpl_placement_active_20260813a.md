[C1.1] This mandate will punish harmless creator workflow long before it stops any actual deception. Almost all common AI voice use for host ads is not faking an endorsement: it is cleaning up a flubbed line after wrapping recording, removing a cough mid-read, normalising audio between sessions, or re-recording a single corrected sentence without dragging the whole host back for an extra hour of studio time. The rule as written makes no exception for this trivial, routine use. It requires disclosure for *any* use of AI voice or likeness replication on host ads, no matter how minor.
[C1.2] Honest small creators will bear almost the entire cost. A part-time podcaster with 1500 regular listeners will have to audit every ad edit, update script templates, and add mandatory disclosure text for every tiny adjustment. Meanwhile the bad actors who generate fully fake unauthorised celebrity endorsements—the only people actually harming audiences—will simply ignore this rule, just as they already ignore existing advertising laws.
[C1.3] We already have law against misleading endorsements. If a creator pretends to endorse something they never agreed to, if a voice is faked to lie about what someone said, that is already illegal. This proposition adds zero new consumer protection. It only creates an overbroad, one-sided compliance burden that will disproportionately drive small independent creators out of advertising entirely.

[P2.1] The opposition’s case depends on treating a cloned performance as if it were cough removal. That collapses the distinction at the heart of this motion. Noise reduction, equalisation, or deleting a stumble does not necessarily involve an AI-generated voice or likeness replication. Generating a new sentence in the host’s voice does: listeners are no longer hearing only the performance the host recorded.
[P2.2] The mandate targets that material substitution, not ordinary production merely because software was involved [C1.1].
[P2.3] Even where a small corrected sentence is covered, the supposed burden is wildly overstated. The creator need not hire lawyers, rebuild studio workflows, or abandon advertising. A brief audible or written notice—“This ad read includes an AI-generated voice”—is enough to satisfy the principle. If a creator knows which ads contain synthetic reads, disclosure is trivial; if they do not know, that is a reason for a basic record, not a reason to keep audiences uninformed.
[P2.4] A part-time podcaster’s inconvenience cannot outweigh every listener’s ability to understand what they are hearing [C1.2].
[P2.5] Existing prohibitions on misleading endorsements do not make disclosure redundant. They address false claims, fake consent, or fabricated endorsements; they do not necessarily tell audiences whether an apparently personal host-read was synthetically generated. An ad can make entirely accurate product claims while still relying on the persuasive intimacy of the host’s familiar voice. Disclosure preserves that distinction without banning the technique or second-guessing the endorsement.
[P2.6] Nor does “fraudsters will ignore it” defeat the rule. That argument would invalidate advertising law generally. Reachable creators, sponsors, agencies, and distributors can comply, while a specific disclosure duty gives enforcement a clearer footing than waiting to prove a separate deception. The choice is therefore not between burdensome censorship and perfectly adequate existing law.
[P2.7] It is between a low-cost sentence of transparency and preserving a commercial ambiguity that benefits only those who would rather audiences not ask whether the performance was real.
