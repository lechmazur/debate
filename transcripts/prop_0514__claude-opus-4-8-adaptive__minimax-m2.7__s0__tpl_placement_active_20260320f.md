[P1.1] The core question is who controls public infrastructure that schools and hospitals depend on for decades: the public that pays for it, or a single vendor that owns the exits. Favoring open standards and switching portability keeps that control where it belongs. Three reasons make this decisive.
[P1.2] First, lock-in is a tax that compounds. When a hospital's records or a district's student data live only in one proprietary format, the vendor knows you cannot leave. Renewal isn't a negotiation; it's a hostage exchange. Prices rise, service quality drifts, and the buyer has no credible threat to walk.
[P1.3] Open standards and guaranteed data portability restore the one thing public buyers usually lack: the ability to say no. Competition at every renewal, not just the first bid, is what disciplines price and quality over a system's twenty-year life.
[P1.4] Second, the stakes are interoperability itself. Health and education are not islands. A patient moves between a clinic, a hospital, and a pharmacy; a student moves between schools, districts, and states. If each runs an incompatible integrated stack, the information that should follow the person gets stranded at the boundary.
[P1.5] That isn't a convenience problem—it's missed allergies, duplicated tests, lost learning records. Open standards exist precisely to let different systems speak. Favoring them in procurement makes the seams disappear where it matters most.
[P1.6] Third, resilience and accountability. A single integrated stack is a single point of failure—one outage, one breach, one company's bankruptcy, and an entire hospital network or school system goes dark with no fallback. Portability means a public body can migrate rather than collapse. And open standards let independent auditors inspect how sensitive child and patient data is handled, rather than trusting a black box.
[P1.7] I'll grant the convenience of an all-in-one stack on day one. But procurement decisions bind the public for decades, and the burden is to serve citizens over that whole span. The motion isn't anti-innovation—it says: buy systems you can leave, that talk to each other, that you can inspect. That is simply responsible stewardship of public money.
