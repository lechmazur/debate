[C1.1] The case for this tax rests on a seductive picture: a warehouse, two hundred pickers, a robot, and a clean bill dumped on taxpayers. That picture falls apart the moment you ask how the tax would actually work.
[C1.2] Start with the definition. "Replacing human workers with robotics" sounds precise until you try to tax it. A firm that expands output with robots and hires no one new—has it "replaced" anyone? A firm that automates and rehires the same workers as robot technicians?
[C1.3] A firm that would have offshored the work instead? Every real tax code needs a line, and every line creates gaming. Companies will lease robots across borders, reclassify workers as contractors before the switch, or simply relocate automation to jurisdictions without the tax. What you get is not fairness; it's an incentive to move capital and jobs offshore simultaneously—strictly worse for the workers this policy claims to protect.
[C1.4] Then there's the economics. Historically, countries that taxed or restricted labor-saving machinery—think of the resistance to mechanized weaving—did not save jobs; they saved them somewhere else, in the countries that embraced the technology. Automation is the main driver of productivity growth, and productivity is the only durable source of wage growth. Taxing it at the point of investment taxes the very mechanism that funds the social programs PRO wants to expand.
[C1.5] Finally, the revenue promise is the weakest link. Earmarked taxes have a long history of dissolving into general revenue the moment budgets tighten. And even if the money arrived intact, retraining programs for mid-career displaced workers have a mixed track record at best—promising retraining as the compensation for a taxed-away job is offering a coupon for a store that's often out of stock.
[C1.6] PRO calls automation an unpriced externality. It's the opposite: it's the priced engine of prosperity. A tax on it doesn't correct the market; it shrink-wraps the economy in amber while competitors race ahead.
