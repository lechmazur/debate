[P1.1] The question is not whether AI can support people in distress. It is whether an automated system should be allowed to hold someone’s mental-health care indefinitely. It should not.
[P1.2] Mental health care is risk-sensitive and changing. A patient may seem stable in week three, then disclose escalating self-harm, substance use, psychosis, or a medication change in week seven. A chatbot can offer coping prompts, but it lacks the clinical judgment, physical presence, and legal duty of care of a licensed professional. A fixed-session handoff creates a recurring checkpoint: a qualified human can reassess diagnosis, suicide risk, trauma history, and whether AI support is still appropriate at all.
[P1.3] A cap also prevents quiet deterioration. AI systems are built to keep users engaged; mental-health care should not be. Without a required endpoint, a struggling user can remain in an endless loop of generic exercises while believing they are receiving treatment. That delays real care and can make a system look like access while functioning as avoidance.
[P1.4] The rule does not ban chatbots. It treats them as a bridge, not a destination. A legal floor can force companies to build referral pathways, staff escalation teams, and maintain records. It also removes the conflict of interest that arises when the same platform decides both that patients are improving and that patients should keep using it.
[P1.5] A fixed number may be imperfect, but law often uses bright lines where private discretion has failed. The alternative is an industry policing its own escalation threshold. When the issue is psychological safety, the burden should fall on those with clinical accountability, not on software optimized for continued use.

[C1.2] It is arbitrary discontinuity dressed as protection.
[C1.3] Consider the patient this rule hurts most: someone in a rural area or on a long waitlist who uses a chatbot at night because it is the only support available. At the fixed session, the platform must push them toward a human professional. But the rule does not conjure that professional. It creates a referral list, a voicemail, a months-long wait—and removes the tool that was actually helping.
[C1.4] PRO calls chatbots a bridge; a fixed cap can make them a trapdoor.
[C1.5] The cap also creates perverse incentives. Patients learn that every session brings them closer to being cut off, so they may withhold worsening symptoms, minimize risk, or restart under a new account. [C1.6] It does not test crisis detection, escalation, privacy, or competence.
[C1.7] The real question is not whether humans should ever take over. They should—when risk, complexity, or patient preference calls for it. A legal regime should require safe escalation, transparent limits, and audit—not force a handoff at an arbitrary number that ignores the person in front of it.
[C1.8] The motion would make care less continuous, less honest, and less available. Reject it.
