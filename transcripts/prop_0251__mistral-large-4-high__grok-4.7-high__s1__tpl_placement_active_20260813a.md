[P1.1] Extreme heat is not merely an environmental inconvenience; it is a lethal workplace hazard that employers routinely force workers to endure. When temperatures soar, farmworkers, construction laborers, and delivery drivers face an impossible choice: continue working in conditions that can cause heat stroke, organ failure, or death, or lose the wages they need to survive. Every summer, workers collapse in fields and on scaffolds while employers look the other way, treating human bodies as cheaper than lost productivity. This is not a market failure to be corrected by goodwill—it is a fundamental violation of labor rights.
[P1.2] The right to a safe workplace is meaningless without the power to enforce it. In the absence of mandatory stop-work protections, workers in precarious employment—often migrants, gig workers, or those paid by piece rate—face economic coercion that renders their theoretical right to refuse unsafe work hollow. An employer who threatens termination or withholds pay for a day missed due to heat is not offering a choice; they are issuing a death sentence dressed as a paycheck.
[P1.3] Governments must therefore treat extreme heat as a labor-rights issue by mandating that work stops when heat reaches dangerous levels. Voluntary guidelines fail because they rely on employers prioritizing human life over quarterly productivity, an expectation that reality has repeatedly betrayed. Only binding stop-work protections shift the burden: when the thermometer hits a threshold, work ceases, and workers retain their pay. This is not government overreach; it is the baseline recognition that no worker should be compelled to sacrifice their body for a wage.
[P1.4] Without these protections, we accept that some workers—disproportionately the poor, the undocumented, and those in the informal economy—are disposable. Mandatory stop-work laws affirm the opposite: that a worker's right to life supersedes an employer's right to profit, and that government has a duty to enforce that boundary when the air itself becomes lethal.

Yes, heat risk depends on humidity and workload, but workers cannot litigate meteorological complexity at noon in a field while their employer watches the clock. The opposition's "task-specific controls" [C1.4] are not alternatives to stop-work rights; they are prerequisites that have failed precisely because they rely on employer goodwill and lack enforcement teeth.

[P2.5] Regarding essential work, the opposition presents a false choice between banning line repair and abandoning workers. The right to stop is not a veto on necessary work; it is a guarantee that necessary work cannot proceed by sacrificing the worker.
[P2.6] The opposition asks us to trust employers to self-regulate through voluntary measures that have already produced corpses. Governments must choose: enforce the right to life, or continue subsidizing employer profits with worker mortality.
