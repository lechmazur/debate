[P1.1] By seventh grade, most public schools have already decided who is a "math person." A placement test, a teacher recommendation, and often a parent's ability to advocate sort children into advanced, regular, and remedial tracks — and that sort tends to stick. A student placed low in sixth grade rarely reaches calculus, not because she cannot learn it, but because the slower track never teaches the prerequisites in time.
[P1.2] That permanence from an early, noisy signal is tracking's core failure. Readiness at eleven reflects prior tutoring, stable housing, language at home, and familiarity with test formats as much as underlying ability. Late bloomers, newcomers, and low-income students are systematically under-placed, then confirmed in that placement because their track covers less material, moves more slowly, and is more often assigned less experienced teachers and lower expectations.
[P1.3] Heterogeneous classes reverse that logic. Teach everyone a rigorous core curriculum at high expectation, and use intensive tutoring to handle real differences in preparation. Whole-class instruction provides what tracking denies most students: exposure to rich problems, stronger peers explaining ideas in different ways, and a teacher aiming at mastery rather than remediation. Frequent small-group tutoring then does the personalized work tracking supposedly does: filling a fractions gap, reteaching factoring, or pushing an advanced student into proof and extension problems.
[P1.4] Tutoring is flexible in a way tracks are not. A track locks a child in for a year or years; a tutoring group can change next week when she masters a skill or stumbles on a new one. Struggling students get acceleration with support instead of repetition at a slower pace, and advanced students get depth without segregation being the only route to challenge.
[P1.5] Most tracking should go because it mistakes early advantage for fixed talent. Heterogeneous instruction plus intensive tutoring keeps the door open, raises the floor, and still stretches the ceiling.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a staffing fantasy for an education plan. Replacing advanced tracking with heterogeneous classes plus intensive tutoring sounds humane, but it collapses on three points: curriculum sequence, resource arithmetic, and the ceiling it claims to preserve.
[C1.2] Math is cumulative and unforgiving. A teacher facing a class spanning fourth-grade arithmetic to high-school algebra cannot teach to the middle without failing both ends. The algebra-ready student waits while fractions are retaught; the student still mastering multiplication drowns in pace. Whole-group "rich problems" do not solve this; they widen the gap by forcing one lesson to serve incompatible needs, and they assume that mixed-level discussion naturally produces mastery.
[C1.3] It does not. Confusion spreads when prerequisites are missing.
[C1.4] Tutoring is the load-bearing claim, and it is the weakest. "Intensive" means high-frequency, small-group sessions—multiple times a week, with ratios low enough to diagnose a specific misconception. Most public schools already struggle to staff basic intervention. Promising this for every student who needs it, across every grade, is not a policy; it is an unfunded mandate.
[C1.5] If the tutoring materializes, it consumes funds that could improve placement and teacher quality within tracks. If it does not, the heterogeneous class becomes the remedial track by another name.
[C1.6] Advanced students lose the most. A weekly tutoring session does not replace a year of daily engagement with proof, abstraction, and accelerated content. Tutoring supplements depth; it does not generate it. The student who could take Algebra I in eighth grade now sits in general math, receiving enrichment on Thursday afternoon.
[C1.7] Tracking's real failure is rigid, early placement—not the existence of acceleration itself. The remedy is better screening, mid-year mobility, and investment in teacher quality. Do not demolish the one structure that protects advanced learning because its placement formula is flawed. Fix the formula, not the architecture.
