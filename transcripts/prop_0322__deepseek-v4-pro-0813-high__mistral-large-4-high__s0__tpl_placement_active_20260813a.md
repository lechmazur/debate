[P1.1] When a host-read ad works, it works because the audience believes a real person they trust is personally vouching for the product. An AI-generated voice or cloned likeness borrows that trust without the person’s live act of endorsement. The listener cannot detect the substitution, and the creator’s identity becomes raw material for an imitation that sounds exactly like the real thing. That is the core harm: the audience is being asked to rely on a human judgment that did not occur.
[P1.2] Disclosure is the minimum condition for consent. If a creator has authorized an AI clone to read copy, the audience still deserves to know the endorsement is synthetic, because “this is my honest opinion” and “this is a generated version of my voice reciting scripted praise” are not the same claim. If the clone is unauthorized, the deception is worse: a creator can be made to appear to endorse a product they never evaluated. A legal disclosure requirement deters this misuse and gives consumers and creators a clear violation to point to.
[P1.3] The rule is narrow. It does not ban AI voices, restrict content, or demand approval of every script. It requires a label at the point of persuasion. Existing advertising rules already require paid relationships to be disclosed; AI-generated identity is simply another material fact consumers need in order to evaluate an endorsement. “Synthetic” is no harder to label than “ad.” The same logic applies to video: an AI-rendered likeness that seems to deliver a personal testimonial is still a fabric of persuasion.
[P1.4] Opponents may frame this as a burden, but the real burden falls on listeners and creators who currently have no reliable way to know when personal identity has been simulated. A disclosure rule protects both the audience’s ability to choose and the creator’s reputation. In a market where trust is the main currency, requiring a clear label is not overregulation; it is basic honesty.

[C1.1] A legal mandate to disclose AI-generated voices is a blunt instrument that compels speech, chills legitimate innovation, and solves a problem existing law already addresses. The proposal cannot define its own trigger: "scripted likeness replication" is so vague it could capture accessibility tools for disabled creators, voice filters in comedy sketches, or authorized posthumous tributes. Faced with this uncertainty, solo podcasters will avoid AI entirely—even for translation or efficiency—to escape liability, while large media companies hire compliance teams to survive the regulatory burden that will consolidate power in major platforms.
[C1.2] The deeper flaw is redundancy. Fraud and deceptive advertising laws already require proof of intent to deceive and actual consumer reliance. If an AI voice is indistinguishable and tricks listeners, existing consumer protection and right of publicity statutes provide remedies. If the audience knows or reasonably should know it is synthetic, no deception occurs and no harm exists.
[C1.3] The proposed rule punishes the tool rather than the deception, creating a new category of liability for speech that causes no measurable injury. Liability should attach to the lie, not the software.
[C1.4] Worse, the mandate inverts the trust relationship it claims to protect. When a creator genuinely endorses a product but uses AI to read the ad in twelve languages—or because they are recovering from surgery—the endorsement remains authentic human judgment. Slapping a legal "AI" warning on that speech treats the creator's real opinion as suspect, compelling them to disclaim their own sincerity to an audience that never doubted them. That is not transparency; it is a government-mandated taint on truthful speech.
[C1.5] The law should target fraud, not technology. We already have the tools to punish actual deception without forcing every independent creator to navigate a regulatory minefield over voice synthesis.

A legal duty creates a pre-publication paper trail: platform ad records, AI vendor metadata, sponsor agreements, and complaint-based audits can expose an omitted label even when the audio is undetectable. Regulators enforce “sponsored” tags the same way—not by hearing the tag in the air, but by reviewing creator and platform records.
[P4.2] The listener’s inability to detect the clone is not an enforcement obstacle; it is the reason the law must shift the obligation from victim detection to creator disclosure.
[P4.3] A2: A required label need not say “inauthentic.” It says “AI-generated voice.” If a host genuinely endorses the product and lets a clone deliver the words, the truthful belief is not copied into audible fact; the audience hears what seems to be the host’s own spoken delivery—tone, emphasis, presence. That false presentation is the deception. Labeling the medium does not taint the endorsement; it prevents a synthetic double from passing as the person. A “reenactment” or “parody” label works the same way: it clarifies form, not sincerity.
[P4.4] The two answers meet at the opponent’s central problem. Fraud law is ex post and depends on a deceived listener noticing and suing. The AI clone destroys that notice. A disclosure mandate is ex ante: it makes the invisible substitution a reviewable omission, and it labels the synthetic delivery without condemning the underlying opinion.
[P4.5] That is why the rule should win—not because enforcement is easy, but because it is the only mechanism that gives both listeners and creators a chance to know what actually spoke.
