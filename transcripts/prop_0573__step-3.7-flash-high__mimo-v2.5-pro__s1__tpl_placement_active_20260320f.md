[P1.1] Streaming platforms currently operate as black boxes for the creatives whose work powers their entire business. They collect exhaustive, granular data on every view, every listen, every second of engagement with copyrighted content, but share only the thinnest, most opaque summaries with the rights holders who own that content—if they share anything at all. Last year, independent musicians reported widespread underpayment by major streaming services, with royalty statements that offered no breakdown of plays by region or genre, leaving artists with no way to verify if their per-stream rates were being applied correctly.
[P1.2] For small production studios licensing content to platforms, the problem is even worse: many deals include clauses that let platforms withhold raw viewership data entirely, so rights holders can’t tell if their content is being buried in recommendation algorithms or if they’re being shortchanged on revenue share. Requiring standardized, anonymized sharing of core viewing and payout data fixes this fundamental power imbalance without imposing meaningful burdens on platforms. The data in question is already generated by platforms’ own internal tracking systems; standardization just requires they share consistent, comparable metrics—total valid plays, geographic play breakdowns, applied per-unit payout rates, and average watch time for long-form content—rather than vague, unverifiable summaries.
[P1.3] This isn’t a radical intrusion into platform operations: it’s a basic condition for fair dealing, giving creators and rights holders the information they need to audit payments, negotiate better contracts, and make informed choices about where to distribute their work. It also benefits the broader creative ecosystem, because transparent payout metrics reduce the structural advantage of already dominant platforms, making it easier for independent creators and smaller studios to compete without having to trust unaccountable corporate black boxes.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds fair—who could oppose transparency? But mandating standardized data sharing would backfire on the very creators it aims to help, and it misdiagnoses the real problem.
[C1.2] Start with the word "standardized." Requiring every platform to report identical metrics across wildly different business models—a music streamer, a video-on-demand service, a short-form platform, a niche indie distributor—doesn't produce clarity. [C1.3] A regulatory body picking which metrics must be disclosed will inevitably favor the dominant platforms' existing practices while forcing smaller competitors to restructure their tracking to comply. That's not leveling the playing field; it's pouring concrete on it.
[C1.4] The real cost falls on newer and smaller platforms. Compliance with a standardized reporting mandate requires building audit-grade systems, legal review, and ongoing regulatory engagement. For Spotify or Netflix, that's a rounding error. For an indie music distributor trying to compete, it's a barrier to entry.
[C1.5] When we create mandates that sound like they protect the little guy, we should ask who actually bears the burden of compliance—and the answer here is overwhelmingly the upstarts.
[C1.6] Third, standardized disclosure exposes legitimate business intelligence. If every platform must publish comparable geographic breakdowns and per-unit payout rates, competitors gain a detailed map of each other's pricing and content performance. The PRO side frames this as creators verifying payments, but the same data lets platforms reverse-engineer each other's deals, undercut rivals, and consolidate further. Creators gain a spreadsheet; dominant platforms gain strategic ammunition.
[C1.7] None of this means creators should stay in the dark. The solution is contractual transparency—negotiated audit rights, deal-specific reporting, and industry-negotiated standards that can adapt as business models evolve. That path already exists and is gaining traction. A blanket mandate locks in a single rigid framework, raises costs for competitors, and exposes strategic data in ways that harm the creative ecosystem it's supposed to protect.
