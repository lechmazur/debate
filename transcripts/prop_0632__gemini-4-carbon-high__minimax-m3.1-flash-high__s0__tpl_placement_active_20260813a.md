[P1.1] Today, a health insurer will routinely cover a patient’s insulin, coronary stent, joint replacement, and bariatric surgery, yet flatly refuse to cover the FDA-authorized medication that treats the underlying obesity driving every one of those conditions. That carve-out is not sound medicine; it is outdated stigma disguised as cost control. Obesity is a chronic metabolic disease. When pharmacotherapy is clinically indicated, insurers should be required to cover it under disciplined, value-tied payment rules.
[P1.2] Left to the voluntary market, insurers fail patients because of a classic collective-action trap: member churn. Because enrollees switch plans every few years, individual insurers resist paying today to prevent heart disease, liver failure, or diabetes progression tomorrow, knowing a competitor will capture the downstream savings. A universal mandate breaks that gridlock so every plan invests in upstream care. Without required coverage, proven anti-obesity therapies are rationed by personal wealth.
[P1.3] Affluent buyers pay cash even for marginal weight loss, while working-class patients with severe metabolic risk, who bear the heaviest burden of obesity, are locked out of treatments their physicians prescribe.
[P1.4] Blanket exclusions are a cruel, blunt response to a pricing problem. Requiring coverage alongside value-based pricing or outcomes-based contracts fixes access and spending together. First, limiting coverage to clinically indicated cases screens out cosmetic demand and allows step therapy or formulary competition among both lower-cost anti-obesity drugs and competing newer therapies.
[P1.5] Second, value-based pricing anchors reimbursement to verified health gains rather than monopoly list prices, while outcomes-based contracts shift performance risk onto manufacturers. If a patient stops therapy early due to side effects or fails to hit meaningful weight and cardiometabolic milestones, the drugmaker owes substantial rebates.
[P1.6] Health insurance exists to pool medical risk, not to exclude the most prevalent chronic disease in modern clinical practice. Requiring clinically indicated coverage under strict value and outcomes guardrails aligns insurance with medical science while forcing pharmaceutical prices to earn their keep.

[C1.1] FDA authorization establishes that a drug may be marketed; it does not establish that every insurer must finance unlimited use of it. The proposition’s central promise—that mandatory coverage plus value-based contracts will control spending—is therefore unproven. Coverage mandates determine who pays; they do not automatically determine how much treatment costs.
[C1.2] “Clinically indicated” is doing dangerous work here. It can mean a physician considers medication appropriate, but obesity pharmacotherapy often involves long-term treatment, substantial discontinuation, and wide variation in patient response. A mandate may therefore convert a selective benefit into broad, recurring coverage without ensuring meaningful benefit for a particular patient. FDA approval alone cannot supply the missing comparative-effectiveness judgment.
[C1.3] Outcomes-based contracts do not solve this problem so neatly. They work best when outcomes are measurable, attributable to one intervention, and observed within a limited period. Anti-obesity treatment must often be judged over years, while weight change is influenced by adherence, side effects, concurrent care, and behavior outside the insurer’s control. Manufacturers may control much of the data, negotiate favorable definitions, or focus on easily gamed proxies such as initial weight loss.
[C1.4] If contracts exclude high-risk patients or impose strict targets, manufacturers can also avoid risk rather than reduce it.
[C1.5] Nor does value-based pricing guarantee lower premiums. A lower net price can be offset by more eligible patients, greater utilization, and the administrative costs of monitoring contracts. Rebates may compensate for mandated coverage while allowing list prices to remain inflated. More importantly, contracts can shift financial uncertainty among plans, manufacturers, and patients without removing the underlying cost.
[C1.6] The proposed policy thus risks both expanding spending and pretending measurement resolves uncertainty. Requiring coverage before establishing who benefits, what outcomes count, and how risk is distributed could make access broader while making the system less sustainable. Clinically justified coverage may merit consideration, but coverage by fiat is not value by itself.

[C2.1] The decisive flaw is the claim that “value-based pricing literally supplies” comparative-effectiveness discipline [P2.1]. A price can be tied to an outcome only after officials decide what counts, over what period, and how much benefit justifies the price. Those choices do not disappear because payment is called “value-based.” If uptake expands, even a lower unit price can produce substantially higher total spending—the very problem [C1.5] identified.
[C2.2] Nor does using existing data solve attribution [P2.3]. Pharmacy records, weights, and laboratory results show what happened, not necessarily what the medication caused. Weight can change with adherence, concurrent treatment, behavior, and ordinary biological variation. Twelve-month persistence is not the same as improved health; tying rebates to persistence could reward retention rather than benefit, while excluding people unlikely to meet an arbitrary target would produce exactly the selection risk [C1.4] describes.
[C2.3] If contracts require full-risk enrollment, manufacturers may simply decline them or demand a higher price. Thus, contracts do not control spending automatically; they redistribute it.
[C2.4] Upfront value-based pricing does not necessarily avoid tracking either [P2.4]. It merely moves measurement upstream. A manufacturer may accept a lower price for a drug whose long-term complications remain uncertain, leaving insurers with uncertainty rather than evidence. Conversely, insurers may demand steep rebates that manufacturers offset through restrictions, patient support costs, or higher launch prices.
[C2.5] Finally, the churn argument [P1.2] does not establish that this mandate is the right response. If future savings follow the patient across insurers, each insurer may rationally fund effective upstream care; if they do not follow, the claimed collective-action failure largely disappears. And the projected savings are uncertain, while the mandate’s costs are immediate and certain.
[C2.6] The proposal therefore guarantees access before resolving who benefits, how value is measured, and how risk is allocated. That can broaden treatment without demonstrating sustainable value. Clinical-indication criteria and negotiated coverage are preferable to an FDA-approval-based mandate with uncertain savings.
