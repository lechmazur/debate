[P1.1] Young children build the core circuitry for reading and writing through the physical act of forming letters on paper. Handwriting recruits motor memory and visual recognition pathways that typing on a tablet bypasses, leaving weaker traces for later retrieval. When early-grade classrooms shift that work to screens, those pathways receive less practice precisely when they are most plastic.
[P1.2] Paper also removes the constant option to switch tasks. A worksheet stays in front of the child until it is finished; a tablet invites taps, notifications, and quick exits. The difference shows up in sustained attention during the same lesson: children on paper stay longer on the target skill before drifting, while device users fragment their effort across multiple apps. That fragmentation compounds across hundreds of school days.
[P1.3] Foundational skills formed this way transfer forward. Students who master decoding and basic composition on paper enter later grades with stronger independent reading stamina. One-to-one tablets introduced after third grade can then serve as research or production tools rather than substitutes for skills never fully consolidated. The device-light window therefore does not block technology; it sequences it after the period when paper produces clearer gains in the skills technology will later rely upon.
[P1.4] Arguments that equate access with outcome overlook this sequence. Equal distribution of tablets does not guarantee equal development of the attention and motor habits that paper cultivates most directly in the early years. A policy that protects those years keeps later digital tools from operating on an incomplete base.

[P2.1] The claim that early literacy programs simply combine paper and screens under teacher choice ignores how the presence of a tablet changes the classroom default. Once devices are in the room for any approved use, they become the faster, more novel option for transitions, rewards, and independent work, pulling time away from sustained paper practice precisely when attention habits are forming. The sequencing argument in the motion is not about banning tools forever; it is about protecting the narrow window when motor and attentional foundations are most efficiently built without competition.
[P2.2] On pathways, the alternative routes offered—typing with visual feedback or tracing on glass—still lack the consistent proprioceptive feedback of pressing a pencil into paper. Children using those methods show faster fatigue and more frequent gaze shifts away from the letter itself. Paper removes the need for that monitoring, letting the loop between hand, eye, and memory close without external prompts.
[P2.3] Equity concerns cut the other way. Children who arrive without home literacy exposure need the most reliable attention practice, not the most varied menu. A tablet that offers quick exits and colorful rewards trains the very switching behavior that later makes independent reading difficult. Paper gives every child the same uninterrupted surface on which to build stamina, narrowing rather than widening the gap between those with and without external support.
[P2.4] The reversal costs cited are real but overstated as a reason to keep an inferior default. Districts already maintain paper systems for testing and art; expanding their use in the earliest grades requires no new infrastructure, only the decision to withhold devices until the skills they will later support are in place. Teacher judgment remains intact for choosing among paper activities; the policy merely removes one category of tool whose side effects on focus are hardest to manage when children are youngest.
