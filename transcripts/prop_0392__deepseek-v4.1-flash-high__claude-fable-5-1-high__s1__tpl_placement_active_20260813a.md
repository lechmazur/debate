[P1.1] A blind parent trying to renew benefits, a Deaf worker checking safety training, a switch user booking a transit ride: when a public-facing digital service repeatedly fails basic accessibility, the barrier is not cosmetic. It is exclusion.
[P1.2] The motion is deliberately narrow. It targets repeated failures, not one-off bugs. It targets basic standards—keyboard navigation, screen-reader labels, captions, contrast—not perfection. And it demands meaningful fines, not ruinous ones.
[P1.3] That leaves room for notice, cure periods, technical help, and penalties scaled to the provider’s size. So the real question is simple: after a service has been told, again and again, that disabled users cannot use it, should there be any consequence that actually changes behavior?
[P1.4] Without credible fines, the answer is usually no. Accessibility becomes a project for a quiet quarter, then forgotten. Complaint-driven negotiation can take years, one user at a time, while the same broken checkout, portal, or video remains live. Each disabled user must become an unpaid tester and reluctant litigant. That is not enforcement; it is endurance.
[P1.5] Fines flip the incentive. They make it cheaper to fix the barrier than to keep excluding people. They fund compliance rather than press releases. They also fit the principle that public-facing services are gateways: benefits, banking, health, transport, education.
[P1.6] If a provider repeatedly blocks disabled customers from those gateways, it is not merely inconveniencing them; it is denying equal access.
[P1.7] Opponents will warn about cost and complexity. But repeated failure after notice is a choice, and the cost of inaccessible design is already being paid—by disabled users, in lost time, money, dignity, and independence. Meaningful fines do not demand a perfect website. They demand that repeated exclusion carry a real price.
[P1.8] The question is not whether we can afford to fine persistent exclusion. It is whether disabled users can afford another decade of polite reminders.

[C1.1] The strongest case against this motion is not that accessibility doesn't matter. It is that fines are the wrong tool, and the places that have tried them prove it.
[C1.2] Look at what fine-driven accessibility actually produces. In the United States, the threat of penalties and damages under web-accessibility litigation spawned an entire industry of "overlay" widgets—one-line scripts promising instant compliance. Blind users widely describe them as making sites worse, because the overlay fights with their screen reader. Yet thousands of businesses bought them, because a penalty regime rewards whatever makes the auditor go away, not whatever makes the checkout work.
[C1.3] Fines optimize for the checkbox, not the person.
[C1.4] Second, fines direct money away from the fix. A fined organization spends on lawyers, compliance consultants, and paperwork, and the fine itself goes to the treasury—not to the blind parent, not to remediation. Every euro paid as penalty is a euro not spent on the developer who could relabel the form. PRO says fines "fund compliance."
[C1.5] They don't. They fund the state and the defense bar.
[C1.6] Third, consider who "public-facing digital services" really covers: the rural clinic's booking page, the food bank's volunteer portal, the small transit agency, the independent news site. Faced with a penalty they cannot absorb, rational operators don't upgrade—they remove. Videos get deleted rather than captioned. Online booking gets replaced with "call us."
[C1.7] Features vanish for everyone, disabled users first.
[C1.8] PRO frames the choice as fines versus "polite reminders." That is a false pair. Binding remediation orders with deadlines, court injunctions, procurement bans, public compliance scorecards, and an individual right to compensation or an accessible alternative are all real consequences—and each ties the pressure directly to fixing the barrier rather than pricing it. A remediation order cannot be paid off.
[C1.9] A fine can.
[C1.10] The test is simple: after the penalty is levied, can the switch user book the ride? Fines answer that question with a receipt. The alternatives answer it with a working page.
