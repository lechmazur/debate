[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a common story for a common citizenship. After civil war, the state is often controlled by one former belligerent, its institutions are distrusted, and communities dispute not merely interpretation but basic facts. Encouraging that state to emphasize one civic account does not reliably produce honesty. It creates a contest over who defines “civic,” with minorities asked to reconcile before they are secure enough to dissent.
[C1.2] PRO says every victim can be named as a citizen. But naming victims does not itself explain why particular people were targeted, which institutions organized violence, or whether responsibility was symmetrical. A monument to a “catastrophe citizens survived together” can sound inclusive while converting ethnic cleansing into impersonal misfortune and perpetration into a fog of collective guilt. Truth sometimes requires distinction, not amalgamation.
[C1.3] Bosnia and Northern Ireland do not prove that separate remembrance causes renewed conflict. Ethnic denial, political partition, and territorial paramilitary murals are abuses of memory, not evidence that victim communities must surrender autonomous remembrance. The alternative to an emphasized shared account is not state-funded hatred. It is protected community remembrance, documentation, and contestation, alongside prohibitions on incitement.
[C1.4] Premature consensus also drives grievance underground. A survivor told that mourning must first speak the state’s language of unity learns that recognition remains conditional. Conversely, children learn civic equality when a government protects a neighbor’s memorial even though it did not write that community’s story. Plural protection demonstrates equal citizenship more credibly than uniform inscription.
[C1.5] Shared civic commitments are desirable; a shared authoritative narrative is not their prerequisite. Preservation keeps testimony available for correction, confrontation, and eventual common understanding. PRO offers the appearance of reconciliation by centralizing memory. CON protects the plural conditions under which reconciliation can become real: no community must abandon its dead, suppress its account of responsibility, or trust its former enemies to narrate its suffering before its voice can be heard.
