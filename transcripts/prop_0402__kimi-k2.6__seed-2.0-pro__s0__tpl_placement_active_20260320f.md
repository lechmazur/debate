[P1.1] Housing is a necessity, yet across the country, qualified families holding lawful housing vouchers are turned away at the door—not because they failed a background check or lack references, but simply because their rent check comes from public assistance. This practice, source-of-income discrimination, transforms a tool meant to break poverty into a dead letter. When a mother presents a spotless rental record and a federally guaranteed voucher, only to hear "we don't take Section 8," the market has failed.
[P1.2] The motion is narrow and precise: it bars rejection based solely on payment type when a tenant is otherwise qualified. A voucher is legally binding government income, often more reliable than a private paycheck. If a landlord accepts a tenant’s credit score, references, and rental history, the logo on the check is economically irrelevant. What matters is whether the rent arrives, and under voucher programs, it does.
[P1.3] Denying housing on this basis inserts a barrier that has nothing to do with tenancy quality and everything to do with class bias.
[P1.4] Beyond individual fairness, the public interest collapses when voucher holders are systematically blocked. Communities spend millions issuing vouchers that expire unused while families languish in shelters or substandard housing. Taxpayers fund a ladder out of poverty, but private actors cut the rungs at the property line. Every refused voucher is a public investment flushed away.
[P1.5] Opponents will invoke property rights, but this is not a mandate to rent to anyone. Landlords retain full discretion to reject on credit, behavior, or past damage. What they cannot do is impose a blanket ban on a lawfully earned income stream that society has deliberately provided. We already prohibit discrimination based on race, religion, and family status because housing access is foundational.
[P1.6] Extending that shield to lawful public assistance is a modest, necessary step: it stops landlords from freeloading on public subsidies like mortgage deductions while refusing the very families those subsidies are meant to house.
[P1.7] Let families with vouchers compete on qualifications, not on prejudice.

[C1.1] This rule sounds fair right up until you stop talking about the check and start talking about everything else that comes attached to it. Nobody is arguing that voucher holders are bad tenants. What PRO will not acknowledge is that accepting a voucher is not just accepting a rent payment—it is agreeing to an entire separate one-sided government contract, imposed with zero compensation, that does not apply to any other tenant. When you rent to a private tenant, you set maintenance timelines, resolve disputes directly, get paid on the first of the month.
[C1.2] When you accept a voucher, you submit to mandatory annual inspections, dozens of pages of extra paperwork every year, payment delays that regularly stretch six weeks or more, and rigid repair mandates that can cost thousands of dollars above normal wear and tear. There is no adjustment to the rent for this extra work and risk. The state writes the rules, then forces private landlords to eat the cost. This is not class bias.
[C1.3] No one forces a plumber to accept government work that pays standard rates but comes with triple the overhead. No one tells a grocer they cannot decline public food benefits if the state reimburses late and requires extra audits. Only small landlords are told they must accept an unfavorable contract, or be guilty of discrimination. Worse, the program itself fails constantly.
[C1.4] Vouchers are cancelled unexpectedly all the time, through no fault of the tenant, leaving the landlord holding months of unpaid rent with no recourse. Landlords are not rejecting the person. They are rejecting the broken government baggage that comes attached to their payment. This motion does not fix the voucher program.
[C1.5] It does not compensate landlords for the burdens it imposes. It just makes refusal illegal. That is not justice. That is offloading public failure onto private people.
