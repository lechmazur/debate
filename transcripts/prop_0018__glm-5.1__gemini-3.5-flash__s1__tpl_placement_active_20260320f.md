[P1.1] Congestion pricing is not an anti-driver policy—it is the only proven tool that can rescue cities from the systemic failure of unpriced road access. When a central district has no entry cost, every additional driver treats the road as free for them but imposes steep costs on everyone else: longer commutes, dirtier air, slower emergency vehicles, and degraded bus service for the transit-dependent majority. This is an externality problem, and pricing is the corrective mechanism.
[P1.2] The empirical record is unambiguous. London's congestion charge cut central traffic by roughly thirty percent and boosted bus reliability. Stockholm saw similar reductions after introducing its charge, and—crucially—public opinion flipped from majority opposition before implementation to majority support afterward, once residents experienced the benefits directly.
[P1.3] This pattern exposes the core weakness of the opposition's stance: driver opposition is based on the pre-implementation status quo, not on the lived reality after pricing takes effect. Drivers predict misery, but what actually arrives is faster travel. Time savings alone often repay the fee many times over for commercial and freight traffic, and the revenue stream funds transit improvements that give everyone a genuine alternative to driving.
[P1.4] More fundamentally, cities must govern for the whole population, not grant a veto to the subgroup that prefers the current arrangement. Commuters from outside the district consume road space and pollute air inside it while contributing nothing toward those costs. Residents who cannot afford a car sit on buses stuck in traffic they did not create. Elevating driver opposition to a blocking veto entrenches this inequity.
[P1.5] The motion asks whether cities should act even against opposition, and the answer is yes—because that opposition is predictable, temporary, and outweighed by the lasting gains in mobility, air quality, and fairness that congestion pricing delivers.
