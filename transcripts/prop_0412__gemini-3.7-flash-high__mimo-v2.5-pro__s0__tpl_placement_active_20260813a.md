[P1.1] Commercial disclosure laws exist for a straightforward reason: consumers have a right to know when an endorsement is purchased rather than authentic. For decades, traditional media personalities—from television hosts to radio broadcasters—have been bound by strict, unambiguous disclosure mandates. Today, top-tier influencers command audiences larger than prime-time television broadcasts, wielding immense market power. When an individual’s reach and commercial function mirror or exceed that of traditional broadcast media, the legal standards governing their advertisements must be equally stringent.
[P1.2] The case for parity is even more urgent because influencer marketing carries a heightened risk of consumer deception. Traditional media maintains clear structural boundaries: commercial breaks, distinct banners, and explicit sponsor segments. Influencers, however, monetize personal trust. They cultivate parasocial intimacy, seamlessly weaving paid endorsements into organic lifestyle content, personal confessions, and daily routines.
[P1.3] When an audience believes it is receiving an honest recommendation from a trusted peer, their critical defenses drop. Burying an ambiguous hashtag deep within a caption does not cure this deception. Without strict, standardized visual and audio disclosures, commercial persuasion masquerades as genuine friendship.
[P1.4] Furthermore, maintaining weaker rules for digital creators creates a perverse regulatory loophole. Brands actively divert marketing budgets toward covert influencer campaigns precisely because lax enforcement allows them to bypass the transparency expected in legacy media, disproportionately targeting younger, more impressionable demographics. Influencers with large audiences are not amateur hobbyists operating in a vacuum; they are sophisticated, highly lucrative commercial enterprises with professional management and major sponsor contracts.
[P1.5] If an individual commands the reach and commercial power of a broadcast network, they must shoulder the corresponding responsibility to be transparent. Consumer protection cannot stop at the edge of the digital screen.
