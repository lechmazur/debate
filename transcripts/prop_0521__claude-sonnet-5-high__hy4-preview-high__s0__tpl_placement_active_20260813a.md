[P1.1] Portable training benefits within a sector are simply the modern equivalent of a pension you don't lose when you switch employers. Nobody thinks that's radical for retirement savings; it shouldn't be radical for skills either. That's the frame this debate should turn on: is job-tied training investment good policy, or is it a relic that punishes workers for doing something completely normal — moving to a better job in the field they already trained for?
[P1.2] Consider a nurse who gets an employer-funded certification in a specialized procedure, then moves from one hospital to another in the same city because the new job pays better or has better hours. Under the current system, she may lose access to continuing education credits, reimbursement for further certification, or a training stipend she was midway through using. That's not a disincentive to job-hop frivolously — it's a disincentive to take the better job at all, even when the move benefits patients, the new employer, and her own household.
[P1.3] Sector-portable accounts fix exactly this without touching the employer's right to hire and fire freely.
[P1.4] Three reasons this matters enough to decide the round.
[P1.5] First, efficiency: the whole logic of sectoral labor markets is that skills transfer. If a welder's certification is useful at any manufacturing plant in the region, tying the training dollars that produced that certification to one specific employer creates an artificial lock-in that wastes the very expertise the training was meant to build.
[P1.6] Second, fairness: workers, not employers, bear the career risk of an economic downturn, an employer's bad management, or a plant closure. Training investment earned through labor should follow the worker, the same way accrued vacation pay or vested retirement contributions already do in most jurisdictions.
[P1.7] Third, sector health: portability raises the incentive for every employer in the sector to invest in training at all, since workers who know their training won't vanish on exit are more willing to accept training-intensive roles in the first place, growing the whole sector's skill base rather than just one firm's.

[C1.1] A portable training account is not a pension [P1.1]. Pensions vest because the worker already earned them through labor; employer-funded training is a conditional investment to meet current business needs, often with repayment terms. Tuition aid and certification stipends are tied to a role, not an owned savings account. Any unspent portion is a conditional promise, not deferred pay the worker carries away.
[C1.2] The decisive question is who still pays. If benefits remain portable across the same sector, the training becomes general, so employers capture less return and rationally fund less. PRO's nurse [P1.2] shows the trap: a hospital will not keep paying for specialized certifications if leftover credits and a stipend follow the nurse to a rival.
[C1.3] Instead, it will tighten repayment clauses, narrow training to firm-specific onboarding, or cancel the benefit. No firm wants to be the trainer whose rival hires the product; the sector's skill base shrinks. That is the opposite of the sector-health gain PRO predicts [P1.7].
[C1.4] Portability also overrides bargained-for terms. Employers fund training under contracts that often require repayment if the worker leaves; that is how they justify outlays on general skills. Mandating portability converts a conditional allowance into an exit subsidy the former employer pays a rival's hire. Non-mobile workers then subsidize mobile ones through lower entry pay or thinner training offers.
[C1.5] PRO's fairness frame [P1.6] inverts: the voluntary leaver keeps the benefit, while the investing employer and co-workers who stay absorb the loss.
[C1.6] Picture a manufacturer's $4,000 advanced-welding allowance. If the balance follows a technician to another plant in the same regional sector, the original firm has funded a competitor's labor quality and will simply stop offering it. Multiply that across firms. The motion sounds pro-worker, but it removes the payer, shrinks the training pool, and leaves the workers it claims to help with fewer skills and weaker benefits.

Employers still fund training when the sector jointly internalizes the benefit, because they all need a trained labor pool to draw from.
[P2.4] CON assumes firms respond to portability only by cutting; the observed response in existing portable systems is co-investment, because everyone needs the same skilled workers.
[P2.5] On C1.4 and C1.5: nothing in this proposition bans repayment clauses for workers who leave the sector entirely or leave within some short vesting window. It only guarantees that a worker who stays in the same sector — the exact skills the training was meant to build — doesn't forfeit them. [P2.6] CON's real argument is "employers will retaliate by training less." That's an argument for designing portability well, not against portability at all.
