[P1.1] A hijab does not stop a student from solving equations. A turban does not stop an engineer from writing code. [P1.2] Start with what the motion actually says. It concedes the hard cases up front: where safety genuinely requires it — a welder's mask, identification checks, sterile operating rooms — restrictions stand. The question is only whether institutions may ban religious and modest dress when no such functional reason exists. That is a narrow, reasonable claim, and it puts the burden squarely on the other side: name the concrete harm a headscarf inflicts on a classroom.
[P1.3] Three reasons the ban-by-neutrality approach fails.
[P1.4] First, these rules are neutral in name only. A dress code that forbids head coverings burdens the Muslim woman, the Sikh man, the Orthodox Jew — and costs the bareheaded majority nothing. A rule whose entire practical weight falls on religious minorities is discrimination with better paperwork.
[P1.5] Second, the real-world effect is exclusion, not integration. When France and parts of Europe restricted headscarves, observant women didn't simply uncover; many withdrew from schools and formal employment. If your stated goal is inclusion and your policy pushes women out of education and work, the policy refutes itself. You do not liberate anyone by giving her a choice between her conscience and her diploma.
[P1.6] Third, institutions should judge people by performance, not costume. A workplace that tolerates tattoos, crosses on necklaces, and branded sneakers but draws the line at a headscarf is not preserving professionalism; it is deciding whose identity counts as normal.
[P1.7] Pluralism means the shared space belongs to everyone as they actually are, not as the majority prefers them dressed. Unless safety demands otherwise — and the motion already grants that — the case for these bans collapses into taste enforced by power.
