[P1.1] Cities that want fewer traffic deaths must look where deaths actually happen: in the moments before a crash, not after it. The single most powerful lever is speed. Kinetic energy rises with the square of velocity, so a drop from 50 km/h to 30 km/h cuts crash energy by more than half and slashes the chance that a collision is fatal by roughly 20 % for every 5 km/h reduction. That relationship is not a theory; it is the empirical backbone of Vision Zero programs in places like New York, where the default limit was lowered to 25 mph and fatalities fell sharply within a few years.
[P1.2] Speed limits alone are not enough; street redesign multiplies the effect. Narrower lanes, raised crosswalks, protected bike lanes, and traffic‑calming measures force drivers to travel slower and make the road environment forgiving of human error. When a street is physically engineered for low speeds, crashes become less frequent and, when they do occur, far less lethal. European cities that adopted 30 km/h zones city‑wide reported 30‑40 % drops in pedestrian deaths, a result that punitive measures never achieved.
[P1.3] Post‑crash penalties, by contrast, act only after a tragedy has already unfolded. Harsher fines or criminal sanctions deter intentional risk‑taking but do little for the majority of fatal crashes, which are caused by inattention, misjudgment, or momentary lapse—behaviors that drivers rarely calculate against a future fine. Enforcement is also uneven, imposing heavier burdens on low‑income drivers while leaving the underlying speed problem untouched. These interventions also cost far less per life saved than expanding policing or courts, making them the fiscally responsible choice.
[P1.4] Therefore, if the goal is to reduce deaths, cities should invest first in lower default speed limits and street redesign, because they prevent crashes and lessen their severity at the source. Penalties can remain as a secondary tool, but they should never be the primary strategy.
