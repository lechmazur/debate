[P1.1] Start with the people this tax supposedly hurts. Type 2 diabetes, obesity, and tooth decay are not spread evenly across society. They cluster in lower-income neighborhoods, the same places where cheap, heavily marketed soda is most consumed. Any honest accounting of who pays must count both the few dollars at the checkout and the amputations, dialysis, and lost working years.
[P1.2] On that ledger, a soda tax is regressive in cents and progressive in health.
[P1.3] My case rests on three claims.
[P1.4] First, price works. Sugary drinks are a near-ideal target: liquid calories that don't fill you up, no nutritional need for them, and cheap substitutes like water and unsweetened drinks. When Mexico introduced a national soda tax of roughly ten percent, purchases fell, and the drop was largest among the poorest households. Several American cities that taxed sugary drinks saw similar declines.
[P1.5] The motion's threshold, "meaningfully shift consumption," matters here. A token tax is pure revenue with little health effect. A serious tax changes the default choice on the shelf.
[P1.6] Second, the tax changes what companies sell, not just what people buy. Britain's tiered levy charged more for higher-sugar drinks, and manufacturers reformulated many products before it even took effect. That means children drank less sugar without anyone paying the tax at all. No education campaign has ever moved an entire industry that fast.
[P1.7] Third, the regressivity objection proves less than it claims. Lower-income households pay more of the tax as a share of income precisely because they are more price-sensitive, which means they also cut consumption the most and capture the largest health gains. The tax is fully avoidable by switching drinks. And the revenue can flow straight back to the same communities through school meals, clean drinking fountains, or pre-K programs, as some cities have already done.
[P1.8] So the real question is not whether poorer families pay a few extra cents. It is whether we accept a status quo that quietly charges them in disease. Leaving soda cheap is the far more regressive policy.

[C1.3] The PRO side calls the tax "avoidable," as if choosing water were as simple as choosing a different brand. [C1.5] The revenue-recycling argument is a political fantasy. [C1.6] The motion explicitly concedes the burden falls disproportionately on lower-income households. It asks us to accept that as the price of doing something. But there is no moral ledger where charging a diabetic mother extra for her child's birthday soda balances against a bureaucratic promise of future school lunches. The state does not get to confiscate the poor's grocery money and call it medicine.
[C1.7] We reject this tax not because we oppose health, but because we oppose balancing budgets on the backs of those with the fewest backs to bear it.
