[P1.1] Early secondary education sits at a developmental crossroads where attention span and social competence are either cemented or eroded. This is not a neutral choice; it actively shapes neural development. One-to-one device programs systematically tip the scales toward erosion. The always-on nature of personal devices fragments focus, conditioning young minds to expect constant switching rather than the sustained concentration essential for complex problem-solving.
[P1.2] Studies consistently find that students with access to laptops or tablets during instruction perform worse on comprehension tests, not because they lack ability, but because the devices invite distraction.
[P1.3] More damaging is the displacement of social learning. When every student has an individual screen, group work becomes parallel solitary work, classroom discussions shrink, and the rich, unscripted practice of reading facial expressions, negotiating differences, and building trust goes missing. These are not soft skills—they are the very capacities that predict long-term academic persistence and career success more strongly than technical proficiency alone. Consider the middle school debate team that must now compete with students scrolling social media during prep time, or the science lab where partners sit side-by-side but screen-to-screen.
[P1.4] These examples illustrate a broader pattern: technology in education too often replaces rather than enhances human interaction.
[P1.5] Meanwhile, the educational case for one-to-one programs is weak: large-scale reviews show no reliable boost to test scores once socioeconomic factors are accounted for. The reliable outcomes are increased off-task behavior, shallower processing of information, and reduced peer interaction. In early adolescence, when the brain's social cognition and executive function networks are rapidly developing, schools must prioritize environments that strengthen these systems. Devices can supplement specific lessons, but as a default medium, they turn education into isolated screen time rather than shared human experience.
[P1.6] The opportunity cost is too high: we are sacrificing the development of sustained attention and rich social bonds—capabilities that serve students for life—for a marginal and unproven academic benefit. That is a poor trade for any school system. We must choose wisely.
