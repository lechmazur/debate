[C1.1] The proposition's slogan—"planning preserves choice" [P1.2]—gets the mechanism backwards. Once a city formally designates a neighborhood for eventual retreat, banks, insurers, employers, and schools read that as a death sentence. Mortgages freeze, insurance retreats, maintenance stops, and property values fall before a single storm.
[C1.2] That is not preparation; it is state-created blight. The residents least able to leave—renters, the elderly, the poor—are trapped in decaying blocks while wealthier households flee. The mandate manufactures the chaos it claims to prevent.
[C1.3] It also bets on knowledge cities do not have. Sea-level and storm projections vary street by street. Subsidence, drainage, levees, and building standards can change timelines. A mandatory plan forces officials to declare land doomed decades early, then defend that verdict against litigation and politics.
[C1.4] If adaptation works, the city has destroyed a viable community for nothing. If the threat is worse, a rigid pre-commitment may still be the wrong response.
[C1.5] And mandates crowd out what actually protects people now: flood defenses, sewer upgrades, elevation, stricter building codes, and buyouts after real repeated losses. Cities have finite money and political capital. A preemptive relocation mandate consumes both, and it makes residents suspicious of every levee or drainage project—because they will ask whether it is protection or a prelude to removal.
[C1.6] The alternative is not denial. It is sequenced, funded, voluntary adaptation: defend where defense is feasible; stop new construction in genuinely unsafe zones; offer generous buyouts and relocation help to those who choose them; and let local communities set the pace with real compensation. If retreat becomes unavoidable, that path moves people with consent and resources. The proposition moves them by making their homes worthless first.
