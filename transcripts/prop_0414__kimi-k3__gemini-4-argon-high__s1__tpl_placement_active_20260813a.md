[P1.1] Venice's day-trippers leave behind mountains of trash. Barcelona's residents watch apartments convert to holiday flats. Amsterdam's trams fill with suitcases while commuters wait on the platform. Tourist taxes exist because tourism imposes real, concentrated costs — the only question is whether the revenue repairs that damage or vanishes into a general ledger.
[P1.2] Earmarking wins for three reasons.
[P1.3] First, justice. A tax should offset the harm its payers create. Tourism's burden is not abstract: it falls precisely on housing supply, transit capacity, and sanitation in the districts visitors flood. When the money instead disappears into general overhead, residents pay for tourism twice — once in degraded neighborhoods, again in watching the compensation evaporate.
[P1.4] Earmarking is the polluter-pays principle applied to overtourism.
[P1.5] Second, durability. Visible benefits are what keep these taxes alive. The gravest threat to tourist levies is not hotel lobbies; it is resident resentment boiling into hostility toward tourism itself, the kind of protests seen across southern Europe. A tax that delivers more frequent metros, cleaner streets, and actual affordable units buys the social license tourism depends on.
[P1.6] An invisible tax buys cynicism — and cynical voters eventually kill the tax, or the welcome.
[P1.7] Third, accountability. General funds are fungible to the point of disappearance, and housing, transit, and sanitation are chronically underfunded precisely because their budgets are easy to raid. Earmarking converts an unreliable promise into a guarantee — and the revenue automatically scales with the stress. Big festival weekend, bigger sanitation budget, no lobbying required.
[P1.8] Note the motion says "primarily." Cities keep flexibility for genuine shocks; they lose only the option to treat a corrective tax as a slush fund.
[P1.9] That sets the clash: flexibility versus repair. Flexibility is exactly how this tax fails the residents who justify its existence.

[C1.3] General budgets force accountability over total funding levels rather than hiding baseline cuts behind earmarked labels.

Under PRO's model, when a downturn hits and visitors stay home, housing and transit budgets collapse at the exact moment struggling residents need them most.
[C1.5] General budgets pool diverse revenues to smooth out downturns; earmarking transmits every tourism slump directly into core infrastructure.
[C1.6] Third, rigid silos misread urban reality. Tourists do not only use trams and bins [P1.1]; they strain emergency rooms, policing, parks, water systems, and historic sites. Moreover, the housing crisis in places like Barcelona [P1.1] requires zoning reform and holiday-let caps, not volatile hotel taxes. Locking the primary share of revenue into three fixed boxes starves urgent municipal priorities, whereas general budgets let elected leaders direct every dollar to where the crisis actually is.
