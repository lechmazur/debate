[P1.1] A policy can be clever and still have no right to govern anyone. Competence answers how well a decision works; legitimacy answers who is entitled to make it, who must bear its costs, and why losers should comply. For coercive public decisions — taxing, mandating, zoning, policing — that second question comes first, because without it competence is just well-organized power.
[P1.2] First, legitimacy is what makes competence usable in the real world. A technically optimal public-health rule, congestion charge, or housing plan collapses if citizens see it as imposed by unaccountable outsiders. They evade, litigate, protest, and vote it out. Democratic authorization, by contrast, buys the trust and forbearance that implementation requires: people accept burdens they dislike when they had a voice, can see reasons, and can punish abuse.
[P1.3] Strip that away and the 'best' policy becomes brittle — efficient on paper, ungovernable in practice.
[P1.4] Second, technocracy smuggles values in as if they were facts. Experts can estimate effects, but they cannot scientifically decide how much inequality to tolerate for growth, what risk to impose on one neighborhood for another's benefit, or whose freedom yields to safety. Those are moral and distributive choices. When unelected specialists decide them under the language of efficiency, they do not remove politics; they hide it from challenge.
[P1.5] Democracy makes the trade-off explicit and gives those who live with it equal standing to contest it.
[P1.6] Finally, legitimacy provides correction that competence cannot provide for itself. Experts share training, incentives, and blind spots, and insulated institutions double down rather than admit error — as with grand planning schemes that demolished neighborhoods or economic models that missed fragility before a crash. Elections, courts, a free press, and protest create feedback: bad rulers can be removed without violence. A system can survive mediocre decisions and learn; it cannot long survive decisions, however polished, that people experience as rule by others.
[P1.7] That is why legitimacy matters more.
