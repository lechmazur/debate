[P1.1] Mass university expansion rested on a seductive fallacy: that if a university degree helped a small fraction of citizens reach the upper-middle class, giving degrees to everyone would universalize that prosperity. Instead, it mistook a positional good for an absolute one. You cannot create twice as many lucrative, high-skill jobs simply by printing twice as many diplomas.
[P1.2] Because the labor market creates jobs based on economic demand rather than graduate supply, flooding the market with degrees primarily triggered a signaling arms race. For employers, a degree often functions as a cheap screening filter. When university attendance became the norm, employers did not radically upgrade the complexity or pay of existing work; they simply raised the entry threshold. Roles that capable workers once entered straight from secondary school—office managers, insurance adjusters, municipal clerks, and retail supervisors—were recategorized as "graduate jobs."
[P1.3] The tasks did not suddenly require four years of academic theory; the diploma merely replaced on-the-job training.
[P1.4] This shift means credential inflation decisively outpaced genuine opportunity. Genuine opportunity means expanding pathways to substantive economic security and upward mobility. Instead, mass expansion turned the bachelor’s degree from an engine of mobility into a defensive necessity. Today’s graduates absorb years of tuition, debt, and forgone wages just to compete for the exact same tier of middle-income stability their parents accessed at eighteen.
[P1.5] Charging young people a four-year toll to stand in the same economic place is not opportunity; it is inflation.
[P1.6] Worse, this inflation actively damaged mobility at both ends of the labor market. Workers without degrees were systematically locked out of stable career ladders they once climbed through proven competence. Meanwhile, as the ordinary bachelor's degree lost its scarcity value, affluent families simply moved the goalposts again—shifting the sorting mechanism toward elite university brands, unpaid internships, and master's degrees. Mass expansion multiplied credentials, raised the price of entry into adulthood, and left the underlying hierarchy intact.
