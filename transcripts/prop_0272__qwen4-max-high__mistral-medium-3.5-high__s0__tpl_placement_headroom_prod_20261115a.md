[P1.1] The operative word is "before." No one need unplug a laptop. The question is which queue gets the next dollar and the next unlocked building hour, and that ordering should follow one test: what can a school supply that a child cannot get anywhere else?
[P1.2] A login is the most substitutable thing a school can hand a child. Enrichment software competes with free videos and a phone in a pocket. A lit field, a kiln, a stage, an adult who can teach a layup or a chord — those cannot be downloaded. When money is finite, fund the scarce input first, not the abundant one.
[P1.3] Consider what that hour trains. Unstructured play is where children invent rules, argue, sulk, renegotiate, and keep playing anyway — rehearsal for self-government no app scripts for them. A team makes a child show up for other people. An ensemble makes fifty strangers breathe together.
[P1.4] Bodies get tired and sleep better. Picture a fourth-grader whose mother works until seven: his alternative is not a curated platform, it is a screen alone. Give him a ball and a coach.
[P1.5] CON will say digital scales cheaply. But the equity gap in most districts is no longer primarily hardware; it is safe, supervised hours, and those are exactly what a screen cannot supply. Software carries subscription churn and device refresh; a gym floor and a drama teacher serve cohorts for decades. And sports and band are often why a disengaged student attends school at all — digital enrichment inherits that motivation, it does not create it.
[P1.6] So the burden is specific: name what purely digital enrichment delivers that a coached, embodied, shared hour does not, and show it beats what it displaces. Sequencing is not rejection. It is spending first where the school is irreplaceable.

[C1.1] Public schools should not artificially cap digital enrichment to favor traditional activities. The premise assumes a zero-sum budget, but even if resources are finite, digital programs deliver superior value across key metrics.
[C1.2] First, accessibility: digital platforms reach students that fields and stages cannot, and can be accessed asynchronously to fit any schedule. The child with a physical disability, the student working evenings to support her family, the rural learner with no bus to after-school practice—all can engage with digital enrichment from any connected device. Physical activities require transportation, fixed schedules, and often fees for equipment, creating barriers digital programs eliminate.
[C1.3] Second, future readiness: digital literacy, coding, and data analysis are no longer optional extras but core competencies. A school that delays these to expand soccer fields leaves students unprepared for a workforce where the vast majority of jobs require digital skills, from basic office software to advanced technical roles. These skills also enable entrepreneurial opportunities that physical activities cannot provide. Traditional activities build character, but they do not teach the specific proficiencies that unlock high-paying, future-proof careers.
[C1.4] Third, efficiency and scalability: digital programs scale at near-zero marginal cost. One platform license can serve an entire district; a single teacher can oversee hundreds of students in an online environment. Contrast this with sports requiring dedicated fields, expensive equipment, liability insurance, and specialized coaching staff—each with ongoing maintenance and replacement costs. The per-student cost difference is staggering, and digital content can be updated continuously, while a gym floor remains unchanged for decades.
[C1.5] Finally, PRO’s claim that digital is ‘substitutable’ misses how modern digital enrichment fosters collaboration and creativity in ways that mirror traditional activities—online coding teams, virtual music production, and digital design studios. The real irreplaceable need is equipping all students for a digital future, not limiting options based on physical constraints.
