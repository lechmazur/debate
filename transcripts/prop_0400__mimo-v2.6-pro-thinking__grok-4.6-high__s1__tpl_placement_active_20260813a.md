[P1.1] When the state decides who gets evicted from disability support, who is deported, or who loses liberty, the person affected needs someone on the other side of the decision who can actually be reached: a human being who can listen, explain, reconsider, and be held responsible. That is what a legal right to a human decision-maker buys, and it is worth protecting even when automation is cheaper.
[P1.2] The strongest reason is contestability. A person harmed by an automated benefits determination usually cannot see the model, the training data, or the weights that produced the "risk score" that cut off their income. They can describe facts a spreadsheet never captured: a hospitalization that explains missed paperwork, a domestic violence situation that explains a shared address, a disability that makes a deadline impossible to meet. An algorithm cannot weigh what it is never told, and it cannot be persuaded.
[P1.3] A human decision-maker is the only party to the decision who can hold a conversation and change their mind in response to it. Without one, appeal degenerates into re-running the same process and getting the same output — a review in name only.
[P1.4] Second, this right is about accountability. Legal rights require duty-bearers. If an automated system wrongly denies a claim and no person owns that judgment, there is no one to explain it, no one to sanction, no one whose professional judgment can be scrutinized. Identification of a responsible human decision-maker is the difference between governance and weather: something that happens to you rather than something done to you, on purpose, by someone answerable.
[P1.5] Third, the right is deliberately modest. It does not ban automated tools. Officials may use risk models to triage, flag, and inform — exactly as doctors use diagnostic software while remaining the clinician. What it forbids is making the machine the final word on a life outcome.

[C1.2] What they need is a correct decision they can understand and challenge, not a Homo sapiens whose name appears on the form. The proposition treats species as a proxy for justice. It is a bad proxy.
[C1.3] Volume makes the right self-defeating. Governments already decide millions of eligibility, status, and charging questions. If every significant outcome requires a human as the final word, the queue is the real decision-maker. A parent waiting months for a benefits correction is not protected; they are rationed.
[C1.4] PRO concedes tools may still triage. That admits the work: machines already do the substance, and the legally required human becomes a signature. Rubber-stamping is not contestability. It is theater.
[C1.5] Calling the right modest because models may still inform does not shrink the mandate: a human last word at life-outcome scale is a production constraint, not a liberty.
[C1.6] Humans are often the less accountable party. Fatigue, caseload, unspoken stereotypes, and local politics leave no audit trail. A versioned eligibility rule can be published, tested, and reversed for everyone it wrongly classified. Inability to see a model is an argument for explanation duties, not for swapping an inspectable process for an official's private hunch.
[C1.7] The right freezes the wrong thing. Due process already demands notice, reasons, and a chance to correct error. Those duties bind whether a clerk or a system applies the statute. Elevating "a human decided" into an entitlement invites litigation over whether the official truly thought, while leaving the biased or exhausted human untouched.
[C1.8] Delay is a denial. Inconsistency is a denial. Mandating a human last click does not buy listening; it buys bottlenecks. Keep explanation and reversal.
[C1.9] Refuse the fetish of a human final actor.
