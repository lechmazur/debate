[P1.1] When the Rams left St. Louis for Los Angeles in 2016, they didn't just change cities—they abandoned a publicly financed stadium that taxpayers were still paying off, leaving behind debt and desolation. This isn't an isolated betrayal; it's a business model. Across the country, franchises extract billions in public subsidies for stadiums, tax breaks, and infrastructure, then weaponize relocation threats to extract even richer concessions from competing cities. The community absorbs the debt and the blight; the franchise captures the profit and the mobility.
[P1.2] Major public subsidies create a binding social contract, not a charitable gift. When a municipality issues bonds to build an arena or grants decades of tax abatements worth hundreds of millions, it acts on a specific promise: shared prosperity through jobs, tourism, local business growth, and civic identity. Relocation annihilates that bargain instantly. Restaurants and hotels that invested around the venue fail.
[P1.3] Municipal budgets hemorrhage. Workers lose livelihoods. The team departs with a subsidized asset funded by the very taxpayers it abandoned.
[P1.4] This is fundamentally about democratic accountability. Public money demands public consent. If a corporation receives substantial government support, it should not wield unilateral veto power over a community's economic future. Allowing subsidized teams to relocate without binding community approval treats citizens as disposable revenue sources rather than rights-bearing stakeholders with legitimate claims on the investments they funded.
[P1.5] Opponents will invoke "free markets" and "mobility," but these teams operate in rigged markets—extracting monopoly rents from public treasuries while socializing their risks and privatizing their gains. A binding approval process doesn't punish ambition; it restores balance by ensuring that when public wealth is at stake, the public retains a veto. Without that check, we don't have sports. We have corporate welfare with a moving van.
