[P1.1] Legalizing and regulating compensated surrogacy with strict safeguards expands reproductive freedom while shrinking the very harms opponents fear. Women who freely choose to carry a pregnancy for others gain access to enforceable contracts, medical oversight, and minimum compensation standards that informal or banned arrangements routinely lack.
[P1.2] Bans do not eliminate demand; they push arrangements into unregulated channels where coercion is harder to detect and redress. In jurisdictions that prohibit payment, intended parents often turn to overseas or underground options with weaker monitoring and no guaranteed post-birth legal clarity for the surrogate. Regulated systems, by contrast, require independent legal counsel for the surrogate, psychological screening, and cooling-off periods that make exploitation detectable before contracts are signed.
[P1.3] The claim that money alone destroys consent collapses once real-world alternatives are compared. Low-income women already make binding choices about dangerous or demanding labor under existing labor laws. Surrogacy under regulated pay and exit rights differs only in degree, not in kind, from those accepted trades. The decisive difference is whether the state supplies enforceable protections or leaves participants to private power imbalances.
[P1.4] Strong regulation therefore delivers two concrete gains unavailable under prohibition: documented consent procedures that courts can review and compensation that reduces financial desperation as the sole motive. These mechanisms do not erase every risk, but they convert an invisible practice into one subject to ongoing oversight and adjustment.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to trust that a market in wombs can be tamed by paperwork and counseling. That trust is misplaced. The PRO claims that bans push surrogacy underground, yet regulated markets in India and the U.S. still produce exploitation, proving that legalization does not solve the problem. Even the best‑drafted regulations cannot eliminate the structural coercion that drives women into compensated surrogacy.
[C1.2] The core problem is not the absence of rules; it is the presence of a price tag on a woman's reproductive capacity. When a contract offers a lump‑sum payment that can lift a family out of poverty, “free choice” becomes a euphemism for economic desperation. Studies of regulated surrogacy in India and the United States show that women who sign contracts are disproportionately low‑income, often lack independent legal counsel, and face pressure from agencies that recruit them. The “cool‑off periods” and “psychological screening” touted by the PRO side are performed by the same intermediaries who profit from the transaction, creating a built‑in conflict of interest that no regulation can fully resolve.
[C1.3] Moreover, the market incentivizes a supply chain that resembles trafficking. International surrogacy hubs in Eastern Europe and Southeast Asia have documented cases where women are lured with promises of work, then confined and forced to undergo repeated pregnancies. Even where domestic law is strict, cross‑border arrangements evade oversight, turning “strong protections” into a façade. The proposition also assumes that compensation reduces coercion, but money itself is the coercive lever.
[C1.4] Paying a surrogate does not neutralize the power imbalance; it monetises it. Women who later regret the decision are bound by contract, and the state’s role becomes enforcing a commercial agreement rather than protecting a vulnerable person. A better path is to invest in adoption systems and altruistic surrogacy, which do not create a commercial incentive to exploit poverty. Legalising compensated surrogacy, however well‑regulated, legitimises the commodification of women’s bodies and entrenches the very exploitation it claims to prevent.
