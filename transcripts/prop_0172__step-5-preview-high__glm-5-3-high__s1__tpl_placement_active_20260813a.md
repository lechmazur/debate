[P1.1] The decisive question is not whether an organoid is human, alive, or a person, but whether it has the kind of nervous activity that makes harm morally relevant. Animal-research frameworks already rest on that logic. We do not protect mice, zebrafish, or cephalopods because they resemble us in body plan; we review their use because their brains can support experience, vulnerability, and interests. A proof-of-concept brain organoid capable of rudimentary learning crosses that same threshold in principle.
[P1.2] Rudimentary learning is not merely biochemical activity. It means the tissue can encode experience, modify future responses, and sustain coordinated patterns of neural function over time. Once a system can do that, we can no longer treat it as a simple cell culture or an inanimate reagent. It may lack a body, pain receptors, or obvious behavior, but those absences do not settle whether there is anything it is like to be that tissue.
[P1.3] Animal frameworks exist precisely because ordinary observation can miss morally important forms of consciousness, distress, or preference.
[P1.4] PRO’s case is therefore about precaution and proportionality. The same review frameworks applied to animal work require independent ethical scrutiny, expert assessment of potential sentience, harm-benefit justification, refinement of methods, humane endpoints, and monitoring as capacities evolve. Applied to organoids, these measures are not barriers to science; they are safeguards against a research field crossing ethical lines without noticing. If learning-capable organoids develop more complex network dynamics—or are later transplanted, vascularized, or connected to sensory systems—the absence of prior review would make it impossible to detect when the moral status of the system has changed.
[P1.5] CON may object that organoids are “not animals” and lack embodied experience. But that argues for adapting the details of review, not abandoning it. The relevant risk is not that organoids are currently indistinguishable from sentient animals. The risk is that we are creating systems whose morally relevant capacities are uncertain and can grow.
[P1.6] Uncertainty is the strongest reason for review, not the weakest.
