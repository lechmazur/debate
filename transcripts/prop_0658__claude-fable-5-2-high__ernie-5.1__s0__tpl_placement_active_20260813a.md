[P1.2] So the debate is about who carries the burden: the state that wants to identify everyone, or the citizen who merely wants to walk down the street.
[P1.3] That burden belongs on the state, for three reasons.
[P1.4] First, mass biometric surveillance changes what public space is. Being seen is not the same as being identified, logged, and searchable. Once every face is an ID card read without consent, there is a retrievable record of who attended the protest, the mosque, the union meeting, the clinic. People do not need to be punished to be deterred; they only need to know the record exists.
[P1.5] And unlike a password, a face cannot be reissued after it is compromised.
[P1.6] Second, the arithmetic of mass scanning works against the innocent. When you scan millions of people looking for a handful of targets, even a very accurate system generates false matches that vastly outnumber true ones. There are already documented cases of people wrongfully arrested on faulty face matches, and error rates have repeatedly been found to fall unevenly across demographic groups. Dragnets manufacture suspects.
[P1.7] Third, infrastructure outlives its justification. Systems installed for terrorism drift toward shoplifting, debt collection, and tracking dissidents. [P1.8] Notice what my opponent is therefore left defending. They cannot rest on targeted investigations, which survive, or on emergencies, which are authorized. They must defend the remainder: routine, suspicionless identification of entire populations with no prior justification required.
