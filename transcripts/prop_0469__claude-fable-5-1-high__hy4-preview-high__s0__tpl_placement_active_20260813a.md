[P1.1] The worst environmental destruction in the world today is legal, or close enough that nobody goes to prison for it. A company can poison a river system for a generation, an executive can sign off on clearing a forest the size of a small country, an official can wave through a tailings dam everyone knows will fail, and the consequence is a fine paid by shareholders and absorbed as a cost of doing business. That is the accountability gap ecocide law closes, and it is the central issue in this debate.
[P1.2] The mechanism is simple: criminal liability for individuals changes behaviour where corporate fines do not. When Deepwater Horizon spilled into the Gulf, the company paid billions, but the environmental harm itself sent no one to prison. Fines are priced in. Personal exposure of a board member or minister is not.
[P1.3] Banks, insurers, and directors respond to criminal risk in ways they never respond to civil penalties, which is why we criminalise bribery and fraud rather than simply taxing them.
[P1.4] Recognition should be international because the harm is. Rivers, rainforests, fisheries, and the atmosphere do not stop at borders, and the states where the worst damage occurs often have the weakest or most captured regulators. An international crime supplies a floor of accountability where domestic law is absent or bought.
[P1.5] This is not a leap into the unknown. The Rome Statute already criminalises severe environmental damage in wartime; ecocide extends that logic to peacetime. Several states already have domestic ecocide provisions, and the definition developed by international lawyers is deliberately narrow: unlawful or wanton acts committed with knowledge of a substantial likelihood of severe and widespread or long-term damage. Ordinary farming, mining under permit, or an honest accident does not qualify.
[P1.6] Reckless, knowing devastation does.
[P1.7] Genocide was not a crime until states decided it should be. The question is whether the deliberate destruction of the living systems everyone depends on belongs in the category of accounting, or the category of justice.

[C1.3] The bottleneck is enforcement, regulation, and capture, not the absence of a global label.
[C1.4] International criminal law cannot fix that bottleneck. The ICC is a court of last resort, dependent on state cooperation, with limited dockets and no police. As [P1.5] notes, the Rome Statute already covers severe wartime environmental damage; that provision has been essentially unused. A peacetime version would face harder proof: a board member’s knowledge, causation across decades, and separating criminal acts from development choices.
[C1.5] So the “narrow” definition in [P1.5] is not narrow in practice. “Wanton,” “severe,” and “long-term” are elastic when applied to a dam, highway, or mine. A government approving hydropower that floods a valley and alters a river for a century has made a tragic policy choice, not an international crime. Vague liability will chill investment in poorer states and be used selectively against weak governments while powerful economies shield their firms.
[C1.6] The relevant comparison is not genocide [P1.7]. Genocide targets people and is often state-orchestrated, leaving domestic courts unable to act. Environmental harm is mostly licensed activity inside states that regulate it. The better path is stronger domestic criminal law, cross-border enforcement, and civil redress reaching corporate treasuries.
[C1.7] Recognizing ecocide internationally lets states perform concern while the ICC cannot arrest anyone. Judge this by pollution prevented and officials convicted at home, not by new crimes proclaimed abroad.
