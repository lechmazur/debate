[P1.1] A parent choosing a drink for a child in thirty seconds is not making the free, informed choice that education campaigns imagine. She faces bright packaging, health claims, cartoon characters and a tiny nutrition table in technical language, designed by companies that spend heavily to engineer craving and obscure excess sugar, salt and fat. Asking a leaflet, website or awareness week to win that contest is not respect for choice; it is abandonment of it.
[P1.2] Governments should therefore require prominent front-of-pack warnings — simple statements such as “High in sugar” or “High in saturated fat” — on ultra-processed foods and sugary drinks. Unlike back-label tables, a warning is visible at the moment of decision, understandable across literacy levels and languages, and impossible to bury under marketing. It does not ban a product, ration it or tell anyone what to eat. An adult who wants the drink can still buy it.
[P1.3] What changes is that consent becomes informed rather than manufactured.
[P1.4] That immediacy is why labels outperform campaigns alone. Campaigns depend on people seeing, remembering and applying abstract advice months later in a supermarket, while labels translate that advice into the exact context where it matters. They also create an incentive campaigns never create: reformulation. When carrying a black warning becomes a reputational cost, manufacturers have a reason to cut sugar or sodium just enough to avoid it, improving even the products bought by people who never read labels.
[P1.5] The stakes are not evenly shared. Children, busy workers and poorer shoppers are most aggressively targeted, have least time to research purchases, and bear the highest burden of obesity, diabetes and tooth decay linked to these products. Telling them to choose better while letting producers hide the relevant fact in small print protects the seller’s freedom to mislead, not the buyer’s freedom to choose.
[P1.6] If choice is the value, make choice real: put the warning where the selling happens.

[C1.1] The PRO side frames this debate as a choice between neglect and warning labels, but that is a false dichotomy. Mandatory prominent warning labels are a superficial, paternalistic policy that fails to address the root causes of poor nutrition, creates perverse industry incentives, and does nothing to expand real choice for the most vulnerable consumers.
[C1.2] First, warning labels oversimplify nutrition and invite harmful reformulation. The PRO side claims labels push manufacturers to cut excess sugar, salt, and saturated fat, but industry will simply swap those ingredients for untested artificial sweeteners, ultra-processed fat substitutes, and "low-fat" or "sugar-free" marketing that masks equally poor nutritional profiles. A soda reformulated with aspartame to dodge a "high in sugar" warning remains an ultra-processed, aggressively marketed product with its own documented long-term health risks. The label lets producers claim they responded to public health concerns while delivering a product no safer than the original.
[C1.3] Second, the labels do nothing for the populations the PRO side claims to prioritize, because they ignore structural barriers to choice. A low-income parent in a food desert, with no nearby grocery store and only a convenience store stocked with ultra-processed, affordable options, will see the warning label and still buy the only accessible product for their family. The label is a shaming tool that imposes a symbolic cost on consumers without expanding their actual options.
[C1.4] Third, the PRO side’s dismissal of consumer choice campaigns is a straw man. Targeted, well-resourced policies—subsidizing fresh produce in low-income neighborhoods, mandatory nutrition education in schools, and strict restrictions on marketing to children—expand real, informed choice far more effectively than a label that does nothing to address why people pick these products in the first place. Warning labels are a lazy, performative alternative to the structural changes that would actually reduce consumption of harmful ultra-processed foods.

[P2.5] Simplicity is not oversimplification when the signal is narrow and true: this product crosses a high threshold for an ingredient linked to harm. Complexity is already on the back of the pack, and it is failing because it demands time and literacy shoppers do not have.
