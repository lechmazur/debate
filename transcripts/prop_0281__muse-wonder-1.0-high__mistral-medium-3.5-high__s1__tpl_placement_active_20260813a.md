[P1.1] Signing up takes two taps. Cancelling takes a treasure hunt.
[P1.2] That asymmetry is not an accident, and it is not trivial. A streaming service, gym, news site, or app can enroll you instantly with a saved card, then bury cancellation in account settings, require you to chat with an agent, sit through repeated retention screens, or — in the classic gym model — visit in person or send a letter. Each extra step is designed to exploit inertia, forgetting, and frustration. Some people give up.
[P1.3] Many delay. All pay longer than they intended.
[P1.4] That is the core of subscription fatigue. Consumers now juggle a dozen recurring charges for video, music, storage, software, delivery, fitness, and news. Individually each looks cheap; together they punish limited attention. Free trials convert silently.
[P1.5] Prices creep. A service used once renews for months because life got busy. When firms then add friction to exit, forgetting becomes their business model. The charge is no longer clear evidence that the customer values the product — it is evidence that leaving was annoying.
[P1.6] This is a textbook consumer-welfare harm: money transferred without meaningful ongoing consent, time wasted navigating mazes, and competition distorted. Honest firms that make exit easy must compete against rivals who retain customers not by being better, but by being harder to leave. Markets are supposed to reward value. Dark patterns reward obstruction.
[P1.7] The remedy is modest and symmetrical: if you can sign up online in minutes, you must be able to cancel online in minutes, through the same medium, with a comparable number of steps. No phone-only cancellation for online sign-up. No hidden buttons, misleading wording, or forced save-a-thons. A firm may make one clear retention offer; it may not make refusal exhausting.
[P1.8] That rule protects autonomy without setting prices or banning subscriptions. It simply restores the condition that makes subscriptions legitimate in the first place: continued payment means continued choice.

[C1.1] Subscription models fund the services consumers demand. The friction PRO calls “dark” often serves legitimate purposes: preventing accidental cancellations, verifying identity to stop fraud, or offering last‑chance value that many users genuinely want. Mandating symmetrical cancellation ignores that sign‑up and exit are fundamentally different actions. Sign‑up is a one‑time consent to start; cancellation terminates a contract with real downstream effects on pricing, revenue, and service quality.
[C1.2] The “fatigue” narrative exaggerates the scale. Most digital subscriptions already allow straightforward online cancellation. The outliers PRO cites—gyms, legacy publishers—reflect legitimate operational needs like managing physical access or liability. Forcing one‑click exits would simply shift these costs onto remaining customers through higher prices or degraded service.
[C1.3] Consumers already have tools: budgeting apps, calendar reminders, credit card chargebacks, and third‑party cancellation services. They do not need regulators micromanaging user interface design.
[C1.4] Worse, this rule creates perverse incentives. If businesses cannot use any retention friction, they will stop offering free trials, reduce quality investments, or move to upfront payments—all worse for consumers than occasional, reasonable friction. The market already disciplines genuine abuse: services with predatory practices lose reputation and customers. Heavy regulation treats all friction as manipulation, ignoring that verification steps protect both businesses and users from fraud and errors.
[C1.5] The motion’s demand for perfect symmetry is arbitrary: sign‑up requires payment verification and term acceptance; cancellation needs identity confirmation. Different processes serve different, valid purposes.
