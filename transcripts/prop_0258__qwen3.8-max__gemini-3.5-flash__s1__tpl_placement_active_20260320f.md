[P1.1] The first question is who statutes are for. In a democracy, law is not a private technical manual; it is a public command. If ordinary citizens cannot read the rules that govern rent, taxes, employment, speech, or criminal liability, then notice, consent, and self-government become fictions. That is why legislatures should be required to draft statutes in plain language, even when doing so trims some lawyerly precision.
[P1.2] The greatest cost of hyper-technical statutes is not occasional ambiguity; it is the transfer of power from the public to specialists. When laws are dense with cross-references, archaic terms, and undefined terms of art, compliance depends on hiring counsel. That privileges corporations and wealthy individuals, while ordinary people are left to guess or rely on unreliable summaries. Plain-language drafting reduces this inequality.
[P1.3] A tenant should be able to read a housing statute and understand when a landlord may withhold a deposit. A small-business owner should be able to see licensing duties without paying for an interpretive map.
[P1.4] Precision matters, but precision and complexity are not the same. A clear sentence can be exact; a long defined term can still be vague in application. Requiring plain language forces drafters to state who must do what, when, and what follows from violation. That discipline often improves legal quality.
[P1.5] Where some marginal precision is lost, the loss is outweighed by fewer surprises, less litigation over interpretive traps, and greater ability of legislatures to be held accountable for the rules they actually adopt.
[P1.6] The decisive comparison is simple: a statute that is perfectly precise only to specialists fails as public law, while a statute that is understandable to the governed preserves legitimacy, compliance, and equal access to justice.

[C1.1] The opposition’s case rests on a comforting but dangerous illusion: that simplifying the text of a statute makes the law more democratic. In reality, sacrificing legal precision in the name of "plain language" does not empower ordinary citizens. It strips them of their strongest protection against arbitrary power and transfers ultimate lawmaking authority to unaccountable judges.
[C1.2] A statute is not a public relations brochure; it is a structural blueprint. Just as we would not mandate that a blueprint for an airliner be drawn in crayon so passengers can understand it, we cannot draft complex regulatory or criminal codes in everyday vernacular without risking structural failure.
[C1.3] When a legislature sacrifices precision, it introduces ambiguity. In law, ambiguity is a vacuum of power, and that vacuum is always filled by the executive branch or the judiciary. When "plain language" laws are inevitably contested, judges must guess at legislative intent. This creates a playground for wealthy corporations, who can afford to exploit linguistic loopholes and fund protracted litigation to define what "plain" words actually mean.
[C1.4] Far from reducing inequality, vague statutes invite endless litigation, reversing the very benefit proposed by the affirmative [P1.5].
[C1.5] Furthermore, "plain language" mandates ignore the value of legal terms of art. Words like "consideration," "bailment," or "mens rea" carry centuries of settled judicial interpretation. Replacing them with colloquial synonyms forces the legal system to restart the clock, relitigating foundational concepts at immense public expense.
[C1.6] We can achieve public accessibility without destroying legal certainty. Governments can and should publish clear, plain-language summaries and compliance guides. But the statutory text itself must remain a highly precise instrument. To require otherwise is to exchange the rule of law for the rule of judicial interpretation, leaving the public far more vulnerable than before.

It is precision aimed at the people bound by it. The motion does not demand nursery rhymes; it demands that the state not hide commands inside a specialist dialect.

Technical statutes create their own ambiguity: circular definitions, dangling cross-references, and terms that only specialists can map. When compliance requires a lawyer just to read the rule, the rule of law has already been outsourced. Judges will interpret statutes either way.
[P2.3] But a plain text gives citizens and courts a common anchor; an obscure text gives experts an interpretive playground.
[P2.4] That answers the corporation point. Wealthy firms do not fear plain words; they profit from complexity. A readable statute shrinks the market for loopholes because the baseline duty is visible. A tenant, landlord, or small business owner can see the line without buying counsel.
[P2.5] Plain language therefore does not invite endless litigation [C1.4]; it reduces litigation over drafting traps, because when people can read the rule, fewer disputes must become lawsuits.
[P2.6] Terms of art are not a trump card. If “mens rea” becomes “intentionally or knowingly,” the legal system is not restarting the clock; it is translating settled meaning into public language. Where some technical edge is lost, the proposition accepts that tradeoff because democratic notice matters more than insider elegance.
[P2.7] Finally, summaries [C1.6] are not enough. They can be wrong, updated late, or ignored. In a democracy, the binding text—not a pamphlet—should tell citizens what the law requires.

[C2.6] Ultimately, the opposition’s focus on "notice" [P2.6] is self-defeating. A statute that is easy to read but impossible to predict does not provide fair notice. It merely masks legal instability under a veneer of accessibility. True notice requires predictability under the law.
