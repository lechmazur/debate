[P1.1] Every ride-hail trip is a two-sided trade, yet the platform sets both sides of the price and shows each party only half the ledger. Riders see their total; drivers see their net; neither sees what the other paid or what the platform kept. That is not a marketplace. It is a black box taking a cut from both sides.
[P1.2] The motion is narrow: require ride-hail services to show the full fare split by default—rider charge, driver earnings, commission, and fees—without disclosing the algorithm. A driver should not accept a surge trip blind while the rider is charged $30 and the platform keeps $12. Show both sides and surge stops looking like theft or gouging; it becomes checkable. A rider who knows the driver gets $18 of $30 can judge whether the surge is scarcity compensation or hidden markup.
[P1.3] A driver who sees the platform's take can decide whether the trip is worth taking. Informed refusal is not cherry-picking; it is the rational response to a take-it-or-leave-it wage.
[P1.4] Opacity also hides the thing most worth policing: commission creep. Platforms can raise effective take-rates behind dynamic pricing, promotions, and fee reclassification. Default disclosure turns a private pricing decision into an auditable number. No one needs source code to see a take rate jump from 20% to 30%; they need the split.
[P1.5] This is ordinary transparency, not radical exposure. Platforms already show totals; adding one clean line—"rider pays $30, driver earns $18, platform and fees $12"—costs little. Event tickets and mandatory service charges already face the same all-in logic. The predictable trade-secret and overload objection fails: the split is not the model, and a simple default is not a data dump.
[P1.6] A broker that sets both the bid and the ask yet shows neither side the other is not a neutral intermediary. Requiring the fare split by default makes ride-hail legible, accountable, and finally honest.

You would create the most manipulable number in the industry and then call it "auditable."
[C1.6] Commission creep [P1.4] is real — and the honest fix is audited aggregate take-rate reporting to a regulator, published quarterly. That catches 20% becoming 30%. A default per-trip line does not, and worse, it lets a platform say "we're transparent" while the aggregate stays hidden.
[C1.7] Third, showing riders the driver's cut doesn't discipline the platform. It relocates the conflict into the car. Surge disputes become the rider arguing with the driver about the driver's earnings, with a rating button in hand.
