[P1.1] Combat is the most extreme act a state performs. In a democracy, every bullet fired in the public's name should be traceable to a decision-maker voters can question, constrain, and punish. That is why private military contractors should be banned from combat roles in international conflicts. The issue is not whether contractors shoot straight or fill manpower gaps; it is that the role itself severs the link between lethal force and democratic consent.
[P1.2] Accountability has three stages: authorization, oversight, and answerability. Privatized combat weakens all three. Contractors are not "troops." A government constrained by a mandate or troop cap can expand offensive capacity by hiring companies instead, turning a public, countable choice into a classified procurement decision.
[P1.3] The political price of war drops because the choice is harder to see.
[P1.4] During the fighting, oversight thins. Contracted force is governed by contracts and corporate chains, not by a single military chain answerable to elected civilians. Commercial confidentiality and layered subcontracting make rules of engagement, incident reports, and disciplinary records harder for legislatures and journalists to obtain. In Iraq and Afghanistan, contractor deaths were often recorded outside military casualty counts, so the public bore fewer visible costs and asked fewer questions.
[P1.5] After harm, accountability fragments among host-state courts, military authorities, corporate arbitration, and national law. Even the Blackwater killings in Baghdad produced years of prosecutions, appeals, and reversals, and ended in pardons—hardly the clear, public reckoning democracy promises when a state kills.
[P1.6] This is not hostility to private security everywhere. It is a line at the point where violence becomes policy. Unarmed logistics or base support can be contracted; deliberate combat cannot. Do not weigh this debate on efficiency.
[P1.7] The test is whether a citizen can know, authorize, and answer for the force used in his or her name. Contracted combat makes that impossible, and a democracy that cannot answer for its violence is not controlling it.
