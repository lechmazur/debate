[P1.2] My first argument is universality. A core subject should cover what nearly every adult will certainly need. Feeding yourself, maintaining a home, and managing money are not niche interests like pottery or robotics. They are the operating system of adult life.

[P1.4] My second argument is equity, and it is where this debate turns. "Optional for students who seek them out" sounds neutral, but it isn't. Once you make that commitment, you are not adding wisdom to a vacuum. You are subtracting from something else.
[C1.2] Schools already face a zero-sum timetable. Every required cooking lab or repair unit consumes time that could go to reading, algebra, science, history, or the arts. And the limiting principle collapses immediately. Why stop at cooking, pipes, and interest rates?
[C1.3] First aid, taxes, childcare, job interviews, mental health, and digital privacy are also universal adult needs. If “adults need it” makes something core, the curriculum becomes an endless life-training catalog and the academic core erodes.
[C1.4] The equity argument actually cuts against a mandate. A required course is only as strong as the school delivering it. Wealthy districts can build kitchens, hire skilled tradespeople, and run real financial simulations. Poorer districts—the ones with the students the proposition says need this most—get a worksheet, a video, or an overstretched teacher covering a subject they never trained in.
[C1.5] Meanwhile, advantaged students still learn budgeting and cooking at home. The mandate can produce a credential without competence.
[C1.6] And feasibility isn’t a footnote. Cooking and repair require equipment, insurance, safety protocols, and specialized instructors. Schools already struggle to staff math and science. Finance can be embedded in existing math and civics, but embedding is not the same as making it a separate core—and if that is all the other side means, then schools should embed where natural, not elevate these subjects to mandatory core status.
[C1.7] The choice isn’t life skills versus ignorance. It’s whether a finite school system should mandate broad, expensive, unevenly delivered courses or offer and embed them well. Importance is not the test. Core status is a claim on everyone’s time, and that claim fails.
