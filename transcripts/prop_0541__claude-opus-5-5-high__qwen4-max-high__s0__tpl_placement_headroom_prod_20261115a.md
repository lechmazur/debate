[P1.1] The question is not whether America should build good weapons. It is whether America can afford to fight its likeliest war with a force built around a few irreplaceable ones. It cannot.
[P1.2] First, mass beats precision when precision is everywhere. China has spent decades building missiles to kill high-value targets like carriers and airbases. A force concentrated in a handful of exquisite platforms hands the adversary a short target list. Lose one destroyer and you have lost years of production.
[P1.3] Thousands of cheaper drones, decoys, and uncrewed vessels flip that math: the enemy must spend expensive munitions on targets we can replace in weeks.
[P1.4] Second, the cost-exchange ratio is already breaking against us. In the Red Sea, the Navy has fired interceptors costing millions to stop Houthi drones costing tens of thousands. That is sustainable for a skirmish, ruinous for a war. Meanwhile Ukraine, with almost no navy, used cheap sea drones to push Russia's Black Sea Fleet out of the western Black Sea.
[P1.5] When a few hundred thousand dollars can threaten a warship, pouring money only into warships is betting against reality.
[P1.6] Third, attritable systems fix our industrial base. Exquisite programs take a decade to field and depend on a few fragile suppliers. Cheap systems can be produced at scale by many firms, iterated in months, and replenished during a conflict. Wars are won by the side that can keep fighting after the first month, and America's stock of high-end munitions might not last that long over Taiwan.
[P1.7] This is not abolition. Exquisite platforms remain, and they get stronger when swarms of collaborative drones extend their sensors and absorb fire. But the marginal dollar now buys more deterrence in mass than in perfection. The Pentagon's Replicator initiative admits as much; the motion asks us to fund it seriously.
