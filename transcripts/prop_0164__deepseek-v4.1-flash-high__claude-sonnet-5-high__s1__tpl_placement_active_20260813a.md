[P1.1] Science advances only when findings survive attempts to falsify them, yet the grant system rewards the opposite: new questions, positive results, and nobody’s time for confirmation. Without a ring-fenced share, replication is always the weakest proposal in the room—unoriginal by design, often “negative” by result, and easy to postpone. Funders say they value replication; budgets reveal what they actually buy. A meaningful fixed share turns a slogan into a commitment.
[P1.2] The proposition does not say fund every replication or replace novelty. It says public funders should hold a protected slice for verification. That is portfolio management, not hostility to discovery. Public research money buys a public good: reliable knowledge.
[P1.3] If a field cannot reproduce its central claims, every novel project built on them is a liability. We have all seen the pattern: high-profile results shape textbooks, clinical trials, and follow-on grants, then fail to replicate. The waste is not the replication study; it is the years of work that assumed the original finding was true.
[P1.4] Opponents will call this a tax on innovation. But novelty is abundant; credible verification is scarce. The marginal novel proposal is often speculative, while replication is unusually high-information: it can confirm, correct, or kill a line of inquiry. A failed replication is not a failed investment; it is a map that stops others walking off a cliff.
[P1.5] There is an incentive effect too. When researchers know that a fixed share of funding will test important claims, exaggeration and fragile methods become riskier. That changes behavior before a single replication is funded.
[P1.6] So the real choice is not novelty versus replication. It is a system that funds novelty on credit forever, versus one that pays at least part of its verification bill. A meaningful fixed share is the minimum price of a science we can trust.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to lock in a number before we know the need. That is the core error. Replication value is not evenly distributed across fields, results, or time. Some claims cry out for verification—splashy, counter-intuitive, policy-relevant findings.
[C1.2] Most published results never get cited again and nobody is harmed if they go unchecked. A fixed share treats a psychology lab's underpowered study and a Phase III oncology trial as interchangeable line items. That is not portfolio management; it is a blunt instrument dressed up as one.
[C1.3] Fixed carve-outs create their own perverse incentives. Once a share of the budget must go to replication, someone has to spend it, whether or not enough high-value replication targets exist that year. Committees will face pressure to approve marginal, low-stakes replications just to hit the quota, while genuinely important but expensive replications—large clinical trials, long-run field experiments—go underfunded because they don't fit the year's allocation. A number chosen for political comfort will rarely match the actual, lumpy, unpredictable demand for verification.
[C1.4] PRO's own framing gives this away: they say the proposal is not "fund every replication," but a protected slice does not know which replications deserve funding—it simply guarantees money to whatever replication proposals show up. That is worse than the status quo, where a replication of a landmark, high-stakes finding can already outcompete a routine novel proposal on pure merit.
[C1.5] The bigger problem is opportunity cost PRO waves away. Novelty is not "abundant" in the sense that matters—breakthrough discovery is precisely what generates the claims worth replicating in the first place. Starve the discovery pipeline and you eventually run out of important things to verify.
[C1.6] Meanwhile the tools that actually fix the incentive problem—preregistration, registered reports, mandatory data-sharing, replication as a condition of tenure or publication credit—target the disease directly, without freezing a chunk of every year's budget regardless of what science actually needs that year. CON does not oppose funding replication; it opposes solving a targeting problem with a quota.

[P2.2] If there are insufficient high-value targets in a year, the pool can support fewer, larger, or multi-year replications. Most published results being low-value is not a reason to have no reserve; it is a reason to target the small set of claims that carry weight.
[P2.3] [C1.3]’s fear that expensive clinical replications will be squeezed is backwards. Today, a large verification trial competes head-to-head with a glamorous novel proposal and usually loses. A dedicated pool is precisely what lets a field set aside money for the slow, costly test of a treatment that textbooks already treat as true. The quota does not know quality; panels do.
[P2.4] That is an argument for governance, not for zero.
[P2.5] [C1.4] claims landmark replications can already outcompete. If that were true, the replication problem would be solved. It is not: review panels reward novelty, positive framing, and new questions. That is the bias the fixed share corrects.
[P2.6] Zero is also a number chosen by default.
[P2.7] The opportunity-cost objection also inverts the risk. A marginal novel grant produces one more uncertain finding. A well-chosen failed replication can stop ten labs from building on a false premise. Discovery is not killed by reserving a fraction; it is poisoned by treating unverified claims as assets.
[P2.8] Finally, preregistration and registered reports are complements, not substitutes. They improve studies not yet done. They cannot test the claims already shaping policy and further research. And they, too, need funding.
[P2.9] CON offers better hygiene for new work and no bill for the old work. PRO offers both.

A marginal novel grant adds one more uncertain claim; a well-targeted replication corrects the record other grants depend on.
