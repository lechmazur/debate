[P1.1] Every graduate will sign a lease or a loan. Every graduate will eat three times a day. Nearly every graduate will one day stand in front of a leaking pipe, a tripped breaker, or a bill they don't understand. None of them will be asked to factor a polynomial by surprise at 11pm.
[P1.2] That asymmetry is the whole case. A subject earns the label "core" not by academic prestige but by three tests: is it near-universally required in adult life, is it hard to pick up reliably on your own, and is the cost of not knowing it steep and compounding? Cooking, basic repair, and personal finance pass all three. A teenager who doesn't grasp how a 25% APR revolving balance works doesn't get a bad grade — they get five years of interest payments.
[P1.3] A young adult who can't cook doesn't fail a quiz; they spend a quarter of their income on takeout and inherit the health consequences. These are the mistakes that quietly set the ceiling on a person's twenties.
[P1.4] Which is why "optional for students who seek them out" is the worst possible design. Who seeks them out? Overwhelmingly, the students who already have someone at home modelling budgeting, cooking, and fixing things — or who already sense the gap. Opt-in provision hands the skill to the confident and the well-resourced and calls it choice.
[P1.5] Meanwhile the student whose parents never had savings to explain, or who is first in the family to sign a tenancy agreement, is the one told these lessons are an extra. Make something optional and you don't remove the burden of learning it; you privatise it, and family background pays.
[P1.6] "Core" doesn't mean these subjects should devour the timetable. It means the school owns responsibility for whether every student leaves with them, rather than leaving that to luck. Schools already claim to prepare students for life. A curriculum that treats compound interest as niche and Latin roots as compulsory hasn't got its priorities straight — it's just got its habits unexamined.
