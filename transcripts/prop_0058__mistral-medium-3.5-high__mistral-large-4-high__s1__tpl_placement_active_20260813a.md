[P1.1] DOGE-style government efficiency drives treat public institutions like private businesses that can be endlessly streamlined for profit, but states don't operate on that logic. The 'waste' they target—redundant roles, unused capacity, slower processes—often constitutes the very slack that makes public systems resilient during crises. When the UK pursued aggressive austerity after 2010, central and local governments didn't just shed fat; they lost the policy memory, stakeholder networks, and surge capacity that allow states to function under stress. The fiscal savings were immediate and quantifiable—easy to celebrate in budgets—but the degradation of service delivery accumulated quietly and relentlessly: potholes went unfilled, social care collapsed, the NHS couldn't cope with winter pressures, and a skeletal civil service had to hire costly external consultants just to keep basic functions running.
[P1.2] These drives create their own ironies. The machinery to measure and enforce efficiency—performance frameworks, compliance regimes, reporting requirements—becomes a costly bureaucracy itself, diverting resources from frontline services. Officials game these systems, chasing measurable targets while neglecting vital but hard-to-measure functions like long-term planning or risk management.
[P1.3] The human cost compounds the damage. Efficiency drives lead to layoffs that disproportionately affect mid-level staff—the very people who understand how systems actually work. These aren't just jobs; they're repositories of institutional knowledge, built over decades through experience that can't be replaced by spreadsheets. When they disappear overnight, the state loses its operational memory.
[P1.4] When the next crisis hits—a pandemic, a financial shock, a natural disaster—the state lacks the depth of expertise and operational capacity to respond effectively.
[P1.5] Most fundamentally, these reforms rest on a false premise: that government 'waste' is always identifiable, removable, and separate from core capacity. But in complex systems, redundancy isn't waste—it's what allows for adaptation and resilience. DOGE-style efficiency drives don't just eliminate waste; they hollow out the state's ability to deliver services when it matters most. The short-term savings are dwarfed by the long-term costs of diminished capacity, eroded trust, and failed delivery when citizens need government the most.

[C1.2] The claim that redundancy equals resilience confuses bloat with preparedness. True surge capacity comes from flexible budgets, cross-trained personnel, and modern IT—not from layers of middle management and overlapping agencies performing identical functions. When two agencies process the same permits, the citizen waits twice as long while both offices bill the taxpayer. Efficiency reduces that friction, increasing effective capacity rather than diminishing it.
[C1.3] The "slack" the proposition romanticizes is often simply protected turf, procedural inertia, and the comfortable assumption that no one will ever ask why three offices maintain separate payroll systems for the same workforce.
[C1.4] Most critically, the proposition ignores that fiscal sustainability is the foundation of state capacity. A government drowning in debt cannot fund hospitals, schools, or disaster relief regardless of how many staff it retains. Every dollar wasted on duplication is a dollar stolen from actual service delivery. The UK example the proposition leans on conflated austerity—slashing services to reduce deficits—with efficiency, which cuts waste specifically to preserve core functions.
[C1.5] When Canada undertook comprehensive program review in the 1990s, it eliminated duplication while maintaining essential services, emerging with a stronger, more responsive state.
[C1.6] The real damage comes not from efficiency but from the paralysis of permanent bloat. By preserving sinecures and overlapping bureaucracies under the guise of "institutional memory," we tax citizens to fund dysfunction. DOGE-style reform is not an attack on the state; it is the precondition for a state that can actually afford to deliver.

[C2.6] Modern states preserve surge capacity through explicit contingency funds, cross-training, and call-off contracts—funded by the savings—rather than by paying permanent slack to wait for a crisis. The damage the proposition predicts is deferred and speculative; the savings are immediate, recurring, and available to strengthen exactly the frontline services it claims to defend.
