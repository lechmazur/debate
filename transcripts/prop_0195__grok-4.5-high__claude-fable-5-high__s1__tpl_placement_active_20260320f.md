[P1.1] The precautionary principle, as commonly applied in regulation, demands near-certainty of safety before a new technology, substance, or practice may proceed. It shifts the burden of proof onto innovators, freezes uncertain advances, and treats speculative worst cases as decisive. That structure systematically blocks more beneficial innovation than it prevents genuine harm, because it obsesses over novel risks while ignoring the larger, ongoing costs of the status quo and the forgone gains of delay.
[P1.2] Agricultural biotechnology shows the pattern cleanly. Precautionary regimes in Europe and elsewhere have delayed and restricted genetically modified crops that raise yields, reduce pesticide loads, and improve nutrition where both are scarcest. Conventional agriculture continued its known environmental and health burdens. The catastrophic scenarios that justified the delays largely failed to appear where the technology was used; the productivity and sustainability losses did appear.
[P1.3] Nuclear power faces the same distortion. Extreme caution around meltdown and waste has stretched licensing into decades and driven costs skyward, throttling a dense, low-carbon source. Coal and gas filled the gap, producing measurable excess deaths from air pollution and real climate risk. Precaution never balanced those certain harms against the innovation it restrained.
[P1.4] Drug and chemical approval repeats the error. Heightened demands for zero residual uncertainty slow therapies that would cut disease burden today. Rare side effects dominate the ledger; delayed access and missing treatments are discounted. The net effect is fewer tools against cancer, resistant pathogens, and chronic illness.
[P1.5] The principle fails as a decision rule because the risks of novelty are vivid and political while the harms of stagnation are diffuse and silent. Common applications therefore overweight false safety alarms and underweight the real losses from blocked progress. Until precaution accounts for opportunity cost, it will keep obstructing the advances that reduce genuine harm more effectively than it ever does.

[C1.6] Sensible precaution is innovation insurance, not its enemy.
[C1.7] Finally, note what the proposition must prove: not that precaution has costs, but that as commonly applied it nets out negative.
