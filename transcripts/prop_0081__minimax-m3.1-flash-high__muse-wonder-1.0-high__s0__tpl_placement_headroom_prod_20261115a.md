[P1.1] The case is not that every dating app must offer every conceivable security tool at zero marginal cost. It is that identity verification, blocking, and effective scam response cannot safely be luxury features. These features determine whether strangers can impersonate, harass, extort, or manipulate a user. Charging for access turns protection into a privilege precisely when a scammer can approach any user regardless of whether that user has subscribed.
[P1.2] That paywall also creates perverse incentives. A free tier that permits messaging but weakens verification, blocking, or fraud warnings is effectively an invitation to fraudulent activity. More unverified interactions generate more complaints, chargebacks, reputational harm, and moderator work, while making it harder for legitimate users to form accurate judgments. If the baseline is unsafe, the premium tier does not merely sell extra comfort; it sells escape from a hazard the business helped create.
[P1.3] Free defaults would let the platform train users and moderators on a larger, safer base, improving the entire ecosystem rather than protecting only subscribers.
[P1.4] Consider a romance scammer contacting several users through a stolen photograph. Free photo-based verification, liveness checks where appropriate, strong block and report controls, and prominent warnings about advance-fee requests would interrupt that pattern early. Subscription-based protection may interrupt it only for customers who know which paid tier to buy—and perhaps only after paying. The relevant comparison is not whether one dollar beats no dollar.
[P1.5] It is whether the platform should knowingly expose non-paying users to predictable deception.
[P1.6] There are legitimate design choices about verification strength, privacy, false positives, and fraud-detection accuracy. None requires excluding basic safety from the default product. Platforms can offer proportionate checks, use phone and email confirmation plus photo or selfie checks, preserve appeal channels, and reserve paid tiers for convenience or enhanced controls. Charging for block buttons and scam warnings, however, makes the ordinary user fund the abuse of other ordinary users.
[P1.7] Free-by-default safety also strengthens the business model. Trust encourages participation, and reduced fraud improves conversion, retention, and payment outcomes. [clipped]

[C1.1] Making every best safety feature free by default sounds protective, but it would weaken safety, degrade access, and force platforms to pay in ways users dislike more.
[C1.2] First, verification is not a cheap button. Real identity checks — document review, liveness matching, database cross-checks, manual appeals for people whose faces, names, or documents do not match neatly — cost money per attempt, and scammers deliberately generate repeated failed attempts. If apps must absorb unlimited verification for every free account, they will lower the bar to a cheap, easily spoofed selfie check, ration rechecks, or offset costs with more ads, data monetization, and higher prices elsewhere. A paid tier can fund the stronger check and human review that catches impersonation.
[C1.3] Mandating free therefore risks universal weak verification instead of optional strong verification.
[C1.4] Second, safety is not one-size-fits-all, and free-by-default verification harms people it claims to protect. Mandatory identity presentation deters undocumented users, closeted users, domestic-abuse survivors, and people where dating exposure is dangerous. They may need anonymity from other users while still wanting access. Tiering lets platforms offer a basic free product with phone confirmation, blocking, and reporting — generally baseline already — while users who want a verified badge or advanced filters pay for added assurance.
[C1.5] Collapsing that choice into a free mandate trades privacy and inclusion for a badge.
[C1.6] Third, block and scam-response tools have operational costs at scale: storing evidence, triaging reports, preserving messages for law enforcement, and staffing teams against adversaries who adapt daily. Subscriptions align payment with intensive protection, like linked-account blocks, priority review, and proactive alerts. If everything intensive is free, queues lengthen for everyone, and urgent cases wait behind low-quality reports. Paying does not buy the right to be safe; it buys capacity, speed, and depth that support a safer baseline for all.
