[P1.1] Credit reports exist for a specific, functional purpose: to predict future financial behavior by assessing how consumers manage voluntary credit. They measure whether a borrower honors contractual obligations—credit cards, auto loans, and mortgages—under terms they entered willingly and understood in advance. Medical debt fundamentally fails this test because illness is not a financial choice; it is an involuntary biological shock.
[P1.2] Unlike conventional borrowing, medical debt lacks predictive power regarding a consumer's creditworthiness. No patient chooses an emergency appendectomy, a sudden chronic diagnosis, or an ambulance ride. Compounding this, healthcare billing is notoriously opaque, delayed, and distorted by complex insurance disputes and arbitrary pricing. When an unpaid medical balance reaches a collection agency, it rarely signals fiscal negligence or an unwillingness to pay.
[P1.3] Frequently, it reflects a consumer disputing a surprise out-of-network charge or simply being blindsided by astronomical fees they had no ability to price-shop or avoid.
[P1.4] Because medical emergencies are exogenous, they reveal nothing about a borrower’s underlying discipline or habit of honoring standard debts. An individual who has paid their rent and utility bills reliably for a decade does not suddenly become an untrustworthy borrower because they caught pneumonia. Standard debts reflect deliberate budgeting; medical debt reflects the unpredictable fragility of the human body.
[P1.5] Injecting medical debt into credit reporting degrades the predictive integrity of underwriting models. It penalizes otherwise prime borrowers, depressing their scores and pricing them out of housing or traditional credit based on pure misfortune. By conflating unavoidable medical crises with financial unreliability, lenders misprice risk and punish consumers for events that carry no signal about their future repayment behavior. Medical debt has no place on a consumer credit report.

[C1.1] "Illness is not a financial choice" is a strong moral claim and a weak underwriting rule. Credit reports are not certificates of blame. They are records of outstanding claims, payment patterns, and collection status used to estimate whether a new lender will be repaid. The report does not list "pneumonia"; it lists an unpaid balance that remains after billing, insurance, notices, and chances to resolve it.
[C1.2] A lender deciding whether to extend credit needs to know that part of an applicant's future income may already be claimed.
[C1.3] Prediction never required that the original shock be voluntary. Layoffs, divorces, disaster repairs, and business interruptions are not chosen, but unpaid obligations arising from them affect capacity to repay. If two households have similar income and one carries a substantial unresolved medical collection, a new loan is riskier, not because the sick borrower is less virtuous, but because the household has less slack and another creditor competing for payment. A meaningful signal need only improve the estimate; it need not prove bad character.
[C1.4] The motion jumps from "some medical bills are opaque" to "none should appear." Disputes, coding mistakes, surprise charges, and insurance delays are reasons for verification, waiting periods, dispute flags, payment-plan accommodations, and deletion of paid or invalid balances. They are not reasons to hide verified, owed, long-unpaid debt. Concealing a whole category of liability makes reports less complete and pushes hidden risk onto other borrowers through tighter credit or higher prices.
[C1.5] Nor is disappearance costless. If medical balances never show up, creditors cannot distinguish a patient with no unpaid obligations from one repeatedly leaving large provider bills unresolved. Providers also lose a strong incentive to bill accurately and resolve claims quickly. The fair line is not illness versus choice; it is accurate, current, contestable information versus error and abuse.
[C1.6] Medical debt can be regulated without being erased, and because unresolved debt constrains future repayment, it belongs on the report.

[P2.1] CON misdiagnoses how credit underwriting actually works. Their claim that medical debt simply reflects reduced "capacity to repay" like a layoff or divorce [C1.3] collapses under scrutiny.
[P2.2] First, this is a false equivalence. A layoff, divorce, or natural disaster does not appear on a credit report as an independent liability [C1.3]. What appears is a delinquency on an auto loan, mortgage, or credit card—voluntary agreements with transparent pricing, clear repayment schedules, and mutual consent. Medical debt is entirely different: an involuntary, non-negotiable charge born of acute physical crisis.
[P2.3] Equating an unexpected hospital bill with a breach of a commercial contract fundamentally misunderstands what credit scores measure: deliberate repayment behavior.
[P2.4] Second, CON argues that unpaid medical debt represents a competing creditor claiming future income [C1.2, C1.3]. Yet medical collections do not function like ongoing, amortizing debt obligations. They are overwhelmingly static, disputed, and rarely resolved through fixed monthly installments. They do not compete for recurring cash flow the way a car note or personal loan does.
[P2.5] Consequently, an outstanding medical balance tells a lender virtually nothing about whether an applicant will pay next month’s mortgage. Treating it as equivalent to consumer default injects noise into underwriting, penalizing prime borrowers who reliably manage their contractual credit.
[P2.6] Third, CON suggests credit reporting is necessary to force patients to settle balances [C1.5]. But credit bureaus exist to assess risk for future lenders, not to act as debt-collection enforcers for hospital billing departments. Using credit scores as coercive leverage perverts their predictive purpose.
[P2.7] Finally, CON’s procedural remedies—waiting periods and dispute flags [C1.4]—concede that medical billing is uniquely chaotic. But administrative band-aids cannot repair an uninformative metric. When an item systematically produces false positives, retaining it does not protect lenders; it misprices risk and unfairly restricts credit. Excising medical debt restores underwriting to its true function: evaluating how borrowers manage voluntary financial obligations.
