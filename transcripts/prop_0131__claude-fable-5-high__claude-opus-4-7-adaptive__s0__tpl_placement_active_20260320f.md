[P1.1] Every developed country runs its long-term care system on an enormous unacknowledged subsidy: the unpaid labor of daughters, sons, and spouses. The value of that labor, in any country that has tried to estimate it, dwarfs public spending on formal long-term care. The proposition simply asks governments to stop pretending this subsidy is free and infinite. It is neither.
[P1.2] First, the demographic math is collapsing. People live longer with chronic conditions, families are smaller and more dispersed, and the women who historically absorbed this work are in the paid labor force. A system built on the assumption that someone will quietly quit her job to provide forty hours a week of care is a system designed to fail. When it fails, the costs don't vanish — they reappear as nursing home placements, emergency room visits, and neglected elders.
[P1.3] Paying caregivers is not a new cost; it is an honest accounting of a cost we already pay in worse forms.
[P1.4] Second, it is better economics. Home care by a family member is typically far cheaper than institutional care, which governments often end up funding anyway through programs like Medicaid. A caregiver stipend that delays or prevents institutionalization is fiscally self-defending. It also keeps caregivers attached to income, pensions, and health coverage, instead of converting today's caregiver into tomorrow's impoverished retiree the state must support.
[P1.5] Third, it is basic justice. Caregiving is skilled, exhausting work — lifting, medicating, managing dementia at 3 a.m. We pay strangers to do it; we impoverish relatives for doing it better, with more love and continuity. That penalty falls overwhelmingly on women and on low-income families who cannot buy their way out.
[P1.6] The real question in this debate is not whether care will be provided — it will, somehow — but who bears its cost and how cruelly. Generous caregiver payments are the cheaper, fairer, and more sustainable answer.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds humane until you look at who actually ends up worse off when governments make family caregiving a paid job.
[C1.2] Start with the money. "Generous" payments to every family caregiver mean paying, at public expense, for an enormous volume of care that is currently being provided anyway. The vast majority of that spending is deadweight — it rewards behavior that would have happened — while only a sliver represents new care drawn into the system. That is the worst possible cost-effectiveness ratio in social policy.
[C1.3] Every euro or dollar spent topping up a daughter who was already caring is a euro not spent on the elder with no family, on respite services, on dementia training, on the paid home-care workforce we genuinely cannot staff.
[C1.4] Second, generous caregiver pay is a trap dressed as a tribute. Offer a modest stipend to a mid-career woman, and you give her a financial reason to leave the labor market, lose pension accrual, lose skills, and re-enter a decade later unemployable. The proposition claims to rescue women from unpaid work; in practice it subsidizes them out of the formal economy and entrenches exactly the gendered division of labor it claims to fix. Sweden and other systems that leaned heaviest on professionalized public care produced better female employment and better elder outcomes than systems that paid relatives.
[C1.5] Third, family caregiving paid by the state is care without oversight. No inspections, no licensing, no colleagues, no substitute when the caregiver burns out or, worse, becomes abusive. Dementia care at 3 a.m. by an exhausted, untrained relative is not made safe by a check in the mail.
[C1.6] The honest alternative is not "assume unpaid care continues." It is to expand professional home care, fund real respite, protect caregivers through leave laws and pension credits, and target cash to the hardest cases. That beats writing a universal check that costs the most and reforms the least.
