[P1.1] Food-delivery apps do not present fulfillment as a clean choice. They open on courier delivery, bury pickup, and load the expensive path with the fewest extra taps. That design is not convenience. It is a surcharge on inattention.
[P1.2] Defaults decide outcomes. Most people accept the first option that looks ready. When delivery is preselected, menus are marked up for the courier channel, and pickup is framed as a lesser workaround, the app is not discovering demand. It is manufacturing the ticket it prefers: delivery fee, service fee, tip prompt, and a larger commission base.
[P1.3] A diner two blocks away, already leaving work, or sitting in the same plaza still gets pointed at a paid rider. The cheaper fulfillment path exists. The interface just refuses to treat it as first-class.
[P1.4] That steer is profitable precisely because it is wasteful for the user. The platform earns more when the order is over-fulfilled. The restaurant often pays a higher take rate. The street absorbs another short trip that never needed to happen.
[P1.5] None of this is required to keep delivery available for people who actually want it. Delivery can remain one tap away. What should stop is the habit of making the costliest route the path of least resistance.
[P1.6] If delivery were the better product on the merits, it would not need this architecture. Users who value speed or weather or kids in the car would still pay. The rest would not be quietly converted into the highest-margin basket. Defaulting more often to pickup or lower-fee options simply aligns the first screen with the user’s money instead of the platform’s take.
[P1.7] Choice remains. Extraction gets harder. That is the reform the proposition asks for, and it is overdue.

[C1.1] The proposition assumes the cheapest visible fulfillment is automatically the best default. That is mistaken. The real cost of a meal is not just the fee at checkout; it is time, travel, weather, mobility, childcare, and the risk of a cold or missed order. Delivery is often the lower-total-cost option even when its sticker price is higher.
[C1.2] A nurse finishing a night shift, a parent with a sick child, an elderly person in a walk-up, or an office ordering lunch does not save by being nudged to pickup. They save by paying for logistics.
[C1.3] Defaulting to delivery is not a trick. It reflects what most users select when they open an app whose purpose is to bring food to them. Pickup tabs, filters, and fee disclosures remain visible; switching requires a tap, not a barrier. If the concern is inattention, the answer is clearer fee breakdowns, not swapping one default for another.
[C1.4] A pickup default would be just as paternalistic—only now it presumes the platform knows the user has a car, free time, parking, and physical ability to collect food.
[C1.5] This redesign would also make the service worse where it is most valuable. Lower-fee options are often slower, less reliable, or limited to nearby restaurants. If apps push users toward pickup by default, marginal orders disappear, restaurants lose delivery volume, couriers lose trips that fit their schedules, and users face longer waits or fewer options. If delivery volume collapses, fixed costs are spread across fewer orders.
[C1.6] The decisive test is not whether one option has a smaller fee, but whether users can choose and see the tradeoff. They can. Delivery is expensive because it is costly, not because a default conceals pickup. The proposition would replace a convenience default with a frugality default, burdening the very people who use these apps to buy back scarce time.
