[P1.1] In the American cities where affordability collapsed, demand for access met a legal ceiling on homes. Jobs and households grew; duplexes, fourplexes, small apartment buildings, and mid-rises on ordinary residential land often could not be built. With large lots, parking mandates, single-family zoning, and discretionary approvals, demand had one outlet: bid up the same stock. That raises rents and the land value of every existing unit.
[P1.2] Legalizing density twenty years ago would have changed the marginal response from “pay more” to “build more.”
[P1.3] Lead time is decisive. Housing is a stock. A modest fourplex approved in the mid-2000s would still be occupied today, along with the cumulative wave of similar projects that by-right rules would have made normal. Builders, lenders, and small contractors would have adjusted; buildable capacity would have been spread across many parcels instead of hoarded as a scarcity premium.
[P1.4] Dividing a valuable location among more homes is what rowhouses, courtyard apartments, and missing-middle buildings do. Banning them does not preserve affordability; it preserves rationing by price.
[P1.5] The benefit does not stop with new units. Some higher-income households would have chosen newer housing, leaving older units with less pressure, more vacancy, and slower rent growth. A unit built fifteen years ago with dated finishes becomes today’s middling-price option; just as important, it keeps one more household from bidding against a nurse for an older bungalow. When cities refuse that release valve, “naturally affordable” stock is renovated, flipped, or priced by scarcity.
[P1.6] A demand objection proves too much: if people and jobs would have come anyway, legal limits determined whether their arrival produced homes or only higher bids per home. If new construction is called too expensive, expensive supply still beats no supply and a rationed ladder. Two decades of additional legal homes would leave a larger, more varied, less bid-up stock. Against today’s capped one, lower rents and prices are just arithmetic.

[C1.1] Lower prices are not "just arithmetic" [P1.6], because urban housing is not built in a vacuum on free land. Legalizing density changes land markets immediately while delivering new homes slowly, selectively, and expensively, leaving American cities no more affordable today.
[C1.2] First, upzoning reprices land broadly while destroying the cheapest homes. In built-out cities, new density requires teardowns. Wealthy enclaves stay largely untouched: expensive single-family homes leave little redevelopment margin, and private HOA covenants block multifamily building regardless of city zoning. Instead, investors target low-cost parcels, especially older rentals and the very "older bungalow" [P1.5] a nurse or working family could afford.
[C1.3] Meanwhile, legalizing density hands a speculative redevelopment option to every eligible lot at once, bidding up land prices and property tax assessments across whole neighborhoods even though only a small share of parcels ever get rebuilt. Where teardowns do occur, naturally affordable homes vanish immediately, replaced by costly modern units priced to cover inflated land, demolition, and code expenses. Because housing takes generations rather than fifteen years [P1.5] to filter down to modest budgets, two decades captures the demolition and land-price shock long before filtering arrives.
[C1.4] Second, lifting zoning caps in the mid-2000s [P1.3] would not have produced a steady wave of modest fourplexes. Look at the actual calendar: mid-2000s legalization lands at the peak of the credit bubble, fueling speculative land flipping right before the 2008 crash wiped out small builders and froze construction lending for nearly a decade. Even after recovery, non-zoning bottlenecks—building codes, staircase and elevator rules, impact fees, financing hurdles for small multiplexes, labor shortages, and post-2020 rate spikes—meant infill rarely penciled out except at luxury rents.
[C1.5] Finally, people and jobs would not simply "have come anyway" [P1.6] in fixed numbers. Dense upscale redevelopment creates amenities and job clustering that draw additional high-income households and investor capital, bidding surrounding housing upward rather than relieving pressure.

Starting in the mid-2000s credit bubble worsened that trap, inflating land basis before 2008 froze small-builder lending.
[C2.3] Nor do covenants and wealthy enclaves merely make the dividend "partial" [P2.3]—they funnel teardowns regressively into working-class neighborhoods where existing homes are cheap enough to scrap. Saying "Land cost is shared four ways" [P2.5] ignores structure: demolishing a depreciated bungalow or walkup destroys cheap shelter and substitutes new infill carrying scattered-site hard costs, utility upgrades, and tight multiplex financing that by-right rules [P2.3] never erase.
[C2.4] After 2008 stalled building, most added units arrived in the 2010s or later [P2.4]—barely a decade old today and still priced above modest budgets.
[C2.5] Finally, relief does not reliably start "at lease-up" [P2.5] because cities are open markets, not fixed queues [P2.6]. Dense upscale infill pulls mobile high earners inward, lets roommates split into separate units, and signals neighborhood turnover—raising rents on surviving bungalows next door while displaced tenants bid for a shrinking pool of cheap homes. Trading depreciated starter and rental stock for upscale infill leaves cities more expensive, not more affordable, today.
