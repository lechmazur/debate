[P1.2] Start with what consent is supposed to do. Consent works when you know what you are handing over. You know what your address is. You know you bought running shoes.
[P1.3] You do not know what your EEG headband reveals, because nobody fully does. Neural signals leak information the user never chose to express: attention lapses, fatigue, stress, arousal, emotional reactions to a face or a product, and potentially early markers of neurological or mental-health conditions. A user cannot meaningfully consent to disclosing something they don't know they're disclosing, to inferences that do not exist yet but will once the models improve. Click-through consent on this data is not an informed choice.
[P1.4] It is a blank check signed in the dark.
[P1.5] Second, look at the three uses the motion bans. Advertising, personalization, and dynamic pricing are exactly the uses where the company's interest runs directly against the user's. That is not better service.
[P1.6] It is reading the lock's tumblers from the inside. Ordinary tracking guesses at your state from behavior; neural data measures it before you act. The asymmetry is qualitatively new.
[P1.7] Third, we already refuse to let consent waive certain harms. You cannot sign away protection against genetic discrimination in many places, cannot contract into certain predatory loans, cannot sell your kidney. We do this when individual consent is structurally unreliable and the harm is severe and irreversible. Neural data qualifies on both counts: once your brain's responses are profiled, you cannot change them like a password.
[P1.8] Finally, this ban is narrow. Neurotech companies can still sell sleep tracking, focus training, and medical tools. They simply cannot monetize your mind against you. [clipped]
