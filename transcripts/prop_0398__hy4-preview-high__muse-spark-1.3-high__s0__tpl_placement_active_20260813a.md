[P1.1] A senior designer leaves San Francisco for Tulsa. She keeps the same output, but her rent, taxes, and local wage competition fall sharply. Should her pay stay welded to a San Francisco band? Generally, no. Compensation should follow the worker into her new labor market.
[P1.2] That is not corporate penny-pinching; it is how every labor market works. Pay has never been a pure reward for output. It bundles local market rates, cost of living, and the price of replacing a person where they live. A headquarters band exists because headquarters sits in an expensive, high-competition city.
[P1.3] When a worker relocates, those conditions change. Preserving the old salary is not “equal pay for equal work”; it is a geographic windfall untethered from productivity or market conditions.
[P1.4] A software engineer in New York City earning roughly $160,000 who moves to a much cheaper city does not become a $160,000 engineer there if the local market pays closer to $110,000. The premium is a transfer—from employer, colleagues, and shareholders—to one employee’s private gain in purchasing power. It also distorts the host community. High-earning remote workers in places like Boise or Lisbon have often bid up housing and priced out local nurses, teachers, and service workers.
[P1.5] Location-adjusted pay avoids exporting coastal inequality inland.
[P1.6] Fairness cuts the other way too. “Same work, same pay” sounds noble until we remember that pay bands already embed location. Multinational firms and public-sector locality-pay systems routinely pay different rates for identical jobs in different places; remote work does not magically erase that logic. If a company explicitly promises a national or headquarters-based band, honor that promise.
[P1.7] But the default expectation should be adjustment.
[P1.8] The sustainable rule is simple: when you move your body and your costs to a cheaper market, your pay should follow the market, not the memory of the old one. Otherwise remote work becomes a geographic lottery rather than a labor-market institution.

[C1.1] Pay should follow the value created, not the cost of the bedroom where the laptop sits. If a senior designer ships the same product to the same customers with the same impact, cutting her salary because she moved to Tulsa is a pay cut for identical contribution. Employers do not pay rent; they buy results.
[C1.2] The old rule that pay reflects local replacement cost collapses with remote work. A remote role is not competing in Tulsa or Boise; it recruits nationally. The relevant market is the talent pool the company actually draws from, and the replacement for a top remote engineer is another remote worker anywhere, not the cheapest local hire. Tying pay to the cheapest geography lets companies arbitrage workers while capturing all the gains of going remote themselves.
[C1.3] Location cuts also punish exactly the mobility employers claim to celebrate. People move to cheaper regions to care for aging parents, afford a family, or escape crushing housing costs. Penalizing that choice turns affordability into a trap: stay in an expensive hub or lose income. It creates two tiers for identical work, breeding resentment when colleagues discover the Tulsa designer earns less for the same output.
[C1.4] Trust, retention, and collaboration pay the price.
[C1.5] And firms already bank massive savings from remote moves — less office space, lower turnover, wider hiring. Demanding a second dividend by slicing salaries is greed dressed as economics. Companies that tried aggressive geographic cuts learned this quickly, facing backlash, quiet quitting, and talent flight to rivals offering location-independent pay. The sustainable rule is the opposite: pay the role, keep the talent, and let workers live where life works.
[C1.6] Nor will pay cuts save Boise or Lisbon. Even an adjusted salary dwarfs local wages while still leaving remote workers able to outbid nurses and teachers, so the housing pressure remains. The fix for strained housing is housing policy, not wage penalties extracted from individual movers.
