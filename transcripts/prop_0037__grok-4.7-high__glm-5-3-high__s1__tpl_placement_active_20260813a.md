[P1.1] Major cities should ban private cars from central districts because a center is a place for people, not a priced queue for vehicles. Private cars consume scarce street space, injure the unprotected, foul the air, and cut districts into corridors. Congestion pricing treats that as a traffic problem. A ban asks what the street is for.
[P1.2] Charging to remain is not ending the mismatch.
[P1.3] Pricing can thin peak flows. It cannot stop drivers who can pay from filling lanes, curbs, and intersections all day. The street stays a roadway. Children cannot cross freely.
[P1.4] Buses sit behind cars that bought the right to be there. Parking sterilizes land better used for housing or open space. The fee sorts access by income and leaves danger, noise, severance, and wasted land untouched, because those harms were never what the charge was built to end.
[P1.5] A ban does what a fee cannot: it frees the space. Lanes become transitways, cycle tracks, and sidewalks. Freight and emergency vehicles can be permitted, timed, and prioritized rather than competing with discretionary trips. People who need door-to-door access are better served by reliable transit, narrow exceptions, and streets safe enough to use than by thousands of ordinary private trips in the core.
[P1.6] Mode shift ceases to be a hope pinned to a price. It becomes what the street rewards.
[P1.7] Nor is this a loss of mobility. A bus, a tram, or two feet move more people per meter than a car. Where private cars have left the core, centers have not emptied: foot traffic sustains local trade, and fewer people are hit when those vehicles leave.
[P1.8] Pricing also builds a veto: pay, and further limits look like a broken deal. A ban states the rule, carves exceptions for freight, emergencies, and essential access, and designs the district around that rule. A center rationed by income remains a car city. A ban makes it a city for people.

[C1.1] Congestion pricing and bans are not competing philosophies; they are the flexible tool and the blunt one. My side defends the flexible tool, and the case rests on three claims: pricing achieves most of the benefit while preserving necessary trips, bans displace their costs rather than eliminate them, and pricing can adapt where a ban cannot.
[C1.2] First, access. A central district is not a leisure zone; it contains hospitals, workshops, care workers on night shifts, tradespeople hauling tools, and residents with disabilities that transit cannot fully serve. Pricing lets those trips continue while suppressing the discretionary ones—the commuter who could take the train but doesn't. A ban has to guess who deserves exceptions, and exemption lists are where good policy goes to die: every lobbyist, every "essential" trip, every hard case becomes a negotiation.
[C1.3] The price system discovers need automatically—those with real, high-value trips pay; the rest shift. That is not access rationed by income; it is congestion rationed by willingness to bear its true cost, which is exactly what economics says we should ration.
[C1.4] Second, displacement. Ban cars from the core and they do not vanish; they circle the boundary, clog the neighborhoods just outside, and push parking pressure onto residents who never voted for the change. Pricing, by contrast, charges vehicles for the congestion they actually cause, wherever they cause it, and can be tuned to keep traffic moving rather than shunting it one street over.
[C1.5] Third, adaptability and evidence. Pricing schemes in cities like London, Stockholm, and Singapore measurably cut traffic, and their rates, hours, and boundaries get adjusted as conditions change. A ban is a one-time bet: if freight logistics break, if a hospital complains, if the boundary creates chaos, you are amending a flagship policy in public, against everyone who was promised cars were gone forever. Pricing is honest—it says cars are welcome if they pay their costs.
[C1.6] A ban is a promise cities cannot keep, made for rhetorical cleanliness. Cities should charge, not exile.

[C2.2] Every permit regime ever run has faced the same pressures they attribute only to pricing. The difference is that under pricing, the default is *pay your costs* and exceptions adjust the edges; under a ban, the default is *nothing moves* and every trip must argue its way in. Their own framework makes politics broader, not narrower.
[C2.3] The equity complaint also misdescribes how pricing actually works. Real schemes discount or exempt low-income drivers, blue-badge holders, and emergency vehicles; revenue funds transit those carers depend on. "Income decides who clears the gantry" [P2.1] is a description of a badly designed scheme, not the tool.
[C2.4] Meanwhile, a ban that exempts the disabled carer, the night nurse, the plumber, the delivery van, the taxi—ask what's left of the car-free district. The exceptions converge toward the status quo, minus the flexibility.
[C2.5] On displacement, "the ring is a design task" [P2.6] is a concession dressed as an answer. Boundary congestion, parking spillover, and freight rerouting are real harms imposed on people outside the core, and a price can be tuned across space and time to manage them. A ban cannot—it has one setting.
[C2.6] Finally, the adaptability "tell" [P2.7] gets it backwards. A tool that revises rates as the city learns is honest governance. A tool that must either hold a total promise or break it publicly is the one that cannot respond when the hospital complains or logistics fracture. [C2.7] It has only promised never to change its mind—that's not principle; it's rigidity with good branding.
