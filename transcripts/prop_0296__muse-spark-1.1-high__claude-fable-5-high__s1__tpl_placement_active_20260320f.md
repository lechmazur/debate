[P1.1] A street sign is not an archive. A school name is not a footnote. It is the government saying, today, this person embodies our values and deserves to be lifted above daily life.
[P1.2] Keeping names that primarily honor colonizers, slaveholders, or dictators is not preserving history; it is preserving a hierarchy. When the claim to fame is conquest, human ownership, or autocracy, the honor is inseparable from the crime. Columbus didn't happen to colonize; colonization is why he is remembered. A slaveholder whose wealth and power rested on slavery, a dictator whose name comes from repression – honoring them is endorsing that foundation.
[P1.3] That endorsement has a present cost. A Black child required by law to attend Jefferson Davis Elementary, an Indigenous family whose town hall closes for a holiday celebrating their dispossessor, a resident who must write a dictator's name on every job application as their address – they are told by their own state that belonging is conditional. Historical continuity for some becomes daily humiliation for others. A democracy cannot claim equal citizenship while forcing that ritual.
[P1.4] The alternative is not erasure. Books remain. Museums remain. Curricula should teach these figures more honestly than a street sign ever could.
[P1.5] Removing a name from a pedestal and putting it in a classroom where it can be questioned is adding history, not deleting it. What we lose is only the lie that this honor was ever neutral.
[P1.6] Governments routinely rename things for far less – for developers, for highways, for marketing. Continuity is a selective excuse deployed only when the oppressed ask for change. Values change because people fought to change them. Renaming is not rewriting the past; it is finally refusing to let the past rewrite us.
[P1.7] Public honors should reflect the public we now aspire to be.

[C1.1] Names, once given, stop functioning as endorsements. They become coordinates. Nobody driving down Columbus Avenue experiences a state ceremony of praise; they experience an address. Linguists call this semantic bleaching: through daily use, a name detaches from its origin and becomes an ordinary label.
[C1.2] The claim that every street sign is the government saying "this person embodies our values, today" describes almost no one's actual experience of public space. If that theory were true, we'd expect residents of these streets to feel implicated constantly. They don't—until a renaming fight makes the origin salient again and forces everyone to relitigate it.
[C1.3] And there lies the practical disaster: the motion's standard cannot hold. "Primarily honors colonizers, slaveholders, or dictators" sounds surgical, but who was Washington primarily? A founder who enslaved hundreds. Jefferson?
[C1.4] Churchill, architect of empire? Once governments accept the principle that moral disqualification demands renaming, there is no principled stopping point—only an endless audit of the map, decided by whichever faction holds power that year. Each cycle of government produces a new purge of the previous one's names. That is not moral progress; it is instability written into the streetscape, and it is precisely how actual dictatorships behave—Stalingrad becomes Volgograd becomes contested again.
[C1.5] Meanwhile the costs land on real people: businesses reprinting everything, residents amending deeds and licenses, cities spending millions on signage—resources that could fund the schools themselves. And the political cost is worse: renaming battles are gifts to demagogues, converting genuine grievances into culture-war spectacle while material inequality sits untouched.
[C1.6] There is a better answer than deletion: contextualize. Keep the name, add the plaque, teach the full record where people actually encounter it. A renamed street teaches nothing; a confronted one teaches everything. Honest reckoning means standing before the difficult name and telling the truth about it—not scrubbing the map until history looks like it never needed reckoning at all.
