[C1.1] The primary purpose of an emergency shelter is triage: saving as many human lives as possible from freezing temperatures, violence, and exposure. While accommodating pets, vast personal possessions, and couples sounds compassionate, it fundamentally breaks the operational reality of emergency triage. We must measure a shelter’s success not just by who it coaxes indoors, but by how many humans it can safely keep alive.
[C1.2] First, shelter space is a zero-sum equation. Every square foot dedicated to a dog kennel, a massive storage locker, or a private room for a couple is space taken from another human being who needs a cot. When a shelter redesigns its floor plan to accommodate large carts of belongings or private partitions, its total bed count plummets. Accommodating one person’s dog or excess belongings literally means leaving another person out on the freezing sidewalk because the facility has hit a reduced capacity limit.
[C1.3] In life-or-death emergencies, maximizing the number of humans sheltered must take absolute priority.
[C1.4] Second, emergency shelters are inherently fragile environments housing deeply vulnerable people. Permitting large, unchecked volumes of possessions introduces severe biohazards—specifically bedbugs, lice, and sanitation issues—alongside fire hazards and blind spots for weapons or drugs. Bringing animals into tight communal spaces introduces allergens, unpredictable noise, and bite risks to a population already enduring severe physical and mental health crises. Furthermore, integrating couples into general or lightly partitioned spaces frequently brings complex, sometimes volatile domestic dynamics into close proximity with trauma survivors who simply need a safe, highly regulated place to rest.
[C1.5] PRO argues that storage and expansive accommodation are part of the exit strategy [P1.6]. But before someone can exit homelessness, they must survive the night. Lowering barriers for a few by radically changing shelter design means raising capacity and safety barriers for the many. By keeping emergency shelters streamlined and focused purely on basic human security, we preserve the footprint and order necessary to save the greatest number of lives.
