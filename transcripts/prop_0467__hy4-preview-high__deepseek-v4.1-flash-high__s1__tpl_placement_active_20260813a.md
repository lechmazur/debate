[P1.1] International law judges violence by consequences, not delivery mechanism. A cyber operation that cuts a city's power in freezing weather, disables water treatment, or sabotages hospital life-support causes the same death and destruction as a bomb. If a state bombed that grid or poisoned that water, it would be an armed attack. Using code instead of explosives should not give the attacker a legal discount.
[P1.2] The proposition does not militarize every hack. Under international law, equivalence to an armed attack is judged by consequences, so cyberattacks on civilian critical infrastructure qualify when they produce significant destruction, death or serious injury, or paralysis of services people cannot live without. The 2015 and 2016 attacks on Ukraine's power grid left hundreds of thousands without heat and light in winter; the attempted poisoning of a Florida water supply showed how a few keystrokes could have caused mass casualties.
[P1.3] Those are not nuisances or crimes. They are uses of force that match armed attacks in scale and effect.
[P1.4] Equivalence matters because the grey zone is an aggressor's playground. When the only lawful answers are indictments, sanctions, or cyber countermeasures, attackers learn they can paralyze a nation while denying responsibility and paying no real cost. Equivalence restores deterrence by making clear that a proportional response is available—a strike on the command node, launch infrastructure, or facility—bounded by necessity, distinction, and proportionality. It does not mandate war; it removes the sanctuary behind 'it was only code.'
[P1.5] CON will warn about escalation and attribution. But the bar is high, responses are limited to what is necessary and proportionate, and attribution is no harder than for kinetic attacks; forensics, intelligence, and allied sharing suffice. The danger is a rule telling a victim that, because the attacker used malware rather than missiles, it must absorb blackout and let its citizens freeze. If the harm is equivalent, the law's answer must be equivalent: a right of proportional self-defense, including military options.
