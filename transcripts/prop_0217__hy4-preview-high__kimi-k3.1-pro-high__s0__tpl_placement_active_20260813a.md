[P1.1] Water shortages repeat; policy should learn, not panic. The main flaw of blunt universal restrictions is that they target all users equally when the actual slack in the system is concentrated in heavy, discretionary use. A household already on a modest essential-use budget is told to cut again the same as an estate irrigating a lawn, filling a pool, or running fountains. Both are asked to sacrifice, yet only one is draining the reservoir.
[P1.2] That is not proportionate; it is visible sacrifice mistaken for conservation.
[P1.3] Rising block pricing fixes that. Keep the first block at a lifeline price for drinking, cooking, and sanitation; then let the marginal price climb steeply in higher blocks. The frugal family pays little and never sacrifices hygiene, while the heaviest users face a bill that keeps rising until they cover the pool, replace turf, recycle greywater, or fix leaks. The incentive is continuous, not episodic.
[P1.4] Recurring shortages make this superiority clearer. Restrictions are emergency orders: when rain returns, watering bans and odd-even schedules are lifted and consumption rebounds because landscapes and habits are unchanged. A steep top-block price is standing policy. It tells homeowners and businesses that the next dry year will also be expensive, so investing in low-flow fixtures, drought-tolerant landscaping, or on-site reuse pays off before crisis hits.
[P1.5] Pricing changes the baseline; restrictions only ration the panic.
[P1.6] Consider the simplest contrast. A universal lawn-watering ban requires officials to police thousands of properties and still lets a determined heavy user hose at night or truck in water. A rising block price needs no police; the meter does the work, and the revenue can fund rebates and repairs. Equity is built in: a lifeline block for essential use, steep surcharges above it, and help for low-income households.
[P1.7] The result is conservation where the water actually is, not shared deprivation disguised as fairness.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes an expensive signal for a dependable shortage plan. During a severe shortage, the first test is whether policy delivers prompt, predictable reductions before supplies are exhausted. Sharply rising prices fail that test: bills often arrive weeks after consumption, customers may not know their current block, and affluent users can simply treat the surcharge as the cost of keeping a green lawn. A restriction specifies the required behavior now; a price merely hopes the response will be sufficient.
[C1.2] PRO repeatedly equates “heavy” with “wasteful,” but the meter cannot make that distinction. It records volume, not whether water serves a large family, a care facility, a restaurant, or a swimming pool. A lifeline block attached to each account does not solve this: household size varies, tenants may share master meters, and leaks can push vulnerable customers into punitive blocks. The result can be a wealthy estate paying comfortably for discretionary use while a crowded household faces financial pressure over essential consumption.
[C1.3] That is targeting by purchasing power, not by social priority.
[C1.4] “The meter does the work” also exaggerates the administrative advantage. Pricing still requires accurate meters, suitable tariff design, hardship protections, billing disputes, and enforcement against nonpayment or circumvention. Meanwhile, visible restrictions create a common public standard: everyone knows what is prohibited, neighbors can identify violations, and officials can concentrate enforcement on serious offenders. They need not mean police inspecting every lawn each night.
[C1.5] Recurring shortages do weaken the case for purely improvised emergency orders, but they do not establish pricing as the superior main instrument. Standing restrictions can preserve outdoor-watering limits, efficiency requirements, and clear drought-stage rules between crises. Conservation norms and investments need not disappear when rain returns.
[C1.6] The decisive comparison is therefore not compassionate prices versus shared deprivation. It is uncertain, unevenly felt financial pressure versus rules that can secure immediate system-wide savings. Modest pricing may supplement those rules, but making sharply punitive prices the principal response risks both missed conservation targets and avoidable hardship precisely when reliability matters most.
