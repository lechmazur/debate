[P1.1] A host does not own the block. Owning a house gives a right to use it, not to make adjoining homes the unpaid buffer for a rotating commercial party. Stronger nuisance rules—quiet hours, occupancy caps, a reachable local contact, escalating fines, and permit revocation for repeat violations—make the person taking the income also bear the disruption.
[P1.2] The harm is concrete: midnight noise, strangers parking across driveways, bottles and trash in the street, and the unease of never knowing who is next door. The guest pays for a weekend; the owner collects the fee; the neighbor absorbs the cost every week: children, older residents, and shift workers who expected a home to be a home. Cities already separate bars and hotels from quiet residential streets for this reason. A short-term rental functioning as an event venue should not escape because the booking happens through an app.
[P1.3] The tourism objection changes the subject. This is not tourism versus empty streets, but flexible profit for a few owners versus a city livable enough to attract visitors at all. Tourists come for safe, clean, stable neighborhoods; nuisance factories turn residents against all short-term rentals and make the destination a “party spot,” not a place worth returning to. Weeding out repeat offenders protects hosts who do not want a 2 a.m. disturbance defining their industry.
[P1.4] Enforcement need not be hostility to visitors. A family renting a flat for a wedding weekend is not the target; a property with repeated noise calls, overcrowding, or police visits is. Clear rules let owners screen guests, cap gatherings, answer complaints, and stop profiting from chaos if they will not. Flexible hosting can remain flexible inside the boundary set by other people’s right to quiet enjoyment.
[P1.5] A city that refuses to draw that boundary is not defending tourism; it is subsidizing a private business with residents’ sleep, safety, and patience.

[C1.3] Unverified complaints and ordinary vacation noise become a case for shutting the listing. Hosts cannot pre-screen every guest into silence, so the cautious response is fewer listings.

[C1.5] It shrinks places to stay, raises prices, and exports the same weekends to the next town.
[C1.6] A family wedding weekend is not spared just because it is not the stated target. Caps and revocation chill the ordinary booking along with the bad one, because the penalty lands on the owner who is not in the driveway at midnight. If the harm is noise, trash, and parking, fine the people who commit those acts. Do not make flexible hosting too risky to offer.

General nuisance laws stop disturbances after they occur; they miss the mechanism that keeps producing them: an absent owner, rotating guests, and no durable responsibility for the block. Fine a drunk guest at 1 a.m.; by morning he is gone, the listing remains, the fee is collected, and next weekend the house is repopulated. Stronger rules attach to the continuing operator because prevention is possible there.
[P2.2] “Treating booking status as guilt” [C1.2] misdescribes the comparison. A kegger in an owner-occupied basement should be punished, but ordinary enforcement can find it next week. A short-term party rental is a commercial use of a home: transient guests, high occupancy, and income that rewards pushing disruption onto the block. Occupancy caps, local contacts, and permit conditions are not punishment for an app; they are normal conditions on businesses inviting transient groups into residential streets.
[P2.3] The “neighbor veto” fear [C1.3] attacks a rule the motion does not require. Escalation can rest on documented noise calls, citations, police reports, overcrowding, or repeated confirmed disturbances, with notice and appeal before revocation. That protects careful hosts: one grumpy complaint does not close a listing; a documented pattern does. Nor must an owner guarantee silence.
[P2.4] He must use tools only he has—guest caps, party bans, deposits, a reachable manager, refusal of problematic bookings—because guests are transient and the property is not.
[P2.5] CON’s tourism story assumes any obligation destroys supply [C1.4] and that ordinary bookings are chilled [C1.6]. The metric should be durable visits without forcing residents to supply unpaid security, cleanup, and lost sleep. Targeted enforcement separates compliant hosts from repeat-offender listings, lowers backlash, and preserves neighborhoods tourists want. “Fine the people who commit those acts” [C1.6] is fair only until the actor is gone or immediately replaced. Cities need not wait for another 1 a.m. call to protect a boundary owners can respect in advance.
