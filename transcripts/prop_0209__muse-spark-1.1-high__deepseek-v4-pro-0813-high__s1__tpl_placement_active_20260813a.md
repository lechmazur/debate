[P1.1] The current system makes geography destiny. Under Dublin, a Syrian family landing in Lampedusa is Italy's problem, not Europe's. That is indefensible in a Union that abolished internal borders and claims a Common European Asylum System.
[P1.2] Burden-sharing quotas fix the incentive structure. When responsibility is allocated by objective capacity — population, GDP, existing reception and job markets — no single state collapses under a few months of boat arrivals. Each state has reason to build adequate reception instead of hoping the boat goes elsewhere. Distribution prevents the 100,000-person camps like Moria that shame Europe, breed abuse, and make fair procedure impossible.
[P1.3] A federation that shares a currency, a budget, and a border must share asylum.
[P1.4] Leaving frontline states alone actively makes European asylum worse. Overwhelmed administrations cannot process claims quickly, so people wait years in limbo. States respond rationally but destructively: pushbacks at sea, prolonged detention, or waving people north without fingerprints, fueling uncontrolled secondary movement. That destroys mutual trust and gives every government an excuse to reimpose internal border controls, killing Schengen.
[P1.5] The status quo is not control; it is organized neglect that externalizes costs onto the weakest link and then blames that link for failing.
[P1.6] Quotas are not an invitation for more arrivals; they are a management tool for arrivals that happen anyway. Boats come whether Greece wants them or not. The question is whether 450 million Europeans carry that reality or 10 million Greeks do.
[P1.7] The EU already lives by financial solidarity in agriculture, cohesion, banking. Article 80 TFEU explicitly mandates solidarity in asylum. A quota system operationalizes that treaty promise, with flexibility — states can contribute funding, personnel or reception places — but no free opt-out from responsibility.
[P1.8] Either asylum is European or it is not. If we mean it is European, we must match responsibility to capacity. Quotas preserve human dignity, make processing functional, and save the common system from unraveling state by state.

[C1.1] Mandatory quotas do not solve the EU’s asylum failure; they redistribute its symptoms while making the underlying problems worse. The proposal assumes a central planner can match people to capacity. But relocation only occurs after arrival. A boat landing in Lampedusa still faces Italian reception, security screening, fingerprinting, and initial asylum processing.
[C1.2] Quotas do not remove those bottlenecks; they add a contested distribution decision on top of them.
[C1.3] The claim that quotas fix the incentive structure [P1.2] gets the incentives backwards. If crossing the Mediterranean gives a claimant access to resettlement across the whole EU, smugglers sell not just a crossing but a Europe-wide placement lottery. Meanwhile assigned destinations can simply tolerate onward movement; the quota becomes fiction. That is what happened when the last binding relocation effort collapsed: several governments refused or took token numbers, enforcement was negligible, and the main output was political fury, not solidarity.
[C1.4] Quotas would worsen the political crisis they claim to cure. Centralized allocation gives nationalist parties a ready-made “Brussels is forcing migrants on you” grievance. That is not a glitch; it is the very dynamic that has empowered re-bordering and weakened Schengen. A policy that cannot be implemented without deepening internal division is not solidarity.
[C1.5] The proposed choice is false. The alternative to leaving frontline states alone is not mandatory redistribution; it is operational support at arrival points, faster asylum decisions, functioning returns, and external migration partnerships. Article 80 requires solidarity, but solidarity can be financial and operational rather than coercive relocation.
[C1.6] Quotas look fair on paper. In practice they would mean the same arrivals, less enforcement, more secondary movement, and a more divided EU. That is not a functioning asylum system; it is a managed illusion of one.
