[P1.1] "Not commercially viable" is not an objection to the proposition — it is the premise of it. If serving rural areas were profitable, no subsidy would be needed and there would be nothing to debate. The whole case for public action begins precisely where the private business case ends.
[P1.2] So judge this on the right question. Not "can a carrier close its internal rate of return on a 40-house valley?" but "does the total return to society exceed the cost of building?" Those two numbers diverge, systematically, because a broadband line generates enormous value the seller cannot bill for. A clinic that can do remote cardiology consults, a school district that can assign homework at all, a grain farmer running soil-moisture sensors, a small manufacturer that can bid on contracts electronically, a 911 dispatch system that works — none of that shows up on a $70 monthly invoice.
[P1.3] The carrier captures the subscription; the community captures the surplus. Whenever benefits are that badly split from revenue, private investment stops far short of the socially optimal build. That is textbook market failure, and it is the standard justification for public money.
[P1.4] We have run this experiment before. In the 1930s, private utilities said stringing wire to scattered farms would never pay. They were right about their balance sheets and wrong about the country. Public lending and cooperatives electrified rural America within a generation, and nobody now argues the farms should have been left dark.
[P1.5] Notice also the false baseline in the opposition's likely case. Doing nothing is not free. Unconnected regions export their young people, lose their businesses, and shift costs onto public systems anyway — patients driving two hours for appointments, students bused to library parking lots, federal programs propping up communities that could have supported themselves. We pay either way; subsidy is the version where we get an asset.
[P1.6] Finally, subsidy does not mean blank checks. Reverse auctions, technology-neutral bidding, coverage and speed obligations, and clawbacks for non-performance make the state a buyer of results, not a donor. [clipped]

[C1.1] Commercial non-viability is information, not an inconvenience to override. When carriers say a sparse valley will not pay, they are usually reporting a hard fact about density, distance, and thin demand. Governments should not treat that signal as a blank check to spend other people’s money.
[C1.2] Subsidy starts from the wrong comparison. The question is not whether a clinic or a farmer would gain something from a fiber drop. It is whether those gains exceed the full social cost of forcing the build—capital burned on low-use plant, ongoing maintenance in terrain that never densifies, and every alternative use of the same tax dollar. Sparse areas fail private return tests for a reason: few subscribers share enormous fixed costs.
[C1.3] Inflating “community surplus” until the math works is advocacy, not accounting. You can always invent unpriced benefits large enough to justify almost anything.
[C1.4] The electrification parallel is romantic and false. Electricity had no close substitute and transformed production itself. Broadband already faces expanding options—fixed wireless, improved mobile, low-Earth-orbit satellite—that improve without condemning taxpayers to strand copper or fiber across empty stretches. Locking public money into yesterday’s architecture crowds out the very competition that is shrinking the gap.
[C1.5] Doing nothing is not the only baseline. Targeted vouchers for households that actually subscribe, spectrum policy that lets innovators reach remote users, and removal of local barriers to private build all address real needs without guaranteeing returns to whoever wins the reverse auction. History of rural telecom subsidies is littered with overbuilds, slow rollouts, and carriers that take the grant then under-deliver. Performance rules look tidy on paper; monitoring sparse networks is expensive and politically soft.
[C1.6] A government that funds every project whose private books look ugly will fund endlessly. Scarcity requires discrimination. When the market says no, the presumption should stay no—unless proponents prove the surplus with hard numbers, not slogans about leaving people behind.
