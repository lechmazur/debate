[P1.1] Peer-to-peer rooftop solar trading is a straightforward proposition: if someone installs solar panels on their roof and generates surplus electricity, they should be free to sell that power directly to a neighbor, a local business, or anyone else on the same distribution network, rather than being forced to route every transaction through a monopoly utility at state-set rates.
[P1.2] The case rests on three load-bearing claims.
[P1.3] First, economic efficiency. Rooftop solar owners currently face a take-it-or-leave-it deal: feed excess power into the grid at whatever compensation the regulator sets, often far below retail rates, or let it go to waste. Meanwhile, nearby homes pay full retail price for power that may have traveled long distances and incurred transmission losses. P2P trading lets buyer and seller split that difference.
[P1.4] The seller gets more than the feed-in tariff; the buyer pays less than retail. Both win, and the transaction reflects the actual local value of the electricity rather than an administrative fiction.
[P1.5] Second, adoption incentives. The decision to install rooftop solar hinges on payback period. When households can earn market rates for surplus generation rather than token compensation, the economics improve sharply. More installations mean more distributed clean capacity, reduced peak demand on centralized plants, and less transmission infrastructure stress.
[P1.6] The climate and resilience dividends compound.
[P1.7] Third, access and equity. Renters, apartment dwellers, and households with shaded roofs are largely shut out of rooftop solar today. P2P trading lets them purchase clean power from a neighbor's excess production. It democratizes participation in the energy transition rather than confining its benefits to owner-occupants with ideal roofs and capital.
[P1.8] The proposition says *allow*. The default stance of governments should be permission, not prohibition, unless opponents can demonstrate concrete harms that outweigh these gains. Countries that permit P2P solar trading unlock local clean energy markets that are more efficient, more accessible, and faster to scale.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds appealing in isolation, but it treats the electricity grid as if it were a flea market — a simple venue for bilateral deals. It is not. The grid is a real-time physical system where supply and demand must balance every second, and where local infrastructure has hard capacity limits. P2P trading breaks the operational model that keeps the lights on, and the costs of that breakage fall on people who never signed up for the experiment.
[C1.2] Start with the grid itself. Distribution networks were designed for one-directional power flow: power stations to homes. When rooftop solar feeds back into the grid, utilities already struggle with voltage fluctuations, transformer stress, and reverse power flows at scale. P2P trading amplifies this problem by encouraging *more* bilateral exchanges across local infrastructure that the utility can neither see nor manage in real time.
[C1.3] Every kilowatt a neighbor sells to another neighbor still flows through the same shared wires — but now the utility, which is responsible for maintaining those wires and keeping voltage stable, has less information about what is happening on its own network. You cannot safely operate a system you cannot observe.
[C1.4] Second, the economics are a shell game. P2P sellers still depend on the grid every night, every cloudy day, every time their panels underperform. The grid provides frequency regulation, voltage support, spinning reserves, and instant backup — services that cost real money to maintain. Under the current system, those costs are recovered through utility bills.
[C1.5] P2P trading lets solar households route transactions outside that billing structure while still relying on the same safety net. The shortfall gets socialized onto remaining ratepayers, who are disproportionately renters, lower-income households, and people in older housing — exactly the group the PRO side claims to champion.
[C1.6] The proposition says "allow," as if permission is costless. It is not. Regulatory infrastructure, billing redesign, grid monitoring upgrades, and consumer protection frameworks all carry enormous costs, and the supposed efficiency gains vanish once you account for the system those gains depend on.

[P2.1] The opponent's entire case rests on a false premise: that P2P trading makes the grid invisible. It does not. Every kilowatt in a P2P transaction still passes through a metered connection point that the distribution utility owns, monitors, and can control. P2P trading is a financial arrangement — who pays whom and at what price — layered on top of physical delivery the utility already tracks.
[P2.2] Smart meters don't stop recording flow just because the seller's invoice goes to a neighbor instead of the utility. The claim that the utility "has less information about what is happening on its own network" confuses billing with observability. The grid operator sees the same electrons, the same voltage, the same transformers. What changes is the settlement layer — and that is a feature, not a bug.
[P2.3] The cost-shifting argument is CON's strongest card, but it misfires. The problem of solar households relying on grid backup while paying less for it already exists under net metering and feed-in tariffs — schemes every country with rooftop solar already operates. P2P trading doesn't create that dynamic; it subjects it to market discipline. Today, a solar household gets a guaranteed administrative price regardless of whether anyone wants their power.
[P2.4] Under P2P, they must find a buyer at a price that buyer accepts. Competition replaces guarantees. Meanwhile, nothing about allowing P2P trading prevents regulators from attaching a grid-service fee or wheeling charge to each transaction to recover infrastructure costs. The framework can charge for the wires the deal uses — it just doesn't have to route the energy payment through a monopoly.
[P2.5] The real question isn't whether P2P trading is costless. It's whether the alternative — forcing every solar kilowatt through a single buyer at state-set rates — is cheaper or fairer. It isn't. Net metering already socializes costs; the difference is that it does so without any market check.
[P2.6] Allowing P2P trading doesn't add a new burden to the grid. It adds a market where today there is only a monopoly.
