[P1.1] The question is narrow: when a studio uses AI to materially change a living actor’s face, body, or voice, it must get explicit consent and pay for it. That is not a ban; it is the minimum legal and ethical floor for using a person’s identity as production material.
[P1.2] Performances are not raw footage owned without limits. They are extensions of a performer’s skill, reputation, and bodily autonomy. De-aging or synthetic voice does not merely polish a take; it can create a version of the actor that never chose the line, the scene, or the product. Imagine an actor hired for a prestige drama, then told the studio has de-aged them to reprise the role in a franchise prequel, or cloned their voice for an ad read.
[P1.3] If the studio can do that under a broad contract without separate permission or payment, the actor becomes a reusable asset rather than a professional whose work has boundaries.
[P1.4] Consent matters because blanket clauses are not meaningful in an industry with steep power imbalances. Extras, stunt performers, and early-career actors cannot realistically negotiate away future synthetic uses. Explicit consent forces specificity: what tool, what scene, what duration, what context. Compensation matters because AI alteration creates new commercial value and new labor substitutes.
[P1.5] A synthetic voice can save the studio a re-recording session; a de-aged performance can avoid hiring the actor for new shooting days. If the studio profits from that substitution, the performer whose identity supplies the value should be paid.
[P1.6] The obvious objection is that this slows production or duplicates existing contracts. But speed is not a reason to bypass personhood, and form contracts are precisely why explicit standards are needed. The principle is simple: if technology lets studios rewrite a living performer’s contribution, the performer must hold the pen and share the profit.

[C1.1] The resolution sounds protective, but look at what it actually asks: a blanket legal mandate requiring separate, explicit consent and compensation any time a studio "materially alters" a performance with AI. That standard cannot be drawn cleanly, and where it is drawn, it will not help the performers PRO cares about most.
[C1.2] Start with the vagueness. "Materially alter" already covers ordinary VFX: color grading a face, smoothing wrinkles, digitally removing a stunt rig, replacing a single ADR line because the actor was sick. Studios have always digitally reshaped performances without a special consent regime, because performers signed general releases covering post-production work. AI de-aging and voice synthesis are a difference of degree, not category.
[C1.3] A rule built for deepfake replacement of an actor's identity will inevitably sweep in routine, harmless digital touch-ups, because no court or contract can reliably separate "polish" from "material change" in advance. That is not a minor drafting problem — it is the whole mechanism collapsing into either total permissiveness or total paralysis.
[C1.4] Now the power imbalance point PRO leans on. They admit extras and early-career actors can't meaningfully negotiate. But a legal requirement for "explicit consent" doesn't fix that — it just moves the boilerplate one clause over. Studios will still write the consent form, still bundle it into the standard contract, still make signing a condition of employment.
[C1.5] The mandate gives powerless performers a piece of paper, not leverage. Meanwhile SAG-AFTRA and similar unions are already negotiating collectively bargained AI protections, with actual enforcement muscle and industry-wide standards — a far better lever than a scattered, case-by-case legal consent requirement that top-tier stars will simply add to contracts they already control.
[C1.6] Finally, weigh the cost. A rigid mandate raises legal risk and friction for legitimate uses — continuity fixes, safety substitutions, minor corrections — making studios more cautious about hiring older or vulnerable performers at all, not less. PRO's cure produces exactly the exclusion it claims to prevent.
