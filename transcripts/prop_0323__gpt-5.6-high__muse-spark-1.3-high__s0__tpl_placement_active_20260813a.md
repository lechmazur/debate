[P1.1] A like button can help people express appreciation; a public scoreboard does something different. It turns every post into a status ranking and every reader into a spectator of the crowd. The sensible default is therefore to hide public like and repost totals in ordinary browsing, while letting users opt back in and letting creators retain private analytics. This is choice architecture, not prohibition.
[P1.2] Visible metrics create a feedback loop. People use early popularity as a shortcut for quality, so a small, perhaps accidental, lead attracts more attention and becomes self-reinforcing. Creators then adapt not to what is accurate, useful, or original, but to what wins the fastest measurable reaction. Outrage, tribal signaling, sensational claims, and imitation have an advantage because they trigger instant engagement.
[P1.3] The count does not merely describe demand; it manufactures it.
[P1.4] Default visibility also imposes costs on people who never affirmatively chose a contest. A teenager sharing art sees a low number as public failure; a user deciding whether to speak sees the likely social penalty; coordinated actors can purchase or mobilize engagement to fabricate legitimacy. Because defaults govern the experience of passive users, burying an opt-out in settings is not meaningful control.
[P1.5] Discovery does not require public scoreboards. Platforms may still recommend material using relevance, freshness, or aggregated engagement; creators may still inspect performance; users may still like and repost. What disappears is the public pressure attached to the number. And if popularity is genuinely informative, an opt-in remains available.
[P1.6] The burden should be on platforms to justify exposing a psychologically coercive ranking, not on millions of users to escape it.
[P1.7] Social media should help people find and exchange worthwhile expression, not make worth synonymous with applause. A reversible default preserves the core functions while removing the most corrosive social cue. That is the clearly better baseline.

[P2.2] The opacity objection confuses popularity with accountability. A public count cannot reveal why the platform recommended something, whether impressions were bought, or whether engagement was authentic. A million likes can be manipulated; ten likes can simply mean a post is new. Real algorithmic accountability comes from recommendation explanations, ad labels, feed controls, and enforcement—not an applause meter.
[P2.3] Under our policy, recommendations and private analytics remain, and any reader who values totals can opt in.
[P2.4] The claim that unknown creators need a public tally also mistakes evidence of value for value itself. Nor does astroturf become safer: the fabricated total is the fraudster’s main instrument of legitimacy, and most scrollers will not perform forensic ratio checks before sharing.
[P2.5] Finally, “comparison will migrate” is not a defense of preserving every comparison mechanism. Harm reduction need not be a cure. Comments convey substance; a like count gives every post an instant, precise rank. Removing that uniquely salient cue reduces pressure even if insecurity survives elsewhere.
[P2.6] Calling this “forced blindness” reverses the facts. The default is reversible; today’s public contest is imposed on everyone, including those who never chose it. And “more context” cannot rescue CON: a contextless integer is not context. Preserve expression, discovery, and user choice; stop making popularity the compulsory lens through which all three are viewed.
