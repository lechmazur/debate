[P1.1] Secondary schools should let AI do what it does best—mastery practice—and keep human teachers at the center of writing feedback. Mastery practice is repetitive, low-stakes, and correctable: comma rules, thesis statements, evidence citation, algebra steps, vocabulary, grammar drills. AI can supply unlimited examples, instant correction, and patient repetition. That is valuable precisely because it frees teacher time.
[P1.2] Writing feedback is not just error correction. It is a judgment about purpose, audience, voice, and truth. A tenth-grade essay on a personal loss may be grammatically clean yet emotionally shallow, or awkwardly phrased yet genuinely insightful. A teacher can say, “This matters; slow down and let the reader see it.” An AI is likely to reward smoothness, predictable structure, and rubric keywords.
[P1.3] If students treat AI as their main feedback source, they learn to write for a prediction engine rather than for readers.
[P1.4] The danger is not that AI is useless; it is that its strengths are misleading. It can be fast and consistent, but speed is not wisdom. It can catch surface errors, but it may miss weak reasoning, unacknowledged sources, or a student’s emerging argument. It may also penalize unusual sentence rhythms or culturally specific language.
[P1.5] Opponents can point to instant comments and reduced backlogs, but those are reasons to automate drills, not to outsource the hard part of teaching writing. In secondary school, writing feedback shapes intellectual confidence and identity. The student who needs feedback most needs a responsible adult reader, not a chatbot optimized for plausible commentary.
[P1.6] So the right division is simple: use AI for drills, scaffolded practice, and instant mastery checks; reserve main writing feedback for teachers who can mentor revision, interpret intent, and hold students to genuine communication. That makes AI a useful tutor, but keeps education human where it matters.
