[P1.1] The proposition asks a simple question: should we accept limited, one-time friction to stop a permanent, growing stream of harm? The answer is yes. The most wasteful single-use takeaway packaging—expanded polystyrene clamshells, superfluous plastic films, non-recyclable composite cups—persists for centuries, fragments into microplastics, and shifts cleanup costs onto public budgets. A phased ban targets the worst offenders, not all convenience.
[P1.2] That narrowing matters: we are not abolishing takeaway food, only the clearly disposable items for which viable substitutes already exist.
[P1.3] Consider the mechanism. Each avoided polystyrene container prevents a product that resists recycling and leaches contaminants from entering streets, rivers, and landfills. The environmental debt compounds daily; the transition cost is paid once. When cities restrict such items, litter surveys typically show visible reduction in food-related debris within a year or two, and businesses adapt by switching to molded fiber or reusable systems.
[P1.4] The saved public funds can be redirected to services that small businesses and residents actually value.
[P1.5] The opposition will lean on consumer inconvenience and small-business strain. But the motion already concedes those costs; the debate is whether they outweigh the benefit. They do not. Small operators already comply with constant health, tax, and labeling changes; packaging swaps are a routine adjustment, especially with phased timelines and modest support.
[P1.6] Consumers faced with a default shift readily adopt reusable containers or accept slight repackaging, much as they did with carry-out bag norms.
[P1.7] Crucially, the status quo is not cost-free. Municipal waste fees, flood clogged by litter, and microplastic exposure are silent taxes on residents and especially on vulnerable communities. Phasing out the worst packaging corrects a market failure where the price of convenience is paid by everyone except the original user. A phase-out also signals producers to innovate, spreading better alternatives at scale and lowering costs over time.
[P1.8] Temporary inconvenience is a fair price for stopping indefinite ecological and fiscal bleed.

Chains can spread that across thousands of stores; a family shop cannot. [C1.4] Second, the environmental gain is blinder than PRO claims. 'Most wasteful' is defined by persistence and visibility—styrofoam and films that litter streets—not lifecycle harm [P1.3]. Reusables only win with high return rates and efficient washing; unreturned cups can cost more water and energy per use than disposables.
[C1.5] Packaging that prevents leaks and spoilage also curbs food waste, whose footprint dwarfs the wrapper. We would trade a visible nuisance for hidden, possibly larger, harm.
[C1.6] The real comparison is not the motion versus the status quo. It is a coercive phase-out versus smarter tools: deposit-return, litter enforcement, producer fees, real composting infrastructure. Those cut waste without imposing a permanent, regressive cost on small firms and their customers. Because this phase-out is both economically concentrated and environmentally blinkered, it fails the cost-benefit test the motion invokes.
