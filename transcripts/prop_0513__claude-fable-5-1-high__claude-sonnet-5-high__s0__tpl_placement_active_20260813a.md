[P1.1] A merger review that counts every dollar a firm can extract from customers but ignores every dollar it can extract from its workers is not neutral. It is a policy choice to protect one group and abandon another, and it should end.
[P1.2] Start with the economics, because it is embarrassingly simple. Monopsony is monopoly with the axes flipped. A firm that buys labor with market power hires fewer people at lower wages, exactly as a seller with market power sells fewer units at higher prices. Same triangle, same deadweight loss, same textbook.
[P1.3] Nothing in the Clayton Act says "sellers only." It condemns mergers that substantially lessen competition in any line of commerce, and the market for labor is commerce. Enforcers who look only at output prices are not applying the statute; they are applying half of it.
[P1.4] Second, labor markets are more fragile than product markets. A shopper who dislikes a merged grocer's prices switches stores in an afternoon. A nurse whose two local hospitals merge cannot switch cities without uprooting a family, and cannot switch occupations without years of retraining. Search is costly, information is asymmetric, and noncompetes and no-poach clauses lock the door further.
[P1.5] Where switching is hardest, market power bites hardest. If anything, a given level of concentration should worry us more on the labor side.
[P1.6] Third, ignoring monopsony does not merely leave a harm unaddressed; it launders it. When a merged firm touts "synergies," a large share of those synergies is often wage suppression: fewer bidders for the same meatpacking workers, the same coders, the same nurses. Under consumer-only review, a transfer from workers to shareholders is scored as an efficiency. That is not analysis; it is accounting sleight of hand.
[P1.7] Finally, this is administrable. Courts already define markets by geography and substitutability; commuting zones and occupations do the same job. A federal court blocked the Penguin Random House acquisition of Simon & Schuster on harm to authors' pay, not book prices. The tools exist.
[P1.8] What's missing is the will.

[C1.1] Nobody on this side denies monopsony exists. The question is not whether labor harm is real, it is whether merger review is the right tool to fix it, and whether mandating "equal seriousness" with price effects makes enforcement better or worse. It makes it worse, for three reasons.
[C1.2] First, the evidence asymmetry is enormous. Product-market price effects are measured against a stream of transaction data: units sold, prices charged, elasticities estimated from decades of retail and healthcare pricing studies. Labor-market power has to be inferred from thin proxies, an HHI built on messy commuting-zone and occupation codes that blur remote work, cross-training, and informal substitution. Mandating equal seriousness doesn't create equal evidence.
[C1.3] It just pressures courts to treat speculative wage models with the same confidence as hard pricing data, which produces arbitrary rulings in both directions.
[C1.4] Second, "as seriously as" is a resource-allocation demand, not a neutral principle. Enforcers already have discretion to bring strong monopsony claims, and they have, hospital-nursing mergers, the Penguin Random House deal PRO cites, which a court blocked using existing law with zero new mandate required. That case is evidence the current framework works when the facts are strong. What "equal seriousness" adds is not better enforcement of good cases, it's mandatory scrutiny of weak ones, diverting scarce agency resources from mergers where consumer harm is well documented toward speculative labor litigation.
[C1.5] Third, parity risks blocking mergers with large, certain consumer and efficiency gains over uncertain, localized wage effects, then calls that outcome principled. If a hospital merger cuts costs passed to millions of patients while modestly softening a nursing sub-market, blocking it isn't protecting workers as a class, it's picking one group of employees over the broader public on a coin flip of contested economics.
[C1.6] The fix for monopsony is direct: ban noncompetes, make licenses portable, strengthen labor organizing rights. Merger review should weigh labor harm where proven, not pretend it is symmetric with price effects it structurally cannot measure as well.
