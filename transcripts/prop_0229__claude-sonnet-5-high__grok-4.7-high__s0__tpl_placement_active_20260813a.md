[P1.1] Universal fuel subsidies in the Gulf and North Africa are one of the most regressive, wasteful transfers of public money in the modern economy, and five years is more than enough time to replace them with something that actually helps the poor.
[P1.2] Start with who benefits. When Egypt or Saudi Arabia subsidizes gasoline and diesel at the pump, the subsidy scales with consumption. A wealthy household running two SUVs, a private generator, and a swimming pool pump draws far more subsidized fuel than a poor household that may not even own a car. Study after study on Middle Eastern and North African fuel subsidies has found the richest fifth of the population capturing several times more of the subsidy value than the poorest fifth.
[P1.3] That is not a safety net; it is a regressive transfer dressed up as social policy.
[P1.4] Second, the fiscal logic is brutal. Fuel subsidies in oil-producing states can consume a double-digit share of government spending, crowding out health, education, and infrastructure — the things that actually build long-term welfare. When oil prices dip, as they did in 2014-16 and again in 2020, these subsidies become unsustainable overnight, forcing sudden, chaotic cuts that hurt exactly the people they were meant to protect. A planned five-year phase-out lets governments manage the transition instead of being ambushed by markets.
[P1.5] Third, subsidies distort behavior in ways that compound the damage. Artificially cheap fuel encourages overconsumption, discourages energy efficiency, fuels smuggling across borders where neighboring prices are higher, and locks in carbon-heavy growth in economies that face acute climate vulnerability — Gulf heat stress and North African water scarcity being the clearest cases.
[P1.6] Targeted cash transfers fix the aim without abandoning the goal. Jordan and Iran have already run subsidy-reform-plus-cash-transfer programs; Iran's 2010 reform, whatever its later inflation problems, proved administratively that direct cash payments to households are technically feasible even in a middle-income, sanctioned economy. If it can work there, it can work in Gulf states with far greater administrative capacity and financial infrastructure.

[P2.3] On targeting, C1.6 treats imperfect registration as a fatal flaw. But universal subsidies have their own exclusion problem — total exclusion of the poor from most of the subsidy's value, since it scales with consumption they don't have. A structurally regressive transfer is not repairable by definition — it is regressive by design.
[P2.4] On Iran, CON's own framing helps me: compensation "lagged prices" — a monetary and administrative sequencing failure, not proof cash transfers can't work. The five-year window exists precisely to fix that sequencing: build the payment rails and registries first, then move prices, and index transfers to inflation without re-linking them to global oil prices, which is what actually caused Iran's spiral.
[P2.5] On the rentier bargain, Gulf governments already rebrand citizen benefits constantly — housing grants, marriage grants, employment guarantees. A visible cash dividend replacing an invisible pump subsidy is not a threat to legitimacy; it's a more legible version of the exact same bargain, and one citizens can see and value directly.
