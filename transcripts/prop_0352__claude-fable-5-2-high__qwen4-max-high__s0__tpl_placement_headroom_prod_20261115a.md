[P1.1] "Inevitable" is the most expensive word in economic policy. Metropolitan concentration is not weather; it is the accumulated result of where governments put the motorways, universities, research labs, airports and civil service jobs. Having tilted the field toward big cities for decades, the state cannot now shrug and call the result gravity.
[P1.2] The motion asks something modest: not saving every hamlet or freezing the map, but doing more to keep smaller places viable instead of treating their decline as settled. Three arguments carry it.
[P1.3] First, abandonment is waste. A declining town already has paid-for schools, hospitals, water systems, roads and houses. When its young people leave, those assets rot while the receiving city must build the same things again at premium land prices. We get empty homes in one place, unaffordable rents in another, and call it efficiency.
[P1.4] Second, people are not as mobile as the models assume. Those who leave are young and credentialed. Those who stay are older, poorer, tied by caregiving or by a house worth too little to fund a move. Government pays either way: through investment that sustains work, or through welfare, ill health and emergency services that merely manage decay.
[P1.5] Third, nations run on their hinterlands. Food, energy, water, minerals and much manufacturing come from outside the metropolis. And regions told they have no future do not vanish quietly; they vote their resentment, destabilising the politics cities depend on too.
[P1.6] And intervention works. Rural electrification transformed countrysides once written off. Germany sustains world-class manufacturers in small towns through vocational training and regional banks. Broadband now lets work travel to people.
[P1.7] Government always shapes geography. The only question is whether it does so deliberately, with places kept worth living in, or by neglect, and then pays the bill for the wreckage.
