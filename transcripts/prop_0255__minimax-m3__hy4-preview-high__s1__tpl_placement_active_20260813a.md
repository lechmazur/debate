[P1.1] When a person deletes a file, clears a search history, or closes an account, they are performing one of the clearest acts of digital autonomy available to them. They are saying: this should not exist anymore. The proposition before us asks whether that act should have legal force, or whether third-party data brokers should remain free to ignore it, archive the residue, and resell it onward. My case is that deletion must mean deletion, and that current industry practice is a structural violation of personal sovereignty that the law has a positive duty to correct.
[P1.2] The harms are not abstract. A domestic violence survivor deletes a new address from her phone to keep an abuser from finding her. A woman in a state that criminalizes reproductive care clears her search history and later finds that history repackaged, sold, and used to target her. A journalist's confidential source sees a deleted message resurface inside a purchased dataset.
[P1.3] These are not edge cases; they are the predictable consequences of treating deletion as a cosmetic UI event rather than a binding instruction.
[P1.4] The opponent will likely retreat to arguments about innovation, "digital exhaust," or security research. Each of those claims asks ordinary people to absorb unconsented surveillance so that someone else may profit, experiment, or comply with subpoenas they would never have received had the data stayed deleted. That trade is not just inefficient; it is illegitimate. The broker's revenue is extracted from a resource the subject never agreed to provide and actively tried to reclaim.
[P1.5] This debate should turn on one question: does a person's act of deletion carry any weight against a third party who was never part of the original transaction? The proposition says yes. Anything else treats private citizens as a permanent raw-material supply for an industry they cannot see, cannot negotiate with, and cannot escape.
