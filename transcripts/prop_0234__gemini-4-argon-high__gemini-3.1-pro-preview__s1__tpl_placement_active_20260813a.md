[P1.1] Every day, millions of workers negotiate their futures in the dark. A large corporation knows exactly what every role pays, who gets promoted, and where careers stall. The employee knows only what a manager chooses to tell them. Requiring large employers to disclose pay bands and promotion rates by role ends this structural information asymmetry—and the loss of managerial flexibility it brings is not a regrettable side effect, but the entire point.
[P1.2] Consider two mid-level analysts offered the same starting salary at different firms. At the first, the pay band caps out quickly and few analysts ever advance. At the second, the band has real headroom and promotion is routine. Under secrecy, those jobs look identical, letting the first firm bait applicants with cheap talk about "fast-track growth."
[P1.3] Disclosing pay bands stops employers from lowballing applicants who guess too low. Disclosing promotion rates stops them from selling dead-end roles on empty promises. Together, these metrics allow workers to take their talent to companies that genuinely invest in them.
[P1.4] We will hear warnings about lost managerial flexibility, so let us be clear about what that phrase actually protects. Because this mandate requires pay bands rather than rigid single-point salaries, managers retain ample room within a range to reward star performers. The only "flexibility" eliminated is the freedom to operate outside defensible boundaries—to pay a loyal, quiet employee far less than an aggressive outside hire doing the exact same job, or to hand out promotions through unexamined favoritism. Secrecy does not foster meritocracy; it shields managers from having to justify arbitrary disparities.
[P1.5] Crucially, this requirement targets large employers. Giant corporations are not scrappy five-person startups where roles reinvent themselves weekly. They already maintain standardized job architectures, HR databases, and internal progression metrics to manage their own budgets. Making those existing realities visible costs them little, while replacing workplace opacity with an honest contract.

When every salary band is public, managers face immense pressure to minimize internal friction. Rather than aggressively compensating a top-tier performer at the very top of a band—which requires justifying that gap to every average employee on the team—managers cluster pay around the median.
[C1.3] To avoid constant grievances, companies shrink the bands entirely. The result is a lowered ceiling where exceptional effort is no longer matched by exceptional reward.
[C1.4] Second, demanding "promotion rates by role" forces companies to publish a deeply flawed, easily manipulated metric. Promotion rates are highly context-dependent. [C1.5] Furthermore, this mandate incentivizes title inflation. To improve their public metrics, companies will simply slice existing roles into artificial micro-tiers—Analyst 1, Analyst 2, Analyst 3. [C1.6] Finally, stripping away flexibility harms the very workers PRO wants to help. Real-world jobs are rarely static. Flexibility allows managers to adapt compensation for employees needing custom arrangements, such as remote work offsets, non-standard equity splits, or hybrid responsibilities. Forcing large employers into rigid, publicized architectures ensures they manage for the metric rather than the individual, trapping workers in standardized boxes that ignore their unique value.
