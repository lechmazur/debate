[P1.1] Return-to-office mandates upend employees’ lives, so they demand rigorous justification, not vague slogans about culture. This isn't about restricting managerial discretion—it's about ensuring that discretion is exercised transparently and fairly. Requiring large employers to justify these policies with role-specific business reasons ensures decisions are grounded in actual operational need rather than managerial whim or nostalgia for pre-pandemic norms. A vague appeal to "culture" cannot explain why a back-office accountant must commute daily, while it might justify in-person requirements for a product designer collaborating with engineers on physical prototypes or a sales team closing complex deals face-to-face.
[P1.2] Broad assertions obscure these critical distinctions, inviting arbitrary enforcement that erodes trust and morale across entire workforces. Without specific, verifiable rationale, mandates risk becoming mere tools of control rather than strategic decisions—driving away top talent in a competitive labor market where flexibility is a key attractor. Consider that many companies saw productivity and innovation hold steady or improve during remote work; if a department’s output and collaboration metrics rise with remote arrangements, a blanket mandate lacks any legitimate business footing.
[P1.3] This standard shifts the burden to employers to prove necessity, not impose convenience. It prevents the one-size-fits-all approach that treats all roles as identical, despite obvious differences in how and where work gets done most effectively. For customer-facing roles, in-person presence may be essential, but for data analysts, software developers, or technical writers, it may be irrelevant. Moreover, role-specific justification protects against discriminatory impacts, as employees with disabilities, caregiving responsibilities, or long commutes face disproportionate hardship.
[P1.4] The alternative—a system where power dictates policy without scrutiny—leads to cynicism, attrition, legal exposure, and a weakened employer brand. Specific, role-based reasons force accountability, ensuring policies serve measurable business goals rather than unexamined tradition or managerial preference.

[C1.1] Culture is not the opposite of a business reason. It names the returns a firm gets from people sharing a place: the junior analyst who overhears how a senior handles a difficult client, the hallway conversation that becomes a product, the trust that makes a hard feedback conversation possible. Those gains are real and irreducibly collective. That is why this motion fails: it demands justification at the level of the individual role for benefits that only exist at the level of the whole.
[C1.2] Take PRO's own accountant. Her ledger work may be remote-compatible. The reason to have her in the building is the two junior staff who learn by proximity to her — a benefit that appears nowhere in her role description and cannot be attributed to her tasks. Under a role-specific test, that reason is inadmissible.
[C1.3] The rule does not neutrally filter good reasons from bad; it filters out an entire class of good reasons because they are hard to write down per job title.
[C1.4] Notice the asymmetry PRO's evidence standard creates. Remote output is countable: tickets closed, reports shipped. Apprenticeship, weak ties, and serendipity are not countable, and their payoff arrives years later in people who stayed. Demand provable necessity role by role, and you will systematically decide against the office — not because the office loses, but because its benefits are the kind that don't fit the form.
[C1.5] And what does compliance look like? Lawyers drafting role-specific boilerplate, managers negotiating exemptions with whoever argues best, and a uniform policy dissolving into a patchwork where the senior and the articulate opt out while the new hire shows up alone. That is not accountability. Employers already answer to a labor market that punishes bad mandates.
[C1.6] Adding a justification tribunal punishes only the ones honest enough to say why.
