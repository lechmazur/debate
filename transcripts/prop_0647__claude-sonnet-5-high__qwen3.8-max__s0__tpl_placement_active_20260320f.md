[P1.2] Widening is a treadmill. Cities that keep stepping on it never arrive anywhere; they just spend billions to stay in the same congested place.
[P1.3] Removal breaks the cycle instead of feeding it. Milwaukee removed the Park East Freeway spur and freed up prime downtown land for redevelopment. Seoul famously removed an elevated highway to restore the Cheonggyecheon stream corridor, revitalizing a formerly blighted district.
[P1.4] These aren't fringe experiments; they're a repeated pattern — cities discovering that removed capacity gets absorbed by adaptation, while added capacity gets absorbed by more driving.
[P1.5] That asymmetry is the heart of my case. Widening treats traffic as a fixed quantity to be accommodated no matter the cost. Removal treats traffic as something responsive to the choices we build into the city — and the evidence says traffic really is that responsive. [P1.6] What's on offer is more pavement, more noise, more severed neighborhoods, more sunk cost — for the same traffic problem five years later.

[C1.2] PRO’s best examples prove the exception, not the rule. [C1.3] Widening, by contrast, can be targeted. A lane added at a merge, a bottleneck fixed, a shoulder converted to a managed lane, or a corridor rebuilt with lids and better exits can improve reliability without severing the city. Yes, added capacity can attract new trips.
[C1.4] But that is an argument for managing demand—pricing, transit priority, freight scheduling—not for assuming deletion is usually superior. If travel demand is responsive, then the consequences of removal are also responsive: people who still must travel face longer trips, and firms face less reliable delivery.
[C1.5] The right test is marginal: which option produces better mobility, safety, and land outcomes in that corridor? Many urban freeways should be capped, rerouted, or paired with transit. But a blanket preference for removal more often than widening ignores the people who depend on the road, the surface streets that would drown, and the fact that modern widening can be done with mitigation. Cities should fix corridors, not default to amputation.
