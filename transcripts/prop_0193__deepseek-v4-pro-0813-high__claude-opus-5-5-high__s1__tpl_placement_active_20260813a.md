[P1.1] Fusion research earns its public funding because it buys an option on the only known form of firm, dense, essentially limitless low-carbon power. Unlike solar and wind, it does not depend on weather; unlike conventional fission, it produces no spent-fuel legacy on the same scale and cannot melt down. If it works, it changes the entire geometry of decarbonization, industrial heat, desalination, and advanced fuels. That payoff is so large that a decades-long horizon strengthens rather than weakens the case for patient public investment.
[P1.2] “Commercially viable may still be decades away” is precisely why private capital alone will not sustain the necessary science. Fusion requires unglamorous advances in plasma control, neutron-resistant materials, tritium breeding, and superconducting magnets—areas with uncertain near-term returns. Public labs have already pushed experimental yields up by orders of magnitude and helped develop high-temperature superconducting magnets that make smaller, cheaper reactor designs plausible. That cumulative knowledge disappears or stalls without steady public support.
[P1.3] The spillovers are not speculative. Decades of fusion-related work have strengthened capabilities in cryogenics, high-field magnets, robotics for extreme environments, vacuum systems, laser optics, and computational simulation. The pattern matches other long-horizon public science that produced MRI, GPS, and modern microelectronics. Cutting fusion research does not simply save money; it surrenders a broad technical capability.
[P1.4] There is also a climate option value. If fusion arrives late this century, it can provide clean firm power for a world far more electrified than today. Given the uncertainty of storage at continental scale and the limits on expanding fission, fusion is a hedge against the most dangerous climate tail risks.
[P1.5] Finally, “large” should not mean unlimited. Public funding can remain milestone-driven, but it must be large enough to keep multiple credible confinement concepts alive. The cost is modest compared with the energy security and climate stakes, and the alternative—waiting until fusion is suddenly urgent—would be far more expensive.
