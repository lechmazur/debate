[P1.1] The choice is not between a perfect law and a messy one; all prostitution law is messy. The choice is where the state places risk, and the right measure is the safety of the person with the least power, not the convenience of buyers or the neatness of licensing forms. Full prohibition places risk on the seller: they become the easiest suspect in the room. A fully legal market places comparatively little friction on demand, turning purchasers into ordinary consumers.
[P1.2] Criminalizing purchase while decriminalizing sale reverses that structure: the buyer carries legal risk, while the seller can seek help without incriminating themselves.
[P1.3] That is a safety mechanism, not a slogan. If a client assaults someone, refuses payment, or demands unprotected sex, the seller can call police or a clinic without their own account becoming a confession. Under prohibition, violent clients count on that silence: arrest, fines, eviction, immigration trouble, or stigma may seem worse than reporting abuse. Decriminalizing sale removes the shield predators use.
[P1.4] It also lets police treat the seller as a witness and victim rather than as the case they must first prove.
[P1.5] Criminalizing purchase reaches the force prohibition misses: demand. Most buyers want a discreet, low-risk transaction. A credible chance of arrest, fine, court date, or public exposure changes that calculation before anyone is hurt. This need not mean punishment maximalism; proportional buyer penalties, expungement, healthcare, housing, immigration firewalls, and strong penalties for violence and coercion make it pressure on demand with an exit door for sellers.
[P1.6] A fully legal market is not safer simply because it is tidier. Once brothels, platforms, and managers can advertise and take a cut, desperation can be repackaged as contract, and exploitation becomes a regulated business model. Legalization protects paperwork; it does not equalize bargaining power. This compromise refuses both failures; countries should take that line: no criminal record for the seller, no lawful storefront for the buyer.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a moral reversal for a safety policy. Criminalizing purchase does not lift criminal law from sex work; it shifts the danger from the seller’s record to the seller’s working conditions. Compared with full prohibition, the change is largely symbolic: criminal law still sets the terms of the market. A buyer who fears arrest demands anonymity, speed, and isolation.
[C1.2] He avoids traceable venues, screening, and repeated contact; he pressures workers to meet quickly in cars, hotels, or remote places. That is not safer bargaining power; it is prohibition by another name.
[C1.3] PRO says sellers can call for help. But decriminalizing sale alone is not decriminalizing work. Police still need buyers to prosecute, so workers may be treated as evidence, surveilled, or pressured to cooperate. Related conduct—advertising, renting premises, working together, managing safety—often remains illegal, leaving workers unable to hire security, screen clients properly, or operate openly.
[C1.4] The seller is formally free from arrest, yet practically dependent on hidden arrangements that predators understand very well.
[C1.5] The demand argument is equally unstable. Most demand does not disappear under threat; it adapts. Where buyer penalties exist, sex workers commonly report rushed negotiations, lower income, and clients who use the fear of exposure to demand concessions, including refusing condoms or meeting in isolated locations. Criminal law does not educate those buyers; it teaches them to avoid detection.
[C1.6] Finally, the proposition is not a safer middle ground compared with a regulated legal market. A legal framework can require age verification, health and safety standards, contracts, complaint mechanisms, and labor enforcement while preserving strong offenses against coercion and trafficking. Buyer criminalization removes those tools and keeps the industry in the shadows. If the measure is safety, the state should empower workers to screen, report, and organize—not make every client a fugitive.
