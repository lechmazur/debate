[P1.1] The central question is not whether dating apps can produce some relationships—they obviously can—but whether the dominant swipe-and-feed model improves relationship formation for most people who use them. It does not, because its design rewards attention capture, not compatibility, and endless browsing, not commitment.
[P1.2] First, the dominant model industrializes first impressions. Users are sorted into rapid-fire decks where split-second judgments about photos and status cues replace slower, context-rich discovery. In ordinary social settings, attraction can grow through shared friends, repeated contact, conversation, and reputation. The app strips away those supports and makes thin profiles the gatekeeper.
[P1.3] That systematically advantages people who photograph well or fit narrow status norms, while filtering out many partners who would be compatible in deeper ways.
[P1.4] Second, the business model conflicts with the goal it advertises. Apps earn through subscriptions, boosts, and keeping users active. A product that quickly pairs people off and exits their lives is a product that loses revenue. So the architecture supplies endless alternatives, notifications, and gamified validation.
[P1.5] The result is choice overload: conversations stall, options feel replaceable, and users become cautious about investing because someone better might be one swipe away. That is hostile to relationship formation, which requires sustained attention and willingness to accept imperfect realities.
[P1.6] Third, the model normalizes low-accountability interaction. Ghosting, breadcrumbing, and simultaneous low-effort chats are not accidental bad manners; they are predictable outcomes of a marketplace where access is cheap and exit is frictionless. For most users—especially those not already highly advantaged—this produces fatigue, misreading, and erosion of confidence rather than stable partnerships. Even when matches occur, the pipeline from match to trust becomes longer and more fragile.
[P1.7] The decisive comparison is simple: the dominant model skews selection toward shallow signals, monetizes prolonged searching, and weakens the norms needed to turn matches into relationships. That makes formation worse for most users.
