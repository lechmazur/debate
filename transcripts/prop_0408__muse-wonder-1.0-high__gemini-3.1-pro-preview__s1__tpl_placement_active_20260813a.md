[P1.1] A diploma should certify readiness for adult life, not just completion of classes. Yet students routinely graduate able to parse a novel or solve an equation, but unable to read a credit-card statement, compare loan offers, or understand a paycheck. Financial literacy is not enrichment. It is survival knowledge every graduate will use within months.
[P1.2] The stakes arrive early and compound fast. At eighteen, students sign student-loan documents, open credit cards, sign leases, and choose jobs with retirement plans and tax forms attached. A small misunderstanding — minimum payments, payday-loan fees, interest that snowballs, a missed tax deadline — can become years of debt, damaged credit, and lost housing or job opportunities.
[P1.3] Leaving this to electives or parents is an equity failure disguised as choice. Wealthier families can model budgeting, explain credit, help with FAFSA and taxes, and rescue a bad decision. Students whose parents work multiple jobs, rent rather than own, or are themselves trapped in debt get silence or bad habits instead. Electives reach the already-motivated and already-supported, while students most exposed to predatory lending are least likely to enroll.
[P1.4] A requirement is the only way the guarantee does not depend on zip code and inheritance.
[P1.5] A serious course — not a one-week unit or an assembly — can teach what experience teaches expensively: building a budget on irregular income, how interest and minimum payments work, reading a pay stub and filing basic taxes, comparing borrowing costs, building emergency savings, spotting scams, and understanding insurance and retirement accounts. Taught through real documents and simulations — a lease, a loan offer, a mock tax return — these are concrete, testable skills, not vague values.
[P1.6] Opponents will call this crowded curriculum. But schools already require courses for a distant possibility while ignoring a certainty. Every student will handle money. We should require them to learn how before the market teaches them at interest.

[C1.1] PRO argues that schools must mandate financial literacy because students need it to survive [P1.1]. But requiring a standalone, "serious" financial-literacy course fundamentally misunderstands how adolescents retain information, while actively displacing the foundational skills that genuinely drive socioeconomic mobility.
[C1.2] First, financial education suffers from severe "just-in-time" knowledge decay. PRO imagines that showing a 16-year-old a mock tax return or a 401(k) simulator will prevent bad decisions at age 24. Behavioral economics demonstrates otherwise: information learned years before it can be practically applied is rapidly forgotten. Teenagers largely lack the real-world context—independent living expenses, steady income, or credit access—to internalize these lessons.
[C1.3] By the time they sign a lease or open a retirement account, the specific tax codes, loan structures, and market conditions taught in high school will have already changed.
[C1.4] Second, PRO dismisses the opportunity cost of a crowded curriculum [P1.6], but replacing core academic time with a finance class actively harms students. Evaluating a predatory loan or calculating compound interest relies on general reading comprehension and strong numeracy. If a student lacks foundational algebra, a specialized finance class will not save them. Schools should build robust, adaptable critical thinking and math skills that empower students to understand *any* contract or formula they encounter, rather than memorizing fleeting financial trivia.
[C1.5] Finally, PRO claims this mandate cures an "equity failure" [P1.3]. In truth, it peddles an illusion. Teaching marginalized students how to budget poverty-level wages does not cure poverty. It merely individualizes a systemic problem, implying that wealth is a product of "smart budgeting" rather than structural advantage and income level.
[C1.6] If we want to equip disadvantaged students for adult survival, schools must prioritize the rigorous academic and vocational pathways that directly increase their future earning power, not a bureaucratic requirement that fades from memory before it is ever used.

Refusing that knowledge in the name of structural critique leaves the structure intact and the student alone against it.
[P2.6] The alternative is still elective exposure or family background, dressed up as general skills. That guarantees students with informed parents get both, and students without them get neither. A requirement timed near graduation answers the retention concern better than abandonment, and gives every graduate the same basic defense before lenders provide the education at a price.
