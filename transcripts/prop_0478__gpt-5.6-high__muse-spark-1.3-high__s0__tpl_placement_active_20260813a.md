[C1.1] Protecting flexible workers by taxing flexible work is self-defeating. A mandatory portable floor adds an employer-style surcharge to every ride, delivery, invoice, and short contract, raising the price of exactly the low-overhead, easy-entry work it claims to preserve.
[C1.2] Most affected workers did not ask for that trade. Skimming a fixed share for a government-defined package of leave, retirement, and injury cover cuts take-home pay today for benefits many cannot use tomorrow, or already hold through family, school, or another job. That is paternalism dressed as portability.
[C1.3] Then comes enforcement. “Substantial income” aggregated across platforms sounds neat until someone must track it. Who totals earnings from three apps, two direct clients, and a weekend catering job? Who chases the homeowner, nonprofit, or startup that hired a designer for two weeks to pay prorated leave? The machinery of accounts, reporting, verification, and reconciliation falls hardest on small clients and new platforms, entrenching incumbents who can afford legal departments.
[C1.4] Costs do not vanish. Platforms raise fees, customers order fewer rides and deliveries, clients replace contractors with automation or salaried staff. To limit liability, platforms will favor high-volume workers and quietly shed casual earners, impose minimums, or exit thin markets. The floor becomes a ceiling on opportunity, paid for by the most marginal workers losing gigs.
[C1.5] Nor does this fix competition; it corrupts it. It imposes employment-like costs while denying employment clarity, inviting endless fights over who counts as a purchaser of work and whether paying the floor proves employee status. If society wants a stronger safety net for illness and old age, fund it broadly and neutrally — not with a special transaction tax that punishes people for working for more than one boss.
