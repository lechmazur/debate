[P1.1] Secondary students need two different kinds of help, and only one belongs mainly to a machine. Mastery practice—working equations until the steps are automatic, testing vocabulary until recall holds, running problem sets until the same error stops repeating—is where AI tutoring earns its place. It is tireless, adaptive, and immediate. A student who misses the distributive property at nine at night can get another calibrated set before bed.
[P1.2] Fluency grows from repetition with rapid correction, and no teacher can supply that volume alone.
[P1.3] Writing feedback is not that task. A paragraph is not an item with an answer key. It is a judgment about what this writer is trying to do for a human reader: whether the claim is worth making, whether the evidence actually bears the weight, whether the voice sounds like a person rather than a template. Systems built to score tend to reward what they can measure—length, transitions, surface coherence, conventional shape—and to sand away the irregularities that often mark real thinking.
[P1.4] A student who treats the chatbot as the main audience learns to write for the model. That is a different skill from writing for a teacher, a classmate, or a reader who can push back.
[P1.5] Human feedback also carries what the model cannot: a teacher who has watched this student argue badly for six weeks and can say, this time you finally risked a real claim. Conferences and classroom talk teach revision as a social act. If AI becomes the main source of writing feedback, that relationship thins exactly when adolescents are forming the habits they will carry forward.
[P1.6] Use the tutor for mastery practice. Keep the main writing conversation between students and the adults responsible for teaching them how to think on the page.
