[P1.1] Legal deposit exists because we learned the hard way that markets do not preserve memory. Most silent films are gone; countless books survived only because copies were forced into libraries. That logic now applies with more force to software. Games, apps, and digital art are not mere entertainment; they are the folklore, design language, and political speech of the last fifty years.
[P1.2] The Oregon Trail taught millions a mythic version of westward expansion. Early graphical adventures, MUDs, and Flash games trained a generation in narrative, humor, and community. COVID-era contact-tracing apps and protest tools captured civic life in real time. Whole genres are server-dependent: an online world exists only while a company keeps the lights on; when Flash lost support, a decade of games and digital art nearly vanished, rescued in part by scattered fan efforts.
[P1.3] When an app store delists a title or a firm abandons DRM, the work does not merely go out of print; it becomes inaccessible and often unrecoverable. That is cultural amnesia by default.
[P1.4] Voluntary donation cannot fix this. Preserving software requires deliberate migration, emulation, source-code escrow, and metadata—costs no creator bears for a product they may abandon. Rightsholders sometimes have an active interest in disappearance: removing controversial games, killing old online modes, or suppressing unflattering campaign apps. A mandate aimed at culturally significant items, not every hobby project, fixes the incentive.
[P1.5] Deposit is not publication. National archives already protect sensitive manuscripts and unpublished films; they can hold code under embargo, redact trade secrets, allow controlled reading-room access, and preserve binaries and contextual documents without stripping creators’ rights.
[P1.6] The test is simple: if a future historian wants to understand how people played, organized, loved, and argued in the early digital age, should they rely on the goodwill of platform companies and licensors? Books and films already answered no. Software deserves the same answer.

[C1.1] The proposition rests on a false analogy. Books and films are self-contained artifacts: give a library a copy and it can sit on a shelf for centuries. Software is not an artifact but a performance—a set of instructions requiring specific hardware, operating systems, dependencies, and often live servers. Depositing a binary without its entire execution environment preserves a corpse, not a living work.
[C1.2] This is not a footnote; it is the reason the analogy fails.
[C1.3] Consider what deposit would actually require. A nationally archived game from 2005 needs period hardware or faithful emulation, documentation of its runtime, and ongoing migration as formats decay. National archives would become perpetual engineering departments maintaining virtualization stacks for every platform they accept. This is not shelving a book; it is running a museum where every exhibit needs constant life support.
[C1.4] Then the selection problem. PRO says "culturally significant"—but who decides, and when? Cultural significance is mostly retrospective. The works that matter most were often the ones nobody flagged at the time.
[C1.5] A mandate means either depositing everything, which is unbounded and incoherent, or appointing a committee to assess the cultural value of every app—a bureaucratic judgment guaranteed to miss what future historians actually want.
[C1.6] The rights problem is worse than PRO suggests. Software embeds third-party libraries, proprietary engines, patented algorithms, and DRM. Even an embargoed archive cannot make a deposited program functional without stripping or licensing components the depositor does not own. For online services, the "work" includes server-side code and user data no company can or should hand over.
[C1.7] Voluntary preservation by communities, academics, and institutions already does this work where it is feasible. A mandate would impose costs that fall hardest on small and independent creators—the very voices preservation claims to protect.
