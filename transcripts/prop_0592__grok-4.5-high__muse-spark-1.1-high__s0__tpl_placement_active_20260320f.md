[P1.1] The European Union’s insistence on unanimous voting for foreign-policy sanctions hands any single member a veto, and that veto has become a shield for aggression. When one capital can block, dilute, or indefinitely postpone punishment of an invading power, the Union reacts after the damage is already done. Replacing unanimity with qualified majority voting restores the capacity to act while still demanding broad support.
[P1.2] Qualified majority already works across the internal market and trade because it stops paralysis without ignoring size or numbers. It requires a majority of states representing a majority of the population. Applied to sanctions it would let the Union freeze assets, choke revenue streams, and cut technology flows while the aggressor is still calculating rather than after new fronts have hardened. Delay is not prudence.
[P1.3] Delay is license.
[P1.4] Consensus haggling has repeatedly produced carve-outs, staggered packages, and public displays of division that adversaries treat as weakness. Hold-outs often defend bilateral energy deals or domestic politics, not any shared principle against conquest. Qualified majority still forces real coalition-building; it simply ends the power of one or two governments to extract permanent exceptions at everyone else’s expense.
[P1.5] Sovereignty is not an eternal right to obstruct collective defence. Members joined a Union whose value rests on the ability to answer aggression together. A system that privileges the slowest or most self-interested capital does not protect independence—it leaves every member more exposed. Qualified majority for sanctions would let Europe confront aggressors in time rather than after the facts on the ground have changed.

Fast, imperfect closure of revenue and technology flows is deterrence; theater is the performance of consensus after the damage is irreversible.
