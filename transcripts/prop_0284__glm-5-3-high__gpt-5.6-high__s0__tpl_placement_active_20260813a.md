[P1.1] Price discrimination has always existed—but algorithmic price discrimination is different in kind. A haggling merchant sized up one customer at a time; an algorithm sizes up millions simultaneously, using browsing history, device type, location, and willingness-to-pay signals, then sets a personalized price optimized to extract the maximum from each person. [P1.2] Three arguments anchor my case.
[P1.3] First, personalized pricing defeats the core function of prices in a market. A posted price is information: it tells you what the product costs and lets you comparison shop. Markets only discipline sellers when buyers can walk away informed.
[P1.4] Algorithmic discrimination quietly removes that foundation.
[P1.5] Second, the practice predictably targets the vulnerable. Willingness-to-pay correlates with desperation and disadvantage—people in rural areas with one supplier, customers with older phones, people who shop at 2 a.m. because they work night shifts, those who can't easily comparison shop. [P1.6] That is not efficiency; it's extraction with a machine-learning gloss.
[P1.7] Third, the remedy is cheap. "Disclose or ban" doesn't require regulators to set prices. But charging different customers differently can increase access, especially for products with high fixed costs, low marginal costs, or perishable capacity. The relevant questions are whether the seller has market power, uses impermissible traits, or deceives customers—not whether software helped calculate the offer. Making the algorithm the trigger confuses a tool with an abuse.
[C1.2] Consider a software service whose additional user costs almost nothing. A uniform profit-maximizing price of $20 may exclude someone willing to pay only $8. An algorithmically targeted $8 promotion can bring that customer in while full-price purchases continue funding development. The same logic supports retention discounts, targeted coupons, and last-minute offers for unused capacity.
[C1.3] Prohibition does not guarantee everyone the lowest price; it may simply eliminate discounts and leave the uniform price high. The supposed fairness of equal prices can therefore mean equal exclusion.
[C1.4] Nor does personalized pricing “defeat” comparison shopping. A customer can compare the actual offers available to them. Markets have never promised that every buyer receives every coupon, negotiated rate, or introductory deal. A generic notice saying “this price may be personalized” provides little actionable information and risks becoming another ignored warning.
[C1.5] Detailed disclosure is worse: it can expose proprietary strategy, reveal sensitive inferences, and enable systematic gaming. Thus “disclose or ban” combines a potentially useless remedy with a damaging one.
[C1.6] Vulnerability is not an inevitable direction of the pricing effect. A signal of high price sensitivity may cause an algorithm to lower, not raise, someone’s price. Where desperation allows exploitation—essential services, local monopolies, or protected-class proxies—the underlying problem is constrained choice or unlawful discrimination. Target those directly through competition rules, privacy limits, anti-discrimination law, and bans on deceptive bait-and-switch tactics.
[C1.7] A presumption against algorithmic differentiation sacrifices beneficial discounts without reliably reaching coercion. Regulate harmful inputs and outcomes; do not usually condemn individualized prices merely because they differ.
