[P1.1] A chocolate bar that was 200 grams last month is 180 grams today, in the same wrapper, at the same £2. Nothing on the shelf says a price rise has happened — yet, per gram, the price has jumped. That silence is the business model, and it is why a clear-display duty is needed.
[P1.2] Shoppers do not shop like auditors. They rely on familiar packs, visual bulk, and the headline price they remember from last week. Shrinkflation exploits exactly that reliance: fewer crisps in the bag, thinner biscuits, a shorter tube, while the branding and price point stay reassuringly still. Yes, a weight is printed somewhere and unit pricing often exists, but in tiny, inconsistent print — per 100g here, per kilogram there, per sheet elsewhere — and on apps it is buried below pack price, images, and promotions.
[P1.3] Information designed to be missed does not create informed choice.
[P1.4] That failure corrupts competition. A firm that honestly raises its headline price risks losing customers to rivals; a firm that quietly shrinks keeps the sale while delivering less. The market then rewards concealment over efficiency, quality, or genuine value. Require a prominent, temporary notice — “Size down: 200g to 180g.
[P1.5] Unit price up 11%” — and the real price becomes comparable again. Shoppers can switch, trade down, or accept the rise, but the decision is theirs.
[P1.6] The obligation is narrow and inexpensive. It is triggered only when a package gets smaller without a matching headline cut, not by ordinary price moves. Supermarkets and apps already store weight, pack history, and unit price to manage stock and listings; when the pack changes, they already change labels and product pages. Adding the old size and the unit-price change is a marginal step.
[P1.7] This does not ban smaller packs, cap prices, or deny firms a response to higher costs. It bans one thing: presenting a real price increase as though nothing has changed.
