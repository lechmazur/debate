[P1.1] When the state keeps the records of its own violence behind paywalls, it tells the descendants of the enslaved, the dispossessed, and the displaced that their history is a commodity, not a right. Government‑led digitization and free public access to family records tied to slavery, forced adoption, indigenous land seizure, and wartime displacement is the only way to fulfil the moral and practical demands of historical recovery.
[P1.2] First, equity. Private genealogy platforms charge subscription fees that price out the very families who need the information most. A Black American searching for a Freedmen’s Bureau marriage certificate, a First Nations member looking for a land‑sale ledger, or a Korean War refugee hunting a displaced‑person file all face the same barrier: the market decides who can afford truth. Public funding eliminates that barrier, guaranteeing that low‑income households, community groups, and scholars can all retrieve what belongs to them.
[P1.3] Second, accuracy and preservation. Commercial databases prioritize speed and profit, often relying on OCR errors or incomplete transcriptions that distort names, dates, and relationships. Government‑run digitization can mandate archival standards, multilingual metadata, and community‑review processes that keep records faithful to the source. The result is a reliable public archive that resists the corrosive incentives of profit‑driven data mining.
[P1.4] Third, democratic accountability. When the state curates and funds these records, it acknowledges responsibility and invites public oversight, fostering reparative dialogue. Private firms operate behind closed terms, extracting value from trauma without any obligation to the communities they serve. Public access turns a painful legacy into a shared resource for education, policy, and reconciliation rather than a revenue stream.
[P1.5] The core clash is simple: a society that treats the recovery of its darkest chapters as a public good, or one that leaves it to the market, thereby perpetuating the very inequities those chapters created. The answer must be the former.

[C1.1] Making painful family records free on a government website sounds like justice, but for the people in those files it can be a second exposure they never consented to, and the state that caused the harm is the worst sole curator of its remedy.
[C1.2] Adoption files were often sealed to protect birth parents and children who are still living. Indigenous records implicate tribal sovereignty, sacred kinship, and land claims that tribes, not ministries, should govern. Descendants of enslaved or displaced families may want discovery on their own terms, not searchable exposure to employers, estranged relatives, or harassers. Mass public digitization treats all of this as state property to publish.
[C1.3] Private, tribal, and community platforms can at least tier access, verify kinship, restrict sensitive fields, and honor withdrawal — safeguards that open government portals structurally undermine.
[C1.4] The conflict of interest is deeper. Governments ran slave registries, removed children, allotted tribal land, and interned or expelled wartime populations, frequently misnaming people or falsifying records to legitimize the policy. Handing that same state monopoly control over scanning priorities, transcription, metadata, and search terms invites sanitization: inconvenient collections wait, official labels harden, and one portal becomes official truth. A plural ecosystem of private firms, universities, tribal archives, and nonprofits cross-checks errors and preserves competing interpretations.
[C1.5] Private genealogy is also simply better at this task. Competition has produced handwriting recognition, global record linking, and crowdsourced corrections at a speed public IT projects rarely match. Universal taxpayer funding would duplicate what subscribers and libraries already provide — many public libraries already offer free on-site access to major databases — while subsidizing hobbyists and commercial researchers. Scarce public money should preserve fragile originals and enforce privacy law, not build an expensive parallel internet archive.
[C1.6] Equity without consent is not repair. Recovery belongs primarily with families and communities choosing among private and local custodians, not with governments publishing their pain by default.

A federally funded portal can embed tiered‑access protocols—sealed adoption files remain closed to the general public, indigenous kinship data can be governed through tribal‑approved metadata schemas, and individuals can request redaction or embargo just as they can with any archival institution.
[P2.2] Private firms, by contrast, answer to shareholders, not to the communities whose trauma they monetize; they routinely lack any mechanism for withdrawal or community review.
[P2.3] Second, the conflict‑of‑interest charge cuts both ways. [P2.4] Third, the claim that libraries already provide free access misreads the reality. Library subscriptions are limited to on‑site terminals, unevenly distributed, and often expire; they do not guarantee the comprehensive, searchable, nationwide coverage that a digitised public corpus delivers. Private platforms excel at speed, but that speed is bought with subscription fees that price out the very families most affected—Black Americans seeking Freedmen’s Bureau certificates, Korean War refugees hunting displaced‑person files, Indigenous peoples tracing land allotments.
[P2.5] Equity, accountability, and preservation are not luxuries; they are prerequisites for any genuine historical recovery. Only a publicly funded, responsibly governed digitisation effort can guarantee that those prerequisites are met, turning the state from a past perpetrator into a present custodian of truth.
