[P1.1] The decisive comparison is not whether consumers deserve information. It is which intervention more reliably changes what people actually eat—and therefore population disease risk. Reformulation changes the food supply; labeling and education require individuals to overcome information gaps at the point of consumption.
[P1.2] Much of the salt, sugar, and fat in ultra-processed foods comes from recipes, not from consumers adding condiments at the table. A shopper may struggle to infer the sodium in a sauce or the added sugar in a savory snack. Even diligent label readers cannot precisely control ingredients in restaurant meals or multi-component products. Labels can improve awareness, but awareness alone does not remove exposure.
[P1.3] Education is weaker still when price, convenience, hunger, and habit determine purchasing decisions. Reformulation instead delivers a repeated nutritional reduction without requiring anyone to read, remember, calculate, or substitute.
[P1.4] Across a population, small but sustained reductions in these nutrients can meaningfully lower risks associated with hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease. That is especially important because exposure is continuous: people consume many processed foods daily, often without choosing their nutrient content deliberately.
[P1.5] Reformulation also prevents the incentive problem that limits voluntary action. If one company reduces sugar, competitors may undercut it with sweeter products or advertise “no added sugar.” Mandatory targets create a level playing field and bring products with different brands, price points, and retail channels toward the same nutritional ceiling. This reaches children, low-income consumers, and people with limited nutritional literacy without demanding special knowledge or extraordinary discipline.
[P1.6] Labels and education should still play a supporting role. But their effectiveness depends on attention and behavior. Reformulation makes the healthier choice the default choice, while labels merely explain choices that remain nutritionally hazardous. The mandate does not eliminate consumer autonomy; it uses the supply side to protect consumers who cannot continuously audit every product.
[P1.7] That is why reformulation is the stronger population-health instrument: it reduces harmful exposure at scale, whereas campaigns chiefly ask already-overwhelmed individuals to counteract it.
