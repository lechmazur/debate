[P1.1] When unemployment spikes, the damage is immediate and often irreversible: families lose housing, deplete retirement savings, and pull children from school. Waiting months for legislatures to negotiate discretionary stimulus is a luxury the unemployed cannot afford. Automatic cash transfers, triggered the moment jobless claims cross a predefined threshold, put purchasing power directly into the hands of those who will spend it immediately on rent, groceries, and medicine. That is not merely humanitarian; it is macroeconomically rational.
[P1.2] Every dollar reaches a pocket with urgent needs, sustaining demand precisely when private consumption collapses and preventing the downward spiral that turns ordinary recessions into prolonged depressions.
[P1.3] The opponent will warn of debt. But downturns are precisely the wrong moment to fixate on public balance sheets. Households and businesses are deleveraging simultaneously; if the government joins them in cutting back, aggregate demand evaporates and the economy shrinks faster than the debt declines. The borrowing incurred today is measured against a temporarily shrunken economy, yet it preserves the productive capacity—workers, small businesses, and supply chains—that generates tomorrow's tax revenue.
[P1.4] History shows that premature austerity converts temporary downturns into lasting stagnation, which ultimately leaves debt-to-GDP ratios worse, not better. Debt is a manageable figure on a spreadsheet; mass unemployment is a social wound that festers for years and erodes the very growth needed to pay creditors.
[P1.5] Finally, the automatic nature of these transfers is strategically essential. Discretionary stimulus is hostage to political theater, veto threats, and ideological bargaining, often arriving too late to halt the bleeding. Automatic stabilizers remove that friction and deliver help at the speed of economic reality. Speed is not a technical detail; it is the difference between a bridge to recovery and a foreclosure notice.
[P1.6] The proposition asks us to accept higher public debt during downturns. We should accept it gladly, because the alternative is preventable human misery, permanently scarred workers, and a self-inflicted economic catastrophe that costs far more to repair than the debt we feared.
