[P1.1] A reputation you cannot see, cannot appeal, and cannot escape is not a reputation; it is a sentence.
[P1.2] Scores compiled by private algorithms now travel with a person across life. A tenant-screening score follows you to every landlord. A background-check file shapes hiring and school admissions. A gig-platform rating shadows you into the next job.
[P1.3] The right at stake is narrow: to say no to being scored, ranked, and sorted across these domains without forfeiting the chance to work, learn, or live somewhere.
[P1.4] Three reasons.
[P1.5] First, these systems are wrong often enough to ruin lives, and wrong invisibly. Tenant-screening reports are notorious for mixed files: your name matched to a stranger's eviction, the rejection arriving as a silent filter rather than a letter you can answer. Gig workers are deactivated by algorithm with no hearing. When an error is portable, it costs you not one apartment but every apartment.
[P1.6] Compounding, unappealable error is precisely the harm law exists to correct.
[P1.7] Second, consent is fictitious. Nobody consents to these systems; you submit or you exit the economy. A bargain you cannot refuse is not a bargain but a condition of ordinary life imposed by private firms. Legal rights exist exactly where individual bargaining power is zero.
[P1.8] Third, portability kills the second chance. We ended the pillory because we decided punishment must end; a record that follows you forever is a pillory with no term.
[P1.9] The efficiency objection — that screening deters fraud and bad hires — mistakes the right. Verification stays legal: references, credentials, court records, direct checks. What changes is that no one may be forced through a proprietary black box as the price of an ordinary life. Institutions that want to judge people can do the work of judging them.
[P1.10] The cost of a bad score falls on the person scored; the convenience accrues to the institution. This right aligns cost with power — which is what rights are for.

[C1.1] Every functioning society relies on reputation to establish trust between strangers. When you rent to someone, hire them, or share a car with them, you take a physical and financial risk. Algorithmic systems did not invent reputation; they replaced an older system that was far more exclusionary—one governed by elite social networks, personal pedigree, and unvarnished human prejudice.
[C1.2] Granting a legal right to disconnect triggers two devastating consequences.
[C1.3] First, it destroys access for the marginalized through adverse selection. PRO imagines that stripping away algorithmic scores forces gatekeepers to judge people more generously. In reality, risk does not vanish because you blindfold the assessor. When individuals can unilaterally opt out, disconnection itself becomes a red flag.
[C1.4] Deprived of standardized data, landlords and employers fall back on blunt defensive heuristics: demanding exorbitant cash deposits, requiring elite institutional credentials, or relying on "gut instinct"—the historic breeding ground for racial and socioeconomic discrimination. Algorithmic records, whatever their flaws, allowed newcomers and workers without insider connections to prove reliability.
[C1.5] Second, portable reputation protects ordinary people, not just corporate balance sheets. When a driver is flagged across platforms for sexual misconduct, or a contractor repeatedly defrauds homeowners, that portable record protects the next rider and the next family. Granting an absolute right to disconnect allows serial bad actors to scrub their history clean, externalizing real danger onto vulnerable third parties who have no way to defend themselves.
[C1.6] The harms PRO identifies—bad data, opaque criteria, and missing appeals—call for rigorous regulation, not total disconnection. We need auditability, dispute mechanisms, and strict accuracy mandates akin to credit reporting laws. But allowing people to simply erase their accountability dismantles the trust architecture that makes a mobile, open society possible, pushing us back toward a world where opportunity depends entirely on privilege and personal connections.
