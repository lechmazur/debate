[P1.1] Recessions are not just bad luck for the unemployed; they are demand cascades. A plant lays off workers. Those workers cut spending. Local businesses lose revenue and lay off more.
[P1.2] The cheapest moment to break that cascade is before it accelerates. Automatic cash transfers tied to unemployment do exactly that: pre-authorized, formula-based, and paid within weeks, not after months of legislative bargaining.
[P1.3] That is the central comparison: automatic stabilizers versus discretionary relief. Discretionary relief arrives after the damage. Automatic triggers are fast and targeted. Unemployed households spend a high share of each dollar, so transfers circulate through rent, groceries, and local services.
[P1.4] That supports small businesses and preserves jobs that would otherwise disappear. It also limits foreclosures, evictions, and skills erosion, all of which reduce future productivity.
[P1.5] Yes, debt rises during downturns. But the relevant choice is not debt with stabilizers versus debt without. It is debt with stabilizers versus a deeper recession. A deeper recession shrinks GDP and tax revenue, raises emergency and safety-net costs, and leaves long-term scars.
[P1.6] Because debt ratios depend on GDP, a weaker economy can produce a worse debt path even with less borrowing. Automatic transfers phase down as unemployment falls, so they are temporary and bounded by a formula.
[P1.7] Opponents will warn about dependency and inflation. But the trigger is unemployment, not idleness. Benefits phase out as work returns, and most recipients are workers who lost jobs, not people choosing not to work. When demand is collapsing, the inflation risk from putting cash in liquidity-constrained families' hands is small.
[P1.8] It prevents deflationary collapse and reduces the need for bigger bailouts later.
[P1.9] The proposition is insurance, not indulgence. Governments already bear recession risk through lost revenue and emergency spending. Making support automatic makes it faster, fairer, and cheaper. Debt taken on to stop a downward spiral is not reckless; it is the price of preventing a much larger debt from mass unemployment and lost output.

Higher yields raise debt service, which forces austerity elsewhere or more borrowing still.
[C1.3] That is how a stabilizer becomes a debt spiral, and a sovereign debt crisis is the deepest recession of all. PRO's own logic — avoid the deeper downturn — cuts against a rule that triggers indiscriminately regardless of the fiscal starting point.

[C1.5] The ratchet is the predictable result: expansion is automatic, contraction is discretionary. Over a few cycles, the baseline welfare state and the debt grow with each recession and never normalize.
