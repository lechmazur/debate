[P1.1] Public universities spend public money for a public purpose. When taxpayers subsidize a seat, they are entitled to ask what that seat produces: a graduate who can work, pay taxes, and fill a need society actually has. Capping bloated low-employment programs and funding shortage fields is simply honest stewardship.
[P1.2] Right now we have the worst of both worlds. Lecture halls overflow in majors where graduates face years of underemployment, retail work, and debt they cannot repay, while hospitals scramble for nurses, schools cannot find science and math teachers, and employers in engineering, computing, and skilled technical fields leave positions vacant for months. That is not student freedom; that is institutional failure. Universities recruit tuition and leave students and the public to pay the cost.
[P1.3] Caps do not abolish the humanities or the arts. They right-size them. No serious program admits unlimited students — medical and nursing schools cap by clinical capacity all the time. Employment capacity is the same principle: keep a viable department for those truly called to the field, but stop mass-producing graduates for jobs that do not exist and shift those subsidized places and faculty lines where they are desperately needed.
[P1.4] Budgets are finite: every subsidized seat kept in an oversupplied field is a nurse, teacher, or engineer the public chose not to train. This most protects the vulnerable. Wealthy students can afford a low-odds degree as a luxury. First-generation and low-income students cannot.
[P1.5] Selling them unlimited seats in oversaturated fields in the name of choice is exploitation, not empowerment. Guiding capacity toward nursing, teaching, engineering, and technology turns public universities into engines of mobility rather than debt.
[P1.6] The alternative is indefensible: unlimited public subsidy for oversupply during crippling shortages. That is fiscal waste, educational malpractice, and a betrayal of students.
