[P1.1] The proposition is narrow: when a digital trace has been deleted, or was never public to begin with, no one should profit from archiving and selling it. This is not a sweeping ban on data brokering. It targets the specific practice of commodifying information that the subject intentionally removed from circulation or that was never exposed at all.
[P1.2] Deletion is an act of autonomy. When someone clears their search history, deletes a post, or closes an account, they are deciding what should exist about them in the world. A broker that captured that trace before deletion and keeps selling it afterward has rendered that decision meaningless. The person found the setting, clicked the button, did everything right—and their privacy was still auctioned off, because someone warehoused the data first.
[P1.3] The harms are not abstract. Someone who deleted posts about a past mental health crisis, a dissolved marriage, or political views they outgrew discovers those traces were packaged into a consumer profile sold to insurers, employers, and landlords. They cannot see the profile. They cannot correct it.
[P1.4] They cannot opt out, because they do not know it exists. That is not a market with consenting participants—it is a market operating behind their backs.
[P1.5] The non-public category is even starker. Location histories, browsing patterns, purchase records that were never shared—these were never offered to the public square. Archiving and monetizing them is not information freedom; it is surveillance with a price tag.
[P1.6] Here is the principle at stake: deletion must mean deletion. If it does not, every privacy setting is theater, every delete button is a lie, and every promise of user control is marketing copy. Prohibiting the trade in deleted and non-public traces is the minimum condition for digital self-determination to mean anything at all.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds protective, but it would actually make privacy worse, not better. When a lender can access a consolidated view of a borrower’s payment history—including transactions that the borrower never posted publicly—it can extend credit to people who would otherwise be invisible to the financial system. Banning the sale of deleted or non‑public traces would strip lenders of that safety net, pushing more borrowers into predatory payday‑loan markets.
[C1.2] Moreover, prohibition would drive the trade underground. Today brokers operate under a patchwork of state privacy statutes and industry self‑regulation; at least we can audit their practices, demand transparency, and impose fines. If the market is outlawed, the same data will be exchanged on black‑market forums, with no oversight, no recourse, and no ability for a consumer to discover that their “deleted” browsing history is being weaponized by an employer or insurer. The proposition’s promise of “digital self‑determination” collapses the moment the only way to enforce it is to hide the activity from public view.
[C1.3] The proposition also rests on a false premise: that deletion equals erasure. In reality, copies proliferate across caches, backups, and third‑party servers the moment a user clicks “delete.” Trying to legislate away a technical impossibility creates a compliance nightmare that benefits large incumbents—who can afford legal teams—while crushing smaller innovators who rely on aggregated, anonymized datasets to build better services. The real path to privacy is not a blanket ban on data brokers but stronger consent mechanisms, mandatory data‑minimization standards, and enforceable rights to audit and delete.
[C1.4] Those tools preserve the economic and societal benefits of data brokering while giving individuals genuine control. That is the case we will defend.
