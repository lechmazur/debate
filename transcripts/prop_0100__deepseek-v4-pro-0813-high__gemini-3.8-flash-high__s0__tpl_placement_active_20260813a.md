Nuclear power remains indispensable to any credible decarbonization strategy.
[C1.2] First, weather-dependent power hits a steep physical wall as grid penetration rises. Solar and wind produce variable energy, not firm capacity. Short-duration batteries can shift power across four or eight hours, but they cannot survive a two-week winter lull—a *Dunkelflaute*—spanning an entire continent. To bridge seasonal deficits with renewables alone requires vast overbuilding, massive curtailment, and astronomical storage costs.
[C1.3] Without a firm, zero-carbon anchor, renewable-heavy grids inevitably rely on fossil gas to backstop the system. [C1.4] Second, the decarbonization challenge is about to expand dramatically. Reaching net zero requires electrifying heating, transport, and heavy industry, potentially tripling electricity demand. Meeting that immense load exclusively with wind and solar imposes staggering land-use requirements, fierce public opposition to new transmission corridors, and severe mineral constraints. Most countries—especially densely populated manufacturing nations across Europe and Asia—simply lack the geography to run entirely on diffuse ambient energy.
[C1.5] Nuclear provides colossal, dispatchable energy on a tiny physical footprint, while supplying the high-temperature industrial heat and clean hydrogen that weather-dependent sources cannot efficiently deliver.
[C1.6] A strategy that abandons firm clean generation is not an acceleration; it is a reckless gamble on unproven seasonal storage and permanent gas backstops. Nuclear is not an optional luxury; it is the structural anchor of a fully decarbonized world.
