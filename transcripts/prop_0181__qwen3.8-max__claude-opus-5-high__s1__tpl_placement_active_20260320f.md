[P1.1] The decisive issue is not whether police want help solving cold cases, but whether a society can allow them to turn private consumer databases into investigative tools without the consent of the people whose genetic data are being used. Law enforcement access to commercial genealogy databases should be strictly prohibited because it converts intimate family information—shared for ancestry, health, or reunion purposes—into a surveillance resource that reaches far beyond the individual who uploaded a file.
[P1.2] Commercial genealogy platforms are not neutral archives. A single user’s decision to upload DNA can expose parents, siblings, cousins, and distant relatives who never agreed to any police use. That is a fundamental difference from a warrant targeting a specific suspect’s home or a convicted offender profile in a regulated forensic system. Genealogy dragnets treat millions of genetic relatives as leads by inference.
[P1.3] Strict prohibition is the only bright line. Case-by-case permission cannot fix this, because genealogical matching is relational: the most useful lead is often not the uploader but an unsuspecting cousin. If the state can mine those connections, consent becomes meaningless and genetic privacy is reduced to whatever a terms-of-service page allows.
[P1.4] The risk is especially grave because DNA is uniquely permanent and familial. Once police normalize this access, mission creep is predictable: today it is murder and sexual assault, tomorrow it may be lesser offenses, protests, immigration investigations, or political surveillance. Communities already over-policed will bear the heaviest costs, and public trust in both medicine and ancestry services can be corroded. Cold cases deserve serious effort, but the proper limit is strict prohibition, not open-ended access.
[P1.5] Police can still use direct evidence, lawful warrants, and tightly governed forensic databases. What they should not have is a backdoor to the genetic tree of ordinary families.
