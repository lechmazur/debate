[P1.1] A feed optimized for everyone is a feed optimized for no one in particular. That sounds like a slogan, but it captures the core defect: when a platform with billions of users runs a single recommendation objective, it must collapse radically different human goals into one proxy—usually engagement. The result is a system that treats a teenager seeking tutorials, a professional tracking industry news, and a retiree staying in touch with family as if they all want the same thing: more time on screen.
[P1.2] The problem isn't just inefficiency. It's that the single-objective model actively works against users' actual aims. Engagement-maximizing systems learn that outrage outperforms nuance, that scrolling beats satisfaction, and that the person who said they wanted to spend less time on the app is the person the app most needs to retain. The optimization target and the user's own goals diverge—and the platform picks its target, not theirs.
[P1.3] Giving users control over recommendation goals flips this relationship. Instead of the platform deciding that "time spent" is your purpose, you could tell the system you want exposure to new viewpoints, or depth on topics you already follow, or prioritization of close contacts over viral strangers. These aren't exotic preferences. They're things people already try to achieve through clumsy workarounds—muting, unfollowing, switching apps, or simply giving up.
[P1.4] The objection will come that users can't be trusted to know what they want, or that control adds complexity. But this condescension is exactly what's at stake. A platform that knows your attention is valuable enough to auction to advertisers can afford to ask you what you're looking for. The status quo doesn't avoid paternalism—it just hides it inside an algorithm nobody voted for.

[C1.1] The diagnosis is right and the prescription is wrong. Yes, engagement-maximization has pathologies. But it does not follow that the fix is handing every user a control panel. The real question is who should bear the burden of fixing the objective—and shifting that burden onto users is the option that fails.
[C1.2] Start with what we already know about controls. Platforms have offered them for years: chronological feeds on Instagram and X, "show less like this" buttons, topic filters, snooze options. Adoption is vanishingly small, and even users who switch drift back to defaults within days. This isn't condescension—it's the most robust finding in behavioral design: defaults dominate.
[C1.3] So "far more control" delivers the worst of both worlds: the vast majority stay on whatever default the platform picks, while the platform gains a new alibi. Every harm becomes "you chose your settings." User controls don't discipline the algorithm; they launder it.
[C1.4] Second, controls actively amplify harm for the users most at risk. A single objective is a guardrail as much as a leash. Let people dial their own recommendation goals and the teenager with an eating disorder tunes toward thinness content, the conspiracy-curious user asks for "more of this," the gambler optimizes for gambling streams. The people PRO imagines—reflective adults requesting "diverse viewpoints"—are precisely the people who least need help.
[C1.5] The people who most need protection will use controls to remove it.
[C1.6] Third, one objective is governable. Regulators, researchers, and journalists can audit a single optimization target, measure its effects, and demand changes—as pressure already has, on self-harm content and teen accounts. Fragment the system into millions of bespoke objectives and accountability dissolves: no baseline to audit, no standard to enforce, responsibility diffused onto "user choice."
[C1.7] If engagement is the wrong goal, the answer is a better goal—well-being-weighted, quality-signaled, publicly accountable defaults—not outsourcing the platform's homework to its most vulnerable users.
