[P1.1] Minimum parking requirements are a regulatory relic that functions as a hidden tax on housing and urban life. When cities mandate that every apartment complex, restaurant, or office building provide a fixed number of parking spaces, they force developers to pave over land that could host homes, parks, or businesses. The result is sprawling asphalt deserts that raise construction costs and push housing out of reach for ordinary families.
[P1.2] The economic mechanism is straightforward. In many cities, constructing a single parking space costs tens of thousands of dollars—underground spaces can exceed fifty thousand. Multiply that by arbitrary minimums, and every housing unit becomes more expensive, whether the resident owns a car or not. This is not a market outcome; it is a government mandate that distorts supply and transfers wealth from renters and homebuyers to parking lot owners.
[P1.3] Proponents of minimums argue they protect car-dependent communities, but this misunderstands the policy. Maximum requirements do not ban cars. They simply prevent developers from being forced to overbuild parking beyond what the market demands. In even the most automobile-oriented region, setting a ceiling—say, one space per residential unit or a modest ratio for retail—still allows ample parking for those who need it.
[P1.4] It merely removes the compulsion to construct vast, empty lots that sit unused ninety percent of the time.
[P1.5] Consider a downtown developer wanting to convert an old warehouse into apartments. Under minimum requirements, they might be forced to demolish an adjacent building to create parking, destroying housing potential and historic fabric. Under a maximum, they could build the housing and provide reasonable parking, letting the market price reflect actual demand.
[P1.6] Cities should replace minimums with maximums because housing affordability, fiscal responsibility, and genuine transportation choice all demand it. The market, not the zoning code, should determine how much parking a development needs.

[C1.5] Developers, seeing no requirement to provide parking, may build to the maximum only if lenders demand it; otherwise they under-provide, because spilled parking is free to them. Small businesses suffer most: a café without customer parking loses trade to a strip mall with a big lot.
[C1.6] The clash is simple. Minimums can be imperfect, but in places without transit they align new growth with the car reality residents already live. Maximums import a transit-rich-city tool into a car-dependent landscape, creating spillover, conflict, and hidden public costs. That is not affordability or choice; it is the socialization of parking demand.

[P4.3] The opponent's case rests on a false dilemma: that without minimums, developers will systematically under-build. The motion does not eliminate parking; it stops forcing cities to pave over housing for spaces that sit empty.
[P4.4] We should trust the market to supply what residents actually need, capped only to prevent excess, rather than trusting a zoning code to mandate asphalt deserts. The alternative is a system where every apartment, every shop, and every office must subsidize automobile storage regardless of whether the occupant drives, pricing housing out of reach for the very families these regions need to retain.
