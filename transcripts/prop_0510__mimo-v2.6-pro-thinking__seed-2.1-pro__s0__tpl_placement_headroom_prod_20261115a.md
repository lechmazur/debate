[P1.1] Elevated debt is precisely the condition under which getting stabilization wrong is most expensive. The proposition on the table concerns automatic stabilizers — unemployment insurance, progressive tax receipts, means-tested transfers — instruments that expand with slack and shrink as the recovery takes hold. Their defining virtue is that they are self-reversing. Refusing to let them operate during a downturn does not permanently reduce the deficit; it converts a temporary fiscal impulse into a permanent loss of output, jobs, and tax base.
[P1.2] Start with the arithmetic debt worriers should care about most. The debt ratio is debt over GDP. A downturn driven collapse in GDP mechanically raises the ratio even if the government does nothing. Worse, hysteresis means a slump left untreated destroys productive capacity, so the denominator never recovers fully.
[P1.3] Every study of deep postwar downturns tells the same broad story: prolonged slack leaves scars in lower participation, weaker investment, and permanently lower potential output. Squeezing stabilizers to protect a ratio can therefore raise the ratio over any horizon that matters. Fiscal consolidation undertaken while output gap is large is not reliably associated with debt reduction; it frequently fails on its own terms because it shrinks the tax base it depends on.
[P1.4] Second, the macro context matters. When monetary policy is at or near the effective lower bound, or inflation is below target alongside mass unemployment, the central bank cannot offset fiscal withdrawal. Automatic stabilizers are the fiscal instrument best matched to that moment because they are fast, targeted to those with the highest marginal propensity to consume, and require no legislative lag. Discretionary stimulus is the blunt, slow, politically contaminated alternative.
[P1.5] If you disable the automatic channel, you are left with either the long wait for legislation or nothing.
[P1.6] Third, the counterfactual is not a debt-free economy. It is one where a slump becomes entrenched, political pressure mounts for worse, more distortionary policies, and the debt ratio ends up higher anyway. Automatic stabilizers are the cheapest insurance a highly indebted government can buy. [clipped]

[C1.1] The proposition blurs a critical line: allowing automatic stabilizers to operate as designed is not the same as letting their cyclical expansion run unconstrained or increasing their generosity during high-debt downturns, and that confusion is fatal to the case for the motion. Existing stabilizers are calibrated to balance short-run support and long-run fiscal credibility; expanding them beyond that design when public debt ratios are already elevated courts predictable, avoidable harm that outweighs the short-run benefits PRO cites.
[C1.2] First, the claim that these expansions are “self-reversing” ignores decades of political economy evidence. Temporary uplifts to unemployment benefits, means-tested transfers, or tax credits deployed in a slump almost never fully roll back as recovery takes hold, because elected officials face severe backlash for cutting support to households still feeling uneven growth. What is sold as a temporary cyclical measure ratchets up permanent structural deficits, sending debt ratios higher over every relevant horizon rather than protecting them as PRO promises.
[C1.3] Second, high debt environments shift the risk calculus entirely. When debt is already elevated, looser automatic fiscal policy can trigger a sharp rise in real sovereign borrowing costs, as investors price in greater default or inflation risk, before any hysteresis benefits materialize. That higher cost of capital crowds out private investment needed to drive recovery, and can force abrupt, pro-cyclical austerity mid-slump when markets lose confidence—exactly the outcome PRO claims to avoid. The eurozone’s early-2010s experience makes this concrete: countries that let stabilizers expand sharply without regard to already high debt faced spiking bond spreads, requiring emergency cuts that inflicted far more lasting harm than a more restrained initial response.
[C1.4] Expanding stabilizers in those conditions does not buy cheap insurance; it gambles precious fiscal space on an assumption of seamless reversal that rarely holds, leaving governments less able to respond to future unforeseen shocks when they hit.
