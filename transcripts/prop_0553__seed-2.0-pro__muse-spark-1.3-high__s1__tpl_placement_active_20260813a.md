[P1.1] Every household in India has that dead device: the phone with a cracked screen the brand charges 80% of a new unit to replace, the washing machine control board that failed 18 months after warranty, the tablet locked out of diagnostic mode for no stated reason. [P1.2] Millions of perfectly functional electronics end up in Indian landfills every year, leaching lead and mercury, because someone could not source a 50 rupee capacitor or a one-page reset guide. That is a public environmental cost imposed on everyone, not a private consumer choice. Second, this creates and protects livelihoods. Millions of neighbourhood repair technicians already exist across the country, but today they are forced to turn customers away when manufacturers lock parts and firmware.
[P1.3] This law will not destroy official service networks. It will open a fair market for everyone. Most fundamentally, this defends basic ownership. If you paid for a device, you own it.

None of those justify stripping every Indian household of the right to maintain their own property. This law does not ban official service.
[P1.5] It does not force anyone to attempt their own repairs. It only removes the artificial, profit-driven barriers that currently leave people with one real choice: throw it away, and buy again.

[C1.1] The frustration PRO describes is real, but the diagnosis is wrong. A screen that costs 80% of a new phone is not proof of conspiracy; it reflects costly displays, logistics, and skilled labour. Forcing every company to warehouse parts, build special tools, and publish service manuals for years will not make repair cheap. [C1.2] India already has what America and Europe dream of: a living repair economy. From Karol Bagh to Lamington Road to every mohalla mobile shop, technicians keep devices alive without a central law. A blanket mandate supercharges that problem by forcing disclosure of schematics and diagnostics that cloners can copy overnight, flooding bazaars with fake batteries and boards that fail faster and burn more often.
[C1.3] That is the safety core PRO waves away. Consumer electronics are not toasters. Puncture a glued lithium battery while following a PDF and you get a house fire. Tamper with a washing-machine controller or microwave inverter and you get electrocution.
[C1.4] Official networks internalize that liability; a neighbourhood shop armed with mandated tools does not.
[C1.5] Ownership of a device was never ownership of its design. Buying a book does not entitle you to the printing press. India's startups, trying to build under Make in India, cannot absorb the compliance burden that Samsung can. The result is less competition, higher prices, and more e-waste from botched repairs — the opposite of what PRO promises.
[C1.6] And who will enforce this across thousands of models sold from Shenzhen to Noida? Inspectors cannot police firmware updates today. The law becomes selective harassment of visible domestic firms while fly-by-night importers vanish — punishing exactly the Make in India brands PRO should want to grow.
