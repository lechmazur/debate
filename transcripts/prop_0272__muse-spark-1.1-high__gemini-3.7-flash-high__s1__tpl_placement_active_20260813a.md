[P1.1] Public schools should be the place that gives kids what they can't get anywhere else. Almost every kid already has endless screen time after the bell. What they don't have is a safe field, a coach who shows up, a set of drums, or an hour where adults let them actually play.

[P1.6] Digital enrichment has value, but it is abundant, cheap to add later, and ineffective without the attention, mental health, and social foundation that movement and creation build. [C1.1] Equating structured digital enrichment with passive screen time fundamentally misunderstands modern education. Digital enrichment is not mindless scrolling; it is competitive robotics, computational thinking, digital arts production, and software design. My opponent dismisses these programs as exhausting screen time, but collaborating on a coding project or building a digital portfolio cultivates the exact teamwork, persistence, and community that PRO champions. Wealthy families already invest heavily in private tech academies; mandating that sports and unstructured play must expand first cements an insurmountable economic divide.
[C1.2] First, equity demands expanding digital access. Low-income students often lack high-speed hardware, specialized software, and technical mentorship at home. PRO rightly notes that affluent families purchase private enrichment, but ignores that they buy coding bootcamps, AI tutoring, and advanced STEM programs alongside travel sports. While affluent peers build technical portfolios, underprivileged students are left as passive consumers of digital media.
[C1.3] The public school is the only equalizer that can transform them into technological creators. Prioritizing physical play before digital expansion guarantees that disadvantaged students fall permanently behind in a technology-driven workforce.
[C1.4] Second, digital enrichment solves the severe scaling and inclusion bottlenecks of traditional extracurriculars. Athletic teams and physical arts face hard physical constraints: rosters are capped, facilities are scarce, and equipment is expensive. A soccer field serves twenty-two students; an after-school digital lab engages hundreds simultaneously with personalized pacing.
[C1.5] Crucially, digital programs provide safe, empowering avenues for neurodivergent students and children with physical disabilities, who are frequently sidelined or bullied in competitive sports and unstructured play.
[C1.6] Finally, the proposition enforces a dangerous sequencing fallacy. Athletic infrastructure requires immense capital, liability coverage, and physical land that dense, underfunded urban schools simply do not have. By contrast, digital enrichment offers immediate, highly scalable, and career-defining returns at a fraction of the per-pupil cost. Public schools must not freeze vital 21st-century skills while waiting decades to fund logistically prohibitive physical facilities.
