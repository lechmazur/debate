[C1.1] The proposition rests on a false dichotomy: that integrating technology in early grades means abandoning pencils, physical books, and motor development. But early education should never be reduced to an either-or conflict between paper and screens. When used intentionally, digital tools are not passive replacements for paper; they are engines of differentiation, accessibility, and equity that static print physically cannot replicate.
[C1.2] First, consider instructional differentiation. Early classrooms contain vast developmental variance: emergent bilinguals, children with speech or auditory delays, and students with undiagnosed dyslexia sit alongside fluent early readers. A static paper worksheet is completely mute. It cannot pronounce an unfamiliar phoneme aloud, provide instant corrective feedback, or dynamically adapt difficulty to keep a struggling child from giving up.
[C1.3] Adaptive, interactive programs offer immediate scaffolding, turning abstract phonics into multisensory learning while freeing teachers to conduct focused, small-group interventions.
[C1.4] Second, equity cuts decisively against a device-light mandate. While PRO cites equipment costs, affluent children already receive guided digital literacy and educational enrichment at home. For under-resourced students, school is frequently the only environment where screens are transformed from passive entertainment into purposeful instruments of inquiry and creation. Denying access during these formative years does not protect vulnerable students; it entrenches the digital divide and sets them up for failure when digital testing and expectations surge later.
[C1.5] Third, digital self-regulation cannot be built through total avoidance. Executive function does not magically mature by fourth grade; it is cultivated through practice. Abruptly handing students one-to-one tablets later creates a jarring transition, inviting the very novelty-driven distraction PRO warns against. Early, moderated exposure under direct teacher supervision demystifies technology and builds disciplined focus when habits are easiest to form.
[C1.6] We do not have to sacrifice handwriting to embrace the power of adaptive technology. A blanket paper-first mandate ties teachers’ hands, ignores diverse learning needs, and withholds vital support from the children who need it most.
