[C1.3] Rational employers respond predictably. They stop paying recruitment costs and push them onto workers — and worker-paid recruitment debt is the single deepest driver of forced-labor conditions worldwide. Or they hire fewer low-skill migrants altogether. Either way, the Bangladeshi electrician in PRO's story loses: he either arrives owing two years of wages to a broker, or he never gets the job at all.
[C1.4] Millions of remittance-dependent families are the collateral.
[C1.5] Bahrain already ran this experiment. Its flexi-permit made workers self-sponsored — and they had to buy the permit themselves, ended up in unregulated day labor with no employer accountable for housing, insurance, or wages, and the scheme was widely criticized and eventually scrapped. Portability without a responsible sponsor doesn't free workers; it converts them into precarious gig laborers who now bear every risk themselves.
[C1.6] Notice, too, that PRO's central abuse — unpaid wages — isn't solved by mobility. Walking away from stolen wages doesn't recover them; it forgives them. The real remedies are enforcement: wage-protection systems, passport-confiscation bans, absconding-law repeal, fast labor courts. Qatar and Saudi Arabia's targeted reforms show sponsorship can be fixed inside the existing framework, preserving the investment logic that makes mass low-skill migration viable while closing the abuse channels.
[C1.7] The choice isn't kafala versus freedom. It's reformed sponsorship versus a market that quietly stops hiring the people this motion claims to save.
