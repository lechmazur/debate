[P1.1] Cities should cut default urban speeds and redesign streets ahead of harsher post-crash penalties because the first two prevent deaths and the third only answers them. Most fatal urban crashes are ordinary error meeting high speed and unforgiving design, not calculated crimes that heavier sentences reliably deter.
[P1.2] Speed decides whether a crash kills. A person hit at neighborhood pace often survives; the same impact at arterial pace often does not. Energy rises with the square of velocity, so dropping typical defaults from the thirties toward twenty saves lives out of proportion to the minutes lost. A lower default also resets the norm: slow is the rule unless a sign says otherwise, not a rare exception fought block by block.
[P1.3] Design does what statutes cannot. Narrower lanes, tighter corners, raised crossings, and protected space for people walking and cycling make the safe speed the easy speed. Drivers obey the road in front of them more consistently than a penalty they expect never to face. Those changes also protect people who never chose the car: children, older adults, cyclists, riders stepping off a bus.
[P1.4] No sentence restores them.
[P1.5] Penalties fail the stated goal on timing and on psychology. They begin only after someone is already dead. The driver who glances at a phone, misjudges a gap, or treats a wide road as an invitation is not running an expected-value calculation about prison. Extreme sanctions can even push hit-and-runs when fear of ruin outweighs fear of being caught leaving.
[P1.6] Accountability for families is real, but it is a different objective from fewer corpses. Measured by lives saved, slower defaults and streets that enforce them reduce both crash chance and crash lethality; severity after the fact does neither.
[P1.7] The choice is not softness against toughness. It is prevention against epitaph. Cities that want fewer traffic deaths should spend their political capital on lower defaults and redesign, and keep harsher penalties secondary at most.

[C1.7] Punish flight harder than remaining and the incentive reverses.
[C1.8] The goal is fewer corpses. That requires changing who takes the risk, not only how forgiving the curb is. Cities that spend political capital on defaults and redesign more than on post-crash penalties get slower paperwork and the same killers. Prevention that cannot punish is a suggestion.
