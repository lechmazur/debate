[P1.1] Colonial looting was not a neutral transfer of custody; it was dispossession by force. That fact, not the quality of a foreign gallery, should decide who holds the object today. Even if a museum genuinely has better climate control, armed guards, or conservators, it is still holding stolen property. A burglar does not earn title to a painting because his house has superior humidity and a good insurance policy; he must return it, and any help preserving it comes after restitution, not instead of it.
[P1.2] The “we preserve it better” claim is therefore an argument about the thief’s storeroom, not about the owner’s right.
[P1.3] Worse, the preservation test has no limiting principle. It says those with colonial extraction can keep the object because they are richer now. If superior storage justifies keeping Benin Bronzes abroad, it would justify a wealthier power keeping anything taken anywhere. That is not conservation ethics; it is a permanent reward for imperial theft.
[P1.4] And it is often factually hollow. Greece built the Acropolis Museum near the Parthenon with modern conservation facilities; returning these objects is not shipping them into neglect. Even where capacity is thin, the answer is partnership, funding, training, and revolving loans, not endless foreign title. Recent returns and loan agreements over Benin Bronzes show exactly this: objects can go home and still travel, be studied, and be conserved jointly.
[P1.5] Finally, physical safety is not the whole of preservation. A mask, relic, or royal regalia kept in a foreign capital may be chemically stable yet culturally dead: cut off from ritual, language, kinship, and place. Return restores the context that gives the artifact meaning. Even taking the foreign museum’s best factual case—better bricks and mortar—the verdict is unchanged: restitution is owed, and preservation is a condition for cooperation rather than permission to keep the spoil.

[C1.1] Colonial coercion is a real moral wrong, but "even" turns restitution into an absolute no conservation ethic can defend. The live question is not whether a taking was wrong; it is whether a present transfer should be ordered when a credible preservation assessment shows it will materially endanger an irreplaceable object. A flaked pigment surface, collapsed textile, or corroded metal in the wrong humidity cannot be repaired by a later apology. Heritage rights are held in trust for the object and future publics, not just for the current claimant.
[C1.2] The burglar analogy [P1.1] is vivid but misdescribes the case. A public museum is usually not the original raider; it is a trustee separated by generations, with public access, catalogues, conservation records, and legal duties. More importantly, returning a car to its owner does not risk the car ceasing to exist. With fragile heritage, custody is part of the right claimed: preserving the thing is the precondition for any justice involving it.
[C1.3] Calling this "the thief's storeroom" [P1.2] is a slogan, not an answer to irreversibility.
[C1.4] Nor does this create [P1.3]'s "permanent reward." The limiting principle is independent, object-specific proof of serious risk, not a wealthy museum's self-serving preference. A bronze that travels safely under joint conservation may go home; a feather cloak or painted bark object that cannot survive existing transport or storage should not be lost because a slogan demands instant title. Deferred return, shared custody, long loans, training, conservation funding, and verified standards are mechanisms by which restitution and survival can coincide.
[C1.5] Nor is "country of origin" a magic owner: objects often predate modern states, and a central government may not be the living community. Most decisively, [P1.5]'s "culturally dead" phrase proves too much: a destroyed object has no ritual, kinship, study, or return. Conditional restitution with conservation safeguards protects both context and existence; automatic return "even when" preservation is better chooses disappearance over justice.
