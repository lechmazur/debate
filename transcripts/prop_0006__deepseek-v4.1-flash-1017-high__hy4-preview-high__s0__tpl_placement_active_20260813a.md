[P1.1] Minimum-wage law has always asked who controls the worker’s time—not whether every minute produces output. A courier logged on, in the delivery zone, ready to accept the next order, and barred by the platform from taking competing work is not free. She is waiting because the dispatch system requires her to wait. If that time is invisible for wage compliance, the platform receives a legal discount for keeping more workers on call than it needs.
[P1.2] The task-only metric guarantees sub-minimum pay whenever demand is thin. Imagine a worker accepts a $6 delivery that takes 15 minutes, then waits 45 minutes for another. Task-time accounting calls that $24 an hour. The worker actually earned $6 for the hour.
[P1.3] A platform can flood the market with couriers, externalize the cost of idle capacity onto them, and call the resulting poverty “flexibility.” That is not a minimum wage; it is a wage floor that applies only during busy periods.
[P1.4] “Active waiting time” is the right boundary because it tracks genuine control. It does not count someone who casually opens the app at home. It counts periods when the worker is available, expected to respond promptly, and practically unable to commit to other paid work. That is the gig equivalent of being on call: the platform controls availability, the worker bears the uncertainty, and the law should not pretend the time vanished.
[P1.5] The compliance rule should therefore include task time plus active waiting time. Platforms may still manage shifts, incentives, and scheduling; they simply cannot suppress take-home pay below the floor by saturating the labor pool. A minimum wage that ignores the waiting built into the platform’s own dispatch model is not a minimum wage at all—it is a subsidy for overcapacity. Count active waiting.
