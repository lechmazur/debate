[P1.1] Consent tells us one important thing: that no one was forced. It tells us nothing about whether anyone was wronged. That gap is the entire debate, and three independent arguments each fill it.
[P1.2] First, consent given under desperation is real consent that still fails to sanitize a transaction. A drowning man who agrees to pay a passing boater his life savings for rescue has consented—genuinely, rationally, with full information. Yet the boater has exploited him, and we all recognize it. Payday lenders at 400% interest, employers offering starvation wages in company towns, brokers buying kidneys from the destitute: these deals are consensual precisely because one party has engineered or inherited the other's lack of alternatives.
[P1.3] If consent alone made transactions ethical, the word "exploitation" would name nothing. It names something.
[P1.4] Second, transactions have third parties. Two people can consent perfectly to a deal that poisons everyone else: a factory owner pays a landowner to dump waste upstream of a village; a lobbyist buys a legislator's vote; a hitman accepts a contract. Consent between the parties is flawless in each case. The ethics are not.
[P1.5] Any theory that evaluates a transaction solely by the agreement of its signatories is blind to everyone the transaction touches.
[P1.6] Third, some goods are corrupted by being sold at all. A citizen who freely sells her vote, a person who consensually signs himself into permanent slavery, a parent auctioning a child's affection—these offend not because consent is missing but because certain relationships and rights are degraded by market exchange, however willing the participants.
[P1.7] Notice the burden structure. To win, my opponent must show consent is *sufficient*—that every consensual transaction is thereby ethical. A single counterexample sinks that claim, and I have offered three whole categories. Consent is the floor of transactional ethics, and floors are necessary.
[P1.8] But nobody thinks a building is sound because it has a floor.

[C1.1] Consent is sufficient because it is the moral mechanism that turns an imposition into an exchange. In a transaction, the relevant ethical question between the parties is whether each authorized the transfer. If a competent adult, informed about the terms, freely agrees, neither party is wronged as a transactor. That does not mean the deal is wise, admirable, or beyond regulation; it means the transaction has crossed from violation to cooperation.
[C1.2] The drowning case confuses a harsh bargain with a wrongful one. The rescuer may be cruel, but if the man chooses his life over his savings, the exchange honors the man’s own ranking of ends. Calling it exploitation smuggles in the judgment that desperation should invalidate choice. That is not protecting consent; it is overriding it.
[C1.3] Payday loans, low wages, or organ sales can be repellent, but if the person prefers the deal to the alternatives, condemning the exchange substitutes our judgment for his.
[C1.4] Third-party harms do not prove consent insufficient inside the transaction; they prove the transaction has external effects. If a factory dumps waste, the wrong is to villagers whose rights were not consented to. The defect is missing consent from affected outsiders, not the inadequacy of buyer-seller consent. Add an injunction or tax; the contract between owner and landowner remains ethically formed.
[C1.5] Restrictions on votes or bodies likewise are collective judgments that some choices are too dangerous to permit. That is policy, paternalism, or moralism—not proof that a private exchange becomes unethical merely because the parties consented. PRO’s standard lets a majority veto autonomy whenever it dislikes a good’s social meaning.
[C1.6] The decisive clash is authorization versus substantive approval. If ethics requires more than consent, whose values fill the gap? Consent alone is sufficient because it respects persons as authors of their agreements; the proper limit is harm to non-consenters, not distrust of consent.
