[P1.1] Population health is shaped by the food environment, not by the nutritional literacy of individuals navigating it. Reformulation mandates attack the problem at its source; labeling and education campaigns ask consumers to solve it at the point of purchase, after the environment has already done its damage.
[P1.2] The core mechanism is exposure reduction. When you cap salt, sugar, or fat across a product category, every consumer of that category benefits automatically—no behavior change required, no label reading, no motivation needed. A person who buys the same bread, the same yogurt, the same snacks every week simply ingests less sodium and less added sugar. That is a population-level intervention operating at population scale.
[P1.3] Education campaigns, by contrast, require a chain of individual actions: notice the label, understand it, care about it, have the time and money to act on it, and sustain that behavior over years. Each link in that chain leaks people. The result is that interventions dependent on individual volition consistently produce smaller effects than structural changes to the environment.
[P1.4] Consider the track record. Trans fat bans achieved in years what decades of consumer education could not—near-elimination of artificial trans fats from the food supply. Salt reduction targets in the UK, even voluntary ones, drove measurable sodium reductions across broad product categories because they changed what manufacturers put on shelves. These interventions worked precisely because they did not depend on consumers becoming nutrition experts.
[P1.5] Ultra-processed foods are engineered for overconsumption. Their palatability, cost, and convenience are not accidents consumers can be educated around—they are structural features. Asking people to resist an environment designed to overwhelm their dietary judgment is not a public health strategy; it is an abdication of one. If the goal is population health improvement measured across entire societies, not just the motivated minority who read labels, reformulation mandates are not just superior to education—they operate in a fundamentally different category of effectiveness.

[C1.4] By contrast, robust front-of-package warning labels and targeted public education do what mandates never can: they demystify ultra-processed products and shift aggregate demand toward whole, unprocessed foods.
