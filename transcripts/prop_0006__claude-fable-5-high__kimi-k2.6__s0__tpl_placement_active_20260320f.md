[P1.1] When a delivery driver logs onto an app, accepts the platform's terms, and sits ready to take the next order, that driver is working. The platform commands their availability, benefits from their readiness, and structures their whole shift around waiting. The only question is whether the company that profits from that time should also have to pay for it. Minimum-wage law everywhere else says yes: a cashier with no customers, a firefighter between calls, a security guard on a quiet night — all are paid because being available on the employer's terms is labor.
[P1.2] Gig platforms have simply invented an accounting trick to escape a rule every other business follows.
[P1.3] Here is the core mechanism, and it should anchor this debate. Waiting time on gig platforms is not idle by accident — it is a deliberate design choice. [P1.4] A driver can be logged in for ten hours, complete tasks for five, and the platform reports a healthy "hourly rate" while the worker's actual take-home pay falls below any legal floor. If waiting time counted, platforms would internalize the cost of their own oversupply decisions: they would calibrate driver numbers, smooth demand, and price honestly. [P1.5] Expect the objection that workers can multi-app or that waiting is "freedom." [P1.6] Imperfect edge cases do not justify a system where the legal minimum wage is, in practice, optional.
[P1.7] Pay for the time you control. That principle decides this debate.

[C1.5] Even California’s narrower “engaged time” rules have pushed platforms toward restrictive batching; full waiting-time pay completes that transformation.
[C1.6] Finally, overlapping apps make the accounting fantasy unworkable. A driver idling with four platforms active cannot honestly bill each for the same hour. [C1.7] The motion promises a wage floor. It delivers the end of the gig economy’s defining bargain: task-by-task freedom in exchange for task-by-task risk. Preserve that boundary, or watch the flexibility vanish.
