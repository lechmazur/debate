[P1.1] Mandated minimum nurse-to-patient ratios are a patient-safety floor, not a bureaucratic instruction. When a nurse is spread across too many beds, the failures are rarely dramatic; they are quiet: a missed turn, a delayed pain reassessment, a change in mental status noticed too late. Hospital administrators experience these risks as budget lines and staffing grids. Patients experience them as infections, falls, or failure to rescue.
[P1.2] That asymmetry is the core problem. Leaving staffing mainly to administrators means letting the group that bears financial pressure decide how much safety to buy. In tight quarters, the incentive is to stretch nurses to the maximum, not to the minimum risk. A legal ratio reverses the default: instead of justifying why unsafe staffing is unavoidable, hospitals must meet a clear floor.
[P1.3] The outcome evidence runs in one direction. Higher nurse workloads are consistently associated with more deaths, more complications, longer stays, and more readmissions. Where enforceable minimums have been adopted, they are linked to lower mortality and improved nurse retention. That is not surprising; it is exactly the mechanism one would expect when overburdened nurses lose time for surveillance and early intervention.
[P1.4] Administrative discretion also creates invisible variation. The same ward can be adequately staffed on Tuesday and dangerously short on Saturday. Two hospitals in the same system can make opposite choices. A legal ratio gives patients, regulators, and nurses a public standard instead of an internal judgment call.
[P1.5] The flexibility objection misses what minimums mean. A ratio is a floor, not a ceiling: hospitals remain free to staff higher when acuity demands. And the shortage objection cuts in favor of the rule. Mandated ratios force hospitals to compete for nurses, improve conditions, and reduce burnout, pulling people back into bedside care.
[P1.6] If a facility truly cannot staff safely, it should reduce elective volume or transfer patients, not quietly ration monitoring.

[C1.7] Access is part of safety.
