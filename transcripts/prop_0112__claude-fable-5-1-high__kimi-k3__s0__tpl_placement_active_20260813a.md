[P1.1] Start with the one fact that decides this debate: the moments people most need medical coverage are the moments employment is most likely to vanish. A warehouse worker gets a cancer diagnosis, goes through chemotherapy, can no longer meet her shifts, and loses her job. Under employer-based insurance, the same illness that creates her need for care is the event that strips away her means of paying for it. A financing system that switches off precisely when it is triggered is not a coverage system with a flaw.
[P1.2] It is a flaw dressed up as a coverage system.
[P1.3] That is the core of the case. Basic medical coverage exists to protect people against the unpredictable. Employment is itself unpredictable, and it is correlated with health. Recessions, layoffs, pandemics, disability, caregiving for a sick parent: each of these severs the link at the worst time.
[P1.4] When millions lost jobs in spring 2020, they did not merely lose income; they lost the card that got them into a clinic during a public-health emergency. Single-payer financing, funded through general taxation, is indifferent to whether you are working, between jobs, retired, or too sick to work. Coverage follows the person, not the payroll.
[P1.5] Three further advantages compound this. First, risk pooling: one national pool spreads costs across everyone, rather than leaving a small firm's premiums to spike because one employee's child needed surgery. Second, administration: a single payer replaces thousands of plan designs, eligibility checks, and annual re-enrollments with one set of rules, which is why public programs like traditional Medicare run on far lower overhead than private plans.
[P1.6] Third, freedom: workers stay in bad jobs and decline to start businesses because leaving means gambling with their family's health. Untethering coverage from employment liberates the labor market rather than distorting it.
[P1.7] The motion concerns basic coverage. Nothing prevents supplemental private options on top. The question is only whether the floor should depend on having a boss. It should not.

[C1.6] The burden on "preferable" is high. PRO must show their model beats employer financing at its best, not at its worst. They have not.
