[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a number for safety. A nurse-to-patient ratio counts bodies, not needs. Four stable post-operative patients and four patients in active deterioration are not the same workload, yet a legal 1:4 floor treats them identically. In high-acuity units, the mandated number can be too thin; in low-acuity units, it can pull scarce nurses away from where they are needed.
[C1.2] The result is not a safety floor but a compliance ceiling—administrators can point to the number while ignoring skill mix, unlicensed support, documentation burden, and patient turnover.
[C1.3] Second, staffing is a real-time allocation problem. Hospital administrators see the ward, the admissions queue, the available specialists, and the next shift. Legislatures and regulators set a number and move on. When a surge hits, a rigid ratio forces hospitals to divert ambulances, board patients in emergency departments, cancel elective cases, or close beds—not because care is unsafe, but because the roster cannot be legally adjusted.
[C1.4] In rural and underserved areas, the nurses may not exist. A mandate cannot conjure them; it can only make the hospital shrink access to satisfy the rule.
[C1.5] The PRO case says patients cannot protect themselves. True—but a legal ratio does not give them protection; it gives them a false assurance. A hospital can meet 1:4 and still leave a deteriorating patient unwatched because the nurse has no aide, no break relief, and too many tasks. Better regulation targets what matters: acuity-based staffing plans, public reporting of staffing and outcomes, nurse input, whistleblower protection, and liability for reckless understaffing.
[C1.6] Those preserve local judgment while exposing failure.
[C1.7] Finally, “flexibility” is not a dirty word. It is how a hospital moves a nurse from a quiet ward to a bursting emergency department. The choice is not ratios versus greed; it is a blunt input mandate versus accountable, acuity-sensitive staffing. Patients need the right nurse at the right time, not a legally convenient number.

[P4.2] Regulators set the floor; ward teams decide how far above it patient needs require. CON conflates discretion to add staff with discretion to remove basic protection.
[P4.3] A2: Where recruitment is genuinely impossible after a phase-in, grant a temporary, publicly disclosed emergency exception with mitigation and a workforce plan—not automatic closure or routine waivers. The floor remains binding ordinarily; the hospital must prove the emergency, and the exception expires. If waivers become routine, enforce the limits and address the shortage. Abolishing the rule recruits no nurses: it merely makes overload the permanent, invisible exception.
[P4.4] Scarcity must be managed under either system. CON says a ratio may “freeze” a nurse on a stable ward, but their own plan cannot move her without considering the patients left behind. A minimum makes the gap explicit, requiring reinforcement or a disclosed emergency response; discretionary staffing can simply assign the abandoned ward’s work to whoever remains.
[P4.5] Nor is meeting the minimum “legally sufficient” for every clinical need. Hospitals can still be required to staff above it, report outcomes, protect whistleblowers, and face liability. CON’s liability responds after harm; its plans and committees supply no hard limit against overload beforehand.
[P4.6] The choice is not ratios versus comprehensive staffing. It is comprehensive staffing with a binding backstop, or comprehensive staffing only when administrators judge it affordable. CON has shown that ratios are not sufficient, never that patients should have no minimum protection at all.
