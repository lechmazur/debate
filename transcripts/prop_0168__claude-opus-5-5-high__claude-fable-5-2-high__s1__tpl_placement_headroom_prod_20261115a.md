[P1.1] Every dollar a space agency spends is a choice about how much of the universe we get to understand. On that choice, the evidence is lopsided: robots and telescopes deliver far more discovery per dollar, and the motion simply asks agencies to act on that fact.
[P1.2] Look at the record. The Voyager probes, launched in the 1970s, are still returning data from interstellar space. Opportunity was designed for roughly ninety days on Mars and worked for about fifteen years. Hubble and Webb have reshaped cosmology and exoplanet science for thousands of researchers.
[P1.3] Probes have visited every planet, landed on Titan, and brought asteroid samples home. Humans, meanwhile, have not gone beyond low orbit in over fifty years.
[P1.4] Now compare cost. The International Space Station has absorbed well over a hundred billion dollars, and its scientific return, while real, is modest against that bill. [P1.5] Robots need none of that, so the same budget buys several missions instead of one. That is the core mechanism: crewed flight spends most of its money keeping people alive, not learning things.
[P1.6] Then there is risk. Losing a probe is a setback; losing a crew is a tragedy that grounds entire programs for years, as Challenger and Columbia did. A robotic portfolio can tolerate failure and spread its bets across many targets.
[P1.7] Notice what the motion does not say. It says "most," not "all." Agencies can keep human spaceflight research alive. CON must therefore defend the current imbalance, not human spaceflight itself.
[P1.8] Expect CON to invoke inspiration. But Webb's deep-field images and rover selfies captivated millions. Inspiration is not a crewed monopoly. Discovery is the inspiration, and robots deliver far more of it per dollar.
