[P1.1] Mass deportation: not a strategy, but a recurring bill for a problem it never fixes. In the Gulf, construction sites, hotels, hospitals and homes run on migrant labor. When a worker loses status—often after a sponsor withholds pay or papers—the vacancy does not vanish. It is refilled by another irregular hire because demand remains.
[P1.2] Deport, and the state pays for removals while employers, recruiters and brokers keep the margins.
[P1.3] Legal labor corridors change the incentive. They give employers a lawful way to hire and workers a lawful way to stay and change jobs without fear, and governments a taxable, visible workforce instead of an invisible underclass. That is regulation, not laxity.
[P1.4] The same logic crosses the Mediterranean: irregular movement is not chaos but demand meeting closed doors. North African states face two linked flows: sub-Saharan migrants already working in informal farms, markets and care jobs, and citizens or residents paying smugglers to reach Europe. Prioritizing legal corridors answers both. People already present can regularize into real contracts, while negotiated work visas let others depart lawfully instead of buying a place on a boat.
[P1.5] A corridor does not mean open borders; it means an orderly queue replacing a dangerous raft and starving the smuggling market.
[P1.6] Prioritizing corridors also strengthens sovereignty. Deportation-only policies hollow it out: they push workers deeper underground, fuel document fraud, and force periodic amnesties that legalize the very irregularity the state opposed. Corridors let governments decide who enters, under what wages and sponsorship rules, and in what numbers. That is control.
[P1.7] Mass removals are control performed for cameras, then quietly undone by the next labor shortage.
[P1.8] The choice is not compassion versus enforcement; it is an endless cycle of removals versus a system where the labor market obeys the law. They must build the corridor, not the deportation pipeline.

[C1.1] The proposition rests on a false cure: that irregular migration is merely suppressed demand, and that swapping deportations for legal corridors will tame it. In reality, prioritizing legal corridors over enforcement tells prospective migrants and smugglers that irregular arrival is a fast lane to regularization. The raft is not replaced by an orderly queue; the queue simply forms behind the raft. When Spain or Italy hinted regularizations in the past, arrivals surged ahead of the announcement.
[C1.2] Gulf and North African states would import that surge.
[C1.3] In the Gulf, citizen employment programs are not footnote. Under systems like Saudization or Emiratization, governments cap foreign hires in key sectors to reserve jobs for nationals. Prioritizing expansive legal corridors over deportations traps states into perpetuating a large sponsored workforce, directly undermining those quotas. The PRO vision of a “taxable, visible workforce” [P1.3] ignores that many irregular migrants occupy jobs nationals should fill; legalizing them entrenches displacement rather than solving it.
[C1.4] For North African states, the corridor idea is equally fragile. Negotiated work visas to Europe drain crucial local labor from farms and care sectors that those economies need, while doing little to stop sub-Saharan migrants already en route. Smugglers do not vanish; they coach clients to enter irregularly, then claim corridor eligibility. Without the credible threat of removal that mass deportations provide, the legal path becomes an amnesty funnel.
[C1.5] PRO claims corridors strengthen sovereignty [P1.6] and deportations hollow it. The opposite holds: sovereignty means a state can say no and make it stick. A deportation apparatus is the backbone of any legal migration system; without it, corridors are toothless invitations. Repeated amnesties PRO laments are the product of weak enforcement, not its cure.
[C1.6] Prioritizing corridors over deportations does not build control; it trades the only lever that deters irregular flow for a paperwork promise migrants will exploit. The path to victory is enforcement-first: deportations sized to signal seriousness, with corridors as a narrow complement, not a priority.
