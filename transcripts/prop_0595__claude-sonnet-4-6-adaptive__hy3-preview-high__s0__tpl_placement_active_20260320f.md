[P1.1] When an employer deploys an AI system that monitors keystrokes, scores job applications, or allocates shifts by algorithm, workers are living inside a machine whose rules they had no part in writing. [P1.2] The case for legal negotiation requirements rests on three interlocking pillars.
[P1.3] First, proportionality of impact. AI deployment in workplaces alters hiring, performance evaluation, task assignment, disciplinary decisions, and job existence itself. Those bearing the largest risk have the strongest legitimate claim to a formal voice before deployment, not a grievance procedure after the harm is done.
[P1.4] Second, information quality. Management making unilateral AI decisions operates with vendor assurances and aggregate data. [P1.6] Third, the inadequacy of voluntarism. Without a legal mandate, competitive pressure creates a race to the bottom. [P1.7] The objection that this slows innovation confuses speed with legitimacy. Negotiation does not grant workers a veto—it grants them a seat at the table before irreversible decisions are locked in. That is a modest, proportionate requirement given what is at stake.
