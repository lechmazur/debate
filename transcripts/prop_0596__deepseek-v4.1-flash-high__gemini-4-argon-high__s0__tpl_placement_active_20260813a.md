[P1.1] Gene editing is turning fatal inherited diseases into one-time cures. That is a triumph—until monopoly pricing turns the cure into a luxury. A public-interest licensing pathway is how countries ensure that when an essential gene-editing therapy exists, access is a public health decision, not only a shareholder decision.
[P1.2] The pathway should be narrow and rule-bound: governments may compel licensing only for essential therapies, after independent review, with compensation and royalties to the innovator and affordable access terms. That is not abolition of patents. It is the same principle as eminent domain and existing compulsory licensing: private rights matter, but they do not include a veto over a society's ability to treat its people. A company still earns a return; it simply cannot demand any price or refuse all supply.
[P1.3] Licenses can also require sufficient supply and technology transfer, because a legal right to manufacture means nothing without practical capacity.
[P1.4] Consider sickle cell disease. CRISPR-based cures are priced in the millions per patient. In wealthy countries, that is already a budget crisis; in countries with the highest disease burden and the tightest health budgets, it is effectively no cure at all. Voluntary licensing is welcome but discretionary.
[P1.5] It arrives when companies choose, on terms companies choose. A government facing thousands of patients needs a lawful backstop, not a begging bowl.
[P1.6] The predictable objection is that this chills innovation. But the pathway preserves compensation and is triggered only for essential therapies. More importantly, much of the underlying science is publicly funded; the public already took the risk. Society is not stealing a discovery, it is refusing to let exclusivity nullify the discovery's purpose.
[P1.7] The decisive question is simple: should a country be able to save its citizens when a cure exists, or must it wait for a monopoly's permission? Requiring a public-interest license answers that question in favor of health, sovereignty, and human dignity.

[C1.3] In biologics, the process is the product; a tiny deviation can kill the patient. Coerced tech transfer creates either an empty paper right or a severe safety hazard.

If governments impose royalty caps whenever a therapy succeeds and becomes "essential" [P1.2], the rare winners cannot pay for the mountain of failures.
[C1.5] Capital will rationally flee one-time cures in favor of lifelong maintenance drugs.
[C1.6] You cannot deliver complex cellular medicine by decree. By replacing collaboration with coercion, this policy gives today’s patients an unworkable legal fiction and tomorrow’s patients no cures at all.
