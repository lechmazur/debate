[P1.1] Taiwan will be decided not by spreadsheets but by whether Beijing believes it can win quickly. Deterrence that promises punishment after the fact does not stop a leader who thinks victory will be fast and irreversible. Deterrence that makes quick victory physically impossible does.
[P1.2] Forward-deployed anti-ship and air defense does exactly that. An invasion needs massed air control and unimpeded movement across a narrow strait. Dispersed, survivable launchers on Okinawa, Luzon, Palawan, and Taiwan itself - Marine littoral units with anti-ship missiles, mobile rocket artillery, Patriot and Japanese and Philippine equivalents - turn that crossing into a gauntlet. They force the PLA to find and kill hundreds of small, mobile targets before it can even load its amphibious fleet, not after.
[P1.3] Layered air defenses blunt the opening missile and air barrage and preserve Taiwanese and allied airfields and sortie generation. That denies Beijing the fait accompli its entire strategy depends on.
[P1.4] Economic deterrence alone cannot do that work. Sanctions are slow, leaky, and politically contingent. Beijing has spent years stockpiling energy, food, chips and reserves precisely to ride out a cutoff, and building alternative payment and trade channels. Any threat of devastating decoupling will be haggled down by allies who depend on Chinese markets and supply chains, and only after an invasion has already happened.
[P1.5] Russia showed a determined, nuclear-armed autocracy will absorb enormous economic pain if it thinks the battlefield favors it. A threat that arrives months late and hurts the sender as much as the target does not change a general's war plan for next week.
[P1.6] Forward posture also lowers war risk by reducing miscalculation. Missiles in place cannot be negotiated away in a crisis; tariff threats can. By raising the likelihood of military failure from hour one, they make the economic costs irrelevant because the invasion never becomes rational in the first place.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes tactical friction for strategic prevention. Packing the Western Pacific with anti-ship missiles and air defenses does not make Beijing less likely to gamble on war; it convinces Chinese leaders that Washington is preparing for inevitable conflict, compressing their decision window and accelerating preemptive plans. When an adversary sees offensive power veiled as defense arrayed along its maritime periphery, deterrence gives way to the security dilemma. The result is not a cooler calculus in Beijing, but a hotter one.
[C1.2] Forward-deployed launchers are not abstract capability; they are fixed tripwires on allied territory that Beijing can map, target, and neutralize in the opening hours of a crisis. Once American and allied forces become frontline obstacles rather than reserve reinforcements, the threshold for violence drops sharply. A localized skirmish over a drone, a blockade exercise, or an accidental radar lock becomes a direct great-power exchange before diplomats can even convene. Your missiles cannot deter if they must fire to survive the first afternoon.
[C1.3] Economic deterrence, by contrast, operates below the kinetic threshold and shapes the fundamental strategic environment over time. Beijing knows that any conflict over Taiwan would trigger instantaneous decoupling in finance, energy, and advanced semiconductors—not because Western capitals will muster political will later, but because global markets, insurance networks, and supply chains would seize automatically at the first salvo. This is not a distant punishment to be bargained down; it is an immediate structural collapse of the export and technology model that underpins Communist Party legitimacy and national stability.
[C1.4] My opponent’s Russia analogy collapses on inspection. Russia absorbed pain because its economy was already isolated and its war aims were limited to a bordering state it perceived as existentially threatening. China’s economy is far more integrated with the very allies now proposed as missile hosts; the disruption of war is therefore an existential, not incremental, cost to regime survival. Emphasizing military forward posture over economic statecraft substitutes theatrical brinkmanship for genuine risk reduction, and invites the very conflict it claims to prevent.
