[P1.1] When a city pays a multimillion-dollar settlement for excessive force, the people writing the check are residents who never made the stop. The officer often keeps the paycheck, the pension, and departmental indemnification. That is not public safety finance. It is a subsidy for misconduct, and taxpayers are the residual insurer of last resort.
[P1.2] Requiring officers to carry personal professional liability insurance assigns the cost to the actor. The officer is the named insured. Premiums follow complaint history, uses of force, and prior claims. High-risk records get expensive, then uninsurable, then incompatible with employment.
[P1.3] Low-risk records stay cheap. Doctors, lawyers, and architects already live inside that discipline. Police officers exercise more immediate power over life and liberty. Treating them as uniquely shielded from the financial consequences of that power is a choice, not a law of nature.
[P1.4] Municipal indemnification socializes the downside of the worst actors while giving clean officers no corresponding credit. Insurance sorts them. Insurers underwrite because they lose money if they do not. Departments cannot quietly warehouse officers the market will not cover.
[P1.5] Even if a city still indemnifies some claims, deductibles, premium spikes, and the threat of cancelled coverage put a price on the officer’s own record. The next settlement is less likely to land on school budgets and trash collection because the risk was priced, year after year, on the person who created it.
[P1.6] This does not starve legitimate policing of funds. It stops using the general tax levy as hidden malpractice coverage for individual abuse of the badge. The current system conceals those costs until a payout hits the municipal budget. A personal policy makes them visible in advance.
[P1.7] Professionals still work under malpractice markets; they work with a record that has a price. Taxpayers should pay for policing. They should not mainly pay for the private liabilities of officers who violate the public trust.

[C1.1] Requiring officers to buy personal liability insurance does not move the bill for misconduct off the tax rolls. It renames the bill and then makes policing worse.
[C1.2] Premiums will not stay on the officer’s ledger. Compensation is bargained collectively, so a coverage mandate becomes a wage demand, a city-paid benefit, or both. Taxpayers fund the premium or the raise that offsets it. When an insurer drops a high-claim officer, the city still faces the lawsuit, the settlement, and the cost of a replacement.
[C1.3] The residual insurer remains the municipality, now with an extra administrative layer.
[C1.4] The market also prices the wrong risk. Complaints and force reports track assignment more than abuse. An officer on a violent night shift generates more contacts and claims than an officer in a quiet precinct, even when both follow policy. Underwriting that history drives competent people off the hardest beats.
[C1.5] Doctors can refuse a patient. Patrol officers cannot refuse a call. Pricing sovereign force like private malpractice punishes exposure, not misconduct, and leaves the neighborhoods that need police most with whoever remains insurable. The officer the market drops is often the one the beat still needs.
[C1.6] A private policy does not improve accountability either. Cities can already fire, retrain, and settle. Insurers optimize loss avoidance, not crime reduction. Denials, intentional-act exclusions, and premium spikes do not separate a bad shooting from a lawful one a jury still awards against.
[C1.7] They do give underwriters a veto over who may wear the badge—employment control shifted from the government that authorized the power to a firm that never answers for rising crime.
[C1.8] Taxpayers should fund policing, including its legal exposure, and insist departments remove officers who abuse the job. A personal policy solves neither cost nor conduct. It shrinks the pool willing to do the work, and the check stays public.
