[P1.1] The liquidity we are being asked to protect is the liquidity that disappears exactly when you need it. That is the whole debate in one sentence.
[P1.2] High-frequency firms post quotes for microseconds and cancel the vast majority before anyone can trade against them. In calm markets this looks like depth. In stressed markets the algorithms pull back simultaneously, because every one of them is programmed to stop taking risk the moment volatility spikes. May 2010 is the textbook case: within minutes, blue-chip stocks printed at a penny and at a hundred thousand dollars, then snapped back.
[P1.3] Nobody's fundamental view of Procter & Gamble changed. The "liquidity" simply evaporated. A market maker who leaves when it rains is not providing an umbrella; he is providing a picture of one.
[P1.4] A small transaction tax on high-frequency activity does three things. First, it makes churn expensive and investment cheap. A fraction of a basis point is invisible to a pension fund holding a stock for five years, but it is ruinous to a strategy that earns a hundredth of a cent per share and needs millions of round-trips a day. The tax discriminates precisely by design, not by accident.
[P1.5] Second, it kills quote stuffing and layering, the flooding of order books with messages meant to be cancelled, which exists only to confuse rivals and slow exchanges. Third, it ends a socially useless arms race: microwave towers and co-location racks bought so one firm can see a price a millionth of a second before another. That money and that talent build nothing.
[P1.6] The motion already concedes the cost: liquidity falls slightly. Spreads on a large-cap stock might widen by a fraction of a cent. Set that against what instability costs: retirement savers stopped out at absurd prices, companies whose market value swings on nothing, and a public that increasingly believes markets are rigged. Stability is a public good; millisecond liquidity is a private rent.
[P1.7] When the two conflict, the choice should not be difficult.

[C1.1] The motion asks countries to tax the activity that keeps prices continuous, then hopes instability falls anyway. That hope is backwards. High-frequency market making is the inventory and the quote between a sudden seller and a crash print. Make that activity more expensive and the book gets thinner precisely when a large order arrives.
[C1.2] Thinner books do not calm markets. They gap.
[C1.3] A transaction tax cannot do the surgical work claimed for it. Spoofing, layering, and quote stuffing are already market-abuse offenses. A levy on high-frequency trades does not read intent. It taxes the two-sided quote, the hedge, the update of a stale price, and the legitimate round-trip alike.
[C1.4] A pension fund may feel nothing on a five-year hold. The market maker who posts the bid that fund eventually hits feels it on every share. Raise that cost and spreads widen, depth shrinks, and the supposedly slight liquidity loss stops being slight the moment volatility rises and fewer firms will warehouse risk.
[C1.5] History already ran the experiment. When Sweden taxed equity and bond turnover in the nineteen-eighties, trading did not become thoughtful; much of it left for London, and the home market grew thinner. France's later levy on share purchases cut volume and pushed activity toward untaxed instruments and venues, without a clear gain in stability. A national tax on speed invites the order book to leave.
[C1.6] Instability is not cured offshore.
[C1.7] The flash-crash story does not carry the tax either. A huge sell program hitting a fragile book, amplified by stop orders and fragmented venues, is not fixed by charging market makers for showing up. Circuit breakers, kill switches, and order-to-trade surveillance attack that failure directly. A tax attacks volume and hopes the failure retires with it.
[C1.8] Stability is not purchased by punishing the quotes that absorb shocks. Less liquidity is a forecast of more instability, not a tolerable side effect. Countries should refuse the bargain.
