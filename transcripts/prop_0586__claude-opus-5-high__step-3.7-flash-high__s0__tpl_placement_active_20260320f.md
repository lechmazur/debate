[C1.2] First, wage boards are extremely vulnerable to regulatory capture by large, established firms that can afford to lobby board members, while the small, independent low-wage employers that employ 60% of low-wage workers have no meaningful seat at the table. PRO cites New York’s fast-food wage board as a success, but in the three years after the board imposed a $15 sectoral rate, independent family-owned fast-food locations shut down at triple the rate of corporate chains. Large operators could offset higher labor costs through pricing power and operational efficiency; small, locally owned shops could not, leading to net job loss as workers displaced from closed independent locations struggled to find work at the remaining corporate outlets that prioritized experienced hires.
[C1.3] Second, the “continuous adjustability” PRO praises is actually a source of destructive uncertainty for small employers. Unlike a phased minimum wage hike that gives 2–3 years of advance notice, wage boards can revise rates with little warning, leading small operators to cut hours, freeze hiring, or automate roles preemptively to avoid being caught off guard by a sudden rate hike. For a five-employee retail shop operating on 3% profit margins, a surprise board-mandated wage increase can wipe out monthly earnings, leading to immediate layoffs long before the new rate even takes effect.
[C1.4] Third, PRO’s claim that sectoral boards avoid a “one size fits all” problem is backwards: a national minimum wage can be calibrated to the lowest-cost labor markets, while state-level boards are often dominated by urban, higher-cost interests that ignore rural realities.
