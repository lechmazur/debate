[P1.1] Elder care is not a commodity to be financialized; it is a duty of care owed to society's most vulnerable members. When private-equity firms acquire nursing homes, they do not merely invest—they extract. They load facilities with high-yield debt, sell the underlying real estate to affiliated entities through sale-leaseback arrangements, and slash operational budgets to service that debt. The result is entirely predictable: skeleton staffing levels, thinner meal programs, delayed medical attention, and a measurable deterioration in resident health outcomes that shows up in hospitalization rates and inspection scores.
[P1.2] Private equity operates on a fundamentally shorter timeline than elder care requires. A fund typically seeks returns within three to seven years, while a nursing home resident may live there for a decade or more. This structural mismatch creates an incentive system that actively punishes quality. To hit quarterly financial targets, managers cut certified nursing assistants, replace registered nurses with lower-paid aides, and defer building maintenance.
[P1.3] Residents cannot simply shop for a better facility; many are Medicaid-dependent, cognitively impaired, or physically unable to relocate. The market cannot discipline this behavior because the consumers lack power, information, and meaningful exit options.
[P1.4] Governments already subsidize this industry heavily through Medicare and Medicaid payments, meaning taxpayers effectively fund the very facilities that private equity strips for profit. Allowing financial firms to treat elder-care beds as assets to be leveraged, recapitalized, and liquidated represents a profound failure of public stewardship. Restricting private-equity ownership—whether through licensing caps, mandatory transparency of beneficial ownership, or prohibitions on certain debt-heavy structures—recognizes that some essential services must be insulated from the logic of short-term financial engineering. We impose strict safeguards on who may operate emergency rooms and hospitals; we should extend that same protective logic to the places where our parents and grandparents spend their final years.
