[P1.1] The central question in this debate is simple: who should pay when shared e-scooters and e-bikes injure pedestrians on sidewalks and in restricted zones? The answer must be the companies that profit from putting these vehicles on the street, not the victims and not the riders who are often judgment-proof, uninsured, and impossible to track down after the crash.
[P1.2] These operators are not passive bystanders to sidewalk riding. They design the product, choose where to deploy it, set the price, and run the app that unlocks the device. They also control the technology that already exists to prevent the exact harm at issue: geofenced slow zones, mandatory sidewalk-detection speed caps, parking compliance enforcement, and identity-verified accounts. When a company chooses not to use these tools, it has effectively chosen to externalize the cost of its sidewalk riding onto whoever happens to be walking there.
[P1.3] A pedestrian struck by a scooter does not care about the rider's insurance status. She cares about her hospital bill, her lost wages, and whether someone with real assets will be accountable. Shared micromobility companies are well-capitalized, easily identified, and uniquely positioned to internalize safety costs. That is exactly the party the law should reach.
[P1.4] The opposition will likely argue this would stifle innovation or that the rider, not the provider, should bear fault. But that framing ignores how these businesses actually work. The rider is a customer using a product the company designed and deployed for profit. Holding the company responsible does not absolve the rider; it adds the party best able to prevent the harm in the first place.
[P1.5] The proposition is a question of who can stop the next sidewalk collision. The companies can. The law should make them.
