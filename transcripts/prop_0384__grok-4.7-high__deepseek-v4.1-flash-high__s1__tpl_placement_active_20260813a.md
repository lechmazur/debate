[P1.1] Public schools serve students of every faith and of none. The fair rule is not a ban and not a mandate, but permission for on-campus religious instruction only when participation is clearly voluntary and comparable secular alternatives exist. That protects conscience without treating religion as uniquely radioactive or uniquely favored.
[P1.2] Equal access is the core. Families often want instruction in their own tradition. Off-campus programs turn that desire into a logistics test: a parent who can leave work, a ride, a safe route. On-campus options remove that tax, which otherwise falls hardest on students with fewer resources.
[P1.3] Requiring a real secular counterpart—ethics discussion, philosophy circle, civic seminar, or similar enrichment—means the school offers a menu, not a sermon. One student can study scripture; another can examine moral reasoning without religion. Neither choice is the default, and neither student is shut out.
[P1.4] Voluntariness is the second lock. Clear opt-in, no credit that pressures attendance, no penalty for declining, and a schedule that does not make refusal costly keep the program from becoming coercion in softer clothes. The school hosts space. It does not preach, grade belief, or treat showing up as a loyalty test.
[P1.5] Neutrality means neither establishing a faith nor discriminating against students who want religious learning on the same guarded terms others want secular learning.
[P1.6] Blanket exclusion is not cleaner. It tells religious students their formation must happen elsewhere or not at all, while secular enrichment may occupy the building. That is a viewpoint burden, not separation. Schools already run voluntary activities with unequal appeal—sports, arts, language clubs—without converting the institution into a church or a clubhouse for one interest.
[P1.7] Religious instruction, held to strict voluntariness and paired with a genuine secular alternative, follows the same principle. The proposition does not put doctrine into required classes. It refuses to make voluntary religious learning the one enrichment that cannot be offered on equal, limited terms.

[C1.1] The proposition presents itself as a compromise, but it builds a chapel inside the public school and calls the door optional. Compulsory attendance is the backdrop. When a school sets aside time, space, and staff attention for devotional instruction, it does not merely permit religion; it sponsors it. An opt-in form cannot erase that institutional endorsement, especially for young children who read adult arrangements as approval.
[C1.2] The “comparable secular alternative” condition is not a safeguard; it is an invitation to entanglement. Who decides whether a philosophy circle is comparable to catechesis, or an ethics seminar to scripture study? The school must evaluate religious claims, compare worldviews, and rank their secular counterparts. That is precisely the doctrinal arbitration public schools should avoid.
[C1.3] And it cannot be done neutrally: every excluded tradition will reasonably ask why its instruction lacks a comparable slot, while every included group will treat the schedule as a school-sanctioned privilege.
[C1.4] Voluntariness is equally unstable in a captive setting. Children face peer pressure, teacher encouragement, hallway stigma, and the ordinary fear of being the one who leaves. A schedule that pulls religious students out while others stay can make refusal socially costly even with no formal penalty. The proposition says “clearly voluntary,” but clarity on paper is not freedom in practice.
[C1.5] Nor does the sports-club analogy hold. Sports do not claim ultimate truth or divide students by conscience. Devotional instruction does. Once a school hosts it, every doctrinal dispute—inclusion, proselytizing, gender, sexuality, salvation—becomes a school administrative problem.
[C1.6] The school becomes referee among faiths.
[C1.7] The clean line is not hostility. Public schools can teach about religion objectively, and students can attend privately organized instruction off campus. What they should not do is turn the school itself into a platform for sectarian formation, then rely on consent forms and a supposedly comparable alternative to sanitize the entanglement. That is not neutrality.
[C1.8] It is establishment with paperwork.

[C2.5] Neither is acceptable.

[C2.8] CON's line is not hostility. Schools can teach about religion objectively, and private instruction off campus remains protected. The answer to unequal logistics is not to convert public classrooms into sectarian platforms. The proposition is not neutrality with guardrails; it is state-hosted religion with a permission slip.
