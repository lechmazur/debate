[P1.1] A hotel that keeps its guests quiet has a front desk, a night manager, an occupancy limit, and a license it can lose. A party house on a residential cul-de-sac has none of that — just a phone number that rings in another state while twenty-five bachelor-party guests are screaming in the pool at 2am. The whole motion comes down to one question: who should absorb that cost, the business earning the revenue or the retired couple next door who never signed up for it?
[P1.2] Nuisance rules answer that correctly. This isn't exotic regulation; it's the oldest principle in property law — your right to use your land stops where it destroys your neighbor's ability to use theirs. An owner who says "my income requires that you not sleep" is not asserting a property right. He's asserting a right to socialize his costs.
[P1.3] And notice how lopsided that trade is. The tourism benefit is diffuse and largely captured by the owner and a few downstream businesses. The harm is concentrated and involuntary: chronic sleep disruption, which is a genuine health issue, plus trash, parking, and the slow conversion of a street where people knew each other into a rotating cast of strangers. Diffuse gains never justify concentrated, uncompensated losses imposed on people with no exit.
[P1.4] The "tourism depends on flexibility" argument also proves far too little. The overwhelming majority of hosts don't run party houses. Enforcement targets a small tail of bad actors — occupancy violations, repeat noise complaints, absentee owners who won't answer the phone — and that tail is precisely what generates the political backlash that produces outright bans. Strong nuisance rules are what save flexible hosting from itself.
[P1.5] The choice is not enforcement versus a thriving market; it's enforcement now, or a citywide prohibition after the third viral video of a shooting at a rented mansion.
[P1.6] Cities that refuse to enforce aren't protecting tourism. They're taxing residents' sleep to subsidize it, without ever asking their consent.

[C1.3] That is the central flaw. "Party house" and "short-term rental" are not the same thing. PRO even calls the offenders a "small tail" [P1.4]. If that is true, then stronger blanket nuisance rules are by definition aimed at many operators who cause no nuisance.
[C1.4] A family renting a basement during a local festival, a retiree renting a lake cabin in high season, a host renting a spare room during a conference—all get swept into new inspections, lower occupancy caps, mandatory local contacts, and potential fines. [C1.5] The tourism argument is not a mere excuse from owners. Flexible hosting lets visitor spending reach neighborhoods and small towns that lack enough hotels. It helps residents cover mortgages and property taxes, and it adds supply when seasonal demand peaks. Weakening that flexibility with stronger rules predictably raises prices, reduces bookings, and pushes tourism back toward concentrated hotel districts.
[C1.6] The motion treats that trade as acceptable because it overstates the harm and understates the benefit.
[C1.7] We should not write policy around a viral horror story. If a mansion becomes a party venue, use existing nuisance law, noise enforcement, and rental-platform delisting. That approach punishes the actual bad actor without taxing every responsible host and without asking a block of families to bear the cost of a blunt rule. The motion asks cities to choose stronger restrictions over smarter enforcement; that is exactly the wrong choice.
