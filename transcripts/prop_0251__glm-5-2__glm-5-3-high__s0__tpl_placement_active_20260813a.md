[P1.1] Extreme heat kills workers. Not in dramatic accidents that grab headlines, but quietly—roofers collapsing on hot shingles, warehouse workers dying in uncooled buildings, agricultural laborers found at the end of rows they were never supposed to finish. The question isn't whether extreme heat is dangerous. The question is who bears the cost when temperatures soar and nothing forces the work to stop.
[P1.2] That burden falls almost entirely on workers with the least power to refuse it. A day laborer doesn't negotiate heat protocols. A warehouse picker doesn't set the thermostat. When the choice is between working in dangerous conditions or losing a shift's pay—or the job—the "choice" isn't real.
[P1.3] That asymmetry is precisely what labor-rights law exists to correct. We don't tell coal miners to individually negotiate their own air quality. We don't say construction workers should just walk off if scaffolding seems unsafe. We mandate protections because the power imbalance makes voluntary safety a fiction.
[P1.4] Extreme heat belongs in that same category. The threat is invisible until someone is dead. Warning signs are easy to override with productivity pressure. The workers most exposed have the least bargaining power.
[P1.5] Voluntary guidelines let employers prioritize output and treat heat illness as an individual failure—drink more water, tough it out. Mandatory stop-work protections flip that logic. When conditions cross a danger threshold, the decision stops being the worker's problem to solve under duress. The work stops.
[P1.6] This isn't speculative. Jurisdictions with mandatory heat protections have seen fewer heat-related workplace deaths and illnesses. Those relying on voluntary compliance have not. The mechanism is straightforward: mandatory rules work because they remove the coercive pressure that keeps people working past safety.
[P1.7] The opposition will call this regulatory overreach. But the cost of a stop-work rule is lost productivity on the hottest days. The cost of not having one is dead workers. Labor rights exist precisely to prevent that trade.

[C1.2] Heat risk is not a function of the thermometer alone. A humidity-adjusted 95 degrees kills; a dry 105 with shade, water, and acclimatized workers may be manageable. A rule that says "at temperature X, work stops" treats a roofing crew in Phoenix and a mail carrier in Miami as the same problem.
[C1.3] They are not. That is why serious heat-safety practice runs on acclimatization schedules, rest-work cycles, shade, water, and buddy monitoring—measures that reduce risk continuously, at every temperature, instead of waiting for a line to be crossed and then pulling a lever.
[C1.4] Second, the lever has a recoil. Stop-work rules fall hardest on exactly the workers PRO claims to champion. Hourly and piece-rate workers lose pay when work stops; a mandatory stop-order with no pay guarantee converts a safety rule into a wage cut. And when legal work becomes economically irrational on the hottest days, work doesn't vanish—it migrates into cash jobs, off-the-books crews, and rushed night work, all with less protection than the regulated workplace had.
[C1.5] The rule doesn't remove the coercion of "work or lose pay." It sharpens it.
[C1.6] Third, the framing is a false binary: dead workers versus lost productivity. Most heat deaths trace to failures in those basics, not to the absence of a stop-work trigger.
[C1.7] PRO says the choice under heat isn't real. True—but the answer is to fix the power imbalance with enforceable duties on employers, not a trigger that treats weather as the sole variable and makes workers pay the price when it's pulled.
