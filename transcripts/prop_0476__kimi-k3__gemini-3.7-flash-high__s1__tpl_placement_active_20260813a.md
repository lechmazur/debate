[P1.1] A dead satellite in low orbit is a multi-ton projectile at eight kilometers a second. When two spacecraft collided over Siberia in 2009, they shattered into thousands of fragments, each a lasting hazard to working satellites. That is the core market failure: debris is an externality. The operator banks the revenue; the collision risk lands on everyone else sharing the shell — and mega-constellations are multiplying it.
[P1.2] Existing fixes fail precisely when needed. Liability after the fact assumes a solvent defendant — yet satellites outlive the companies that launch them, and a dead spacecraft owned by a bankrupt shell cannot be fined. Design mandates cannot guarantee a fifteen-year-old thruster fires on command. Regulators demand deorbit plans; they lack financial assurance.
[P1.3] A plan is a promise. A bond is money.
[P1.4] Deorbit bonds fix the incentive at the only moment it can be fixed: before launch. Nor is this an experiment. Governments already require reclamation bonds from miners and plugging bonds from drillers for precisely this reason — abandonment costs fall on the public unless cleanup money is escrowed in advance. Orbit deserves the same treatment as the mine and the well.
[P1.5] Three features make bonds better than taxes, fees, or mandates. They are refundable: the operator who retires its spacecraft pays nothing, so the policy prices failure rather than punishing activity. They scale with risk — mass, altitude, constellation size. And they survive insolvency: the cleanup fund exists even when the operator vanishes, exactly when taxpayers would otherwise pay for removal or absorb the collision.
[P1.6] The burden is modest: surety markets can underwrite these bonds for a premium small next to launch costs, leaving responsible startups untouched while speculative operators lose the free option of abandoning hardware.
[P1.7] The clash is one question: when a profitable satellite dies in orbit, who holds the risk — the operator who was paid, or the public and every other user of the sky? Bonds put the risk where the profit went.

[C1.2] First, orbital abandonment is almost never an economic calculation; it is an engineering failure. A mining company abandons a quarry to save cash. A satellite becomes stranded because a propulsion valve jammed, space weather fried the avionics, or a micrometeorite severed communications. Forfeiting a bond cannot reignite dead thrusters or alter orbital mechanics.
[C1.3] Penalizing operators for unpreventable hardware anomalies does not clear the orbital shell—it merely taxes spaceflight while leaving the dangerous projectile in orbit.
[C1.4] Second, bonds trigger dangerous jurisdictional arbitrage. Orbit has no borders, but bond mandates are strictly national. If responsible spacefaring nations tie up vast capital reserves for every spacecraft in a mega-constellation, commercial operators will simply relocate, register, and launch from permissive foreign jurisdictions with zero oversight. Far from securing the orbital commons, deorbit bonds create orbital "flags of convenience," actively driving space commerce toward lower global safety standards.
[C1.5] Third, bonds offer the dangerous illusion of remediation. PRO claims forfeited funds will clean up dead satellites [P1.5]. Yet active debris removal—rendezvous, grapple, and deorbiting of uncooperative tumbling objects—remains technologically nascent and astronomically expensive. A bond sized to fund actual mechanical retrieval would bankrupt commercial spaceflight; a bond sized to be affordable simply collects government revenue while the collision hazard circles Earth for centuries.
[C1.6] Real orbital stewardship comes from binding technical standards: strict five-year disposal rules, mandatory component passivation to prevent explosions, and redundant deorbit systems. We need reliable engineering that prevents orbital decay failures before launch, not financialized penalties that abandon the sky once the money is paid.
