[P1.1] Single-family-only zoning is not a neutral land-use category. It is a legal command that on this parcel, it shall be illegal to build a duplex, a triplex, a cottage court, or a small apartment for a teacher, a nurse, or a grandparent. That command is the engine of the modern housing shortage, and density bonuses do not turn it off. They negotiate around it.
[P1.2] The case for an outright ban on *new* exclusively single-family districts rests on three points.
[P1.3] First, incrementalism has been tested and it loses. Cities have spent decades layering ADU allowances, lot-size tweaks, and bonus programs on top of exclusionary base zoning. The base zoning wins. Builders face discretionary review, neighbor appeals, and parking minimums that make the "bonus" pencil out only for luxury infill.
[P1.4] If the default remains "one house, one lot," the default is what gets built. Banning the category forces the baseline itself to be inclusive.
[P1.5] Second, exclusive single-family zoning imposes costs on people who never get a vote in the hearing room: the renter priced out to the exurbs, the worker commuting two hours, the child whose school district is closed to her family's income. [P1.6] Third, the proposition is narrow and reasonable. It bans *new* single-family-*only* districts. Existing neighborhoods keep their houses. Anyone who wants to build a single house on a single lot still can.
[P1.7] What disappears is the legal weapon used to forbid neighbors from building anything else. Minneapolis and Oregon moved in this direction without bulldozers rolling down cul-de-sacs.
[P1.8] The clash is simple. CON must defend why a city should retain the power to make small homes, duplexes, and modest apartments categorically illegal on large swaths of its land. That power is the problem. Removing it is the reform.
