[P1.1] A supermarket that bins edible bread, yogurt, or vegetables is not simply losing inventory. It is wasting the land, water, energy, transport, and labor already embedded in that food, while someone nearby may be skipping meals or struggling with grocery bills. Treating that outcome as an ordinary overhead does not make it harmless; it merely makes it routine.
[P1.2] The requirement should be understood practically: food that is safe and edible must go to donation where a reasonable recipient and collection route exist, or be sharply discounted before its disposal window closes. Genuinely unsafe food remains disposable. This is not an order to donate every dented tin, guarantee that charities accept every delivery, or keep shelves empty. It changes the default from “throw away unless inconvenient” to “recover value unless there is a sound reason not to.”
[P1.3] First, disposal privatizes convenience and socializes costs. Supermarkets benefit from abundant displays, cautious date management, and rejection of cosmetically imperfect produce, but landfill burdens, emissions, and wasted resources extend beyond their balance sheets. A business should not be free to choose the cheapest internal option when the public absorbs part of its real cost. The rule corrects that incentive and encourages better ordering, earlier markdowns, and donation partnerships rather than merely managing the bin more efficiently.
[P1.4] Second, the alternatives are useful, not symbolic. Donation can supply community organizations; sharp discounts let shoppers buy food they would otherwise forgo while allowing the store to recover some revenue. A crate of bananas marked down this afternoon creates more value than the same crate collected as refuse tomorrow. If protecting full-price sales is the objection, that is precisely the perverse incentive the motion rejects: edible food should not be destroyed to preserve pricing convenience.
[P1.5] Third, large supermarkets are the appropriate duty-bearers. They already run sophisticated inventory systems, scheduled markdowns, waste contracts, and distribution networks. Compliance can use phased deadlines, food-safety exemptions, reasonable-effort standards, and recognition of places where collection is impracticable.

[C1.1] The motion's seductive because the villain is easy to picture: a skip full of bread behind a gleaming store. But a legal mandate to donate or steeply discount is a blunt instrument aimed at the tail of the problem, and it creates serious collateral damage along the way.
[C1.2] First, most retail waste isn't callousness—it's food safety and demand uncertainty. Stores already discount aggressively because marked-down stock recovers revenue; nobody burns money for fun. The residual waste is largely goods past safe dates, damaged packaging, or surplus nobody would take. Mandating donation of that fraction means either shipping charities food they must then screen and dispose of themselves, or pressuring stores to hand over items of marginal safety.
[C1.3] Food-borne illness from donated goods falls on the charity and the recipient, not the supermarket. That's not socializing costs; it's relocating them onto the most vulnerable.
[C1.4] Second, the mandate attacks the symptom while entrenching the disease. If donation becomes a compliance box, ordering discipline weakens—overstock now has a guaranteed moral outlet. The real lever on waste is upstream forecasting, and a disposal-side rule dulls it.
[C1.5] Third, forced deep discounts distort retail economics. Predictable, mandated markdowns train customers to wait, cannibalizing full-price sales. The predictable response is smaller orders and thinner displays, less choice, and higher base prices. Shoppers who don't live near the store—or can't arrive at markdown hour—pay for the policy.
[C1.6] And in low-income areas where large stores are thin on the ground, the compliance cost falls hardest on the format the motion targets.
[C1.7] Fourth, charities aren't free disposal infrastructure. Cold chains, sorting, and volunteer time cost money. A mandate that arrives with no funding turns goodwill into unfunded logistics, and unsuitable donations into a second waste stream.
[C1.8] None of this argues for celebrating waste. It argues that hard legal compulsion is the wrong tool: it misallocates risk, weakens ordering incentives, and burdens shoppers and charities. Voluntary recovery, tax treatment, and streamlining liability protections for donors achieve the aim without the coercion. The motion as written should be rejected.

[P4.2] A2: The supermarket funds the reasonable recovery arrangements on which it relies, using the logistics and compliance resources that make large chains appropriate duty-bearers. Charities are not compelled to accept loads, screen unsafe food, or finance a store’s legal obligations. Unsuitable food cannot be laundered into a “donation” merely to create paperwork. Where donation is not workable, sharp discounting remains available; where neither route is reasonably practicable, disposal is permitted.

[P4.4] This exposes the false dilemma at the center of CON’s case: the rule is supposedly either toothless because it recognizes practical limits, or dangerous because it has teeth. [P4.5] CON’s “negative-value cases” argument also assumes the very threshold this motion rejects. Recovery need not maximize the store’s private profit to be worthwhile. A sharply discounted crate of food returns some revenue and puts food on someone’s table; disposal returns nothing edible and still consumes disposal capacity. Because donation and markdowns require effort, stores retain an incentive to order better rather than treat recovery as a free overstock outlet.
[P4.6] Nor does voluntarism answer CON’s own liability objection: voluntary donations involve the same handling risks. It merely preserves the option to recover nothing.
