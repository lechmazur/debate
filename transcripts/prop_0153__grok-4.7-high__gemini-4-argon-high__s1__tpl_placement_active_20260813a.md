[P1.2] admits that without payment, the pool of willing carriers shrinks dramatically. That concession gives the game away. Compensation is not a neutral thank-you; it is the financial inducement required to get women to accept nine months of severe physical risk they would otherwise refuse.
[C1.2] If regulators truly screened out everyone motivated by economic need [P1.4], the commercial supply would vanish. Wealthy intended parents never hire wealthier surrogates; the trade always runs down the steep slope of class.
[C1.3] Nor can pregnancy be governed by "ordinary tools of labor" [P1.5]. A typical worker can walk off a job site at five o'clock or quit mid-contract. A pregnant woman cannot clock out of gestation, and there is no clean "exit" [P1.3] at month six without severe medical and emotional trauma. Worse, [P1.4] promises both that the surrogate keeps control over her body and that contracts will secure the transaction.
[C1.4] What happens when those promises collide—when intended parents demand a selective reduction of twins, an abortion after a prenatal diagnosis, or a cesarean section the surrogate refuses? Either the state enforces the contract over her body, or it exposes the legal guarantees as hollow whenever a crisis hits.
[C1.5] Finally, domestic legalization will not end cross-border workarounds [P1.6]. Mandating independent lawyers, clinical panels, and comprehensive insurance [P1.4] makes domestic surrogacy prohibitively expensive. Legalization simply legitimizes commercial womb-renting, expanding overall demand, while pricing out ordinary buyers who will still travel to cheaper, laxer jurisdictions abroad. The state can reimburse actual medical expenses in altruistic arrangements, but it should never turn women's bodies into a regulated rental market.
