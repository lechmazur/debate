[P1.1] Automatic enrollment with an opt-out increases long-run household financial security more effectively than larger tax deductions because it directly solves the main failure in retirement saving: not lack of subsidy, but failure to start and stay enrolled.
[P1.2] Most households do not fail to save because the tax price of saving is too high. They fail because enrollment requires attention, paperwork, and an active choice at a moment when daily expenses are pressing. Behavioral inertia means the default is decisive. When workers are automatically enrolled at a modest contribution rate and can opt out, saving becomes the path of least resistance.
[P1.3] The worker still has full freedom to leave, but the burden of action shifts from overcoming inertia to simply maintaining the status quo. That produces large gains in participation, especially among employees who would otherwise postpone the decision indefinitely.
[P1.4] Tax deductions, by contrast, are mainly a reward for people who already intend to save. To benefit, a worker must first choose to contribute, have enough taxable income to value the deduction, and understand the incentive. Higher earners can exploit deductions, but many lower- and middle-income households receive little marginal benefit because they owe little income tax or cannot spare cash until payday. If the problem is nonparticipation, subsidizing contributions after the decision is made misses the target.
[P1.5] Deductions may raise contribution rates among motivated savers, but long-run security depends more on expanding participation than on rewarding those already committed.
[P1.6] Automatic enrollment also builds long-run security through earlier starts and compounding. A worker enrolled at age 25 at even a modest rate accumulates far more than one who starts at 35 after finally deciding to opt in. Combined with automatic escalation, it turns small initial contributions into durable savings habits without requiring repeated decisions.
[P1.7] The better policy is not to reward saving that would have happened anyway, but to make saving the normal starting point for every covered worker.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a change in the default for a change in wealth. Automatic enrollment can raise participation, but long-run household financial security is measured by sustainable retirement balances net of liquidity strain, not by the share of workers with an account.
[C1.2] Many automatic-enrollment defaults are deliberately modest. A worker enrolled at only a few percent of pay and left there for decades accumulates a small supplement, not dependable retirement income. Participation statistics therefore overstate the policy: if the default contribution is low and nothing requires it to rise, millions of households may hold underfunded accounts that look successful in enrollment reports but do little to prevent old-age shortfalls. Compounding helps only if the contribution rate is adequate; starting at 25 with a token rate is not automatically better than starting later with a serious one.
[C1.3] The default also moves money away from other household needs without an individualized judgment. A worker carrying high-interest debt or lacking an emergency reserve may be made worse off by a forced-by-default payroll deduction, especially if later cash-outs or hardship withdrawals trigger taxes and penalties. [C1.4] By contrast, larger deductions for voluntary contributions improve the after-tax return at the moment a household decides whether to save more. They can reach self-employed savers, older workers increasing contributions, and employees who lack access to an automatic plan. They reward additional saving instead of relabeling a small payroll deposit as success.
[C1.5] PRO’s strongest example depends on automatic escalation, which is not the policy being debated. Enrollment alone changes the starting point; it does not ensure an adequate contribution rate. The side that produces stagnant balances, liquidity strain, and avoidable withdrawals has not shown the more effective route to long-run security.
