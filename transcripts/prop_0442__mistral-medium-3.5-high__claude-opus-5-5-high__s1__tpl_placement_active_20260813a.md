[P1.1] International shipping accounts for roughly three percent of global greenhouse gas emissions—more than most countries—and without regulation, this share will grow as global trade expands. The IMO is the only body with the authority and expertise to impose uniform rules across all flags and routes. It has already demonstrated this capacity with the 2020 sulfur cap, which slashed harmful emissions without collapsing trade. Voluntary pledges, by contrast, have consistently underdelivered because individual operators have no incentive to act when competitors can free-ride on their restraint.
[P1.2] Yes, freight costs will rise in the short term. But the social cost of shipping’s current emissions—estimated at hundreds of billions of dollars annually—dwarfs these increases. A single large containership can emit as much CO2 in a year as 70,000 cars, and with over 50,000 commercial vessels globally, the aggregate impact is immense. These climate externalities are not theoretical; they translate directly into economic damage through extreme weather, health costs, and ecosystem collapse.
[P1.3] Moreover, a clear, binding mandate will accelerate the investment cycle for green ammonia, hydrogen, and other zero-carbon fuels, driving down costs through economies of scale and technological learning. This is exactly what happened with solar and wind power: once governments set firm, binding targets, costs plummeted far faster than predicted.
[P1.4] Most importantly, shipping cannot decarbonize through incremental efficiency improvements. The physics of moving heavy cargo across oceans means zero-carbon fuels are essential. Only mandatory deadlines will force the sector to make the capital investments and R&D commitments needed. The IMO’s own strategy already targets net-zero by 2050; converting this into a binding, enforceable timetable is the only way to turn aspiration into action.
[P1.5] Delaying this transition out of fear of short-term costs simply defers the inevitable and makes it more expensive. History shows clear policy signals consistently make clean technologies cheaper faster than expected—shipping will follow this pattern if the IMO leads.

[C1.1] Nobody here disputes that shipping must decarbonize. The question is whether the right tool is a fuel mandate on a fixed IMO clock, enforced "even if freight costs rise." That design fails on three counts.
[C1.2] First, it mandates fuels that do not yet exist at scale. Green ammonia and green methanol are currently produced in tiny quantities relative to what the global fleet burns, and they compete for the same scarce renewable electricity that grids, steel, and fertilizer also need. A timetable cannot conjure supply. When the deadline arrives and green fuel isn't there, operators reach for whatever qualifies as "low-carbon" on paper.
[C1.3] That means LNG, which leaks methane, or crop-based biofuels. We have seen this film before: Europe's biofuel mandates helped drive palm-oil expansion and deforestation. A deadline set ahead of supply tends to produce paperwork compliance and hidden emissions rather than decarbonization.
[C1.4] Second, PRO's own precedents cut against them [P1.1, P1.3]. The 2020 sulfur cap worked because the compliance fuel already existed. Refiners could make low-sulfur oil, and scrubbers were available. Solar got cheap through modular manufacturing, subsidies, and gradual deployment, not through a worldwide order that every user switch by a fixed date or stop operating.
[C1.5] A ship lasts twenty-five years and an ammonia engine is still early-stage technology. If the IMO forces owners to bet their capital on a regulator's chosen fuel, a wrong bet strands whole fleets.
[C1.6] Third, "even if costs rise" is not a neutral clause. The costs fall hardest on the poorest. Small island states and least-developed countries import food, fuel, and medicine by sea and have no rail alternative. A cost-blind mandate is a regressive tax on Tonga and Malawi to fix a problem caused mostly by rich-world consumption.
[C1.7] The better path is price-and-performance, not prescription. That means a carbon levy whose revenue is recycled to vulnerable nations and to fuel R&D, plus technology-neutral efficiency standards. It also means rewarding measures available today: slower steaming, wind-assist sails, hull optimization, and route efficiency. These cut emissions now while the fuel market matures.
