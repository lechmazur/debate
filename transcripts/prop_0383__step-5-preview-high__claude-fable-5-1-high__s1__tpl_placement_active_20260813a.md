[P1.1] The proposition targets the *most wasteful* packaging—not every wrapper, lid, or cup. The qualifier matters: we are not debating whether to eliminate all takeaway containers, but whether the single worst performers deserve protected status. Think of expanded polystyrene clamshells used for a fifteen-minute meal and discarded for centuries, or plastic cutlery, straws, and sauce packets that outlive the person who used them. These are not neutral conveniences; they are subsidies for pollution, paid by everyone except the consumer holding the takeaway bag.
[P1.2] The core clash is temporal. Inconvenience is a one-time transition cost borne by consumers and small businesses, while packaging waste is a recurring harm accumulated across millions of meals and decades. When we phase out the worst offenders, we do not ban food—we force innovation in materials and delivery systems. Reusable container programs, compostable fiber bowls, and deposit-return schemes already exist in cities around the world; regulation merely accelerates their adoption by aligning price with true cost.
[P1.3] A graduated timeline gives vendors room to adapt, but the direction is clear.
[P1.4] CON will emphasize transition costs. But those costs are visible, finite, and shared, whereas the environmental toll is diffuse and compounding. Every jurisdiction that delays pays the same price later with dirtier infrastructure, higher cleanup bills, and fewer alternatives already tested. Meanwhile, businesses that adapt early gain competitive advantage in a market already shifting toward sustainability, attracting customers who prefer vendors with visible environmental commitments.
[P1.5] The asymmetry is decisive: a world with slightly less convenient takeaway is still fully livable. A world drowning in non-degradable packaging, clogged waterways, and overflowing landfills is not. Refusing to act is not neutrality; it is a choice to keep externalizing harm onto future generations and vulnerable communities. We should act where the burden is trivial and the damage is catastrophic.
[P1.6] That is the mandate of the proposition, and it is sound.

[C1.1] Everyone agrees polystyrene clamshells floating in a river are ugly. The question is whether a government ban on a category of packaging actually produces less harm, and the honest answer is: usually it just produces different harm, paid for by people with the least room to absorb it.
[C1.2] Start with the substitution problem. A "phase out" doesn't make the sandwich disappear; it moves it into whatever the vendor can afford next. Foam clamshells get replaced by plastic-lined paper that is heavier to ship, hard to recycle, and still ends up in the same bin. "Compostable" fiber bowls routinely go to landfill because almost no city has the industrial composting to process them, and some rely on chemical grease barriers nobody wants near food.
[C1.3] Reusable containers only beat single-use after dozens of cycles, each involving hot water, detergent, and a delivery van. PRO promises innovation; what bans reliably deliver is the cheapest compliant alternative, not the cleanest one.
[C1.4] Second, the harm PRO describes is a collection problem, not a packaging problem. Litter and clogged waterways come from containers escaping the waste stream. Bins, deposit schemes, and enforcement address that directly and cheaply. Banning materials addresses it indirectly and expensively, while leaving the escaped paper cup to join the escaped foam one.
[C1.5] Third, "some inconvenience and transition costs" is a phrase written by people who won't bear them. Picture a family-run noodle stall on thin margins: compliant containers cost several times more, leak, and ruin the food in transit. That stall either eats the cost, raises prices on customers who chose takeaway because it was cheap, or closes. Chains with procurement departments adapt fine.
[C1.6] The motion's "trivial burden" is a regressive tax on the smallest operators, and PRO's "early adapters gain advantage" quietly admits the losers are the little ones.
[C1.7] Finally, "most wasteful" is not a stable category. Once governments start ranking packaging, the list grows, and the costs recur with every revision. Fix collection, price the externality, and stop pretending bans are free.
