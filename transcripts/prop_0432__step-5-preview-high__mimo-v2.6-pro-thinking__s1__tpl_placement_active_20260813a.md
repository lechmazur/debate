[P1.1] The motion asks whether the default should be exclusion or permission backed by a priced risk. PRO’s answer is permission, because blanket pet bans solve a manageable problem with a blunt instrument. A ban does not ask whether a tenant has a well-trained older dog or an untrained puppy; it treats every animal as a presumption of damage and every owner as a liability. That is not careful stewardship of property; it is risk avoidance transferred to tenants and to animals that could live safely in apartments.
[P1.2] Standardized deposits or damage insurance change the calculus without pretending damage never occurs. If a unit is scratched, stained, or subject to odor, the landlord can document the cost and recover it through a predictable mechanism rather than through litigation or an oversized deposit. Insurance spreads the rare expensive incident across many pet-owning households; deposits make the individual owner internalize ordinary wear. The landlord’s core interests—repair cost, rentability, neighbor complaints—are still protected, but through compensation and lease enforcement rather than ex ante exclusion.
[P1.3] Defaults matter. When pet ownership is prohibited by default, tenants hide animals, skip veterinary care, or forgo suitable housing altogether. Permission under a standardized regime creates transparency: owners disclose animals, accept conditions, and carry coverage. It also reduces the hidden, unsupervised animals that increase real risk.
[P1.4] CON may point to severe cases. But severe cases do not require a categorical ban; they require actual enforcement against violations. Society already manages riskier activities through insurance and conditions rather than universal prohibition. Pets belong in that category.
[P1.5] The decisive comparison: PRO asks landlords to absorb less uncertainty by pricing it. CON asks all tenants and pets to be excluded because some may cause damage. That is not proportionate.

[C1.1] The motion rests on a category error: it treats pet damage as a purely monetary, landlord-facing risk that can be neatly priced and prepaid. It isn't. Apartment buildings are dense, shared environments where the largest harms from pets fall on third parties—neighbors—not on the leaseholder's wallet. A deposit does not compensate the family next door for barking at 2 a.m.
[C1.2] Damage insurance does not restore sleep to a shift worker, does not remove allergens from shared hallways and laundry rooms, and does not un-bite a child in a stairwell. PRO's mechanism prices scratches and stains while leaving the actual externality untouched. That alone defeats the claim that insurance or deposits substitute for discretion.
[C1.3] Second, "default to allowing" is not neutral. It is a legal and practical entitlement that flips the burden onto landlords and, critically, onto neighboring tenants. Today a landlord who wants a quiet, low-allergen building can say no once. Under PRO's rule, the same landlord must accept the pet, wait for the damage or nuisance to occur, document causation, dispute wear versus damage, and chase a claim—often with the noisy or aggressive animal still in the building.
[C1.4] PRO waves at "actual enforcement against violations" [P1.4], but eviction is slow, expensive, and uncertain, and courts and housing rules are rightly reluctant to remove tenants. A remedy that arrives after eighteen months of barking is not a remedy.
[C1.5] Third, standardized deposits systematically underprice tail risk. Deposits are capped and shared across a tenancy; a single severe event—flooding from a chewed pipe, extensive subfloor odor remediation, liability after a bite—can exceed them, and causation disputes favor whoever has already moved out. Insurance, meanwhile, is not free: if premiums actually reflected this risk, PRO's "priced risk" would price many tenants right back out—while insurers exclude breeds, conditions, and gradual damage anyway. A pool that excludes the bad cases isn't coverage; it's a certificate that lets the tail land on the building.
[C1.6] Screening is not discrimination against animals; it is how landlords manage a non-diversifiable, third-party burden. Keep the default where it belongs.
