[P1.1] Every year, tens of thousands of people on dialysis die waiting for a kidney that altruism never delivered. That is not a hypothetical harm. It is a queue with names in it, and the policy that keeps it long is the one we currently call ethical.
[P1.2] The decisive comparison in this debate is not compensation versus a world of pure generous giving. Pure altruism was tried. It has been the global default for fifty years, and it has never once cleared the waiting list in any country. The real choice is between regulated, transparent compensation and what we have now: mass premature death plus a thriving black market in which brokers fly desperate sellers to back-alley clinics, pay them a fraction of what was promised, and abandon them with no follow-up care.
[P1.3] Prohibition did not abolish the organ trade. It privatized it, criminalized the poorest party in it, and stripped away every safeguard.
[P1.4] What "strict regulation" means matters, so let me commit to it. A single public agency as sole buyer — no private bidding, no rich patients jumping the line. Organs allocated by the existing medical criteria, so recipients get access by need, not wealth. Standardized compensation set by law, not haggled: a fixed benefit package, plus lifetime health coverage and follow-up care for the donor, which is more protection than altruistic donors get today.
[P1.5] Independent medical and psychological screening, a mandatory cooling-off period, and criminal penalties for brokers that survive intact.
[P1.6] That structure answers the exploitation worry rather than dodging it, because exploitation is not caused by money changing hands — we already pay plasma donors, surrogates, and clinical trial participants — it is caused by unequal information, coercion, and no recourse. Regulation supplies all three.
[P1.7] And note what the current ban actually does to the poor person who wants to sell. It does not make him richer. It just tells him that his poverty is so degrading that the state must protect its own conscience by refusing him the choice, while the patient beside him dies.
