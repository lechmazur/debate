[P1.1] “Decades away” is the case for public money, not against it. No private investor funds a thirty-year program whose main output is knowledge competitors can copy. That is a public good, and it is why the field's landmarks — JET's plasma records, the ignition shot at the National Ignition Facility — came from publicly funded labs, with startups building on that science base rather than replacing it. Step back and the option is not purchased more cheaply later; it is forfeited, along with magnet engineers, tritium chemists and materials scientists who take a decade to train and cannot be reassembled by press release.
[P1.2] The premise of stalemate is also outdated. High-temperature superconducting magnets have shrunk the machines required for a given performance, and a laboratory plasma has now returned more fusion energy than the driver delivered to it. Those are changes in the cost curve, not slogans.
[P1.3] Judge this by the right test. Not “when does the first kilowatt-hour arrive,” but whether a small premium buys a large possibility. Global public fusion spending is a rounding error beside annual fossil fuel subsidies; cancelling it would not fund a meaningful fraction of the wind and storage we need. It would only close a door.
[P1.4] And that door matters. Grids running mostly on wind and solar still need firm clean power for the windless, sunless weeks of winter — the hardest, most expensive slice of decarbonization. Fusion is one of very few physically credible candidates: abundant fuel, no chain reaction, no weather dependency.
[P1.5] We are not betting the grid on fusion. We are refusing to sell the option cheaply because the payoff date is uncertain — which is exactly when options are cheap, and exactly when abandoning them is irreversible.
