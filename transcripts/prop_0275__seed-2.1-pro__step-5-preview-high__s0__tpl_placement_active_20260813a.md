[P1.1] Drip pricing on vacation rentals is no accidental design flaw—it is a deliberate strategy to lure users in with artificially low advertised rates, then pile on hidden costs and restrictive terms only after they have invested time filtering dates, scrolling photos, reading reviews, and narrowing options to the final checkout step, wasting hours of their time even if they ultimately walk away from the surprise terms. Right now, a traveler might see a $90/night lake house top their search results, only to reach the checkout screen and find a $180 mandatory cleaning fee, a $35/night resort fee for amenities they never intended to use, a strict no-visitor policy that rules out their planned family gathering, and a house rule imposing a $250 fine for early checkout—terms that would have ruled the property out immediately if visible upfront.
[P1.2] Requiring platforms to display these fees and core house rules before a user clicks through to book or checkout does not dictate what hosts can charge, or what rules they can set. It simply eliminates the bait-and-switch dynamic that currently lets hosts and platforms win clicks by hiding the true cost and terms of a stay. When users can compare total, all-in price and core rules side-by-side before taking that step to book, competition works as it should: hosts cannot compete on fake low base rates, and are pushed to keep junk fees reasonable instead of padding profits on customers who feel too locked in by the time they see the fine print to walk away.
[P1.3] This is not heavy-handed regulation. It is basic, table-stakes consumer protection that aligns platform design with honest, fair competition, instead of rewarding the most deceptive listings with the most user traffic and bookings.

Hosts who bundle everything into a higher room rate do not make travel cheaper; they simply hide the cost of cleaning inside a number that looks larger, while removing the flexibility that lets a family staying three nights pay a fraction of what a couple staying one night pays in cleaning labor.
[C1.2] That unbundling is often progressive: the guest who dirties the least pays the least, while the group that uses the home most pays the real cost of turnover.
[C1.3] Nor is the market silent. Vacation-rental platforms already compete on trust. Reviews, ratings, and host badges punish hosts who spring surprises on guests, while payment systems hold funds until terms are accepted. Existing consumer-protection law already outlaws deceptive advertising.
[C1.4] The problem is not a lack of regulation; it is selective enforcement of fraud.
[C1.5] The real danger is the mandate itself. “Core house rules” is a category no regulator can define. Is a no-party rule core? A quiet-hours rule? A pet restriction?
[C1.6] A ban on outdoor grills? [C1.7] Finally, timing is not deception. Travelers expect a browsing experience that is fast and scannable; they do not want a wall of fine print before they can see photos or compare neighborhoods. If they are misled at checkout, existing law gives them a remedy. If they are merely annoyed by a cleaning fee, that is a preference, not a violation.
[C1.8] Competition, not compulsion, delivers honest listings. Let the market sort the deceptive few from the honest many.
