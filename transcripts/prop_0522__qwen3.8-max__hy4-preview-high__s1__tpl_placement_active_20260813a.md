[P1.1] The central question is not whether climate-displacement law is finished; it is whether people can move safely now. High-income countries should expand climate-linked humanitarian pathways now because the harm is already occurring, the legal gap is dangerous, and early action creates the practice that sound law later needs.
[P1.2] First, urgency. Sea-level rise is already contaminating wells in low-lying islands; repeated cyclones and droughts are erasing homes and livelihoods faster than families can recover. If admission routes exist only after a perfect treaty, protection arrives too late. Humanitarian pathways—temporary protection, disaster visas, planned relocation, and complementary protection assessments—can be scaled incrementally and adjusted as evidence arrives.
[P1.3] Second, waiting rewards the smuggler. When people have no lawful route after a storm surge or saltwater intrusion, they turn to irregular movement, dangerous journeys, and ad hoc border crises. Expanded pathways give governments control: screening, planning, allocation, and dignified entry. That is more orderly than pretending climate displacement will stop at the waterline.
[P1.4] Third, law develops through practice. The refugee system did not emerge fully formed; it matured as states responded to concrete emergencies. Climate-linked visas and temporary protection create administrative expertise, burden-sharing precedents, and trustworthy standards. That settled practice is exactly what future courts and treaty drafters need.
[P1.5] CON may argue that acting early invites legal confusion or unfair burdens. But the alternative is worse: forcing vulnerable people to fit into categories that do not fit, or leaving them without any category at all. Expansion need not mean permanent open borders. It can begin with narrow, reversible, monitored programs for persons facing existential environmental harm, with review mechanisms and cooperation with origin states.
[P1.6] The decisive point is simple: legal certainty is valuable, but human survival is more urgent. High-income countries have the resources, institutions, and moral standing to build safe routes today.

After a cyclone, a family may move because their home was poorly built or relief was blocked; after drought, a herder moves partly because markets and water points failed.
[C1.3] Without settled law, officials must guess where environmental cause ends and poverty or bad luck begins. That is not control; it is arbitrary line-drawing with life-changing consequences.
[C1.4] PRO says early practice will mature into law [P1.4]. It is just as likely to harden into something worse. [C1.7] There is a better urgency. Legal certainty is not a luxury we indulge after survival; it is what makes survival routes legitimate and durable.
