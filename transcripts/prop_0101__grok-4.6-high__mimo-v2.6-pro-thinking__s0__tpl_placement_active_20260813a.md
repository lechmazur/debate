[P1.1] A thirteen-year-old is not a small adult with a phone. The brain systems that weigh delayed consequences are still unfinished, while the systems that chase likes, status, and novelty are already highly sensitive. Social platforms are built around that mismatch. Infinite scroll, streaks, public metrics, and ranking algorithms convert adolescent vulnerability into time on screen.
[P1.2] Parental controls and softer design changes do not rewrite that business model. They ask families to out-engineer a product designed by specialists whose job is to keep the child engaged.
[P1.3] That is why we do not regulate cigarettes, alcohol, or casinos mainly through household software. We set an age floor because the harm is foreseeable, the product is sticky, and access is too easy. Social media meets the same test. Children create accounts without parents noticing, borrow devices, and lie about their age.
[P1.4] Parental skill, time, and authority vary wildly. Gentle redesigns leave the engagement loop intact. A dismissible bedtime reminder is not a ban.
[P1.5] A government prohibition on accounts under sixteen changes the default and solves a coordination problem parental tools cannot. If peers are on the platform, locking down one child either fails or isolates that child. A legal floor means nobody under sixteen is supposed to be there. It also equalizes protection: children of exhausted or less technical parents should not receive a harsher product than children of digitally fluent ones.
[P1.6] The costs are concrete: displaced sleep, public ranking of appearance, conflict that never clocks off, and contact that is a few taps away. Some will still evade a ban, as some drink early. The question is not perfection. It is whether the default should be open access plus optional family software, or a legal floor that treats under-sixteens as off-limits customers.
[P1.7] If sixteen is still childhood for driving and drinking, it is childhood for a machine that monetizes attention and comparison.
