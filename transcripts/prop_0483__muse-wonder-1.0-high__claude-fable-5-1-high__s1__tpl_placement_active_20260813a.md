[P1.1] Standardized IQ-style tests are impressive at one thing: ranking people on short, timed tasks involving pattern completion, vocabulary, mental rotation, and working memory under artificial pressure. That is a real skill, but it is a thin slice of cognition. It does not show who can frame an ill-defined problem, learn from feedback over months, explain an idea to a confused colleague, spot a flawed assumption in messy data, generate an original solution, or persist when the first approach fails.
[P1.2] Yet we let that slice act as a gatekeeper for gifted programs, selective schools, advanced tracks, and jobs. That outsized role would be defensible only if the test captured most of what makes someone able to learn and perform. It does not. Performance also depends on creativity, practical judgment, social understanding, self-regulation, motivation, and domain knowledge built through experience — capacities that are cognitive in any meaningful sense, but largely invisible to an IQ-style format because they unfold over time, in context, with other people.
[P1.3] Defenders point to correlation with grades and job ratings, but correlation is not coverage. These tests partly reward prior schooling, comfort with test conventions, language familiarity, and freedom from anxiety or stereotype pressure, so they can recycle advantage while claiming to measure raw potential. And a predictor that works at group level can still misclassify many individuals: the slow, deep thinker penalized for speed, the inventive thinker tripped by format, the capable applicant from an under-resourced school scored as low ability.
[P1.4] When sorting is high-stakes, those misses compound. Children placed early get richer or poorer curricula, teachers expect more or less, and employers never see the people their screen discarded. Broader tools exist that sample the wider band directly: work-sample tasks, portfolios, structured problem-solving over time, apprenticeships, and supervised trials. An IQ-style score can be one data point among many.
[P1.5] As a dominant filter for opportunity, it mistakes precision on a narrow task for judgment about a whole mind.

[C1.1] The proposition rests on a confusion between what a test measures directly and what it tells you. A thermometer measures one thing. Nobody calls it "too narrow" to justify its role in triage, because its narrow reading predicts a great deal about the whole patient. The test for a gatekeeper is not coverage; it is whether the signal it gives beats the alternatives at acceptable cost.
[C1.2] On that test, IQ-style measures win, and the proposition fails.
[C1.3] Start with "thin slice." Scores on pattern completion, vocabulary, and working memory correlate with each other and with performance on almost every mentally demanding task anyone has bothered to study, including the very things PRO lists: learning from feedback, explaining ideas, catching flawed assumptions. That is the whole point of general ability. It is not a trick on timed puzzles; it is the capacity that makes novel problems tractable, and it shows up in months-long training outcomes, not just on test day.
[C1.4] A measure that forecasts how fast someone acquires a new domain is not narrow in any sense that matters for sorting.
[C1.5] Now the alternatives. PRO offers portfolios, apprenticeships, supervised trials, and holistic judgment. Picture a sorting system built on those. Portfolios reward families who can buy tutors and polish.
[C1.6] Apprenticeships reward connections. Interviews reward charm, height, and accent, and interviewers are notoriously confident and notoriously wrong. The whole reason standardized tests spread was to stop admissions and hiring from running on pedigree. When selective colleges dropped test requirements, several reinstated them after finding the scores had been their best tool for spotting strong students from weak schools; without them, those applicants simply disappeared into the pile.
[C1.7] Finally, "outsized" is backwards. In most workplaces and schools cognitive testing plays a modest role, hedged by interviews, grades, and references that are less valid and more biased. The honest comparison is not test versus whole mind. It is test versus the gut.
[C1.8] The gut loses.

An IQ-style score is a composite performance on school-like, culturally loaded tasks, used as an imperfect proxy for later performance. When that proxy is shaped by preparation, language familiarity, and test comfort, narrowness is not a philosophical complaint.
[P2.2] It produces systematic misses of people whose cognition works differently.
[P2.3] Correlation does too much work on the other side. That these scores cluster and predict average training success shows they tap something real; it does not show they cover the wider band in dispute. Many validating tasks share the test's DNA: well-defined problems, one right answer, solo work, short horizons. Of course it predicts its cousins.
[P2.4] It distinguishes far less well who will generate a valuable idea, judge a conflict between rules and reality, read a team, sustain self-direction over months, or keep learning after training ends. Beyond a basic competence threshold, those differences often separate adequate from excellent — and a timed score flattens them into invisibility.
[P2.5] Nor is the choice test versus gut. Standardization is not synonymous with IQ-style testing. A supervised work sample is standardized. So is a structured problem-solving exercise scored by rubric, or a trial project with common prompts and blind review.
[P2.6] Those keep the anti-pedigree virtues — same task, same criteria, little room for charm or connections — while sampling cognition closer to real demands. That an unsupervised portfolio can be polished argues for designing broader assessments properly, not retreating to matrices and vocabulary.
[P2.7] The college example proves the same point. Using a score in context to spot a strong student from a weak school is the modest, compensatory role this proposition allows. A cutoff that eliminates applicants before their work is seen is something else. That veto power is the outsized role: other evidence is never weighed because a narrow screen has already decided who counts as able.

[P4.1] A1: The objection is to giving a narrow instrument an outsized role; the two cannot be separated. A score can help spot a strong student from a weak school when read alongside other evidence and able to raise a case. It becomes objectionable when that thin signal becomes a first-pass veto deciding whose other evidence is worth seeing. Administrators skipping the second look are not unrelated misuse — that veto is the outsized role named here.
[P4.2] If the test covered the band, a veto might be defensible. Because it does not, it is not.
[P4.3] A2: No one-for-one substitute is needed. For a fourteen-year-old, use repeated evidence of response to teaching over a term: common novel units, blind rubric scoring, and growth after instruction, not one timed sitting. For ten thousand novices, use a staged screen: everyone gets the same brief training, then a later application task plus a separate sustained problem, with combined scores and a retest route. False rejections should be lower not because errors vanish, but because they are not all the same error.
[P4.4] A bad day, unfamiliar format, or slow-but-deep style need not repeat at every gate.
[P4.5] That also defeats the threshold defence. Minimum trainability might justify a low, specific, revisable hurdle. It does not justify ranking children into enriched and diminished tracks, or ordering applicants for scarce jobs, by finer differences in speeded abstraction. Above that minimum — where most sorting happens — creativity, judgment, persistence, and learning over time decide who excels.
[P4.6] Nor does long-range correlation rescue the veto. Predicting average outcomes leaves discarded individuals unobserved and uncounted, while survivors receive the trials and mentoring that make the prediction look wise. Cheapness explains why institutions reach for the test; it does not prove the first cut is fair. A screen this narrow should inform judgment, not pre-decide who gets fully judged.
