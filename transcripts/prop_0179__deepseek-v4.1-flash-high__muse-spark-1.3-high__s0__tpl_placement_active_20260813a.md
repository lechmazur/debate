[P1.1] An octopus is a solitary, exploratory predator with a nervous system so distributed that much of its cognition sits in its arms. It can learn mazes, unscrew jars, recognize individual humans, and alter its body and color in ways that look less like reflex and more like communication. That is enough to trigger a simple moral rule: when an animal can solve novel problems, remember, hide, hunt, and avoid harm, confining it in an industrial tank is not neutral. It is an imposition on a mind.
[P1.2] We need not prove their inner lives match ours; we only need enough evidence that they can suffer, and enough power to refuse to impose that suffering for luxury.
[P1.3] Farming makes that imposition inescapable. Octopuses are not herd animals. They are territorial and largely solitary; crowding them produces stress, aggression, injury, and cannibalism. Barren or enriched tanks alike cannot give them the range, complexity, and control they seek.
[P1.4] Handling, sorting, transport, and slaughter add further terror and pain. The industry's logic is maximum biomass in minimum water, which is precisely the opposite of what a cognitively complex, solitary animal needs. No welfare code fixes that; it is a structural mismatch.
[P1.5] A patchwork ban is not enough. Octopus production is globally traded; if one country bans it, producers can shift to another and ship the product back. A global ban treats cruel confinement as a floor no jurisdiction should undercut. And this is not a staple we cannot replace.
[P1.6] Octopus farming is a luxury export market, not a solution to hunger. We already have abundant protein alternatives and wild-caught octopus is not necessary for nutrition. When a practice is indefensible, the fact that it might be profitable elsewhere is not a reason to allow it.
[P1.7] The question is not whether octopuses are human. It is whether we can recognize complex minds and still build factories for them. We cannot. Ban it.

That is precisely when welfare standards, enrichment requirements, and slaughter rules can shape the industry — prohibition would freeze that learning before it starts.
[C1.5] Finally, a global ban is blunt imperialism dressed as compassion. It overrides coastal communities in Spain, Mexico, Japan and elsewhere that have harvested octopus for generations, destroys a chance for secure local livelihoods, and invites a black market we cannot police. Humane regulation protects animals without pretending hunger and culture will obey a decree.
[C1.6] We should farm better, not ban blindly.
