[P1.1] Every serious danger to life has a window before the harm and a window after it. Criminal law works almost entirely in the second. A conviction requires a completed crime, proven beyond reasonable doubt, months later. By then the person who texted "I'm going to end this tonight," or posted a threat against a school, has already acted.
[P1.2] The question is simple: when credible evidence shows someone is about to kill, must a court wait for a body before it can act?
[P1.3] The PRO case rests on three points.
[P1.4] First, the gap is real and lethal. Families, police, and teachers often see the warning signs: explicit threats, a sudden weapons purchase after a breakup, a veteran in acute crisis talking about his rifle. Without red-flag laws, the honest answer to a terrified mother is, "Call us after he does something." That is not caution.
[P1.5] It is abdication.
[P1.6] Second, temporary removal works because crises are temporary. Suicide is the clearest case. Many suicidal crises are intense but brief, and method matters enormously. [P1.7] Separating a person from a gun for weeks can turn a death into a recovery. The same logic applies to domestic violence, where a gun in the home dramatically raises the odds that abuse becomes homicide.
[P1.8] Third, this is due process, not its absence. The motion requires a court, credible evidence, and imminent risk. A judge, not a neighbor, decides. Emergency orders are short and followed promptly by a full hearing where the respondent can contest the evidence.
[P1.9] Removal is time-limited, and property is returned when the risk passes. We already accept this structure everywhere: restraining orders, bail conditions, emergency psychiatric holds, child protective removals. None requires a conviction, because each is about preventing harm rather than punishing it.

[C1.1] The motion rests on a false choice: either courts may confiscate guns before conviction, or judges must wait for a murder. That is not the alternative. A judge with clear evidence of an actual planned attack already has legal tools: arrest, emergency custody, mental health intervention, or a protective order tied to specific conduct. Red-flag laws add something different—a system for taking firearms from people who have not committed a crime, often before they can answer the accusation.
[C1.2] This is prior restraint. “Credible evidence” and “imminent risk” sound narrow, but in practice they come from ex parte affidavits by angry ex-partners, estranged relatives, school officials, or neighbors. The respondent is disarmed first and heard later. A hearing two weeks later cannot restore the week lost, the public humiliation, the lost job clearance, or the armed confrontation at his door. Due process after the deprivation is not due process for the deprivation.
[C1.3] Worse, the core prediction is unreliable. Even trained clinicians are poor at predicting rare acts of violence; most people labeled high risk never commit the predicted act. Red-flag laws therefore guarantee a stream of false positives. Those false positives are not a minor cost: they fall heavily on lawful gun owners, on people in messy divorce or custody battles, and on already vulnerable groups.
[C1.4] The state acts not because of what someone has done, but because of what someone says they might do.
[C1.5] “Temporary” does not cure this. It still means the state seizes property and suspends a fundamental right on a forecast. If the crisis is suicide, the answer should be real treatment, not merely disarmament—and families may avoid calling for help if a call triggers confiscation and confrontation. For domestic violence, courts already have tools keyed to actual threats and prior conduct.
[C1.6] The restraining-order analogy cuts the other way. Those protections are generally tied to findings about conduct, and even they can be abused. This motion asks for a lower, vaguer standard: take the gun now, justify it later. That is a dangerous precedent.
