[P1.1] The central question is not whether children under fourteen can cause harm; they can. The question is whether treating them as fully punishable offenders is a just or effective way for a state to respond. It is neither. Criminal responsibility assumes the capacity to understand proceedings, control impulses, and appreciate long-term consequences.
[P1.2] Developmentally, most children below fourteen lack these capacities in the mature form adult punishment presumes. Societies already recognize this when they withhold full adult rights and duties around voting, contracts, driving, and military service. Punishing a child as fully culpable while denying them adult autonomy is a contradiction. Condemnation without comprehension is not justice; it is power exercised against a developing person.
[P1.3] Raising the age to fourteen also serves public safety. Treating young children as criminals tends to funnel them into detention, stigma, and peer networks that harden offending rather than interrupt it. Deterrence depends on rational calculation, which is weakest at these ages. A child labeled “criminal” early is more likely to internalize that identity than to be deterred by it.
[P1.4] The stronger response is accountability designed for children: supervision, family interventions, trauma-informed treatment, education, restitution where possible, and victim-centered repair. These mechanisms address the causes of behavior and reduce repeat harm without sacrificing a child’s future to a show of blame.
[P1.5] Very low ages also invite arbitrariness. When nine- or ten-year-olds can be fully punishable, discretion falls hardest on children with disabilities, poverty, trauma, or minority status—those least able to navigate court systems and most likely to be seen as dangerous rather than in need. A fourteen-year minimum creates a bright line: children below it are not beyond accountability, but they are handled in systems built for development and rehabilitation, not adult retribution. It also reduces pressure on police and prosecutors to improvise adult-style responses to childhood crisis.
[P1.6] The proposition asks countries to align law with fairness, prevention, and the real goal of fewer victims.

[C1.1] The proposition forces a choice that does not exist: either keep very low ages meaning younger children are treated as fully punishable offenders, or raise the age to fourteen and handle them only through welfare and treatment. That is a false dilemma. Even granting no child should be condemned as a fully adult offender, [P1.2]'s picture of "punishing a child as fully culpable while denying them adult autonomy" attacks a straw man. In jurisdictions with low minimum ages, the ordinary response is not adult retribution.
[C1.2] It is a separate youth process with modified procedure, doli incapax, capacity assessments, and child-focused disposals. The real question is whether criminal responsibility should be abolished below fourteen. It should not.
[C1.3] Fourteen is an arbitrary cliff. Development is gradual, not switched on. A rule that immunizes a child one day and declares full accountability the next is no more principled than the low ages it replaces. If maturity is the criterion, the answer is individualized assessment and graded responsibility, not a magic number.
[C1.4] A rigid fourteen line removes the criminal-law option for grave harm by twelve- and thirteen-year-olds. Consider a thirteen-year-old who commits an armed robbery or a sexual assault: a fourteen threshold tells the victim the criminal law has no answer, even where a youth court could impose supervision, restriction, and repair. Communities are then left with either impunity or unaccountable welfare confinement, which can involve less due process and less voice for victims than a youth court.
[C1.5] The PRO also overstates the point that "deterrence depends on rational calculation" [P1.3]. Criminal law is not only about rational deterrence. For children who commit grave violence or sexual harm, the state needs lawful authority to supervise, restrict, and treat. Welfare-only rhetoric can sound humane while operating with weaker procedural protections.
[C1.6] Keeping jurisdiction below fourteen, wrapped in strict juvenile safeguards, is the better path: it takes children's development seriously without pretending fourteen is a moral switch.
