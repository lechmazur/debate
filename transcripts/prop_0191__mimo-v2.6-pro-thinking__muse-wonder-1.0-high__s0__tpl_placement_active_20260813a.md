[P1.1] A drug approved on a narrow population is not a safe drug; it is an untested drug for everyone outside that population. The question is not whether representation is nice to have. It is whether the evidence we generate is fit for the purpose we claim it serves: treating the whole public.

[P1.3] Common genetic variants in drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters vary in frequency across ancestral populations. Comorbidities and concomitant medications differ too. If these variables can change efficacy or flip a side-effect profile, then a trial that excludes them has an internal validity problem disguised as an external one. The answer "we'll study it after approval" means the least controlled, least monitored phase of a drug's life is where entire populations first become subjects.

[P1.5] The harm of approving on thin data is diffuse, retrospective, and added to nobody's ledger. That accounting asymmetry is exactly why a requirement is needed rather than a guideline.

[C1.1] A drug delayed is not neutral. For a patient with metastatic cancer, ALS, or a child with a progressive rare disease, months spent filling enrollment quotas are months of irreversible progression or death. The proposition asks us to accept that cost in every trial, for every drug, across age, sex, and ethnicity at once. That mandate confuses representation with good science.
[C1.2] First, proportionate to what? Diseases are not proportionate. Alzheimer's concentrates in the old, sickle cell disease in people of African ancestry, cystic fibrosis disproportionately in people of European ancestry, prostate cancer only in males. A census-mirroring quota would over-enroll people at low risk and under-enroll those who actually develop the condition, while trying to recruit children, the elderly, and ethnic subgroups for conditions where some barely occur.
[C1.3] Scientific enrollment should follow disease prevalence and biological plausibility, not demographic symmetry.
[C1.4] Second, proportionate enrollment does not deliver what its defenders promise: reliable subgroup answers. If women are half of a 300-person trial, that still cannot reliably detect a modest difference in efficacy or a rare liver injury in women. To power every age-by-sex-by-ethnicity combination would multiply sample sizes many times over, making trials impossibly long or impossible altogether, especially for rare diseases. The result is either false reassurance from token numbers, or no trial at all.
[C1.5] Third, mandates will shrink the pipeline. Recruitment is already the main bottleneck; hard quotas for groups facing transportation, language, mistrust, work, and comorbidity barriers will raise costs, drive sponsors to easier countries, or cause small firms to abandon marginal drugs. Minority patients gain nothing from a perfectly representative trial that never finishes.
[C1.6] The better rule is risk-based: prespecify subgroup concerns where pharmacology suggests them, explain exclusions, stratify where feasible, and use conditional approval, registries, and post-marketing studies to fill gaps without withholding a promising therapy from everyone. A requirement that applies even when delay is material, and even when biology does not demand it, sacrifices identifiable patients now for statistical symbolism later.

[P2.1] CON's headline objection — "proportionate to what?" — attacks a requirement I did not defend. Proportionate means proportional to the population that will actually take the drug. Sickle cell trials should be overwhelmingly Black; prostate cancer trials all male; Alzheimer's trials skew old. CON's own disease-prevalence examples are specifications of the motion, not refutations of it.
[P2.2] Where a disease is confined to one group, the requirement is satisfied trivially. The cases that bite — hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, depression — are exactly those where the treated population is diverse and the trial is not.
[P2.3] Second, "token numbers" is what PRO forbids. The standard is enrollment powered to detect clinically meaningful differences within each group, not headcounts for a photograph. When CON says powering subgroups is impossible, that concedes the point in disguise: if we cannot detect whether the dose works in older adults or women, we do not know that it works for the eighty-year-old with the same diagnosis. Approving anyway does not dissolve the uncertainty; it externalizes it onto patients who were never measured.
[P2.4] And "impossibly long" is the very cost the motion asks us to accept — speed cannot be a trump card when it buys a label the data cannot support.
[P2.5] Third, conditional approval and registries do not close the gap. Post-approval evidence is confounded by who gets treated, monitored, and reported; it cannot separate a pharmacologic interaction from differential care. Proportionate enrollment is fully compatible with accelerated pathways for ALS or metastatic cancer — enroll the population that actually suffers the disease and still hold the trial to subgroup power standards within it.
[P2.6] The clash is not speed against symbolism. It is whether we knowingly approve drugs whose behavior in older adults, women, and minority patients is unmeasured, and then mislabel the resulting harms as rare side effects rather than missing data.

[C2.4] Nor does uncertainty justify holding the studied patients hostage.
