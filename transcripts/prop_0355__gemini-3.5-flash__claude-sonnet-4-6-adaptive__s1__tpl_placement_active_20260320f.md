[P1.1] The global transition to electric vehicles cannot succeed if it remains exclusive to suburban homeowners. Currently, millions of apartment renters are locked out of the EV market because they lack a reliable way to charge at home. Landlords hold near-absolute power to delay, complicate, or outright block the installation of charging infrastructure. To achieve critical climate goals and ensure economic equity, we must shift the legal balance of power: renters deserve a robust, enforceable right to request and receive EV charging access that supersedes a landlord's arbitrary ability to obstruct it.
[P1.2] First, housing and transportation are deeply linked. Renters, who represent a massive portion of the urban population, face a structural "charging desert." Without overnight charging, owning an EV is highly impractical. Denying renters this access not only stalls national emission-reduction efforts but also penalizes tenants by denying them the long-term fuel and maintenance savings of electric transit.
[P1.3] Second, the current system suffers from a classic split-incentive market failure. Landlords have little motivation to upgrade property infrastructure or approve tenant-led initiatives, as they do not directly benefit from the tenant’s fuel savings. Consequently, even in jurisdictions with nominal "right-to-charge" laws, landlords routinely exploit legal loopholes—such as demanding exorbitant liability insurance, requiring excessive security deposits, or dragging out engineering reviews indefinitely—to effectively block progress.
[P1.4] A stronger legal right solves this. It establishes a clear presumption in favor of approval, setting strict, expedited timelines for landlord responses and capping the financial or bureaucratic hurdles landlords can impose. This does not mean forcing property owners to absorb unreasonable costs; rather, it ensures that when tenants are willing to utilize modern, safe, and often self-funded charging solutions, landlords cannot act as arbitrary gatekeepers. Equity, environmental necessity, and basic fairness demand that we grant renters the power to plug in.

[C1.1] The proposition asks whether renters should have a *stronger legal right* than landlords currently have to delay or block EV charging. That framing already concedes that landlords have *some* legitimate standing—otherwise there'd be nothing to override. The real question is whether that balance should be legally tipped. It shouldn't.
[C1.2] Start with property rights and real costs. Electrical infrastructure is not a minor amenity. Retrofitting an older multifamily building for EV charging can require panel upgrades, conduit runs through shared walls, load-balancing systems, and fire-rated installations. These aren't bureaucratic fictions invented to obstruct tenants—they're genuine engineering challenges.
[C1.3] A small landlord operating a 12-unit building from the 1960s faces real capital exposure when one or two tenants want dedicated charging circuits. Telling that landlord their hesitation is legally subordinate to the tenant's preference ignores who actually owns the infrastructure being modified.
[C1.4] The PRO case names split incentives as the core failure, but the proposed remedy overshoots. The landlord's reluctance isn't irrational; it reflects that they bear the liability and structural risk while the tenant captures the benefit. A blunt legal right tilted toward the tenant doesn't resolve that asymmetry—it just forces one party to absorb it. The predictable result is that risk-averse landlords exit the rental market or price the new liability into rents, which harms the renters the policy was meant to help.
[C1.5] Notice also where the PRO argument gets vague: it promises that stronger rights "don't mean forcing landlords to absorb unreasonable costs," but then the debate turns on who defines unreasonable. Once the legal presumption flips to tenant approval, landlords must litigate to establish that their concerns qualify—an expensive, chilling outcome for smaller operators.
[C1.6] The legitimate goal—broader EV access for renters—is better served through incentive programs, public charging expansion, and voluntary landlord-tenant agreements, not by legally demoting property owners into presumed obstacles.

[C2.2] When a tenant installs a dedicated charging circuit, the landlord's building insurance, fire liability, and electrical panel certification all become implicated—regardless of who wrote the check. If a charging installation contributes to a fire or overloads aging wiring, the landlord faces legal exposure and potential coverage disputes whether they approved the project willingly or under legal compulsion. "The tenant paid" does not transfer title to the risk.
[C2.3] The PRO then promises that a clear statutory checklist eliminates these concerns [P2.3]. But notice the assumption packed in: that a checklist can fully anticipate every relevant building condition. A 1960s concrete building with a partially upgraded panel, an HOA with its own fire-code requirements, or a utility service connection already near capacity cannot be covered by a one-size list. Real engineering determinations require someone with authority over the building to make them.
[C2.4] That party is the landlord. Stripping their judgment through a legal presumption of approval doesn't make those determinations go away—it just strips the responsible party of standing to make them.
[C2.5] The PRO also attacks public charging as an equity trap [P2.5]. But the real alternative isn't "public fast-chargers only"—it's shared Level 2 infrastructure at the building level, served by landlord-managed systems with equitable access rules. Several jurisdictions already incentivize this model with tax credits. That approach solves the equity problem without requiring individual landlords to become presumed obstacles to their own tenants.
[C2.6] The central flaw in the PRO position is this: they keep calling landlord objections "arbitrary" [P2.6], but that label is doing enormous work. Under a stronger renter right, a landlord must litigate to prove their concern isn't arbitrary. That burden inversion—not the checklist, not tenant funding—is what the proposition actually demands. It's a real, substantial shift in property rights dressed up as mere procedural tidying.
