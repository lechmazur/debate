[P1.1] Public universities should reinstate standardized test requirements because the central problem in undergraduate admissions is not that tests exist, but that without them admissions becomes less transparent, less comparable, and more dependent on credentials shaped by wealth.
[P1.2] A required test score gives admissions officers a common academic yardstick across wildly unequal high schools. A 3.9 GPA can mean rigorous coursework, easy grading, or grade inflation; a class rank can hide a weak curriculum. Public universities draw students from hundreds or thousands of schools, and admissions readers cannot personally know each transcript’s meaning. Standardized results, while imperfect, supply one comparable data point about whether a student can handle college-level work.
[P1.3] This matters most for applicants the current test-optional era can hurt. Affluent students can package themselves with polished essays, expensive consultants, curated activities, and inflated grades. A high-achieving student from an under-resourced school may not have those signals. If tests are optional, the students who submit them tend to be those with resources and preparation, while talented students who lack access or confidence are less visible.
[P1.4] A universal requirement, paired with fee waivers and test-day supports, forces the system to evaluate everyone with at least one shared academic measure.
[P1.5] CON will say tests correlate with family income. That is true, but abandoning tests does not erase inequality; it shifts weight to more opaque indicators. Essays reflect coaching, activities reflect money, recommendations reflect social capital, and grades are inconsistent across schools. [P1.6] The proposition is not “admit by score alone.” It is: require a common academic signal so public admissions are more honest, more comparable, and more defensible to the taxpayers and students they serve.
