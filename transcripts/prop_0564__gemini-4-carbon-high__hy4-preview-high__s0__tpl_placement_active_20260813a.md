[P1.1] No major city puts turnstiles on sidewalks or bills pedestrians per streetlight passed. Yet cities treat buses and subways not as essential public infrastructure, but as commercial tollbooths. Making public transit permanently fare-free through broad-based taxation fixes that mistake. Even with modest initial ridership gains, this shift wins on economic justice, operational speed, and fiscal stability.
[P1.2] First, fares act as a regressive flat tax on the workers who keep cities running. A hospital cleaner pays the same fare as an executive, forcing low-income households to ration trips to clinics, job interviews, or affordable groceries. Means-tested discounts routinely fail through red tape, strict income cliffs, and lapsed renewals. Universal fare-free transit removes every barrier while matching costs to real beneficiaries.
[P1.3] Employers gain citywide labor, storefronts gain customers, and drivers face less gridlock because millions ride transit. Because the whole city profits from mass mobility, the broad tax base, not just the commuter at the turnstile, should fund it.
[P1.4] Second, eliminating fares makes transit faster and more resilient, defeating the false choice between free rides and good service. Fare collection drains budgets on ticketing vendors, gate upkeep, and policing that turns poverty into volatile enforcement encounters. Operationally, fareboxes bottleneck every stop as passengers queue at the front door. All-door, fare-free boarding cuts dwell times immediately, letting existing fleets run faster and more reliably without buying new vehicles.
[P1.5] Above all, dedicated broad-based taxes end the transit death spiral, where ridership dips starve budgets and trigger service cuts that drive away more riders.
[P1.6] Finally, permanence explains why modest initial ridership gains miss the real story. Households do not sell a car or sign a new lease for a temporary pilot; only permanent fare abolition reshapes long-term habits. Meanwhile, immediate gains like faster buses, unpoliced stations, stable budgets, and real savings for working families prove transit succeeds not merely by converting drivers, but by liberating the people who already move the city.

The motion’s own concession—initial ridership gains may be modest—destroys its case.
[C1.2] If few drivers switch, the city still absorbs a permanent, large tax increase to replace fares, while congestion, peak crowding, and maintenance backlogs remain.
[C1.3] The funding swap is also falsely progressive. City-level “broad-based taxation” usually means sales, payroll, or property taxes. Those fall on the same hospital cleaner through rent and receipts. A worker may escape a modest per-ride fare only to pay more each year in higher sales and property taxes passed through housing costs.
[C1.4] [P1.2] calls fares regressive, but the replacement can be just as regressive—and less transparent, because the cost is bundled into every purchase and rent check rather than paid per trip.
[C1.5] Operationally, [P1.4] confuses fare abolition with fare modernization. Off-board payment, all-door boarding, and proof-of-payment already cut dwell time without making rides free. Free transit, by contrast, removes the price signal and induces discretionary trips that strain buses and operators, especially at the margin where service is worst. [P1.5] claims broad taxes end the “death spiral,” but fares are themselves a dedicated stream; eliminating them makes transit wholly dependent on annual appropriations and politics.
[C1.6] That is more fragile, not less.
[C1.7] Permanence, [P1.6], is only worth the cost if it changes behavior. With modest gains, it locks in a permanent new entitlement, taxes everyone for trips largely taken by existing riders, and forgoes revenue needed for reliability. The real clash is not sidewalk versus tollbooth; it is whether cities should convert a constrained service into a permanently tax-funded entitlement on the promise of a change the motion admits may barely arrive.
