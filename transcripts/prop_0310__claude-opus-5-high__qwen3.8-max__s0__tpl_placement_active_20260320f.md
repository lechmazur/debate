[P1.1] A school subject earns the label "core" when two things are true: nearly every adult will need it, and the informal channels for learning it fail unevenly. Reading and arithmetic qualify. So do cooking, basic repair, and personal finance — and the second condition is the one that decides this debate.
[P1.2] Consider who actually "happens to seek out" an elective in budgeting. Overwhelmingly, it is the student whose parents already talk about money at the dinner table, who already knows that a credit score exists and matters. The kid whose family has never held a savings account, who has never watched an adult fix a running toilet instead of paying someone, is precisely the kid who doesn't know what he doesn't know. Optionality doesn't distribute this knowledge by need; it distributes it by prior advantage.
[P1.3] An elective system takes an inequality the school could correct and quietly ratifies it.
[P1.4] And the harms compound in a way most school content does not. Forgetting the causes of the War of 1812 costs you nothing at 24. Not understanding that a payday loan's fee structure annualizes into triple digits can cost you years. A young person who cannot cook eats a decade of expensive, worse food; a tenant who cannot identify a slow leak or reset a breaker pays for callouts and, worse, is easily taken advantage of by anyone who can.
[P1.5] These are the interest-bearing ignorances.
[P1.6] "Parents should teach it" is not a rebuttal, it's a description of the failure. Household transmission has thinned — more dual-earner homes, more rentals where nobody fixes anything, financial products vastly more complex than the ones parents learned on. Leaving it to families means leaving it to luck.
[P1.7] Nor is this an assault on academics. Core does not mean enormous; it means guaranteed. Amortization schedules are algebra with stakes. A lease is a reading comprehension passage that can evict you.
[P1.8] Teach everyone, or teach the already-fortunate. There is no neutral third option.

[C1.1] The strongest case against this motion is not that cooking, repair, and budgeting are unimportant. They are. It is that “core” means required, assessed, time-consuming, and universal, while school hours are finite. If we make three practical domains core, we displace the foundational skills that determine whether a young person can navigate any practical problem later.
[C1.2] A student who cannot read a contract will not be saved by a lesson on leases; a student who cannot handle percentages will not be protected by a warning about payday loans. Literacy, numeracy, scientific reasoning, and civic knowledge are the transferable life skills, and they are the subjects most tightly linked to long-run autonomy. PRO’s equity concern should push us toward strengthening those foundations, not diluting them.
[C1.3] PRO imagines a clean fix: mandate the skills and inequality shrinks. But mandates do not create capacity. Cooking requires kitchens, supervision, allergy controls, and safety; repair needs tools, insurance, and space; finance needs teachers who can avoid both outdated advice and salesy platitudes. Wealthier districts can absorb those costs; under-resourced schools will offer worksheets and videos.
[C1.4] The same mandate then becomes another unequal burden, with the label “core” hiding the gap.
[C1.5] Optional need not mean hidden or self-selected. Schools can offer these modules widely, put them in advisory rotations, run workshops, and partner with community organizations without forcing every student into the same sequence. A student aiming for nursing, welding, coding, or university calculus has different immediate needs. A one-size-fits-all requirement treats plural futures as a defect.
[C1.6] Real access lets students go deeper where their path actually lies.
[C1.7] Finally, PRO’s “interest-bearing ignorances” are real, but many are better attacked outside the classroom: consumer-protection rules, clearer lease disclosures, plain-language contracts, and public information campaigns. Schools cannot become the repair shop for every social failure. The fairest curriculum is not the fullest checklist; it is the foundation that lets every student learn whatever life demands next.
