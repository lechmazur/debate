[P1.1] Speed determines whether a mistake is survivable. Kinetic energy rises with the square of speed, so a crash at 40 mph carries far more energy than one at 30, and a pedestrian struck faster is far more likely to die. Lowering real-world speeds, consistently, saves lives. Automated enforcement does that; more police stops largely do not.
[P1.2] Deterrence works mainly through certainty, not occasional punishment. A signed fixed or average-speed camera tells every driver on a dangerous corridor: you will be caught if you speed, today, tonight, and tomorrow. Drivers slow for the whole stretch, not just when they spot a cruiser. That sustained compliance matters on high-speed arterials and rural roads where fatal crashes concentrate and no department can station officers around the clock.
[P1.3] A traffic stop is a lottery. One driver in hundreds who speed is pulled over, based on where an officer parks and which car catches attention. Others learn speeding usually pays. Even when a stop occurs, speeds dip near the patrol car and rebound a mile later, while the cost in officer hours is enormous — paying scarce personnel to sit on shoulders instead of responding to impaired driving and crashes.
[P1.4] The externalities diverge just as sharply. Every added stop is a roadside encounter on a high-speed shoulder, dangerous for officer and occupants, with potential to escalate over a warrant, a search, or a misunderstanding. Because stops require discretion about whom to pull over, expanding them expands pretextual stops and racial and economic disparities. Those harms are built into discretionary roadside enforcement, not just bad implementation.
[P1.5] Cameras are imperfect, but their flaws are designable: visible signage, warnings first, and revenue directed away from the enforcing agency. What cannot be redesigned away is the math. An automated system enforces the same limit on every car, without a confrontation, at a fraction of the cost. For fewer deaths and fewer dangerous encounters, that is the superior trade.
