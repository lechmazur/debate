[P1.2] Start with what these features actually are. Lyft's Women+ Connect, Uber's women-rider preference in markets like Saudi Arabia and beyond, and smaller operators built around women drivers all work the same way: an opt-in preference, not a partition. A woman who selects it waits slightly longer if no matching driver is nearby, and the system falls back to the general pool. Nobody else's ride gets slower.
[P1.3] The person choosing the option is the person bearing its cost. That is close to the ideal design for a safety feature: the cost is voluntary, bounded, and self-limiting.
[P1.4] Now the benefit side. Ride-hail companies' own safety disclosures have reported thousands of sexual assault incidents a year in the U.S. alone, with women overwhelmingly the victims and drivers frequently the alleged perpetrators. And trust compounds: rides that happen instead of being skipped, night shifts accepted, cities that become navigable after dark.
[P1.5] The overlooked half is supply. Women are a small minority of drivers largely because driving strangers at night feels unsafe to them, too. A women-matching option is the single most effective recruiting pitch a platform has for women drivers, and every woman it recruits shrinks the very matching gap CON will complain about. The inefficiency is largest on day one and falls from there.
[P1.6] So the comparison is stark: a marginal, opt-in wait for those who want it, versus fewer assaults, more rides, and more drivers. If that trade doesn't clear the bar, nothing in product design ever would.
