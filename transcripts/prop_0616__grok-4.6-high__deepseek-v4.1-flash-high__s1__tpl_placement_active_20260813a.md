[P1.1] Advanced semiconductor manufacturing equipment is the chokepoint of modern military power. Frontier AI for targeting, intelligence fusion, electronic warfare, and autonomous systems runs on leading-edge chips. Those chips require a tiny set of tools—extreme ultraviolet lithography, advanced etch, deposition, and metrology—that only a few firms can make and that take years of tacit knowledge to master. Restricting those exports does not merely inconvenience a rival.
[P1.2] It delays the physical capacity to manufacture the compute military AI needs.
[P1.3] That delay is the point. Military advantage here compounds. A country that cannot run high-yield advanced nodes trains fewer models, fields fewer accelerators, and iterates more slowly. In peacetime that is a technology gap.
[P1.4] In a crisis it is worse sensors, slower kill chains, and command systems that cannot match an opponent’s tempo. Markets price lost tool sales. They do not price a lost war or a coerced settlement. Extra engineers and extra data do not replace missing leading-edge wafers.

[P1.6] An adversary denied the machines substitutes more slowly and less completely than critics claim, because process knowledge, specialized parts, and service ecosystems travel with the tools. Once indigenous production stands, the window closes.
[P1.7] Chip-level end-use rules fail: the same silicon serves hospitals and missile guidance. Equipment sits upstream of that ambiguity. Allied control is feasible because the industry is already concentrated.
[P1.8] Selling the factory that makes this compute is not ordinary commerce. A delayed adversary military AI program is worth a large bill: hardware is the binding constraint, and time at the frontier does not come back. Accept that cost rather than subsidize the other side’s closing of the gap.
