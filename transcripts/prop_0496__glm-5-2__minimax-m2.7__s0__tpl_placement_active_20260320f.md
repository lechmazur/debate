[P1.1] The core problem is structural, not incidental. When a state delegates combat roles to private military contractors, it inserts a profit-driven corporation between the democratic public and the lethal force exercised in its name. That insertion breaks the accountability chain that makes democratic consent to war meaningful.
[P1.2] Democratic accountability over force requires that citizens can know when force is used, who authorizes it, and hold responsible parties answerable through elections, oversight, and criminal prosecution. Uniformed militaries, whatever their flaws, are embedded in institutions built for this: public budgets, military justice, congressional oversight, and a chain of command running to elected civilians. Private contractors replace each with corporate equivalents governed by shareholder value, nondisclosure agreements, and proprietary confidentiality.
[P1.3] The result is an accountability vacuum by design. When contractor misconduct surfaces, the response is typically not courts-martial or public hearings but quiet contract renegotiation or sealed civil settlements. Elected officials can claim ignorance. The public cannot exercise informed consent because information about how force is deployed is treated as a trade secret.
[P1.4] Profit incentives compound the damage. A contractor's financial interest lies in contract renewal and expansion, not mission completion or drawdown. This creates structural pressure toward prolonged engagement that operates entirely outside democratic debate.
[P1.5] This is not a regulatory problem solvable by better contracting. The gap is inherent in the model. A corporation accountable to shareholders cannot simultaneously be accountable to a democratic electorate for lethal decisions. Fiduciary duty runs one direction; democratic accountability runs the other.
[P1.6] Banning combat roles for PMCs restores the principle that when a state kills in its citizens' name, the people who authorize and execute that violence must be answerable through institutions designed for public accountability—not private profit.
