[P1.1] Minimum wage is a floor on hourly pay, not a bonus for the minutes a worker is carrying a parcel. The proposition asks platforms to count what labor law recognizes: when a worker is engaged to wait, that waiting is work. A gig courier who is logged in, inside the app's active zone, watching for pings, and required to be ready within seconds is not 'between jobs' on the app. She cannot take another shift, nap, or leave the area without risking her standing.
[P1.2] Her time is consigned to the platform; that standby capacity is what makes the service marketable. If a restaurant must pay a server kept onsite at least minimum wage, a delivery app that assigns a courier to a hot zone and dangles priority dispatch for staying logged in should get no exemption.
[P1.3] The practical stakes are arithmetic. Consider a rider who logs in for a four-hour dinner shift. She waits actively for two and a half hours and delivers for one and a half. If the platform pays only trip time—say $30 plus tips—her apparent '$20 an hour' hides that she was available and constrained for four hours.
[P1.4] Measured over active waiting, her real rate is $7.50. The platform has not paid for a task; it has paid to hold labor in reserve. Excluding standby time lets platforms flood the market with more workers than demand, keep everyone waiting, then report high 'engaged' rates while workers earn less than minimum.
[P1.5] Counting active waiting does not make platforms pay for off-duty life. It makes them price the labor they actually use: availability. If the work needs a pool of ready workers, the cost belongs on the platform's ledger, not the worker's unpaid time. Minimum-wage compliance should follow hours of constrained readiness, because that is the labor the platform buys.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds fair until you ask who defines “active waiting.” A courier logged in at home doing laundry, one sitting in a car in a hot zone, and one running a personal errand with the app open are all “available” in some sense. Only one may be genuinely constrained. The proposed metric cannot tell them apart without turning the phone into a surveillance device: location tracking, response-time policing, forced zone occupancy. And every definition is set by the platform itself, so the number that triggers minimum-wage liability is the number the platform has the strongest incentive to shrink.
[C1.2] That is not a compliance rule; it is a litigation trap.
[C1.3] The economics are worse. If every logged-in minute becomes paid time, platforms will stop tolerating open access. They will cap logins, schedule shifts, punish low acceptance, and reserve hours for workers who fit a narrow profile. The worker who logs on after class or between caregiving tasks—the person gig work supposedly serves—loses the ability to try for work.
[C1.4] PRO’s arithmetic [P1.3] assumes waiting hours are costless to count, but the predictable response is fewer available hours and more competition for them. A minimum wage that destroys access is not a win for low-income workers.
[C1.5] Multi-apping exposes the category error. Workers often keep two apps open; the same hour cannot be fully purchased by both. Counting active waiting forces platforms to police exclusivity or double-count the same standby capacity—neither is workable.
[C1.6] Minimum wage should track work actually performed and controlled, not a platform-defined state of readiness. The real problem PRO identifies is opaque, volatile earnings. The answer is transparent task rates, clear acceptance rules, and no unpaid mandatory waiting after assignment—not reclassifying every open app as a wage hour. The proposition promises a floor; it would deliver rationed access, surveillance, and fewer opportunities.

[P4.1] A1: Platforms cannot escape by relabeling. Wage law looks to control, not vocabulary. If an app simultaneously requires zone presence, tracks response times, and penalizes ignored offers, its own logs prove dispatchable status no matter what label it uses. A restaurant cannot call a server’s required side work “off the clock” to dodge minimum wage; an app cannot call constrained standby “inactive.” Relabeling to “inactive” while keeping the same constraints is evasion, and the platform’s routing and penalty data are the auditable rebuttal.
[P4.2] If it truly loosens zones and penalties, waiting becomes genuinely free—and then no one owes for it. Either the control disappears or the liability remains; the label is not a magic shield.
[P4.3] A2: Multi-apping is allocated by exclusivity, not real-time spying. The platform that commands exclusive readiness—enforced zone, response window, acceptance penalties—pays the full standby hour; its own timestamped offer, location, and acceptance logs prove the purchase. If neither app commands exclusivity, neither buys the full hour: each owes only for time it actually dispatched or controlled, and open availability shared between two apps is not full active waiting by either. Comparing the two platforms’ separate records in a wage proceeding resolves overlaps after the fact; competitors never need a live feed.
[P4.4] Those answers collapse CON’s administrability case. The rule does not require a platform-written definition or new surveillance; it uses telemetry platforms already keep and tests substance over labels. CON’s fallback—transparent task rates [C2.6]—still yields $7.50 for four constrained hours [P1.3], because it excludes the reserve labor the platform is using. Counting active waiting does not ration work; it stops platforms from staffing demand with unpaid inventory.
[P4.5] A courier held ready is not free. That is the whole dispute: who pays for the pool of ready workers? Minimum wage should follow control, or platforms will keep writing “free” where they mean “unpaid.”
