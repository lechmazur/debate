[P1.1] Buying an e-book, film, album, game, or reference library is presented as acquisition: one price, a title in “my library,” a durable household resource. Death should not make that shelf vanish. The rule is narrow: for paid, durable, asset-like digital content, inheritance law should guarantee that personal use rights are inheritable by the next of kin identified by existing law. That is continuity of a purchase, not a license to pirate.
[P1.2] Copyright is not the obstacle. Families inherit physical books, discs, cartridges, and annotated volumes even though publishers keep copyright. What changes online is a boilerplate label: non-transferable license. CON’s case lets that label defeat substance.
[P1.3] It makes a paid cultural collection a life-terminating permission slip: the seller keeps the price, while the family loses the value at the moment inheritance exists to preserve continuity.
[P1.4] Mandatory inheritance rules already override purportedly “personal” terms when ordinary assets are at stake; otherwise banks, landlords, and employers could write death-forfeiture clauses into every relationship. Digital purchases deserve the same baseline. The heir would receive only the same personal, noncommercial access: no resale, sublicensing, mass sharing, or distribution. Platforms could verify death through probate documents, close unrelated communications, and convert the library to a successor account under existing technical limits.
[P1.5] If a firm’s architecture cannot recognize succession, that is a design defect in the product it sold, not a reason to nullify the purchase.
[P1.6] Standard objections either miss the asset or prove too much. This is not succession to private messages, social feeds, or temporary subscriptions; it is the paid library, the game shelf, the purchased archive. Privacy is answered by scope. Security is answered by account conversion, not password sharing.
[P1.7] Licensing economics cannot justify a rule in which businesses market durable access in substance but enforce mortality in form. CON must defend forfeiture at death; this rule protects the expectation that what a person bought for the household can remain with the household.

[C1.1] PRO treats a digital library like a shelf of hardcover books [P1.2]. But a physical book does not require the publisher to maintain servers, authenticate logins, update software, or renew upstream clearances for the next century. Non-transferable digital assets are not static objects; they are ongoing service and licensing relationships priced for a single user’s lifetime.
[C1.2] First, mandating perpetual inheritance destroys the consumer bargain. Non-transferability is not a "boilerplate label" [P1.2]; it is the economic boundary that allows a one-time fee to cover indefinite hosting and access. Physical goods degrade and require zero vendor upkeep, whereas digital libraries require continuous infrastructure. If the law forces platforms to service a single purchase across generations, firms will either hike prices for living consumers or abandon one-time purchases entirely for pure subscriptions—which PRO admits are uninheritable [P1.6].
[C1.3] Mandating inheritance will simply erase the "buy-to-keep" model in practice.
[C1.4] Second, transferring to "next of kin" [P1.1] collides with copyright and upstream contracts. Platforms rarely own the media they distribute; they hold limited licenses from studios and publishers. When an estate passes to multiple heirs—say, three siblings—a physical collection is easily divided item by item. How do three heirs split a unified digital account?
[C1.5] Duplicating the library violates copyright, while forcing platforms to fracture integrated accounts, software bundles, and delisted titles requires sublicensing rights platforms do not even possess.
[C1.6] Finally, PRO wrongly claims "privacy is answered by scope" [P1.6]. A lifetime archive of purchased e-books, films, and games is an intimate map of a person’s beliefs, struggles, and identity. Consumers buy sensitive media under the explicit contractual assurance that a personal account dies with them. Automatically sweeping non-transferable libraries into probate strips the deceased of post-mortem privacy, while overriding the clear contractual terms that made affordable digital access possible in the first place.

Breaking that contract harms the living and exposes the dead.
