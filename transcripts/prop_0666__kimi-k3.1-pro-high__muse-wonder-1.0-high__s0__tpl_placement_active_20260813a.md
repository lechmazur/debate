[P1.1] When an automated system denies a family an apartment, cuts off a borrower’s credit, screens out a job applicant, or delays medical coverage, “the model decided” is not a reason. It is an assertion of power without accountability. Courts should recognize a limited right to explanation because meaningful reasons are the minimum condition for correcting errors, contesting discrimination, and making any other legal protection real.
[P1.2] The right is narrow in both trigger and content. It applies only when automation significantly affects access to housing, credit, employment, or healthcare—not every recommendation or advertisement. The affected person should receive the principal reasons for the adverse decision, the material information relied upon, and enough clarity to identify and challenge factual errors. This does not require publishing source code, exposing proprietary model weights, or making every algorithm intelligible to a layperson. “Your income was calculated using an outdated employment record” is an explanation; a technical manual is unnecessary.
[P1.3] First, explanation prevents wrongful exclusion from becoming permanent. Automated decisions can inherit stale records, mismatched identities, proxies for protected traits, or assumptions that do not fit the individual. Without reasons, the person cannot even know what to dispute. A rejected tenant cannot correct an eviction belonging to someone else, and an applicant cannot answer a concern based on inaccurate credentials.
[P1.4] Opacity therefore defeats both accuracy and due process.
[P1.5] Second, explanation disciplines decision-makers. If consequential reasons must be articulated, firms have stronger incentives to validate inputs, monitor outcomes, and retain meaningful review. [C1.1] A denied applicant deserves a remedy, but a court-invented right to explanation gives her a plausible story, not reliable recourse — and that story can affirm a bad decision instead of exposing it.
[C1.2] First, complex systems often have no stable “principal reasons” for courts to order disclosed. A credit or hiring model may weigh hundreds of interacting variables, where income matters only with debt patterns, employment history, and local conditions. Post-hoc tools can list important factors, but different methods rank them differently and none proves why this person, causally, was denied. A mandated explanation thus risks becoming a polished rationalization: reassuring enough to defeat a challenge, too simplified to reveal a bad proxy, coding error, or discrimination hidden in interactions.
[C1.3] Rights built on fictions do not discipline power; they launder it.
[C1.4] Second, courts are the wrong institution to design this regime. Defining significant, automated, principal, and understandable — while balancing trade secrets, privacy, fraud prevention, and security — requires technical standards and ongoing updating. Case-by-case litigation produces inconsistent duties across districts and sectors, discovered only after a firm is sued. Legislatures and regulators can instead strengthen tools that target real harms: accurate-data and dispute rights, adverse-action notices, anti-discrimination enforcement, and independent outcome audits testing whether a system excludes protected groups.
[C1.5] Those ask whether the decision was wrong, not whether a vendor can narrate it.
[C1.6] Finally, even limited disclosure creates collateral damage. Revealing decisive factors helps sophisticated applicants game credit, tenancy, hiring, and health screens, while less savvy people gain little. It exposes fraud signals and proprietary systems despite redactions judges are ill-equipped to calibrate. Compliance and litigation risk will push hospitals, small landlords, and employers to delay useful automation or return to human discretion — often more biased, no more explainable, and conveniently outside this right.
[C1.7] The remedy for wrongful exclusion is enforceable accuracy, contestability, and non-discrimination imposed by accountable lawmakers. A judicial right to explanation substitutes narrative for proof and should be rejected.

[P2.1] The opposition’s central mistake is to treat explanation as a substitute for proof. It is the condition that makes proof possible. A rejected tenant who learns that a file attributes someone else’s eviction to her can obtain records, dispute the input, and show the court what failed. [P2.2] [C1.2] answers a demand I never made. The right does not require a complete causal map of hundreds of interactions. It requires the principal reasons actually used, the material information relied upon, and enough specificity to contest them. Complexity may shape how reasons are expressed; it cannot make “too complex to justify” an acceptable answer when someone loses housing or healthcare.
[P2.3] And an explanation is evidence to test, not immunity from challenge. A false or incomplete account can be exposed through later evidence; silence gives the affected person no target at all.
[P2.4] The institutional objection also collapses into an argument for legislative improvement, not judicial inaction. Courts need not set technical standards for every model to decide whether a particular denial received a minimally usable justification. Nor are [C1.4]’s alternatives truly alternatives: data-dispute rights need disclosed data, adverse-action notices are themselves explanations, and audits cannot tell an individual which error ruined her application. Courts can supply that floor while lawmakers build above it.
[P2.5] Finally, the collateral-damage case attacks disclosures outside this motion. Individual reasons need not reveal thresholds, fraud signals, source code, or proprietary weights. “Outdated employment record” neither helps sophisticated applicants manipulate a system nor exposes a trade secret. Returning to opaque human discretion is not a safeguard; it merely changes who can avoid giving reasons.
[P2.6] The opposition offers a choice between polished narrative and enforceable rights. That choice is false. Explanation makes enforceable rights usable—and a decision that cannot be explained at that basic level should not escape scrutiny merely because it was automated.

[P4.1] A1: Neither post-hoc ranking automatically satisfies the right. The court does not crown a preferred explainability algorithm; it requires a good-faith, decision-specific account of the material grounds and information on which the operator relied, with relevant limitations disclosed. If incompatible accounts show that no stable ground can be identified, that undermines the decision’s contestability—it does not justify leaving the applicant guessing. Assessing that account’s adequacy and candor is ordinary judicial evaluation of evidence, not redesigning the model.
[P4.2] A2: No automatic reversal follows merely because an explanation is inadequate. The proportionate remedy is an order requiring a compliant explanation, correction where testing reveals error, and appropriate procedural relief such as costs. A demonstrably sound decision can therefore stand. But the right still supplies enforceable recourse against unjustified secrecy, and “demonstrably accurate” often becomes demonstrable only after the affected person can test the inputs and grounds.
[P4.3] Procedural duties do not become pointless whenever an outcome later proves correct.
[P4.4] This exposes the opposition’s central equivocation. When explanation is useful, they rename it an accuracy or disclosure right; when no legislature has supplied that right, they offer future regulation. The motion concerns precisely the gap in between. An eviction record helps the tenant only if she knows it was used against her; an outcome audit cannot give her that individual notice.
[P4.5] Their dilemma is therefore false. The right can require both material information and its role in the adverse decision without demanding a complete causal anatomy of the model. Courts supply a minimum floor, not a comprehensive technical code.
[P4.6] Finally, possible cumulative gaming does not justify certain blindness. Redaction can protect fraud-sensitive details while preserving an actionable ground. The decisive comparison is not narrative versus proof, but proof that can begin versus proof foreclosed. When consequential automation cannot provide even contestable reasons, the burden should fall on its deployment—not on the person excluded.
