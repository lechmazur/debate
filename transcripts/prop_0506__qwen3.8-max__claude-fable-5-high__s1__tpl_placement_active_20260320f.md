[P1.1] The proposition creates a narrow negligence rule, not strict liability. Platforms are not liable merely because a scam exists, or because an AI imitation is posted. They become liable only after repeated, verified reports identify a synthetic impersonation scheme and they still fail to act. That knowledge requirement is the decisive line.
[P1.2] It is ordinary tort logic: specific knowledge plus control creates a duty.
[P1.3] AI impersonation fraud is different from ordinary abuse because it is industrialized and platform-dependent. A cloned voice, a stolen face, or a fake verified profile can be replicated instantly, pushed through recommendation systems, and monetized through ads, links, or payment rails. The victim often cannot identify the scammer, but the platform controls the account, the distribution, and the repeat-offense data. It is also the practical gatekeeper, because victims usually cannot identify or sue the anonymous fraudster.
[P1.4] If the platform receives multiple credible reports—say, from the impersonated person, the bank, or victims with transaction records—and does nothing, it is no longer a passive host; it is allowing a known fraud to continue.
[P1.5] Civil liability is the right tool because damages do two things that fines and moderation pledges do not. First, they compensate real losses: drained savings, ruined credit, emotional harm. Second, they make ignoring verified reports more expensive than addressing them. A platform that can avoid liability by suspending the account, deboosting the scam, preserving evidence, and blocking obvious re-creation will do so.
[P1.6] That is the incentive structure we want.
[P1.7] The opponent will warn about overremoval and impossible scale. But the motion targets fraud, not satire, and targets verified repetition, not one unverified complaint. Platforms already make similar judgments for copyright takedowns, child-safety reporting, and repeated harassment. Asking them to act on confirmed impersonation scams is not asking them to police everything; it is asking them to stop enabling a known harm once they have been shown the proof.

Degrade the reporting pipeline, route complaints through opaque forms, never confirm receipt, never build the "repeat-offense data" PRO celebrates. Knowledge-conditioned liability creates the moderator's dilemma — the platforms that investigate most diligently accumulate the most legal exposure, while the negligent ones stay judgment-proof through ignorance.
[C1.4] The motion rewards exactly the behavior it means to deter.

Payment rails and banks sit at the choke point where fraud becomes loss; they hold the transaction records.
[C1.6] Making the platform the deep-pocket defendant converts every fraud loss into speculative litigation over what a trust-and-safety team "should have" connected, with vague standards — "repeated," "ignored" — decided years later by juries.

[P2.1] My opponent claims this regime “punishes knowledge.” But the proposition does not punish verification; it punishes refusal. If a platform receives repeated, verified reports—say the impersonated executive’s team, bank chargebacks, and victim screenshots all point to the same cloned-voice account—and it lets the account keep running ads, liability attaches. If it investigates, suspends, deboosts, preserves records, and blocks clones, it has done what the duty requires. That is not a moderator’s dilemma; it is a safe harbor for reasonable care.
[P2.2] “Verified reports” is not mystical. Platforms already authenticate identity signals, trusted-flaggers, payment-linked accounts, repeat-offense patterns, and matching hashes. Verification can mean a matching account, proven identity or loss, and multiple independent reports converging—so isolated, strategic complaints do not trigger liability. The rule does not demand certainty after one complaint; it demands a reasonable response after repeated, corroborated notice.
[P2.3] The DMCA analogy overreaches. Copyright takedowns often involve fair use and competing ownership. AI impersonation fraud is narrower: a fake account using someone’s name, face, or voice to extract money. When the warning is repeated and corroborated, the risk of error is lower, and the cost of error is asymmetric.
[P2.4] Taking down a genuine scam account is a modest burden; leaving it live can drain savings. If a platform removes too little, victims suffer; if it removes too much, a scammer loses a fake account. That comparison favors acting.
[P2.5] Nor are banks the only right defendant. Banks can freeze a transfer after the money leaves; platforms control the fake profile, recommendation feed, messaging, ad tools, and account resurrection. They can stop impersonation at the source. Payment rails cannot unpublish a cloned face.
[P2.6] Finally, regulatory standards and civil damages are complements. Standards can define process; damages compensate victims and make indifference costly. My opponent offers no compensation and no reason platforms should profit from distribution while ignoring verified fraud.
