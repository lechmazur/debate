[P1.1] The board decides whether a large public company closes a plant, levers up for a buyback, or starves training to hit this quarter's number. The people who will live with that choice are almost never in the room. Directors answer to owners who can sell tomorrow and to executives who control what those directors see. Knowledge from the floor, safety, and the firm's long-run health are systematically underweighted.
[P1.2] Require large publicly traded companies to reserve a significant share of board seats for worker-elected representatives, and you correct that failure at the vote. The seats must be elected by the workforce and numerous enough to matter. Advisory councils and management-chosen employee directors let incumbents imitate voice without sharing power.
[P1.3] Worker directors know the firm from the inside. Outside directors mostly see the version management prepares. Someone on the line can stop a plan that is elegant on paper and disastrous in practice, before the write-down, the injury, or the lost customer. That is an independent channel into a board otherwise captive to its own agenda.
[P1.4] They also lengthen the horizon. Capital exits in an afternoon. Workers cannot cash out their skills, their pensions, or the town built around the plant. Their presence checks the extraction of cash through debt and buybacks while the enterprise thins out.
[P1.5] Codetermination in Germany has left large manufacturers globally competitive while forcing labor into strategy before the decision, not after. The two goals are compatible.
[P1.6] Workers create the value and absorb the shock, and limited liability already privileges the firm. They deserve a real governing share. Only workforce election, not CEO appointment, keeps it honest. Boards will not impose this on themselves.
[P1.7] Investors remain in the room. They simply lose the monopoly. A legal floor makes that real, and it defines performance as a firm that can still produce and compete in a decade.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes representation for governance. A public-company board is not a parliament where every constituency sends a delegate; it is a decision-making body that must act confidentially, quickly, and in the interest of the enterprise as a whole. Legally reserving “a significant percentage” of seats for worker-elected representatives changes what a director is: instead of one fiduciary duty, you create a bloc accountable to electors. That is the wrong reform if the goal is better decisions.
[C1.2] Start with accountability. Worker-elected directors owe their seats to the workforce, not the shareholders who own the residual claim or the company as a whole. When a plant must close, a merger must be negotiated, or a pension deficit must be confronted, those directors face a built-in conflict between the company’s interest and re-election by the group most directly affected. Some will handle it honorably; a legal structure should not depend on heroism.
[C1.3] Boards work because members deliberate frankly and own a collective decision. Constituency seats invite end-runs, leaks, and deadlock.
[C1.4] Then the design problem. “Significant percentage” is arbitrary. Who elects in a multinational with contractors, part-time staff, and operations across countries? Domestic employees? All workers?
[C1.5] Union members? A threshold large enough to matter can block urgent restructuring; small enough to be safe is symbolic. Law cannot calibrate firm by firm, and boards will game the boundary.
[C1.6] The German example [P1.5] does not carry the weight. Codetermination sits inside a different corporate system—two-tier boards, works councils, consensus labor relations—and even there it is contested. Compatibility in one institutional setting is not proof that a legal quota improves governance everywhere.
[C1.7] Workers’ knowledge and long-term stakes are real. But the remedy is information rights, strong works councils, employee ownership, and genuine consultation—not converting the board into a constituency assembly. Voice is valuable; this mandate purchases it by damaging the accountability and coherence that make boards useful. That is a bad trade.

[P4.1] A1: A workable statute is not a mystery formula. Reserve one-third of the seats—enough for committees and a recorded challenge, short of control—for directors elected by employees of the listed company and the subsidiaries it controls. That is the control test consolidation already uses. Contractor and franchise shells meet a substance-over-form rule: if the firm directs the work and keeps the output, those workers count, and artificial shifts cannot shrink the reserved share.
[P4.2] Firms game bright lines throughout corporate law; that is why the mandate looks through form. Calling one-third arbitrary only means a legislature chose a threshold, as securities law does constantly. A formula no firm can ever redraw is not a standard. It is a veto.
[P4.3] A2: The duty binds as it binds shareholder-elected directors, who also answer to people able to remove them. Personal liability for bad faith, conflict disclosure, injunctions, and staggered terms do not turn on who holds the ballot. Electoral pressure is real; we already live with it when activists threaten directors who refuse a buyback. Structure decides the hard case.
[P4.4] A minority cannot stop an efficient closure the rest of the board will carry. Her electorate can punish her vote. It cannot repeal the decision. Duty need not make her a hero for restructuring to occur.
[P4.5] It must keep process, confidentiality, and self-dealing honest—obligations courts already enforce. Apply CON's test honestly and independent directors fail it too: nomination and removal already sit with owners and incumbents.
[P4.6] Those answers close both exits. The share can be specified, and the duty can be enforced, without handing workers a veto. What is left is owner monopoly plus consultation that can be heard and ignored [C2.7]. On the buyback or the closure, people who cannot exit need the file challenged before the vote.
[P4.7] Investors remain, and they keep the majority. They lose only the sole franchise. That is the failure a legal floor corrects.
