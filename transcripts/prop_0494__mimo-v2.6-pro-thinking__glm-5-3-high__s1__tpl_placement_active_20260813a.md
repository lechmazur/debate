[C1.1] The proposition bundles two reforms and claims both make review "work better." Each one fails on its own mechanism, and together they make things worse.
[C1.2] Payment first. The problem peer review faces is not that reviewers are unpaid; it is that reviewer time is scarce and expertise is narrow. Adding money does not create more qualified reviewers — it changes who wants the job and why. A paid reviewer is not a better reviewer; they are a person doing a task for a fee, and the rational move under payment is to accept more assignments and spend the same hour on each.
[C1.3] Fee-for-service review invites exactly the industrialized, low-effort report the opponent says payment will eliminate — the difference is that now journals can formally demand it. Meanwhile, the money has to come from somewhere: journal revenue, which means author charges or library subscriptions, which means another toll on the same researchers being "compensated." It is a laundering operation, not a fix. And it touches the deeper issue that much of the willingness to review carefully is professional duty, not wage labor.
[C1.4] Put a price on it and you redefine it as piecework.
[C1.5] Openness is worse. Anonymity is not a loophole in peer review; it is the mechanism that buys candor. The junior researcher asked to judge a powerful senior figure's manuscript can say what she actually thinks only because her name is not attached. Publish signed reports, and the rational strategy is the safe, bland, hedged report — praise the framing, request more citations, accept.
[C1.6] Retaliation in hiring, grants, and conference acceptances is not hypothetical paranoia; it is the ambient condition of academic life. The bold, specific critique the opponent celebrates becomes career risk with no offsetting gain.
[C1.7] So the trade is this: in exchange for money that mostly recycles researchers' own funds and bylines that mostly punish honesty, we get paid-per-report incentives and diplomatic mush. Both reforms target symptoms while dismantling the two things review currently gets right — duty and candor.

What we have now is the same hour-per-report incentive with none of the levers: an unpaid reviewer maximizes throughput too, and no editor can dock pay that doesn't exist.
[P2.2] CON romanticizes "professional duty," but duty is precisely what is failing — the decline rates, the ghosted invitations, the two-line dismissals.
