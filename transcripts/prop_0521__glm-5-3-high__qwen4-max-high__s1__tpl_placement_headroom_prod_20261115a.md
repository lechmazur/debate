[P1.1] When a nurse finishes a hospital-funded certification in critical care, or a welder completes an employer-paid safety credential, something odd happens: that skill walks out the door with the worker, but the funding that produced it stays chained to the employer. If the worker leaves for another hospital or another fabrication shop, training credits, education stipends, and upskilling entitlements simply evaporate. That arrangement is not a market necessity. It is a policy choice, and it is the wrong one.
[P1.2] The core problem is lock-in. Training benefits that vanish on departure function as exit penalties. A worker weighing a better offer across the street must mentally write off thousands in education entitlements, which means employers can suppress wages below market rate because staying is artificially cheap relative to leaving. Portability breaks that leverage.
[P1.3] If training accounts follow the worker within the sector, competition for labor starts to reflect actual wages and conditions rather than hostage economics. Firms that treat workers well win them; firms that don't, lose them.
[P1.4] Second, sectoral portability aligns who pays with who benefits. Skills like phlebotomy, forklift operation, or cybersecurity fundamentals are sector-wide assets, not proprietary ones. Today's employer trains a worker the whole industry can use. Portable accounts simply make that explicit: contributions vest over time, and the balance moves to any same-sector role, much as retirement savings vest and follow workers already.
[P1.5] Nobody calls a vested 401(k) an outrageous confiscation of employer property, and nobody should call a vested training account one either.
[P1.6] Third, mobility plus training compounds. A worker who can change jobs without sacrificing development keeps accumulating credentials instead of restarting from zero. Sectors facing shortages, healthcare and trades foremost, gain a deeper, continuously upgraded labor pool.
[P1.7] Employers will warn about free-riding and poaching. Fair concerns, but they argue for design, like graduated vesting, not for keeping workers captive. The principle stands: earned development belongs to the person who earned it.
