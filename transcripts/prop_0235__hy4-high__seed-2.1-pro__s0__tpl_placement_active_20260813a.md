[P1.1] The choice is not between protection and abandonment. It is between a policy that keeps moving people and public money back into the path of water, and one that uses wealth to move them out before the next disaster.
[P1.2] Hard defenses are attractive because they preserve the map. On high-risk coastlines that map is the liability. Seawalls, levees, bulkheads and revetments do not remove surge, groundwater rise, or sea-level rise; they hold the line until overtopping or a larger storm exposes the same exposure again. Each rebuild is another public invoice, while dangerous lots stay valuable because government promises defense.
[P1.3] That is the trap: protect, subsidize, rebuild, repeat.
[P1.4] Managed retreat attacks the cause of loss: homes, roads, schools, hospitals and wastewater plants sit where water will keep reaching. A funded program can buy properties at pre-disaster values, relocate households near jobs and schools, keep communities together where possible, turn cleared land into wetlands or buffers, and bar new building in the hazard zone. Done early, it can be cheaper than serial disaster payments and less cruel than calling post-catastrophe flight a “choice.” Wealthy countries have the tax base and planning capacity to make that fair.
[P1.5] The equity objection cuts the other way. Unmanaged retreat is already happening: renters are displaced, richer owners rebuild, insurers withdraw, and poorer households inherit mold, debt and repeated evacuations. Publicly funded retreat can pay people before homes become unsellable, protect tenants, preserve social networks, and stop a shorefront minority from consuming disaster budgets. Restored marshes and dunes absorb surge instead of walls shifting erosion onto neighbors.
[P1.6] Walls may still serve dense assets during transition. But priority means the default investment. For high-risk coastlines that default should be planned relocation with compensation, not a promise to rebuild the same wall after every storm. A wall defends a property line; retreat protects lives, public finances, and the next generation from a known risk.

[C1.1] The proposal frames hard defenses as an endless, doomed cycle, but that misrepresents both modern coastal engineering and the irreversible harms of prioritizing retreat as a default. Adaptive, well-integrated hard defenses—from sea gates and setback levees to hybrid structures paired with restored marsh buffers—do not merely hold the line until failure. They protect dense, irreplaceable coastal assets that cannot be picked up and moved: global economic hubs, working waterfronts supporting hundreds of thousands of fishing, trade, and tourism jobs, Indigenous and historic communities with centuries of continuous ties to shoreline land that relocation severs permanently.
[C1.2] Prioritizing retreat writes off these places by default, treating all high-risk coastlines as disposable rather than distinguishing between scattered, low-density floodplain development and connected, high-value communities worth protecting. On cost, incrementally upgrading adaptive defenses as sea level projections sharpen is far cheaper than buying out millions of properties, extending new infrastructure to relocated communities, and writing off trillions in existing coastal investment. The “protect, subsidize, rebuild” trap proponents decry is a failure of bad zoning and misaligned insurance subsidies, not an inherent flaw of defense.
[C1.3] We can build adaptive, future-rated hard defenses for established communities while banning new high-risk construction, ending unwise development subsidies, and requiring risk-priced flood insurance—without forcing households to abandon homes and social networks on a planner’s timeline. Nor does prioritizing retreat deliver the equity its supporters promise. Buyout programs consistently undervalue low-income and working-class properties, push displaced households farther from jobs and public transit, and rarely keep communities intact even with good intentions. Hard defenses deliver universal, predictable protection to everyone in a defended zone, rather than offering voluntary buyouts that let wealthier owners take the money and leave marginalized households stranded with collapsing property values.

We do not need a retreat default to end foolish rebuilding: we can ban new high-risk construction, end wasteful development subsidies, invest in adaptive defenses for established communities, and reserve narrow, voluntary buyouts only for the most scattered, unprotectable parcels.
