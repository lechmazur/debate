[P1.1] Body cameras were sold as a technological shortcut to accountability: deter misconduct, provide an objective record, resolve complaints, discipline wrongdoers, and rebuild public trust. The proposition does not claim they achieved nothing. It claims they delivered less than those transformative expectations. They did, because recording an encounter is not the same as holding anyone responsible for it.
[P1.2] Accountability requires a chain: misconduct must be captured, the footage preserved and disclosed, reviewers must interpret it fairly, and institutions must impose consequences. Body cameras strengthen only the first link—and even that link is unreliable. Officers may fail to activate cameras, activate them late, position them poorly, or encounter situations where sightlines and audio omit crucial context. A chest-level camera cannot show everything the officer saw, much less fear, intent, prior events, or actions outside the frame.
[P1.3] Footage therefore often relocates disputes rather than resolves them: people now argue over what an incomplete video means.
[P1.4] More importantly, the same institutions being scrutinized commonly control activation rules, retention, access, redaction, and internal review. If supervisors excuse violations, prosecutors decline action, discipline is weak, or footage reaches the public only after delay and litigation, additional evidence does not become additional accountability. Cameras can even legitimize existing decisions: ambiguous footage is treated as vindication, while the mere existence of video creates an appearance of transparency.
[P1.5] Nor is deterrence automatic. It depends on officers believing misconduct will be visible, recognized, and punished. Once they learn that policy violations bring little consequence—or that camera perspective often favors the wearer—the behavioral pressure fades. Meanwhile, public expectations rise, so missing or withheld footage can deepen distrust.
[P1.6] The decisive distinction is between documentation and institutional change. Cameras can clarify some encounters, dismiss some false complaints, and support some prosecutions. But advocates expected routine deterrence, objective truth, and systemic reform. A device that records without guaranteeing disclosure, interpretation, or consequence cannot meet that promise.
[P1.7] Body cameras added evidence; they did not reliably add the power, independence, or will required for accountability.
