[P1.1] Prohibition is organized crime's business plan. It outlaws a product millions already use, bans legitimate competitors, and hands price, supply, and territory to armed groups. Legalizing and regulating cannabis nationally reverses that transfer: the state sets the rules, licensed sellers take the customers, and cartels lose a market prohibition gifted them.
[P1.2] First, it cuts a high-volume source of easy money. Cannabis is bulky, widely demanded, and simple to grow, making it ideal for financing transport routes, lookouts, weapons, and bribes. A legal market that is affordable, accessible, and quality-controlled gives consumers no reason to fund a dealer. Every pharmacy, cooperative, or licensed shop sale is revenue denied to an illegal network and tax revenue gained for enforcement against predatory crime.
[P1.3] Cartels can try to undercut, but they cannot match legal safety, consistency, and availability without abandoning the secrecy their business requires.
[P1.4] Second, regulation frees a state wasted on the wrong war. Police, prosecutors, and prisons across the region are clogged with users, small growers, and low-level sellers — easy arrests that leave commanders untouched. Ending cannabis prohibition removes countless opportunities for police extortion and cartel bribery, and lets scarce resources target homicide, cocaine trafficking, extortion, and human trafficking. It also stops branding young, poor people with criminal records that make cartel recruitment easier.
[P1.5] Third, legal licensing breaks armed control in producing regions. Under prohibition, rural farmers in places like Mexico, Colombia, and Paraguay must sell cheaply to whoever has guns, then face eradication or violence for doing so. Bringing those growers into a traceable legal system with permits, fair buyers, and banking access weakens the coercion, intelligence, and local taxation cartels extract from the countryside.
[P1.6] This will not make organized crime vanish, and it does not need to. The question is whether governments keep protecting its cannabis franchise or compete it away. Legalization weakens cartels financially, institutionally, and territorially — prohibition strengthens them on all three.

Cocaine shipments are lucrative but episodic and risky; domestic cannabis sales pay drivers, lookouts, growers, and bribed officials week after week. [P2.2] That is weakening, and weakening is the motion.
[P2.3] The export argument also proves too little. Legalization need not eliminate every cross-border sale; it must move large domestic demand in Mexico, Brazil, and Colombia from dealers to licensed sellers. Licensing also aids enforcement: registered, traceable production makes unlicensed fields stand out, freeing police to target export networks instead of consumers. A cartel pushed into a smaller, riskier export niche is weaker than one owning the entire home market.
[P2.4] The avocado example [C1.4] cuts the other way. Cartels may extort legal crops, but prohibition gives them total control of cannabis. An illegal grower cannot call police, use banks, enforce contracts, or choose another buyer without risking arrest; the cartel is supplier, customer, court, and threat. Legalization breaks that monopoly with multiple buyers, documented payments, permits, cooperatives, and the ability to report coercion without confessing a crime.
[P2.5] A business vulnerable to extortion is still harder to own than one existing only by cartel permission.
[P2.6] Weak state capacity likewise argues against prohibition. Prohibition is the most demanding option: police every user, grower, and street sale while generating bribes that corrupt the police. Regulation narrows the task to licensing and auditing visible firms, and funds enforcement against violence. Keeping cannabis illegal does not avoid capture; it guarantees the market starts captured.
[P2.7] The alternative preserves the domestic franchise, rural coercion, and wasted enforcement that legalization directly attacks.
