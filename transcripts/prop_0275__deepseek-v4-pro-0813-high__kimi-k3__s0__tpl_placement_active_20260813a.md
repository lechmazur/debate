[P1.1] A $139-per-night listing should not become a $467 weekend stay because a $120 cleaning fee, a resort fee, and a “no guests after 10 p.m.” rule appear only at the payment screen. Vacation-rental platforms currently withhold some of the most material terms until the user has selected a property and reached checkout or request-to-book. That design is not neutral: it exploits sunk-cost effort and makes genuine price comparison nearly impossible.
[P1.2] The core harm is drip pricing. The nightly rate is the number shown in search results and comparison pages. But mandatory cleaning and resort fees are part of the cost of staying, not optional add-ons. When they are revealed late, a $140 listing can look cheaper than a $160 listing even though the first costs more after a $180 cleaning fee.
[P1.3] Consumers sort, click, and plan around false low prices. Requiring early display simply moves information the platform already holds from the last step to the first point of comparison. This is a transparency rule, not a price control.
[P1.4] House rules are often worse than hidden fees because they can make the rental unusable. A family needs to know before requesting that a property bans children under 12, prohibits cooking after certain hours, or forbids service animals. A traveler with mobility needs needs to know about stair-only access before committing. These terms are binding, and hiding them until checkout turns a suitability question into a pressured decision.
[P1.5] A user who has already entered payment details should not learn for the first time that the property forbids the very reason for the trip.
[P1.6] The standard should be informed consent before material commitment. Clicking into checkout or request-to-book is exactly that point of material commitment. If a term is important enough to enforce against the guest later, it is important enough to show before that step. The data already exists; the only real effect of the rule is to stop platforms from advertising one price while charging another.

[C1.2] First, the motion misses its own target. PRO's core harm is drip pricing in search: the $140 listing that looks cheaper than the $160 one. That comparison happens on the results page, long before checkout. Requiring disclosure only "before users click into checkout" leaves search rankings, map views, and comparison pages untouched — travelers still sort by misleading nightly rates.
[C1.3] By PRO's own metric, the mandate regulates the wrong screen.
[C1.4] Second, "house rules" is an unbounded category. It covers pet bans and noise curfews, but also trash instructions, thermostat quirks, and parking notes — often dozens of items per listing. Forcing all of that before checkout yields one of two outcomes: a wall of text nobody reads, which informs no one, or officials deciding which rules count as "material," which puts regulators in the business of editing every host's listing. Real suitability needs — children, pets, step-free access — are far better served by structured search filters, which platforms already provide and refine.
[C1.5] Third, the mandate is late to a problem the market is already solving. Leading platforms have introduced total-price display and upfront fee breakdowns because travelers demanded them, and general consumer-protection law already reaches deceptive drip pricing. A statute freezing one interface moment cannot adapt to the next design, and compliance costs fall hardest on small hosts before being passed back to guests.
[C1.6] Fourth, the "material commitment" framing is wrong. Clicking into checkout commits nothing: no money moves, and abandoned carts are routine. Fees revealed at checkout, before payment, still permit cost-free exit. The sunk cost is a few minutes of browsing, not lock-in.
[C1.7] The motion targets the wrong screen, buries guests in fine print, and duplicates tools already working. That is not consumer protection; it is interface micromanagement.
