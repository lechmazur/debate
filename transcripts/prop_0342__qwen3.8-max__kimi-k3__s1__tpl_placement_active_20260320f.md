[P1.1] The proposition wins on one simple question: when a skill is necessary for every student’s safety, citizenship, and work, should schools guarantee it or merely offer it? AI and manipulation-resistant media literacy are no longer niche topics. Students already live inside algorithmic feeds, generative text, synthetic images, automated scams, and AI-assisted homework. If schools treat these realities as an elective, they are choosing to leave preparedness to luck, parental resources, and individual teachers.
[P1.2] Core status matters because it changes institutional incentives. A core subject gets scheduled time, trained staff, sequenced learning, and assessment. An optional module gets a poster, an assembly, or a half-day workshop. That difference is decisive.
[P1.3] Recognizing a manipulated video, understanding why a recommendation system amplifies outrage, or knowing when to trust an AI answer is not learned from one lesson. It requires repeated practice: checking provenance, testing claims, spotting emotional triggers, comparing model outputs, and learning the limits of automated judgment.
[P1.4] The civic stakes are especially clear. Democratic communities depend on citizens who can tell evidence from fabrication. Deepfakes, impersonation scams, microtargeted political ads, and bot-driven outrage are not hypothetical; they are ordinary features of the current information environment. A student who cannot resist manipulation is not merely a weaker learner; they are a more exploitable voter, consumer, and neighbor.
[P1.5] Making this literacy optional means some students get protection while others do not.
[P1.6] Opponents may say the curriculum is full or the technology changes too fast. But core does not require chasing every new app. It teaches durable capacities: data awareness, model skepticism, verification, privacy, and responsible use. Schools already make mathematics, writing, and civic education core because they are foundational to participation.
[P1.7] AI literacy is now part of that foundation. The issue is not whether schools should teach it, but whether they should teach it seriously and to everyone.

[C1.1] The question here is not whether AI literacy matters. It is whether making it a core subject is the right instrument — and that is where the proposition fails.
[C1.2] Start with the arithmetic. Timetables are zero-sum: a new core subject takes hours from reading, mathematics, science, and writing — subjects where large numbers of students already finish school below proficiency. A system that is failing to deliver its existing core should not answer every new social problem by expanding it. The promised "scheduled time, trained staff, and assessment" is exactly the cost: those hours and teachers are subtracted from somewhere, and in practice they are subtracted from the students already furthest behind.
[C1.3] Second, core status is the wrong technology for fast-moving content. Core subjects run on stable syllabi, approved materials, standardized tests, and multi-year curriculum cycles. AI respects none of that. A syllabus written this year will describe obsolete tools by the time it clears approval, and deepfake-spotting rules taught to a test will age like milk.
[C1.4] What core status actually produces is a frozen snapshot of today's technology, drilled for an exam. The durable capacities the proposition names — verification, skepticism, data awareness — are not a new subject. They are the oldest parts of history, science, and mathematics, in need of reinforcement rather than duplication.
[C1.5] Third, isolation defeats transfer. A student who aces "spot the manipulated video" in a standalone class still shares outrage bait on their own feed, because the skill does not travel from worksheet to feed. Manipulation resistance is learned in context: weighing a historical source, checking a scientific claim, noticing an argument's emotional levers.
[C1.6] The alternative to core status is not neglect. It is embedding these capacities where they genuinely transfer, paired with flexible modules that can be revised annually rather than decennially. The proposition offers the appearance of protection as one more tested subject. Schools should deliver the real thing.
