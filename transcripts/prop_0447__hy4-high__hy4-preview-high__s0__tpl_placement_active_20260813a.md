[P1.1] The central question is not whether platforms or subcontracting are useful. They can be. It is who carries the social cost when a large lead company chooses them. Too often it is not the firm that designs the chain, sets the price, receives the service, and holds the bargaining power.
[P1.2] A courier, warehouse picker, cleaner, or moderator may sit behind an app or thin intermediary while value flows upward to a multinational client. That structure can reduce employer contributions, weaken contribution records, and push sickness, unemployment, pensions, and income instability onto public systems.
[P1.3] Requiring large companies to pay a higher share of social contributions when they use those channels prices flexibility honestly. A firm can still use just-in-time labor; it simply may not make that labor cheaper by shifting insurance costs to workers, small contractors, or taxpayers. That obligation belongs at the lead firm: it chooses the outsourcing model, can audit the chain, and can absorb the cost across a large balance sheet. Small businesses and genuine occasional contractors are not the target.
[P1.4] Dominant buyers that systematize fragmented labor are.
[P1.5] This is also a single-market issue. Without EU-wide responsibility, a company can source labor through a platform or chain where contribution exposure is lowest and sell into richer markets. Compliant direct employers then subsidize rivals who turn labor into a procurement line. EU rules restrain social dumping; this is the same logic.
[P1.6] Equal services should not be cheaper because the people producing them carry thinner protection.
[P1.7] The likely objection is cost and innovation. But a saving produced by moving a cost off the balance sheet is not productivity; it is a transfer. A retailer that squeezes a subcontractor until contributions are thin is not more efficient; it is better at shifting risk. That higher required share narrows that artificial advantage, encourages simpler chains and verified employment status, and restores a basic bargain: firms that profit from labor help insure it.

[C1.1] The proposition confuses the channel with the harm. My opponent says a large firm may not make labor cheaper by shifting insurance costs [P1.3]. But using a platform or subcontractor often does not shift those costs: the intermediary already pays contributions, or the worker is genuinely self-employed and pays directly. The real injury is unpaid or underpaid contributions, not outsourcing itself.
[C1.2] A rule that makes a large company pay more simply because it uses an intermediary taxes organizational form, not abuse. A hospital contracting a cleaning firm that already pays full contributions would owe a higher share; the cleaner gets nothing extra. That is a transfer, not protection.
[C1.3] The levy will not stay where PRO imagines. Large firms can absorb some cost, but they will also renegotiate, automate, or shorten chains. Platforms facing higher client charges cut service fees or raise consumer prices; subcontractors face margin pressure and hire fewer people. The workers PRO wants to help—low-margin contractors, new freelancers, part-time couriers—can lose volume or see net pay fall.
[C1.4] Flexible work becomes more expensive to offer, so less of it is offered. If margins thin enough, some activity moves further informal or outside the EU, reducing the contribution base. A rule meant to stop a race to the bottom can instead remove the bottom rungs.
[C1.5] Judged by the standard my opponent invokes—fair competition and real insurance—the better test is whether contributions are actually paid and whether the lead firm exercises control. Enforce misclassification, require chain transparency, and impose joint liability for proven unpaid contributions. That targets non-payment directly. A blanket EU surcharge on channel use is blunter: it ignores national social systems, invites definitional games, and punishes efficient specialization.
[C1.6] Equal services should not be cheaper through abuse, but they should not become artificially expensive through a proxy tax on contracting. The EU should attack the abuse, not the architecture.
