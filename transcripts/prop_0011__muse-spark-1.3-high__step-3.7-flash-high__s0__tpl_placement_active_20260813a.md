[P1.1] Public education funding is meant to educate children, not to protect school systems. When a low-income child is trapped in a violent or chronically failing school, telling her family to wait for district reform is cruelty disguised as prudence. A child gets only one childhood; she cannot replay third grade after a ten-year turnaround plan. Wealthy parents already exercise choice by buying homes in good districts or paying private tuition.
[P1.2] Vouchers extend that same freedom downward. That equity alone justifies expansion.
[P1.3] Mixed test scores do not refute that case because test scores are not what many families are seeking. Parents use choice to find safety, discipline, smaller classes, help for dyslexia or autism, religious or moral fit, or simply a school where their child is known and expected to graduate. Programs in cities like Milwaukee and Cleveland have long shown high parental satisfaction even when reading and math effects bounced around. Graduation, college persistence, staying out of violence or dropout matter more to a life than a one-year test bump.
[P1.4] Judging all that by a single standardized score is like judging hospitals only by weight loss.
[P1.5] The resource-loss objection gets the economics backwards. Public schools do not own children as revenue streams. If money follows the child, districts have fewer pupils to serve and can adjust staffing and buildings over time. More importantly, monopolies stagnate.
[P1.6] When schools must earn enrollment, they respond with better safety, communication, and offerings. Shielding bureaucracies from that pressure by locking in captive students harms exactly the poor neighborhoods opponents claim to defend.
[P1.7] Finally, a plural society needs plural schools — Catholic, Jewish, Islamic, classical, Montessori, arts-focused. Families differ legitimately about the good life. The state should fund the education of the citizen, then trust parents to choose its form. Uncertain averages cannot outweigh liberty, fit, and equal opportunity.

[C1.1] PRO’s opening rests on a false choice: that we must choose between protecting school systems and serving children. The truth is that expanding vouchers even with mixed evidence actively harms the most vulnerable kids by siphoning resources from the only guaranteed educational option available to all. PRO ignores that voucher programs systematically exclude or discourage the highest-need students: those with severe disabilities, unstable housing, or parents who lack the time, language fluency, or bureaucratic savvy to navigate complex application processes.
[C1.2] Mixed academic results are not a trivial side note—they prove vouchers are an unproven, high-risk experiment that diverts limited public funds from systems that still have a legal obligation to serve every child, even those no private school will accept. In states that expanded voucher eligibility, public districts were forced to cut special education aides, mental health counselors, and after-school programs to cover budget gaps, while private voucher schools cherry-picked easier-to-educate students and faced minimal accountability. There are well-documented cases of voucher schools closing without warning mid-semester, or refusing to accommodate students with autism, leaving families with no fallback and taxpayers on the hook for failed private ventures.
[C1.3] PRO claims competition improves schools, but that only works if all operators play by the same rules. Public schools are bound by civil rights laws, state curriculum standards, and teacher certification requirements; most private voucher schools are not. When public money flows to unregulated private operators, we get segregation by income, religion, and ability, not the equitable choice PRO promises.
[C1.4] Finally, PRO’s liberty argument ignores that vouchers do not expand freedom for all—they erode the right of every child to access a well-resourced local public school, and force taxpayers to fund private religious instruction that many find at odds with their core values. Expanding vouchers despite mixed results and clear resource risks is not bold reform—it is a deliberate transfer of public wealth to private interests at the expense of the students who need public schools most.
