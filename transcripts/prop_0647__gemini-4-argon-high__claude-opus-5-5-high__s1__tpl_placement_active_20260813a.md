[P1.1] For seventy years, cities treated traffic like a plumbing problem: if the pipe backs up, make it wider. That experiment failed. Urban freeways are not pipes; they are physical barriers occupying the most valuable land in a metropolis. As aging mid-century highways reach the end of their lifespans, cities should remove urban freeways far more often than they widen them.
[P1.2] First, widening urban freeways fails on its own terms. Because of induced demand, adding lanes in a dense metro area does not cure congestion; it invites more car trips, pulls riders off transit, and subsidizes sprawl until the new lanes fill right back up. Worse, a wider freeway simply dumps more vehicles onto constrained downtown surface streets that cannot expand without demolishing the city itself.
[P1.3] By contrast, when cities have removed urban freeway segments—such as San Francisco’s Embarcadero, Milwaukee’s Park East, or Rochester’s Inner Loop—predicted traffic apocalypses failed to materialize. Through-traffic routed onto outer beltways, local trips dispersed across a restored street grid, and marginal trips shifted to other modes.
[P1.4] Second, removal reverses a massive fiscal and economic loss. Dense, connected land is a city’s greatest asset. An urban freeway consumes vast swaths of prime real estate that pay zero property taxes, house no residents, and depress the value of surrounding blocks. Widening spends billions of public dollars to condemn even more tax-generating property.
[P1.5] Replacing an aging highway with a surface boulevard is often cheaper than rebuilding elevated structures and unlocks acres of land for housing, commerce, and parks that pay permanent fiscal dividends.
[P1.6] Finally, removal corrects a deep civic harm. Mid-century planners routinely routed urban highways through low-income and minority neighborhoods, severing local economies and concentrating toxic exhaust, brake dust, and noise where families live. Widening pushes concrete and pollution even closer to homes and schools. Inside the urban core, adding lanes is a proven failure; reclaiming the city’s fabric should be the rule.
