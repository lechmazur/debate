[P1.1] Asylum decisions are matters of life and death. Yet states are increasingly delegating them to automated systems whose criteria remain hidden even from the applicants whose futures they destroy. A global minimum standard for algorithmic transparency is not a technical luxury; it is the essential precondition for due process in the twenty-first century.
[P1.2] Without transparency, error and bias become invisible and unchallengeable. When an algorithm rejects a visa, flags a refugee as a security risk, or ranks an asylum claim as low credibility, the affected person must know what data was used and what logic was applied. Secrecy does not merely violate fairness; it makes meaningful remedy impossible. An applicant who cannot see the basis for a decision cannot correct a mistaken identity match, a mistranslated document, or a training bias that systematically disadvantages a particular nationality or language group.
[P1.3] In immigration contexts, where appellants often lack counsel and face language barriers, opacity is not neutrality—it is a structural barrier to justice.
[P1.4] A global standard is necessary because immigration enforcement is already a race to the bottom. States eager to externalize border control have strong incentives to adopt opaque automation that accelerates rejection while evading political and legal accountability. If one jurisdiction demands openness and another does not, vulnerable people are simply routed through the darker channel, and protection gaps widen. Only a binding international floor prevents this regulatory arbitrage and ensures that fundamental safeguards do not vanish at the border.
[P1.5] This standard need not prescribe specific source code. It requires only that automated decision-making be explainable, auditable, and disclosed to affected individuals in a form they can actually use to challenge outcomes. We already accept that evidence against a defendant must be revealed before punishment. The same principle must apply when software decides whether a family reaches safety or is returned to persecution.
[P1.6] The question is whether we will permit algorithms to judge refugees in the dark, or insist on the light that human rights law has always demanded.
