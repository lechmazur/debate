[P1.1] Schools can require algebra for graduation, yet send students into adulthood without guaranteeing they can compare two loan offers, read a paycheck, or build a budget. That is not neutrality toward family life; it is a decision to make essential instruction depend on family wealth, confidence, and available time. A required, assessed financial-literacy course replaces that lottery with a common baseline.
[P1.2] The case is not that a course will make every graduate wealthy or solve low wages, expensive housing, or medical debt. It is that unavoidable decisions should not be first encountered when mistakes become expensive. An eighteen-year-old may focus on a loan’s manageable monthly payment while missing its total repayment cost. A new worker may budget from gross pay rather than take-home pay.
[P1.3] A borrower may not understand how carrying a credit-card balance turns a purchase into a much larger one. Knowledge cannot remove every constraint, but it can reveal options, prompt better questions, and prevent avoidable harm.

Strip the rhetoric and the motion spends schools' scarcest resource — mandatory seat time — on an intervention whose defenders will not promise changed outcomes. The question is not whether financial knowledge matters; it is whether a graduation mandate delivers it in a form that survives contact with adulthood. It does not, for three reasons.
[C1.2] First, timing. We would teach seventeen-year-olds about mortgages, deductibles, and withholding years before most touch any of them. Knowledge learned long before use fades; that is why standalone courses reliably underperform expectations. We already require civics almost everywhere, and adult civic knowledge stays stubbornly thin — one-time coursework does not build durable habits.
[C1.3] The errors PRO lists, confusing gross and net pay, fixating on the monthly payment, are decision-moment errors, best met by guidance at the moment of signing, not a unit remembered dimly at twenty-six.
[C1.4] Second, opportunity cost. Graduation requirements are zero-sum; every mandated credit displaces an elective, an arts course, a career pathway, or credit recovery. PRO invokes students "already stretched by work or coursework" [P1.6] — then adds one more hurdle between exactly those students and a diploma. Raising dropout pressure to teach content that decays is not proportionate; it is perverse.
[C1.5] Third, the mechanism is the bluntest available. Interest, taxes, and budgeting are applications of arithmetic and reading; existing required math can absorb them without a new mandate. PRO frames the choice as requirement or nothing [P1.6], but the real comparison is mandate versus integration, and integration reaches every student without displacing anything.
[C1.6] What this motion guarantees is coverage, not competence: a checked box, a passed test, a district that can say it acted while loan terms stay just as confusing. Schools should certify what education can actually deliver, not perform concern. Vote CON.

[P2.5] Integration is CON’s strongest alternative, but it concedes the central point: these skills are teachable and belong in universal education.
