[P1.1] Transmission is the bottleneck. Wind, solar, batteries, factories, and heat pumps can be approved on paper, but if wires cannot reach load centers, they are delayed, curtailed, or never built. Faster line permitting is not an industry favor; it is the precondition for reliability, lower bills, and any credible clean-energy buildout. The choice is what sets the timetable when a regional network meets a local objection: the public need for a working grid, or one jurisdiction’s ability to stop the corridor.
[P1.2] Local veto sounds democratic until you picture a line. Routes cross towns, counties, watersheds, and parcels. If each can block, the rule becomes unanimity along the path. One no cancels benefits to hospitals, homes, factories, and generators beyond it.
[P1.3] That is not self-government over local effects; it is a serial veto over other people’s electricity. Towns should demand route changes, feasible burial, construction limits, habitat protection, host payments, and compensation. They should not convert those interests into a power to make a regional grid impossible.
[P1.4] Delay is not neutral. While a line sits in siting review, cheaper generation cannot interconnect, congestion raises costs, operators lean on older, dirtier plants, and storm hardening or interregional transfers arrive late. If one county blocks the only practical corridor, a wind-rich region is stranded while a city runs a gas peaker in a heat wave. That is veto in practice.
[P1.5] Diffuse harms rarely pack a hearing room, but ratepayers, downwind communities, and future customers pay them. Local veto spotlights the visible burden and hides larger ones.
[P1.6] Enforceable voice is the fairer replacement for veto: early notice, transparent route comparison, one reasoned regional decision, strict environmental standards, mitigation, and benefit agreements. Communities keep protection without a chokehold on shared infrastructure. Reform should prioritize faster transmission because the real comparison is not participation versus none; it is consultation and compensation versus expensive, unreliable, dirtier power, with every link able to say no.

[C1.1] The premise that we must sacrifice local veto to speed transmission mistakes a symptom for a cure. Speed without legitimacy creates fragility, not reliability. When corridors are imposed over community objection, the result is not a faster grid but a longer one: lawsuits, injunctions, and political backlash routinely add years to projects that were supposed to be expedited.
[C1.2] Consider the pattern in several recent high‑voltage proposals. Planners skirted town hearings, selected routes that ignored local geography, and then faced injunctions that halted construction for half a decade. The eventual lines were built, but at double the cost and with eroded public trust that now makes every new proposal harder to site. Faster on paper, slower in practice.
[C1.3] Local veto is not a selfish block; it is a democratic check on environmental injustice. Transmission corridors cut through farmland, wetlands, and historic neighborhoods. Communities that bear those burdens are the ones who notice soil erosion, noise, property‑value loss, and health impacts. Stripping their power concentrates decision‑making in distant agencies that lack the granular knowledge to choose the least harmful path.
[C1.4] When locals are excluded, planners often default to the cheapest, not the safest, alignment.
[C1.5] Moreover, the claim that “one no cancels benefits to hospitals” frames local objection as irrational. In reality, communities routinely negotiate route changes, undergrounding, compensation packages, and habitat mitigation. Those concessions produce lines that serve the region while honoring local interests. The alternative—centralized siting with a single “no” removed—produces uniform corridors that ignore better alternatives identified by residents, such as existing rights‑of‑way or less sensitive terrain.
[C1.6] Prioritizing speed over participation sacrifices durability for a mirage of efficiency. A grid built on coerced routes will face continual legal challenges, higher maintenance costs, and public resistance that undermines the very reliability the PRO touts. The wise reform is not to blunt local voice but to streamline genuine consultation, ensuring that faster buildout does not become faster failure.
