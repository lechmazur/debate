[P1.1] The relationship between employer and employee is fundamentally defined by an asymmetry of power. In the modern labor market, this imbalance is drastically widened by the covert deployment of automated decision systems (ADS). When algorithms dictate who gets hired and who gets fired, decisions altering the course of human lives are obscured behind a black box. Legal disclosure is not an administrative burden; it is the absolute baseline of procedural fairness.
[P1.2] First, disclosure is a prerequisite for accountability and the right to redress. Existing anti-discrimination laws are rendered toothless if victims of bias do not even know a machine made the decision. Automated tools are trained on historical data, meaning they frequently codify and scale systemic biases against marginalized groups—such as filtering out resume gaps caused by medical leave or misinterpreting speech patterns in automated video interviews. Without mandatory disclosure, candidates and workers cannot identify when they have been subjected to algorithmic discrimination, leaving them unable to seek legal recourse.
[P1.3] Second, mandatory disclosure acts as a crucial market corrective against the reckless adoption of unverified technology. Many current systems rely on pseudoscience—such as analyzing facial expressions or tracking arbitrary keystroke metrics—to evaluate human capability. When employers are legally forced to disclose their reliance on these systems, they are compelled to perform due diligence. The reputational risk of admitting they use automated systems forces companies to choose tools that are valid and fair, rather than hiding behind the plausible deniability of a third-party vendor.
[P1.4] We cannot allow the fundamental right to fair treatment to be automated away in secret. By requiring employers to disclose when algorithms meaningfully shape hiring and firing, we restore transparency, protect civil rights, and ensure that technology serves human progress rather than exploitation.
