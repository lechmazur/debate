[P1.1] Right now, when a neighbor’s life is destroyed by chronic bass at 3 AM or a landlord ignores repeated complaints about a party unit, the legal system often asks the victim to prove the unprovable. We rely on diaries, subjective testimony, and the occasional police report—evidence that courts and landlords routinely dismiss as anecdotal or biased. The result is a status quo where noise-makers win by default, and victims are trapped in their own homes without recourse.
[P1.2] Certified noise-monitoring evidence changes this calculus. A calibrated sound level meter operated by a qualified technician produces timestamped, objective decibel readings that cannot be gaslit away. When a tenant claims a neighbor violates quiet hours, or a landlord needs to prove a lease breach, this data transforms a credibility contest into a factual inquiry. It captures the pattern of abuse—the fifty nights of violations, not just the one night an officer happened to visit—that subjective complaints can never establish.
[P1.3] This matters most in rental disputes, where lease enforcement depends on documented violations. Landlords should welcome certified evidence because it protects them from false accusations while giving them the tools to evict genuinely disruptive tenants. Tenants need it because it levels the playing field against landlords who dismiss complaints as “just city noise.” In nuisance cases, objective measurements separate chronic, unreasonable interference from ordinary living sounds, giving judges a standard rather than a hunch.
[P1.4] The proposition asks only that we treat this reliable evidence more seriously than we currently do—not to create new rights, but to stop discounting proof that already exists. When we allow certified monitoring to carry weight, we replace he-said-she-said with what actually happened. That is not an invasion of privacy; it is the minimum requirement for justice in disputes where one party’s noise becomes another party’s prison.

[C1.1] A decibel meter measures sound. It does not measure nuisance. That gap is where this proposition collapses.
[C1.2] Start with the supposed objectivity. A certified meter placed in a complainant's apartment captures everything: the elevator, street traffic, plumbing, the complainant's own television. A spike at 3 AM proves a sound existed—not who made it, and not that it was unreasonable. In multi-unit buildings, source attribution is the entire dispute, and a timestamped number answers none of it.
[C1.3] Low-frequency bass, the paradigmatic complaint, is notoriously poorly captured by standard measurement methods. Certified evidence doesn't end the credibility contest; it relocates it to a more expensive battleground of dueling experts over calibration, placement, and weighting curves. The tenant who couldn't prove anything before now loses the same argument with a bigger bill.
[C1.4] Second, who gets to wield this tool? A certified technician with calibrated equipment costs real money and takes real time. The wealthier, more litigious neighbor generates admissible files; the poorer one still brings a diary. And monitoring aimed at a neighbor's home is surveillance—it captures voices, schedules, arguments, life.
[C1.5] Hand landlords certified monitoring and you hand them a turnkey eviction dossier against tenants they already want gone, selective enforcement laundered through science.
[C1.6] Third, the failures PRO describes are enforcement failures, and the law already has an answer where objectivity is genuinely needed: municipal noise officers and court-appointed experts, neutral parties with authority and accountability. The proposition doesn't merely admit existing proof; it deputizes private parties to audit each other continuously. Babies crying, footsteps in old buildings with thin floors—ordinary living becomes a logged violation, and every neighborly friction becomes a compliance case.
[C1.7] Current law asks whether interference was unreasonable in context—the character of the neighborhood, the hour, the duration, the alternatives. That holistic judgment is not a bug. Replacing it with a number doesn't produce justice. It produces neighbors surveilling neighbors, and punishment for the ordinary sounds of living near other people.

[P2.4] The privacy objection confuses continuous surveillance with targeted evidence-gathering for a specific dispute. A tenant recording noise in their own unit, with calibrated equipment, for a defined period, is not invading a neighbor's bedroom; they are measuring the intrusion into their own home.
[P2.5] Finally, access cuts both ways. Today, the wealthy hire acoustic consultants to defend against complaints; the poor bring diaries. [P2.6] The status quo doesn't protect the ordinary sounds of living; it protects the chronic abuser who knows victims cannot prove a pattern. We don't need fewer tools to document reality—we need more.
