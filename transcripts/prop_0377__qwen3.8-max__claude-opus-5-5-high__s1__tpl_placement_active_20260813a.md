[P1.1] A society with equal opportunity cannot treat a billionaire's heir and a talented child from a working-class home as if they are merely different contestants in the same race. Large inheritances are not ordinary income. They transfer portfolios of assets, control over firms, safety nets, political access, and time horizon. They convert one generation's success into permanent advantage for descendants.
[P1.2] That is why large inheritances should be taxed much more aggressively.
[P1.3] High earnings are a flow. They can reflect skill, effort, or luck, but they are usually taxed annually, can stop with retirement or recession, and rarely by themselves create a lineage. A surgeon earning very well can buy opportunities, but unless she can convert that income into durable dynastic capital, her children still need talent and effort. Inherited wealth is different: it is stock, compounded, protected by trusts and planning, and often escapes ordinary income taxation through unrealized gains and preferential capital treatment.
[P1.4] It creates an ownership class whose status depends less on contribution and more on ancestry.
[P1.5] The threat to opportunity is especially corrosive because it operates before the heir ever competes. Large inherited wealth buys elite education, unpaid internships, start-up capital, legal protection, and political influence. It also buys failure insurance: heirs can take risks that others cannot, because losses are absorbed by family wealth. That does not merely reward success; it rigs the starting line and turns accidents of birth into lifetime claims on opportunity.
[P1.6] Aggressive inheritance taxation at the top is a direct way to address this. We are not discussing modest homes or family farms, but concentrated dynastic transfers. A steep progressive estate or inheritance tax, paired with closing loopholes for trusts, gifts, and stepped-up bases, would raise revenue for opportunity—child accounts, education, health, housing mobility—while preventing wealth from becoming caste. If equality of opportunity matters, the state should tax inherited dynastic power far harder than the annual earnings of successful workers.
