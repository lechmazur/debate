[P1.1] Start with a woman in rural Malawi who cannot open a mobile money account, register a land claim, or prove her child is hers when the clinic changes staff. Across Africa, something like half the population lacks a robust legal identity. That is not an abstract privacy question. It is exclusion from credit, from vaccination records, from pensions, from voting, from the ability to move and work legally.
[P1.2] Exclusion is the status quo harm, and it is certain, ongoing, and concentrated on the poorest.
[P1.3] Here is the choice the motion actually poses. The counterfactual is not a continent free of digital ID. Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, Ethiopia, Rwanda and others are already building national digital ID systems, often with foreign vendors, procurement opacity, and no independent data protection authority worth the name. The surveillance train has left the station.
[P1.4] The only live question is whether it runs on tracks the AU lays, with common standards, or on fifty separate tracks built by whoever sells the cheapest biometric kit.
[P1.5] That is why a continent-wide framework is the pro-privacy answer as well as the pro-delivery one. A framework is where you bind data minimisation, purpose limitation, tokenised authentication rather than raw biometric sharing, independent oversight, and a right of redress into the technical standard itself — so a government that wants to abuse it must visibly break a continental commitment rather than quietly exploit a legal vacuum. Refusing to act does not withhold power from states. It withholds coordination from citizens.
[P1.6] And the upside compounds. AfCFTA means little to a cross-border trader who cannot prove who she is in the next country. Portable identity makes migrant workers' wages and pensions traceable, makes cash transfers reach displaced people, makes health records follow a patient across a border.
[P1.7] Concede the surveillance risk openly. Then weigh it: a manageable, governable risk against a guaranteed, generational exclusion that Africa's poorest are paying for right now.

[C1.1] A continent-wide digital ID does not restrain the surveillance already underway. It scales it, locks it in, and makes escape harder for the people it claims to serve.
[C1.2] National systems can still be narrowed, sued, delayed, or refused. Once the AU blesses interoperability, linkage, biometric retention, and cross-border queries look like compliance, not choice. The visible break [P1.5] counts on becomes ordinary use of the system as designed.
[C1.3] Standards do not bind states that refuse to be bound. Purpose limitation collapses when a security agency, an election body, or a lender demands the underlying data. Many member states lack independent regulators with real power. The AU cannot create them by writing them into a framework.
[C1.4] What it creates instead is a shared technical grammar of templates, unique identifiers, and authentication protocols, shipped long before any redress mechanism works.
[C1.5] That grammar does not reliably deliver services. The rural woman without papers is not included by a bigger system. She is included only if enrollment reaches her, biometrics read her hands, the network holds, and a clinic accepts a fallback when the token fails. The connected move faster while the poorest meet a new gate, and portability makes that failure follow her.
[C1.6] Cash transfers, records, and permits become conditional on a database she cannot audit and cannot leave.
[C1.7] Exclusion is serious, but it does not require a continental biometric commons. Local enrollment, paper credentials, and service-specific records can move vaccines, pensions, and wages without a queryable identity layer across distrustful regimes. Interoperable ID traces a worker's wages and traces a trader, a refugee, or a dissident to every government on the route.
[C1.8] [P1.7] treats rising surveillance as manageable against certain exclusion. The weights run the other way. This framework's product is a larger, connected means of watching and sorting people. Do not build it.
