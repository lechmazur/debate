[P1.1] Modern immigration policy suffers from a fatal disconnect: national governments issue visas, but local communities absorb the physical impact. When universities double international enrollments for tuition revenue or corporations recruit heavily into tight urban markets, they pocket the gains while local housing markets, clinics, and transit networks buckle. The resulting rent spikes and strained services fuel an inevitable public backlash that threatens openness itself.
[P1.2] Tying capped but guaranteed migration allocations to local capacity resolves this crisis through two decisive mechanisms.
[P1.3] First, it forces institutional responsibility. Status-quo immigration allows institutions to externalize the costs of growth onto surrounding neighborhoods. When allocations are tethered to local housing and service headroom, institutions can no longer treat municipal capacity as an infinite, free resource. If a university wants to recruit five hundred international students, it must ensure five hundred beds exist—either by building student housing or investing in regions with surplus capacity.
[P1.4] This directly aligns talent recruitment with infrastructure development, driving investment toward areas equipped to absorb it.
[P1.5] Second, it replaces administrative chaos with certainty. Today, universities and employers navigate capricious visa lotteries, sudden policy reversals, and reactive national crackdowns. A capped but guaranteed allocation offers sponsors a binding promise: if your local capacity supports it, your talent pipeline is secured. This predictability enables long-term business planning, research continuity, and capital investment without the fear of sudden political disruption.
[P1.6] The alternative is what we see today: a system that ignores physical reality until strained infrastructure provokes crude, blunt restrictions on all immigration. Anchor guaranteed visas to actual capacity, and you protect host communities, provide stability to institutions, and sustain the political legitimacy of an open economy.

[C1.1] The motion builds a visa system on a number that does not stay still. Housing and service capacity change with investment, migration, and public budgets. A “capped but guaranteed” allocation tied to that moving target cannot be both. If capacity improves after the cap is set, the guarantee excludes people who would have been fine; if capacity worsens, the scheme must either revoke the guarantee—making it not guaranteed—or let strain continue—making it not tied.
[C1.2] The two promises cancel.
[C1.3] The deeper problem is incentive. Once visas become a reward for showing spare capacity, institutions will optimize the metric, not the outcome. A university can build dormitory beds in an already expensive city, claim headroom, and receive guaranteed slots while local rents and clinic wait times still rise. The formula counts beds, not affordability, displacement, or whether the new housing is where workers can live.
[C1.4] Employers will lobby for favorable definitions of “capacity” and fight any downward revision as a taking of their entitlement. That is not certainty; it is a new litigation and lobbying industry.
[C1.5] It also gets the causal order backwards. A hospital in a tight housing market may need to sponsor nurses precisely because understaffing is straining local services. The motion tells it to wait until the city builds housing first. But the nurses are part of the capacity to deliver care, and their wages help fund the housing.
[C1.6] Capacity and migration are simultaneous, not sequential. A rigid local quota turns a shortage into a permanent blockage.
[C1.7] The motion entrenches regional inequality and zero-sum politics. Areas with vacant housing but weak demand get unused allocations; high-opportunity cities with tight markets get cut off from talent. Communities learn that adding capacity means more migrants, so some will resist the infrastructure the scheme claims to encourage. The backlash this scheme fears becomes a local veto point.
[C1.8] The better path is flexible visas and public investment, not guaranteed entitlements.
