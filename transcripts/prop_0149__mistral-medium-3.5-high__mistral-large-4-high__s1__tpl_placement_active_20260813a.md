[C1.1] The motion treats “party house” and “short-term rental” as interchangeable. They are not. A spare-room host, a family renting their home for two weeks, and a commercial party house are different actors with different effects. Stronger nuisance rules aimed at the third group will sweep in the first two, because enforcement responds to complaints, bookings, and addresses, not intent.
[C1.2] Once a city adds escalating fines, permit revocation, and hotlines, a single bad weekend—or a false neighbor complaint—can end a household’s ability to host. For many owners, that income is what makes the mortgage, property taxes, or upkeep possible. It is not a lobbyist’s abstraction; it is how homeowners stay in their homes.
[C1.3] The tourism claim is not a threat; it is arithmetic. Flexible hosting expands lodging supply where hotels cannot: during tournaments, festivals, graduations, and in neighborhoods underserved by hotels. Visitors in rentals spend at nearby restaurants, hire local cleaners, and stay longer than hotel guests. Shrinking that supply does not make visitors disappear; it raises prices, pushes them into distant suburbs with more driving, or sends them to competing cities.
[C1.4] The revenue that pays for police, street cleaning, and infrastructure comes substantially from those visitors.
[C1.5] The PRO examples prove too much. Nashville, Lisbon, New Orleans, and Berlin have noise, occupancy, tax, and emergency-response laws. The problem is uneven enforcement and understaffing, not an absence of rules. The “success” stories are not portable: Barcelona and Amsterdam are dense global capitals with large hotel sectors and different housing markets.
[C1.6] Their caps have not magically produced affordable housing; they have pushed demand into unregulated, less safe listings. A mid-sized tourism city lacks that buffer.
[C1.7] The right test is proportionality. Punish party houses with existing tools: noise citations, occupancy limits, tax audits, revocation for repeated violations, and an accountable host. Do not impose a presumption of nuisance on flexible hosting. A city that protects residents by destroying the lodging that funds its welcome is not preserving tourism; it is taxing it to death.
