[P1.1] The Security Council exists for one job: stopping threats to international peace. The veto guarantees it fails at that job exactly when it matters most. Judge this debate by a simple question: which Council can actually protect people, one where five states hold a kill switch, or one where decisions rest on broad agreement?
[P1.2] First, the veto paralyzes the Council in the gravest crises. Across more than a decade of war in Syria, resolutions on chemical weapons, aid access and accountability died by veto while civilians were gassed and besieged cities starved. When Russia invaded Ukraine, the Council could not even condemn the invasion, because the aggressor cast the veto. American vetoes have repeatedly shielded allies in the Middle East.
[P1.3] Every permanent member uses this power the same way: to protect itself and its friends. A fire department that needs the arsonist's permission to respond is not keeping the peace; it is certifying the fire.
[P1.4] Second, the veto corrupts the legitimacy of the entire system. The oldest principle of justice is that no one judges their own case. The veto breaks it for five countries, frozen at the winners of a war that ended eighty years ago. That tells every other state that international law binds the weak and exempts the strong, which is precisely why small states hedge, arm, and stop trusting collective security.
[P1.5] Third, the withdrawal threat should not deter us. Major powers gain enormously from the UN: diplomatic legitimacy, a seat where agendas are set, influence across its agencies. Walking out means ceding that ground to rivals, so the threat is far weaker than it sounds. And even if a power does leave, compare the outcomes honestly.
[P1.6] Today the Council already cannot restrain a permanent member. Losing a member that could block everything costs nothing we currently have, while gaining a body able to act on the many crises where the great powers are not the aggressors.
[P1.7] Keeping the veto to preserve great-power participation means preserving their participation in paralysis. Abolish it.

[C1.1] The veto is not a defect the Council's founders overlooked. It is the reason the Council exists at all. The League of Nations had no veto, and the strong simply left: Japan walked out over Manchuria, Italy over Abyssinia, Germany when it pleased, and the United States never joined. The League then passed principled resolutions against aggression that nobody enforced, and it died.
[C1.2] The UN's designers watched that happen and concluded something uncomfortable: a security body without the great powers inside it is not a stronger institution, it is a letterhead.
[C1.3] So judge this debate by one question: does a Council that has driven out Russia, China, or America protect more people than the Council we have? It does not, for three reasons.
[C1.4] First, enforcement. Sanctions on North Korea, the Iran nuclear framework, arms embargoes, seventy-odd peacekeeping missions over the decades: every one of these ran on permanent-member money, troops, intelligence, and customs enforcement. Strip the veto, lose a permanent member, and those regimes do not become more robust. They become leaky within months, because the state you expelled is now the world's largest sanctions-buster with no reason to pretend otherwise.
[C1.5] Second, restraint. The veto's ugliest moments are real, but it has also done something quieter for eighty years: it has never let the Council authorize force against a nuclear power. A Council that can outvote Russia can also, in principle, order a coalition against it. That is not collective security; that is a license for great-power war wearing a blue helmet.
[C1.6] Third, alternatives already exist that PRO ignores. The General Assembly condemned the Ukraine invasion within days, by an overwhelming margin, through the very mechanism designed to bypass a veto. Moral condemnation does not require abolition. Enforcement requires the powers.
[C1.7] PRO's fire-department image cuts the other way. Fire departments need fire trucks. The permanent members own the trucks. Abolish the veto, watch them leave, and you have a station full of volunteers voting bravely about fires they cannot reach.
