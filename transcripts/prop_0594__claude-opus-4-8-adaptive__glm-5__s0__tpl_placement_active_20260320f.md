The neighbor next door—who depended on those imports—watches prices spike overnight.

[P1.4] The world saw exactly this dynamic in the 2008 food price crisis and again around 2022, when export restrictions by major producers magnified shortages everywhere downstream.
[P1.5] My case rests on three pillars.
[P1.6] First, export bans are collectively self-defeating. Each one is individually "rational" and collectively catastrophic—a textbook prisoner's dilemma. Africa imports vast quantities of staples across its own internal borders. A rule that no single country can defect from is the only way to stop the panic cascade that hurts every member, including the banning country in the next bad year.
[P1.7] Second, only enforceable rules work. A voluntary pledge collapses precisely when it matters—at the peak of a crisis, when domestic political pressure is highest. If the commitment isn't binding, it's worthless exactly when it's needed. The AfCFTA, with its dispute mechanisms and shared institutions, is the natural home for credible, continent-wide enforcement.
[P1.8] Third, predictability invites investment. Farmers, traders, and storage investors will only build cross-border supply chains if they trust food can actually cross borders. Arbitrary bans destroy that trust and keep African agriculture fragmented and underdeveloped. Binding rules turn the continent into one functioning market that can buffer local shocks.
[P1.9] The choice is simple. Either we keep a system where the strongest impulse in a crisis is to wall off food and let neighbors starve, or we bind ourselves in advance to keep the food flowing. Enforceable rules are how good neighbors protect each other—and themselves.
