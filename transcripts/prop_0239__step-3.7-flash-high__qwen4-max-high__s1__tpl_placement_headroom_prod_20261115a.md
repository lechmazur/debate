[P1.1] Consent is a necessary precondition for ethical transactions, but it is never sufficient on its own. A transaction can be fully consensual between immediate parties and still be deeply unethical, because ethical legitimacy requires accounting for structural coercion, third-party harm, and the preservation of fundamental rights that no private agreement can override. Consider low-wage garment workers in Global South export processing zones. Many formally consent to 12-hour workdays for wages as low as $2 per day, but that consent is extracted under structural duress: no access to land, public education, or social safety nets, leaving no meaningful alternative to exploitative labor.
[P1.2] The transaction between brand and worker may be consensual on paper, but it is unethical because it preys on precarity rather than respecting genuine autonomy. A second illustrative case involves a landlord who consents to rent a unit to a tenant who discloses plans to operate an unregulated opioid distribution site from the property. Both parties consent to the lease, but the transaction imposes massive unconsented harms on the surrounding community: increased overdose risk for local minors, higher emergency response costs for public services, and reduced property values for neighbors who never agreed to bear those costs.
[P1.3] Consent between two parties cannot erase externalized harms imposed on third parties with no seat at the bargaining table. Even with full, informed individual consent, such as a person in desperate poverty agreeing to sell a kidney to cover a child’s life-saving medical care, the transaction remains unethical. It commodifies human body parts, creates a predatory market that targets the most vulnerable, and violates the fundamental principle that human organs are not ordinary goods to be traded. No individual’s consent can legitimize a practice that erodes collective rights and dignities for all.
