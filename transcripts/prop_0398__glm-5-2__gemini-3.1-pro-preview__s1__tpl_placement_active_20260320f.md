[P1.1] Pay has always been tied to place. A software engineer in San Francisco earns more than one doing comparable work in Boise—not because the work differs, but because housing, taxes, and the local labor market differ. Remote work doesn't erase that logic; it relocates it.
[P1.2] When someone moves from a high-cost headquarters city to a significantly cheaper region, their cost of living drops dramatically. A salary calibrated to sustain a household in San Francisco becomes a windfall where a spacious home costs a third as much. Location-adjusted pay isn't punishment; it's the same principle that has always governed compensation—pay reflects the local cost of replacing a worker and sustaining a comparable standard of living.
[P1.3] The alternative creates internal inequity companies can't ignore. Two employees doing identical work, one who stayed near headquarters and one who moved somewhere cheap, would earn wildly different amounts for no work-related reason. The mover isn't producing more value or facing higher costs. They made a geographic choice.
[P1.4] Paying a premium for that choice isn't fairness—it's arbitrage. When colleagues notice, resentment follows, along with pressure to inflate everyone's pay regardless of location, which many employers cannot sustain.
[P1.5] Workers who relocate aren't victims. They're voluntarily choosing a lower-cost area and typically gain quality of life—more space, lower expenses, less crowding. Accepting a pay adjustment is a reasonable part of that trade. The adjusted salary still buys a comparable or better life in the new location.
[P1.6] The real risk to remote work isn't adjustment; it's the unsustainable promise that salaries track the most expensive city regardless of where anyone lives. If companies must pay headquarters rates everywhere, many will restrict remote work to headquarters-region employees only—and the door closes for everyone else. Location-adjusted pay keeps that door open. If the goal is broad, durable access to remote opportunities, pay should reflect where work gets done, not where it used to happen.
