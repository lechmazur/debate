[P1.1] Stability is not the absence of monsters; it is the presence of functioning order. Before 2003, the Middle East was brutal but bounded: Iraq was contained—its army degraded by war and sanctions, its air force grounded by no-fly patrols—and the regional system rested on a rough balance between a Sunni-led Iraq and a Shia Iran. The invasion did not topple a threat; it removed the keystone of that balance and replaced a sovereign police state with a collapsed one.
[P1.2] The occupying powers dissolved the army and purged the state, then rebuilt politics around sectarian identity. That converted a dictatorship into a competition for ethnic and sectarian survival. The result was not a quick democratic peace but a civil war that killed on a scale far beyond Saddam's repression and displaced millions. Al-Qaeda in Iraq metastasized in the chaos, and its successor, ISIS, exploited Iraqi Sunni alienation and Syrian collapse to seize territory across both states—which a still-functioning Iraqi security state would almost certainly have prevented.
[P1.3] Regionally, the war reversed the strategic map. Iraq shifted from Iran's main Arab counterweight to a sphere of Iranian-aligned Shia parties and militias, drawing Tehran deeper into Lebanon, Syria, and later Yemen through supply lines and sectarian networks. Gulf states responded by bankrolling Sunni proxies, hardening the region into a sectarian cold war. Spillover fed the Syrian civil war, refugee flows into Jordan and Europe, and the re-legitimization of transnational jihadism as a mobilizing cause.
[P1.4] Even critics of Saddam must confront the counterfactual. The Iran-Iraq war was long over; sanctions and inspections had blunted his capabilities. Keeping him contained did not promise freedom, but it avoided the specific cascade that followed: state collapse, sectarian civil war, Iranian expansion, and a jihadist renaissance. The Arab Spring or Syrian uprising might have brought turmoil regardless, but without a broken Iraq, that turmoil would have been far less violent and far less regionalized.
