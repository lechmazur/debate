[P1.1] Sovereign debt relief is not charity; it is a restructuring of claims paid for, ultimately, by taxpayers, multilateral balance sheets, and future creditors. If that relief simply restores a vulnerable country to the same exposure that created the distress, it has failed. The proposition ties relief automatically to climate-resilience investment because it converts a fiscal rescue into a prevention strategy.
[P1.2] The central clash is simple: unconditional relief treats climate shocks as bad luck; automatic contingency treats them as foreseeable risks that can be priced and managed. Countries such as low-lying island states or flood-prone coastal economies often face a brutal choice between debt service and basic adaptation—sea defenses, drainage, drought-resistant seeds, early-warning systems. When a hurricane or flood hits, they borrow again, and the cycle repeats. Making relief conditional on resilience investment breaks that cycle.
[P1.3] It ensures that debt space created by creditors is used to reduce the probability of the next default, not merely to delay it.
[P1.4] Automaticity is the decisive advantage. Discretionary climate conditions arrive late, get bargained away, or become political punishment. A pre-committed rule—relief triggers investment in nationally identified adaptation priorities—makes financing predictable and removes some leverage games from crises. It also protects creditors: every dollar directed to resilience lowers future disaster losses, future emergency lending, and future write-downs.
[P1.5] The opposition may say this imposes burdens on vulnerable states. But the burden is already imposed by climate risk; the question is whether debt relief finances escape from it. With transparent criteria, use of national adaptation plans, and independent verification, the condition can be targeted rather than intrusive. If we are going to cancel or reschedule debt at public expense, we should require that the relief reduce the next disaster’s toll.
[P1.6] That is not coercion; it is basic fiduciary sense.

[C1.2] First, debt distress is an emergency, and emergencies punish delay. When Zambia defaulted in 2020, its restructuring dragged on for years while arrears piled up, growth stalled, and public services shrank. The resilience dividend never arrives if the economy collapses waiting for the paperwork.
[C1.3] Second, automaticity misdiagnoses. Debt crises erupt from commodity crashes, wars, pandemics, currency mismatches—climate is one cause among many. A rule that unlocks relief only through climate investments tells a government facing a food crisis or a currency collapse that it must fund mangroves before it may stabilize its budget. Earmarks imposed from outside crowd out the debtor's own, better-informed priorities.
[C1.4] That is not prevention; it is paternalism with a ledger.
[C1.5] Third, we have run this experiment. Decades of structural-adjustment conditionality taught a consistent lesson: imposed conditions produce compliance theater, not ownership. Programs get designed to satisfy creditors on paper and quietly abandoned after the signature. Meanwhile the real innovations in this space—Barbados's hurricane clauses, Grenada's disaster-linked terms—were chosen by debtor governments and negotiated case-by-case, which is exactly what "automatically contingent" forbids.
[C1.6] PRO wraps this in fiduciary language. But a creditor's true fiduciary interest is a fast, durable restructuring that restores a paying borrower, not a conditional one that festers. And if donors want to fund sea walls, they have a clean instrument: grants. They should not take the one lifeline a drowning country is offered and tie conditions to the rope.
[C1.7] Relief should be fast, unconditional, and paired with climate finance offered freely—not weaponized.
