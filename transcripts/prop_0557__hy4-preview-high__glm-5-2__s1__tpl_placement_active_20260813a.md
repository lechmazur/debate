[P1.1] The central question is not whether standardized tests are flawless. It is whether public universities can allocate scarce seats fairly without a common academic yardstick. They cannot. Reinstating test requirements is the most equitable, most transparent, and most publicly accountable way for public institutions to judge readiness across wildly unequal high schools.
[P1.2] When tests disappear, measurement does not. Admissions offices substitute grade-point averages, course rigor, essays, recommendations, and extracurriculars—inputs more sensitive to family income and school context than a two-hour exam. An A from a well-funded suburban school partly reflects the school; an A from an under-resourced rural school can hide both talent and gaps. Tests add measurable power in predicting first-year performance and persistence, and they let a student from a little-known high school prove readiness on a national scale.
[P1.3] They are not a perfect talent detector, but they are the only comparable, hard-to-inflate signal available to every applicant.
[P1.4] Test-optional policies are often sold as a blow for equity, yet they can quietly entrench advantage. Privileged applicants with test prep, counseling, and stable schedules submit scores when they help and withhold them when they do not. Less advantaged applicants often lack that coaching about the process itself. Holistic review then leans on polished essays and costly extracurriculars.
[P1.5] Large public systems have often seen application booms after 2020, but little change in the enrollment share of low-income or first-generation students, even as file readers get less time per applicant. That is not fairness; it is opacity.
[P1.6] Public universities also answer to the public. Taxpayers and legislatures deserve criteria that are consistent, auditable, and resistant to back-channel preference and grade inflation. A test, used alongside records and coupled with real need-based outreach and fee waivers, gives that accountability. Reinstating tests does not treat the SAT or ACT as sacred; the motion simply insists that public opportunity requires a public yardstick.
[P1.7] Remove it, and the system rewards those already best at navigating it.

[C1.1] A "common yardstick" is only equitable if the yardstick itself is fair. Standardized tests are not. They correlate more tightly with family income than with academic potential. The same student, sitting in a well-funded suburb with twice-yearly retakes, a private tutor, and PSAT drills woven into the school day, produces a score 100 to 200 points higher than she would have in a district where the exam is an afterthought.
[C1.2] That gap does not measure readiness. It measures accumulated advantage—and then stamps it with a number that looks objective.
[C1.3] The PRO side argues that without tests, admissions leans on essays and extracurriculars that favor the privileged. That concern is real, but mandatory testing makes the disease worse. A requirement does not eliminate gaming; it makes gaming universal. Every applicant must now pay for preparation, secure testing seats, retake for superscoring, and navigate fee waivers that are often poorly publicized.
[C1.4] Students who already have counselors handling this are fine. Students who do not now face a hard barrier, not a strategic choice.
[C1.5] High school GPA, read in context of what a school actually offers, remains the strongest single predictor of college success. Tests add modest incremental predictive value at the individual level but impose large access costs at the population level. For public universities whose charter includes broad access, that tradeoff runs the wrong direction.
[C1.6] The claim that tests let unknown schools be recognized sounds compelling, but admissions offices already contextualize school profiles. They know which high schools are under-resourced. What they need is better outreach and investment, not a gate that filters out the very students the system claims to serve.
[C1.7] Public accountability does not require a test score. It requires transparent, published standards and auditable decisions. GPA bands, course thresholds, and contextual data provide that. Reinstating mandatory testing takes a flawed but improvable system and locks in its most regressive feature.
