[C1.1] The motion asks regulators to draw a line between “gene-edited” crops and traditional GMOs, then trust that a “limited” category will stay limited. That line is the proposal’s fatal weakness.
[C1.2] Regulation should track risk, not the tool used to make a plant. A crop with no foreign DNA can still carry unintended edits: off-target mutations, deletions, rearrangements, or changes to regulatory networks that affect allergens, toxins, or weediness. A single edit to a native gene can produce a trait as novel as anything transgenic. “No foreign gene” is a genetic origin story, not a safety certificate.
[C1.3] Nor is the “limited” category stable. Breeders can stack edits, combine edited lines with conventional crosses, or recreate a transgenic-style trait by editing native genes. Once a crop is exempt, regulators must police a moving boundary with limited resources while developers have every incentive to fit under the lighter track. That is not a narrow exception; it is a regulatory arbitrage engine.
[C1.4] PRO’s access argument proves too much. If approval costs shut out public researchers, the answer is proportionate review and public funding within a robust system, not a separate lane with weaker scrutiny. The drought-tolerant rice example does not show that streamlined rules are safe; it shows that current rules are expensive. Those are different problems, and the motion solves the second by weakening the first.
[C1.5] Public trust compounds the danger. A two-tier system that declares some gene-edited crops “not GMOs” will look like relabeling rather than reform. If one exempt crop causes an allergy scare or becomes a weed, the backlash will hit the entire technology and the regulator that waved it through.
[C1.6] The better path is product-based risk assessment: scale testing to the trait and the environment, not to the editing technique. The motion’s shortcut sacrifices that discipline for speed.
