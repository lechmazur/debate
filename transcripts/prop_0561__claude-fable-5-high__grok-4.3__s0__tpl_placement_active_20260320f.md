[P1.1] A driver who works thirty hours a week for Uber generates the same revenue, faces the same road risks, and gets just as sick as a thirty-hour employee at a delivery company. The only difference is who pays when something goes wrong. Today, the answer is: everyone except the platform. The taxpayer covers the emergency room visit, the worker absorbs the unpaid sick day, and society inherits the retirement shortfall.
[P1.2] That is not a free market outcome — it is a subsidy, extracted by some of the world's most valuable companies through a classification loophole.
[P1.3] The case for prorated, hours-tied benefits rests on three pillars.
[P1.4] First, it corrects cost-shifting. Platforms profit from labor while externalizing labor's predictable costs — illness, injury, old age. When those costs land on public programs, traditional employers who do provide benefits are effectively taxed to subsidize their gig competitors. Requiring prorated contributions simply makes platforms pay the true price of the labor they consume, restoring honest competition.
[P1.5] Second, proration is precisely what preserves flexibility — the thing gig work is supposedly about. The all-or-nothing employee/contractor binary creates the real rigidity: platforms cap hours and avoid commitments to dodge employee status. Tying benefits proportionally to hours dissolves that cliff. Work ten hours, accrue ten hours' worth.
[P1.6] No platform has an incentive to throttle workers, and no worker must choose between autonomy and a retirement account.
[P1.7] Third, it is administratively trivial for these companies specifically. Platforms already track every minute worked, every mile driven, every dollar earned — it is their entire business model. If anyone can compute prorated contributions in real time, it is an algorithmic dispatch firm.
[P1.8] The question this debate should turn on is simple: should the cost of a workforce's basic human needs be borne by the businesses profiting from that workforce, or quietly dumped onto workers and taxpayers? Proration answers that question without sacrificing what makes gig work valuable. That is why the proposition stands.

[C1.1] Mandating prorated benefits will shrink total earnings and hours available to gig workers by raising the cost of each hour platforms are willing to offer. When platforms must add health, leave, and retirement contributions on top of existing pay, they face a direct choice: absorb the expense, cut driver or delivery rates, or simply dispatch fewer trips. Most will do the last two. The result is less work for the people the policy claims to help, not more security.
[C1.2] Workers already select gig arrangements precisely because they trade benefits for immediate cash and scheduling control. A parent who logs fifteen hours between school runs or a student who works twenty hours around classes rarely wants a forced contribution split that reduces take-home pay today in exchange for a distant pension credit. Forcing proration overrides that preference and treats autonomy as a bug rather than the feature that draws millions into the sector.
[C1.3] The administrative claim also collapses once real operations are considered. Platforms can track minutes, yet they cannot force independent contractors to submit eligibility paperwork, verify other coverage, or remain engaged long enough for benefits to vest. Workers who treat the app as occasional income will trigger repeated enrollment and disenrollment costs that smaller platforms and new entrants cannot absorb. Larger firms will simply tighten hour caps or shift volume to full-time employee fleets, narrowing the very flexibility proration is said to preserve.
[C1.4] Traditional employers already face these costs and therefore limit variable staffing. Replicating the same structure on gig platforms imports the same rigidity under a different label, converting a low-barrier entry point into another regulated labor market segment. The net effect is fewer total opportunities, concentrated among fewer workers, and higher consumer prices for the services that remain.

[C2.1] Mandating benefits raises the effective cost per hour even when spread marginally, so platforms will reduce the total volume of work dispatched. [C2.2] The portable-account fix does not eliminate the core friction. Platforms must still calculate, withhold, and remit contributions in real time across thousands of variable schedules, while workers must monitor balances, file claims, and navigate multiple small accruals. These ongoing compliance layers favor larger incumbents that can spread the overhead and penalize new entrants or part-time users whose hours never justify the administrative burden.
[C2.3] Worker choice is not a phantom. Surveys of active drivers and couriers consistently show that a sizable share treat the work as supplemental income and explicitly trade predictable benefits for same-day payouts and the ability to stop without notice. Forcing the tradeoff into every hour overrides that revealed preference rather than expanding it.
[C2.4] The externality argument also cuts both ways. Taxpayers already absorb costs from many low-hour or transitional jobs; the distinctive feature of gig platforms is that they expand total labor participation rather than displacing traditional employment. Shrinking that expansion to fund portable accounts simply relocates the subsidy from one public program to another while reducing the number of people who can participate at all.
