[P1.1] Speed without scientific validity is not medical progress; it is outsourced risk. When regulators rush a drug to market based on trials dominated by convenient, homogenous cohorts—historically young, healthy, white, and male—they do not accelerate cures. They simply shift unmonitored experimentation from clinical laboratories into the general public.
[P1.2] A drug’s safety and efficacy are not universal constants. Human biology varies systematically across age, sex, and ethnicity. Differences in body composition, hormonal environments, and organ clearance alter pharmacokinetics. Dosing that is therapeutic for a thirty-year-old man can easily be toxic for an eighty-year-old woman.
[P1.3] Genetic polymorphisms affecting metabolic pathways, such as the cytochrome P450 enzyme family, mean different ethnic populations can process cardiovascular, psychiatric, or oncological therapies at radically different rates. If a trial fails to represent the patients who will actually receive the prescription, it has not scientifically established efficacy or safety.
[P1.4] Our opponent will argue that proportionate recruitment creates costly delays that hold back lifesaving therapies. But an approval based on an unrepresentative sample is not an early approval; it is an incomplete one. What benefit is gained from a rapid approval if a drug triggers severe adverse events in women, proves ineffective in ethnic minorities, or causes fatal toxicity in the elderly? History is filled with post-market corrections—such as the late realization that women required half the standard dose of insomnia drugs—because trials treated one demographic as the human default.
[P1.5] Tolerating delays to ensure proportionate representation is not administrative inertia; it is the basic standard of empirical science. The burden of recruitment belongs on pharmaceutical sponsors, not on excluded populations who are currently forced to serve as unwitting Phase IV subjects. If a drug cannot be proven safe for the whole public, it is not ready for the public market.

The ethical choice is to accept a modest, manageable risk of post‑approval discovery in exchange for delivering therapies now, not to sacrifice present lives for a statistical mirage of perfect representation.
