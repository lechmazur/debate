[P1.1] The right to die with dignity is fundamental to personal autonomy. When a competent adult faces unbearable suffering from a terminal or degenerative illness, the law must respect their capacity to choose. Consider a patient with advanced ALS: fully conscious but progressively paralyzed, facing months of slow suffocation while trapped in a non-responsive body. The status quo offers only cruel alternatives: prolong unrelenting agony or attempt a violent suicide that may fail, leaving survivors traumatized.
[P1.2] Medical assistance in dying provides a compassionate third option that prioritizes patient autonomy and minimizes harm to others.
[P1.3] Comprehensive safeguards ensure this choice remains free and informed. Eligibility requires certification by at least two independent physicians that the patient has a grievous, irremediable condition causing unbearable suffering. Mandatory waiting periods prevent impulsive decisions. Explicit, voluntary consent must be reconfirmed multiple times, including immediately before the procedure.
[P1.4] Decades of experience from jurisdictions like the Netherlands and Oregon show these systems work safely, with assisted deaths remaining a small, stable fraction of all deaths and no evidence of coercion or systematic abuse.
[P1.5] Denying this right preserves suffering, not life. Palliative care cannot always relieve pain. For many, loss of autonomy and dignity is itself unbearable agony. Forcing continued existence against their will is tyranny, not compassion.
[P1.6] The law already recognizes the right to refuse life-sustaining treatment. There is no ethical distinction between that and assisted dying—both respect autonomy. This is not a slippery slope; it is a carefully bounded right that enhances human dignity.
