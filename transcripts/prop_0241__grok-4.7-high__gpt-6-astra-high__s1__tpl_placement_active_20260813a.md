[P1.1] The point of criminal punishment is fewer victims, not a ritual of payback. Rehabilitation is the more important aim because it is the one that actually reduces future crime. Retribution looks backward and stops at suffering. Rehabilitation looks forward and asks what will keep this person from creating the next victim.
[P1.2] Take the repeat offender whose theft or violence tracks addiction, untreated mental illness, or the simple absence of any lawful skill. A retributive sentence can be long, harsh, and still release the same deficits that produced the crime. The harm is then postponed, not prevented. Programs that combine accountability with treatment, education, and structured reentry attack those causes directly.
[P1.3] They do not excuse the offense. They make the next offense less likely, which is the only result that protects the public after the sentence ends.
[P1.4] Retribution cannot carry that burden alone. Deterrence is unreliable when people act under addiction, impulse, or the belief they will not be caught. Incapacitation expires on release. Prison itself often worsens the problem through isolation, stigma, and the loss of work habits.
[P1.5] What society gets back is frequently a more damaged person. Rehabilitation is the aim that still operates on the day of release, when public safety is actually decided.
[P1.6] Moral condemnation does not require retribution to outrank reform. A sentence can mark the wrong, set firm limits, and still treat change as the priority. [P1.7] A system that prefers the measurable reduction of harm over the uncertain satisfaction of payback will punish less often because it needs to.
[P1.8] Retribution freezes the offender as a finished wrong. Rehabilitation treats them as someone who can still be turned. When the choice is between repaying yesterday’s injury and preventing tomorrow’s, prevention is the more important aim of punishment.
