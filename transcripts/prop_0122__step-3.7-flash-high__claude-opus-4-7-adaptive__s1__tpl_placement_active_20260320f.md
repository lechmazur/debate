[P1.1] Treating children under 14 as fully punishable offenders is a failure of both developmental science and basic justice. The prefrontal cortex, the brain region responsible for impulse control, long-term foresight, and consistent moral reasoning, does not reach full maturity until mid-adolescence, meaning children under 14 lack the cognitive capacity to be held fully culpable for their actions in the same way adults are.
[P1.2] Low age thresholds do not keep communities safe—they funnel vulnerable kids into a justice system that fails them, instead of addressing the root causes of their behavior. A 12-year-old who shoplifts food because their family is homeless, or acts out due to unaddressed trauma or undiagnosed neurodivergence, is not a budding criminal: they are a child in need of support. When we give them a permanent criminal record instead of counseling, educational support, or family services, we bar them from school, employment, and housing for life, and make recidivism far more likely.
[P1.3] This isn’t compassion—it’s bad policy that creates more harm, not less.
[P1.4] The international standard for this reason is clear: the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child explicitly recommends 14 as the minimum age of criminal responsibility, a floor rooted in decades of rigorous child development research. Raising age thresholds to at least 14 doesn’t mean ignoring harmful behavior—it means responding to it in ways that actually keep communities safe, by breaking the cycle of incarceration that starts when we punish children for actions driven by unmet needs, not deliberate criminal intent.

Low minimum ages coexist with juvenile courts, doli incapax presumptions, diversion programs, sealed records, and sentences that look nothing like adult punishment.
[C1.2] The real question isn't whether a child belongs in an adult prison—nobody defends that—but whether the law should retain jurisdiction when a child causes serious harm.
[C1.3] That jurisdiction matters. Consider the 12-year-old who stabs a classmate, or the 11-year-old who sexually assaults a younger child. Rare, but real. A blanket floor at 14 strips the state of legal authority to compel assessment, treatment, or supervision through the criminal process.
[C1.4] The alternative is often coercive civil commitment or welfare detention with weaker due process protections than juvenile court. Defense counsel, evidentiary standards, the right to contest allegations—these exist in juvenile justice precisely because it is a legal proceeding.
[C1.5] PRO's developmental science cuts less sharply than it sounds. A 12-year-old's prefrontal cortex isn't fully mature, but they overwhelmingly know that stabbing, arson, and sexual assault are wrong. Maturity also varies enormously between individuals; a categorical bar at 14 is just as arbitrary as one at 10. That's why many low-MACR systems use individualized capacity assessments rather than a bright line—asking whether this child understood the wrongfulness of this act.
[C1.6] The reforms PRO actually wants—diversion, services, no permanent records, rehabilitation over punishment—can be delivered without raising the age. Many countries with MACR at 10 or 12 already do exactly this; the shoplifting 12-year-old gets services, not a cell. Raising the floor doesn't fix bad juvenile justice; it removes a flexible tool and replaces it with a binary that fails both children and victims at the margins. Better to fix the response than abandon responsibility.
