[P1.1] Sports broadcasting has moved from cable to streaming, but the pricing model still behaves like old cable: take everything or take nothing. The proposition wins because it matches the product to how fans actually value sport. A supporter of one club, a follower of one tennis tour, or a parent whose child plays a niche sport usually wants a narrow slice of rights, not a portfolio of leagues. Broad bundles force those viewers to subsidize content they rarely watch, creating a predictable result: many price themselves out, pirate, or disengage.
[P1.2] The central issue is not whether bundles are profitable for distributors; it is whether broadcasters should be permitted to make the bundle the default trap for most fans. Cheaper team-specific or competition-specific passes would let broadcasters practice honest price discrimination. Fans with intense, narrow preferences pay for the exact asset they value. Casual fans can enter at a lower threshold.
[P1.3] That expands the paying audience instead of squeezing the same devoted customers. A club pass for a Premier League team, a pass for a single cricket tournament, or a league pass for a domestic basketball competition can convert viewers who would never pay for a full sports package.
[P1.4] This also protects the sport itself. Access drives habit. When children can cheaply follow one team or one athlete, they become future fans, participants, and merchandise buyers. Locking sport behind expensive all-in-one bundles narrows the funnel and encourages piracy, especially among younger viewers.
[P1.5] CON may argue that bundles cross-subsidize smaller sports. But the motion does not ban bundles; it says broadcasters should offer cheaper narrow options rather than forcing most fans into them. A healthier market can combine broad bundles for superfans with targeted passes for everyone else.

[P2.1] CON’s core arithmetic mistakes a discount for a downgrade. The “one-club obsessive” [C1.1] is not the only person paying today; many are paying because the bundle is the only legal route to the matches they care about. If a cheaper team or tournament pass appears, those fans can choose it, but broadcasters can still charge premium prices for full access, multi-view, no-blackout, or simultaneous streams. That is ordinary versioning: a lower fare fills seats that would otherwise fly empty without eliminating first class.
[P2.2] CON also treats fixed rights fees as a reason to avoid narrower pricing, but that cuts the other way. When rights are expensive, the danger is relying on a shrinking pool of bundle subscribers to cover them. Cheaper narrow passes widen the base among fans who currently say no, pirate, or watch highlights. A small payment from many previously excluded viewers can beat zero from the same viewers.
[P2.3] The competitive-balance objection confuses the packaging of rights with the distribution of money. The proposition does not require leagues to abandon collective selling. A league can centralize revenue and still let broadcasters sell competition-specific passes, or team passes whose proceeds are pooled. If Spain’s old model caused imbalance, the lesson is not “never unbundle”; it is “share revenue properly.” Blackouts and fragmented team schedules are real, but they are design problems, not reasons to keep the all-or-nothing default.
[P2.4] Competition passes—cup tournaments, tours, league packages—can follow the fixture list better than CON’s worst-case three-pass fan.
[P2.5] Finally, CON’s claim that bundle prices rise assumes broadcasters lose loyal buyers without gaining pricing discipline. If narrow passes convert casual fans and reduce churn, bundles become premium options, not leftovers. Fans win when they can pay less for exactly what they watch, and broadcasters win by monetising more of the audience.
