[P1.1] A wage buys an agreed amount of labour; it should not buy a permanent lease on someone’s evening, sleep, and family life. Yet smartphones let employers extend the working day without either scheduling or paying for it. A right to disconnect restores the boundary that technology has quietly erased.
[P1.2] The model is straightforward: for most employees, outside agreed working hours there is no duty to monitor or answer routine work communications, employers must organise non-urgent contact accordingly, and workers cannot be penalised for being offline. Genuine emergencies, compensated on-call arrangements, and sector-specific rules for areas such as healthcare, emergency response, and critical infrastructure remain available. This is not a ban on flexibility or on every late email: messages can be scheduled, and genuinely voluntary arrangements can accommodate different working patterns.
[P1.3] First, the right protects a basic condition of sustainable work: recovery. The harm is not merely the ten minutes spent answering a message. It is the continuing anticipation that one might arrive—the phone checked during dinner, the child’s bedtime interrupted, the inability to relax because “urgent” is defined unilaterally by the sender. Individually, refusing a manager’s message carries risk; collectively, a clear default makes switching off normal rather than an act of disloyalty.
[P1.4] Second, this is sound workplace organisation, not hostility to productivity. Constant availability encourages managers to export poor planning into employees’ private time. Quiet-hour rules force earlier prioritisation, clearer escalation procedures, and proper staffing for work that genuinely requires continuous coverage. Rested employees are also better able to concentrate and less likely to burn out.
[P1.5] CON must not compare an idealised frictionless workplace with regulation: the existing friction is already being imposed, invisibly, on workers and their families.
[P1.6] Finally, sector-specific exceptions answer the strongest objection before it is made. A hospital need not follow the same protocol as an accounting firm, and an emergency need not wait until morning. [C1.2] First, it is unenforceable where it matters. The junior employee whose manager messages at 10pm already has the formal option to ignore it; what she lacks is the power to use it safely. A statute does not change the performance review, the promotion decision, or the quiet comparison with the colleague who replies. France has had this right since 2017, and chronic overwork persists there — because the law regulates the message, not the workload.
[C1.3] You can be perfectly disconnected at 7pm and still drowning from 9 to 6. The right treats the symptom and leaves the disease untouched.
[C1.4] Second, where it does bite, it punishes workers who chose flexibility. The parent who leaves at three for school pickup and logs on at eight. The developer collaborating across time zones. Anyone who prefers a late start and a late finish.
[C1.5] A legal no-contact regime forces employers to police hours, and the rational employer response is to withdraw the flexibility itself — if I cannot reach you at eight, I cannot let you start at eleven. PRO's "voluntary arrangements" clause either swallows the rule, since pressure to "volunteer" recreates the original problem, or protects nothing.
[C1.6] Third, the exception mechanism is incoherent. Healthcare qualifies — but logistics? Finance tied to Asian markets? Media?
[C1.7] Every industry will lobby for its carve-out, line-drawing becomes political rather than principled, small firms carry the heaviest compliance burden, and mobile work quietly migrates to contractors outside the protection entirely.
[C1.8] The genuine harms — overload, understaffing, bad management — are answered by enforcing working-time and overtime law and by collective bargaining. This motion offers a symbol that ties flexible workers' hands while the overloaded stay exactly as busy as before.
