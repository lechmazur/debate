[P1.1] Central banks’ single-minded focus on consumer prices leaves a gaping hole in financial stability: asset bubbles, especially in housing, can grow unchecked until they trigger system-wide collapse. The 2008 crisis demonstrated this brutally. While consumer price inflation was subdued, US housing prices had more than doubled in the decade before the crash, fueled by reckless lending and speculative excess. When the bubble burst, it didn’t merely correct an imbalance—it wiped out vast household wealth, destroyed major financial institutions, and triggered the worst global downturn since the 1930s.
[P1.2] Explicit housing-price targeting would compel central banks to act preemptively, before imbalances become existential threats. The tools are proven: countercyclical capital buffers, loan-to-income limits, or rate hikes when price-to-rent ratios spike. It drives consumption via wealth effects, shapes household borrowing, and underpins bank collateral.
[P1.3] Moreover, housing costs already influence CPI through rents, yet asset prices remain untargeted, creating a dangerous asymmetry.
[P1.4] Volatile housing prices amplify inequality, locking younger and lower-income households out of ownership. Norway and Sweden show housing considerations can be integrated without sacrificing inflation control. The alternative is periodic booms and busts, with the vulnerable suffering most. Prevention costs are minimal; cleanup costs are astronomical.
[P1.5] Housing is too systemically important to remain outside the central bank’s explicit mandate. Asset-price stability, especially for housing, must be a core objective alongside inflation.

Asking the same instrument to also defend a housing-price target means that, when CPI and house prices point in opposite directions, one will be sacrificed.
[C1.3] Given how politically invisible CPI misses are compared to how visible housing costs are to voters, guess which one gets dropped. Credibility is built by doing one job exceptionally well, not by doing two jobs badly.

Sweden, the UK, and Switzerland have used these tools successfully.
[C1.5] They are finer instruments: they bite on the risky segments of the mortgage market instead of penalizing the entire economy with higher rates.
[C1.6] There is also the identification problem. Housing booms are notoriously hard to distinguish from fundamentals-driven re-pricing. [C1.8] Macroprudential policy, not asset-price targeting, is the lesson of the crisis. Keep the rate on inflation, and defend stability with the tools built for it.
