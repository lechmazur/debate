[P1.1] Cash is not a nostalgic preference; it is a form of money that lets a person complete a purchase without surrendering personal data, obtaining a smartphone contract, or trusting a payment system to function. A requirement that major retailers accept cash for essential goods would establish a practical access floor. Consider a shopper whose phone has expired, whose bank account is frozen, or whose prepaid data has run out. If groceries, medicine, and basic household supplies require cards or apps, that shopper is not merely inconvenienced.
[P1.2] They may be unable to obtain necessities.
[P1.3] Retailers cannot reasonably claim that accepting cash is impossible: they already handle currency routinely, and cash transactions avoid card-processing charges. Requiring its acceptance for essential goods also prevents large chains from using their market power to make an entire category of spending conditional on digital access. Individual stores may compete on convenience, but they should not be able to define that convenience so narrowly that it excludes consumers. The relevant public good is not unlimited payment choice; it is access to necessities.
[P1.4] The strongest objection is cost and administrative burden. Those burdens are real, but they are modest when the rule is limited to major retailers, essential goods, and ordinary cash transactions. Governments can permit reasonable safeguards, prohibit discriminatory surcharges, and provide exceptions for genuine system failures or immediate safety risks. Such narrow implementation prevents the policy from becoming an indiscriminate mandate on every small transaction.
[P1.5] More importantly, a retailer that refuses cash imposes costs elsewhere: on cash-dependent customers, on communities with limited broadband, and potentially on taxpayers when digital exclusion generates demand for emergency assistance.
[P1.6] Cash also provides resilience during outages, cyberattacks, and disasters, when stores remain open and electricity is available even if payment networks are not. Finally, it preserves financial privacy. Consumers revealing their identities, purchase histories, and movements at a checkout is not an inevitable price of buying necessities.
[P1.7] The decisive question is whether essential goods should be available on ordinary terms to ordinary consumers. [clipped]

[C1.1] The core flaw in PRO’s case is its refusal to weigh the regressive costs of mandatory cash acceptance, which fall hardest on the low-income shoppers it claims to protect. PRO dismisses cash handling as a “modest” burden, but these costs are real: major retailers spend billions annually on armored transport, employee time counting currency, theft prevention, and cash loss insurance. These expenses are not absorbed by corporate profits—they are passed to all customers via higher shelf prices. For a household already devoting half its income to rent, food, and medicine, even a 1.5% price hike on essentials is a far more tangible harm than the rare card reader outage PRO cites as justification.
[C1.2] PRO also ignores the public health and safety tradeoffs of forcing cash back into essential retail. Contactless payment adoption during the pandemic was a voluntary shift that reduced disease transmission for immunocompromised shoppers and low-wage frontline staff, the very groups PRO says it prioritizes. Mandating cash would roll back this protection. While PRO points to “immediate safety risks” as a narrow exception, these carveouts are vague and unenforceable: retailers in high-crime areas would be forced to keep large cash reserves on site, raising the risk of armed robbery that endangers staff and shoppers alike.
[C1.3] Finally, PRO’s framing treats cash access as a universal right, but no consumer is currently barred from using cash for essentials. Shoppers can already choose cash-accepting major retailers or smaller stores that opt in. The mandate does not create new access; it forces all major retailers to subsidize a payment choice that imposes hidden costs on their entire customer base. The burden of that subsidy falls not on consumers who prefer cash, but on low-income families who will pay higher prices for groceries and medicine to cover the mandate’s expenses.
