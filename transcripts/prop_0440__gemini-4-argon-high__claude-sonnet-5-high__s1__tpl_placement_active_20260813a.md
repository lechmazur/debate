[P1.1] Urban gun violence is not a citywide weather pattern; it is an epidemic concentrated within tiny, identifiable social networks. In almost every major American city, a fraction of one percent of the population drives the vast majority of shootings, usually through cycles of retaliation. Because the problem is surgical, a surgical response will always beat a blunt instrument on cost-effectiveness.
[P1.2] Consider what a city actually buys when it expands general police staffing. Hiring a new officer does not just mean paying a base salary. It requires academy training, equipment, vehicles, liability coverage, and decades of pension and healthcare obligations. Yet once deployed, generalist patrol officers spend the overwhelming majority of their shifts handling traffic accidents, noise complaints, routine service calls, and administrative paperwork.
[P1.3] Expanding the force is an extraordinarily expensive way to buy millions of patrol hours that never touch gun violence.
[P1.4] By contrast, focused deterrence and violence-interruption programs direct every single dollar straight at the people most likely to shoot or be shot. Focused deterrence does not require a massive new workforce; it coordinates existing police, prosecutors, social workers, and community figures to deliver an unmistakable message to known high-risk individuals: the violence must stop, real help is available if you want out, and swift legal consequences will follow if you shoot.
[P1.5] Meanwhile, violence interrupters—credible messengers who actually know the street networks—step into active disputes and hospital trauma wards to talk down retaliatory shootings before they happen.
[P1.6] The fiscal math is decisive. Interrupters and program coordinators cost a fraction of sworn officers, carry no lifelong pension liabilities, and spend all their time preventing violence. Better still, by stopping retaliatory shootings without relying on broad, heavy-handed street sweeps, cities avoid the downstream fiscal drains of mass pretrial detention and eroded neighborhood trust. When you target the exact people driving the crisis, you save far more lives per dollar.

[C1.5] Finally, think about durability. Interrupter programs lean on scarce credible messengers who burn out, get threatened, or relocate.
