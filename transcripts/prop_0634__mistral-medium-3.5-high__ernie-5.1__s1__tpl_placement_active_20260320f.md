[P1.1] Germline gene editing represents an unprecedented moral and biological risk. By altering the DNA of embryos, we do not merely change an individual—we rewrite the genetic code for all their descendants, binding future generations to choices they cannot influence or undo. A single error, whether from off-target CRISPR effects, unintended changes to neighboring genes, or disruptions to poorly understood regulatory sequences, becomes a permanent mutation in the human gene pool. These mistakes cannot be recalled; they propagate through families, potentially surfacing as new diseases or vulnerabilities decades later in people who never had a say.
[P1.2] This is not speculative: research has already shown CRISPR can cause large deletions and chromosomal rearrangements that may evade initial detection.
[P1.3] Crucially, we do not need to take this risk. Non-heritable gene therapies provide a safer alternative that is already saving lives. Therapies that edit a patient’s own blood cells to treat diseases like sickle cell disease and beta thalassemia demonstrate conclusively that we can achieve medical progress without altering the germline. If the same therapeutic ends can be met through means that do not jeopardize future generations, the ethical path is prohibition.
[P1.4] Beyond safety, germline editing threatens the foundations of human equality. It transforms children from gifts of chance into products of design, opening the door to a new eugenics. Once we accept editing embryos to prevent disease, the same logic justifies editing for enhancement—selecting for traits like intelligence, appearance, or athletic ability. This risks a future where genetic privilege becomes a new and entrenched dimension of inequality, accessible only to the wealthy, and where human worth is determined by engineered genetics rather than inherent dignity.
[P1.5] The precautionary principle is unequivocal: when harms are permanent, benefits unnecessary, and societal costs incalculable, countries must ban heritable gene editing.

[C1.5] Finally, the precautionary principle, invoked by the PRO side, demands inaction when harms are uncertain. Therefore, countries should permit germline editing under strict regulation, not impose a blanket prohibition.
