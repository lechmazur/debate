[P1.1] Streaming contracts that tie pay to audience reach cannot function fairly if only one side can see the scoreboard. Today, a platform can classify a series as a hit or a niche title, decide what counts as a view, and set bonus thresholds behind proprietary dashboards. Filmmakers and performers receive money, if they receive any, based on numbers they cannot inspect. That is not a market; it is an audit-proof black box.
[P1.2] The proposition simply requires that when compensation is triggered by audience reach, the data triggering it be standardized and shared. It does not ask for a favor; it treats data as the payroll record it is.
[P1.3] The core principle is verification. Traditional media developed workable reporting: box-office grosses for theatrical release, ratings for broadcast, sales reports for physical media. Streaming should not be less accountable merely because it is newer. Standardized metrics—such as hours streamed, unique accounts, completion rates, and territory-level availability—let participants compare outcomes across titles and periods.
[P1.4] Without standardization, each service can define “view” differently, making cross-project comparisons impossible and letting platforms move the goalposts. Voluntary dashboards can be polished, partial, and changed unilaterally. A performer owed a bonus after a certain number of households cannot know whether the threshold was reached, and a filmmaker with backend participation cannot tell whether edits, algorithmic promotion, or regional licensing changed their earnings.
[P1.5] This requirement also corrects a bargaining imbalance. Studios and streamers already collect detailed data continuously; individual creators usually lack resources to investigate. Mandatory disclosure levels the field without demanding subscriber identities or full business plans. Confidentiality protections can shield competitive details while still providing enough granularity to verify pay.
[P1.6] If compensation depends on audience reach, the people entitled to that compensation must be able to see how it was measured. Otherwise, “audience reach” becomes whatever the payer says it is.

[C1.1] The proposition treats audience data as a payroll record. It isn’t. It is a contested, context-dependent business metric—and turning it into a legally standardized disclosure would create more injustice than it cures.
[C1.2] Start with the illusion of the neutral scoreboard. There is no single “audience reach” that measures value to a film or performance. A completion matters differently for a 90-minute drama than a 30-minute comedy; a rewatch is not a new household; a background stream is not attention; a territory-specific licensing deal changes economics entirely. Standardize these into a mandated metric, and you do not reveal truth—you crown one definition.
[C1.3] Platforms and sophisticated parties will then game that definition. The metric becomes the target, and the target ceases to measure what creators actually care about: durable audience, cultural impact, future opportunities.
[C1.4] Second, mandates bypass the mechanism that already handles this: contracts. If compensation depends on reach, the parties can specify which data, which thresholds, which audit rights, and which confidentiality rules. Guild agreements and individual deals can require independent verification without forcing every service, including small niche platforms, to build a regulator-approved reporting bureaucracy. Mandating disclosure for all services is a blunt instrument; it punishes the platforms least able to bear compliance costs and rewards incumbents with legal teams.
[C1.5] Third, data is not costless to share. Even aggregated viewing data can expose subscriber behavior, regional demand, licensing strategy, and competitive intelligence. More disclosure means more leak risk, more litigation over metric disputes, and more pressure to optimize for reported numbers rather than creative risk. The creatives who gain a dashboard may still lack the power to audit the underlying logs.
[C1.6] The real fix is enforceable contracts and independent audits, not a universal data mandate. If compensation depends on audience reach, let the parties define reach and verify it in the deal. A government-standardized scoreboard does not level the field; it freezes the game in favor of whoever writes the rules.

[P2.6] Standardized data does not eliminate judgment, but it allows comparison, dispute resolution, and trust. The clash is simple: CON wants pay disputes resolved in the dark under private leverage; PRO makes the scoreboard visible enough to enforce the bargain.
