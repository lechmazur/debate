[P1.1] Social media platforms design, profit from, and hold unique visibility into the algorithmic systems that supercharge coordinated harassment campaigns, making a narrow legal duty to mitigate foreseeable offline harm from those campaigns both fair and urgently necessary. This is not a mandate to scrub every offensive comment or police individual political speech. It applies only to organized, networked harassment: swarms of coordinated real and fake accounts sharing home addresses, assembling target lists, coordinating call floods, or issuing explicit calls to confront private individuals, frontline workers, or small business owners offline.
[P1.2] Platforms already have the internal tools to detect this activity: they can spot synchronized account behavior, repeated cross-posting of a single target’s personal information, and spikes in threatening language tied to a coordinated hashtag or group. When that activity reliably precedes swatting, in-person intimidation, workplace disruption, or forced displacement of people from their homes, that harm is not a surprise—it is foreseeable. We already impose this exact kind of duty on every other commercial entity that controls a space where foreseeable harm can occur: a mall that ignores a planned, visible assault on its property faces liability for failing to take reasonable steps to intervene.
[P1.3] There is no coherent reason platforms should get a special pass, especially when their algorithms actively amplify that harassing content to drive engagement and ad revenue. Unlike random, individual negative comments, these campaigns are organized, traceable, and identifiable long before harm spills offline, so mitigation never requires guessing at ambiguous, isolated speech. Reasonable mitigation steps are low-burden: throttling the reach of coordinated harassment networks, removing shared personal information of targets, and flagging imminent threats to law enforcement. This duty protects both public safety and core free expression, because people driven out of public life by targeted, coordinated offline threats cannot exercise their speech rights at all.

Opponents warn of rampant over-censorship, but that objection proves too much: we already hold cafes, malls, schools, and shopkeepers to this exact standard, and none of those entities respond by banning all heated public speech to avoid liability, because the law does not reward random over-censorship as a valid liability shield.
[P5.2] Existing platform rules banning doxxing and explicit threats are toothless without legal accountability, because platforms profit when coordinated harassment drives engagement, and face zero penalty when they let algorithmic amplification turn a small hateful cluster into a campaign that drives a target from their home. It is between giving profit-driven platforms a special, permanent immunity pass for the offline violence they supercharge, even when some bad actors also organize elsewhere, or requiring the same basic reasonable care every other commercial space already follows to protect people from foreseeable, coordinated harm that silences targets before they can exercise their own speech rights.
