[P1.1] Housing is not like televisions or groceries. It is simultaneously the single largest expense for families and the foundational collateral of the banking system. When central banks tolerate housing inflation out of fear that tightening might trigger a downturn, they make a disastrous trade: they invite a permanent cost-of-living crisis and a catastrophic asset bust tomorrow just to dodge a manageable, cyclical slowdown today.
[P1.2] First, unchecked housing inflation drives systemic financial crises. Because real estate is bought with heavy leverage, rising prices fuel speculative borrowing and stretch bank balance sheets. If a central bank flinches from tightening to protect short-term growth, investors assume policymakers will always backstop property values. That moral hazard breeds massive bubbles.
[P1.3] As the 2008 financial crisis proved, a downturn caused by deliberate rate hikes is a temporary, cyclical adjustment; a downturn caused by a bursting housing bubble freezes credit and scars the economy for a decade. Risking a mild recession now prevents a systemic collapse later.
[P1.4] Second, overall price stability is impossible without taming shelter costs. Housing dominates household budgets. When rents and home prices spiral, workers must demand higher wages just to stay housed, embedding inflation across the broader economy. Runaway shelter costs also paralyze labor mobility—preventing workers from moving to productive cities—and lock non-owners into permanent inequality.
[P1.5] Because central banks fuel housing booms through cheap credit, they cannot wash their hands of the resulting inflation.
[P1.6] Opponents will claim that tightening hurts vulnerable workers or slows construction. Yet cooling speculative investor demand and inflated land values is the prerequisite for a functional market. More importantly, compare the harms: a cyclical recession is temporary, and governments have targeted fiscal tools like unemployment insurance to cushion displaced workers.
[P1.7] By contrast, there is no fiscal safety net big enough to rescue an economy with unanchored shelter inflation or a shattered financial sector. Central banks must prioritize breaking housing inflation.

[C1.2] That is too blunt an instrument for a sector-specific imbalance. If the goal is to prevent a bubble, the right tools are loan-to-value limits, debt-service ratios, and targeted supervision—not economy-wide recession risk.
[C1.3] Most central-bank mandates balance price stability with employment because recessions are not costless. When unemployment rises, housing hardship worsens: missed rent, evictions, distressed sales, and falling construction. PRO assumes a mild recession can be cushioned, but tightening that induces recession also tightens credit and reduces household income. A central bank that trades jobs for a component of inflation it cannot quickly control invites political backlash and undermines credibility.
[C1.4] PRO invokes 2008, but the crisis came from lax underwriting, opaque securitization, excessive leverage, and regulatory failure—not simply high house prices. Recession as punishment does not fix those failures; it can force distressed sales and bank losses. If speculation is overheating, regulators should constrain mortgage credit directly. If shelter costs are high, governments should increase supply and reform land-use rules.
[C1.5] Finally, housing inflation is sticky and measured with lags. A lagging shelter index is a poor reason to create unemployment. Rates work slowly and across the whole economy. By the time tightening changes rent measures, recession risk may already be realized.
[C1.6] Central banks should incorporate housing into a balanced inflation target, lean against financial excess with macroprudential tools, and avoid manufacturing a downturn. Stable prices, stable employment, and stable housing beat a recession gamble.
