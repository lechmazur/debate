[P1.1] Tracking is not a transaction. It is an extraction performed on people who cannot see it, cannot measure it, and cannot meaningfully refuse it. Every website visit triggers dozens of background requests to entities the user has never heard of, never agreed to, and cannot identify. This is not a market functioning with informed participants.
[P1.2] It is a market built on their blindness.
[P1.3] The case for legal mandating system-level, opt-out blocking rests on three facts that the opposition cannot honestly dispute.
[P1.4] First, defaults are destiny. When browsers ship with tracking enabled, the overwhelming majority of users remain tracked. Not because they consented, but because the path of least resistance runs through their privacy. Apple's Intelligent Tracking Prevention and Mozilla's Enhanced Tracking Protection demonstrate that browser-level intervention works technically.
[P1.5] But voluntary adoption by a few principued vendors is not enough when the dominant browser maker derives its revenue from advertising. A legal floor ensures the protection does not depend on the goodwill of the very firms that profit from its absence.
[P1.6] Second, the harm is structural, not individual. A single tracker seems trivial. But the aggregate profile assembled across thousands of sites—location, health searches, financial behavior, political reading—creates a surveillance dossier available to data brokers, advertisers, and whoever purchases access next. No user can negotiate this individually.
[P1.7] It is a collective harm requiring a collective remedy.
[P1.8] Third, "opt-out" preserves choice. This proposition does not ban advertising. It does not prohibit tracking for users who affirmatively want it. It requires that platforms offer a robust, accessible mechanism for those who do not.
[P1.9] Contextual advertising—serving ads based on page content rather than behavioral profiles—funded media for decades. Surveillance capitalism is a choice the industry made, not a law of nature.
[P1.10] The question is whether people deserve a real exit door or just the illusion of one. Voluntary, vendor-controlled privacy has been tried. It produced consent pop-ups designed to exhaust, settings buried five menus deep, and extensions that break on update. The experiment failed.
[P1.11] Structural protection is now the proportionate response.

[P2.2] The Apple ATT example cuts against CON, not for them. Yes, revenue shifted. But users gained a real, functional choice where none existed. [P2.4] The "who writes the standard" objection ignores how technical regulation actually works. Agencies routinely set performance floors without writing source code.
