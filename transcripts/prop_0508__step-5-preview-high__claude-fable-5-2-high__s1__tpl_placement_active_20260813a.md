[P1.1] Modern trade is no longer dominated by the ship and the factory. It is dominated by the server, the consultant, the engineer, and the nurse. Services already dominate output and employment in advanced economies, yet they remain the least liberalized part of trade policy. Tariff lines on physical goods are already low in most major blocs, and the marginal gains from shaving another percentage point are small, politically expensive, and often captured by a narrow set of incumbent interests.
[P1.2] The real trade barriers now are regulatory: incompatible professional licensing regimes, data-localization mandates, and duplicative digital compliance rules. These walls stop a Canadian nurse from working in the United States, prevent a European cloud architect from servicing clients across the Atlantic, and force an accounting firm to rebuild the same audit from scratch in every market. Mutual-recognition agreements directly dismantle these barriers. When blocs recognize each other’s digital-service standards or professional credentials, they create immediate, compounding value: labor mobility for critical sectors, faster market entry for services firms, and deeper supply-chain integration that no tariff schedule can replicate.
[P1.3] Consider the difference between cutting a tariff on steel and recognizing an engineer’s qualification. The tariff benefits a domestic producer but raises costs for downstream users; recognition unlocks scarce talent, raises productivity, and lets services scale across borders without multiplying compliance overhead. Prioritizing recognition also respects the political reality that tariff cuts invite distributive fights over which factory wins and which loses, whereas recognition deals generate diffuse, broad-based gains for consumers, professionals, and small digital enterprises alike. The question is not whether trade matters, but where the next dollar of negotiating effort produces the most welfare.
[P1.4] It produces far more when it tears down credential and digital regulatory walls than when it trims an already-low tariff. That is why trade blocs should prioritize mutual-recognition deals.
