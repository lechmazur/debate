[P1.1] Cash bail does not ask whether someone is dangerous; it asks whether someone can pay. A wealthy defendant assessed as high risk can purchase release, while a poor defendant charged with a minor offense remains jailed. Replacing that system with validated risk assessment, judicial review, and appropriately supervised release targets detention at risk rather than wealth. That is why jail populations fall: people who would previously have been confined solely because they could not afford bail can safely await trial in the community.
[P1.2] This is not automatic release, and it does not require abandoning pretrial detention. Serious flight or safety risks can still justify detention under governing law. For others, supervision can include check-ins, electronic monitoring where warranted, treatment requirements, and reminders of court dates. Validation matters: tools should be tested for predictive performance and disparate outcomes, disclosed to the parties, and used to inform—not replace—judicial judgment.
[P1.3] The relevant comparison is not a perfect system against an imaginary risk-free cash-bail regime; it is structured risk management against money-based detention.
[P1.4] New Jersey’s experience after its 2017 reform illustrates the central tradeoff. Its shift away from money bail was accompanied by a substantial reduction in pretrial jail populations, without a corresponding surge in violent crime establishing that reform made communities more violent. That pattern fits the mechanism: releasing people assessed as lower risk does not suddenly place large numbers of violent offenders on the street, while supervision and selective detention address those presenting greater concerns.
[P1.5] Opponents will likely offer frightening individual cases. But a violent offense committed by someone on release is not, by itself, evidence that violent crime *rates* increased, much less that bail reform caused an increase. Cash bail also produces preventable tragedies when dangerous defendants can pay. Nor should rearrest for any offense, a missed court date, or a technical supervision violation be silently converted into “violent crime.”
[P1.6] The motion makes two comparative claims: fewer people needlessly jailed, and no resulting rise in violent crime. Risk-based release achieves both by removing wealth as the gatekeeper while reserving coercive intervention [clipped]
