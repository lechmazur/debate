[P1.1] A voice is not a prop. It is the thing an actor spent a career building — the rasp, the timing, the breath before a line. When a studio uses AI to make that voice speak Portuguese, or speak a rewritten scene, or speak in a sequel filmed after the actor has left, it is not reusing footage. It is generating new performances the performer never gave.
[P1.2] The only question is whether the performer gets to say yes to that specifically, or whether a clause on page 34 says yes on their behalf forever.
[P1.3] First, boilerplate is not consent in any meaningful sense. Real consent requires knowing what you are agreeing to. When a background actor in 2019 signed a "all media now known or hereafter devised" clause, nobody in the room contemplated a model that could generate an infinite number of new line readings in their voice. Applying old language to a technology that did not exist is not honoring a bargain; it is a windfall extracted from ignorance.
[P1.4] Second, the bargaining conditions make boilerplate especially indefensible here. The people most exposed are not the stars with lawyers — they are the day players, the dubbing artists, the voice actors who take the job or lose the rent. A take-it-or-leave-it clause presented at a scanning booth is not a negotiation. Opt-in restores the one thing that makes contracting fair: the ability to price a specific use, or refuse it.
[P1.5] Third, opt-in is cheap and the industry already knows how to do it. Productions obtain specific releases for nudity, for stunts, for likeness in merchandising, for music rights. Adding a discrete grant for synthetic voice generation costs a signature and a line item. Compare that to the cost of the alternative: an actor's cloned voice hawking a product, endorsing a rewritten political scene, or moaning in a sequel they refused.
[P1.6] The default should sit with the person whose body made the sound. Everything else is paperwork.
