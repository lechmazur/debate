[P1.1] Who decides what fills a child’s attention? Right now, the answer is an engagement algorithm optimized to maximize watch time and ad revenue, not the parent standing in the room. The proposition is simple: parents should have more power to block or delay these recommendation engines than app makers currently permit, because the present default strips caregivers of meaningful authority over a child’s digital environment. That authority should include the specific mechanism—algorithmic curation—that now dominates what children see, hear, and believe.
[P1.2] Children are developmentally vulnerable to the precise mechanics algorithmic recommendations exploit: variable reward schedules, infinite scroll, and emotional triggering that pulls young users down rabbit holes. A nine-year-old lacks the prefrontal development to resist a system engineered by behavioral psychologists to hijack impulse control. Parents possess legitimate moral and legal authority to set boundaries on everything from bedtime to television ratings, yet they are denied granular, enforceable tools to delay recommendation features until a child is cognitively ready—or to block them entirely during homework hours, meals, or late nights when the brain is especially susceptible to compulsive loops.
[P1.3] The incentive mismatch makes this urgent. Platforms make money when children stay glued to screens; parents bear the costs in sleep loss, attention fragmentation, and narrowed worldviews. App makers could easily build switches that force chronological or subscription-only feeds, disable autoplay recommendations, or lock in age-gated delays the child cannot override.
[P1.4] Instead, current “parental controls” are often buried, blunt, or reversible by the child with a few taps because friction harms daily active users. A parent who can block explicit content but cannot block the recommendation funnel that leads to it has been given a lock without a door.
[P1.5] This is not a call to ban algorithms. It is a recognition that when corporate design choices systematically overpower family judgment, the party with the child’s best interest at heart deserves veto power. Give parents the keys to the recommendation engine, or stop pretending the digital household belongs to them.

[C1.7] Safety rules, age defaults, and content filters are legitimate; converting every recommendation engine into a parent-gated feature is not.
