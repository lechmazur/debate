[P1.1] Regulation should track plausible harm, not the name of the breeding tool. Limited gene editing can make a small, targeted change—such as disabling a gene that makes a crop vulnerable to disease—without adding foreign DNA. The same outcome might arise through conventional breeding or random mutation, but less predictably. Subjecting that crop to the full traditional GMO regime merely because laboratory scissors produced the change is scientifically incoherent.
[P1.2] “Streamlined” should not mean unregulated. Developers should notify regulators, describe the edit and intended trait, provide molecular characterization, and meet ordinary rules on food safety, seed quality, and environmental protection. Crops engineered to produce a new toxin, alter major nutritional pathways, or carry more complex inserted constructs would still receive fuller review.
[P1.3] This is proportional screening, not a blank check.
[P1.4] The gains are practical. Disease-resistant crops can reduce pesticide use and crop losses. Drought or heat tolerance can help farmers cope with harsher conditions. Edits that slow browning or spoilage can reduce food waste.
[P1.5] None is automatically safe or successful, but delaying all of them through years of compliance designed for fundamentally different transformations imposes real costs while adding little protection.
[P1.6] Heavy traditional GMO regulation also entrenches the largest firms. [P1.7] The precautionary comparison is not gene editing versus a risk-free world. Existing agriculture already relies on pesticides, vulnerable monocultures, and breeding methods that shuffle thousands of genetic differences at once. Countries should therefore scrutinize hazardous products rigorously while allowing low-risk, limited edits to move faster.
