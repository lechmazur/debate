[P1.1] Start with what a voice actually is. It is not a prop the studio rented for a shoot. It is the one instrument an actor cannot put down, the thing audiences use to recognize a person across a lifetime. When a studio clones that voice and makes it speak Mandarin, Hindi, or Portuguese, it is not reformatting a performance the actor gave.
[P1.2] It is manufacturing performances the actor never gave, in languages she may not speak, with inflections she never chose, attached to her name in every market on earth.
[P1.3] Boilerplate was never written for that. The standard "all media now known or hereafter devised" clause was drafted to cover distribution—DVD, streaming, airplane cuts. It covers how a performance travels, not whether a synthetic double can generate new ones. Stretching that language to cover voice cloning is not honoring a contract; it is retroactively inventing a right the actor never knowingly sold.
[P1.4] The day player who signed a three-page deal memo in 2016 to earn scale for two lines did not consent to becoming a permanent, reusable voice asset.
[P1.5] That points to the real clash: who holds the default. Under boilerplate, silence means yes, and the party with all the leverage, all the lawyers, and all the technology decides. Under opt-in, the actor has to actually say yes—to this use, for this project, at this price. The cost of that is trivial: a signature line, a rate card, a tickbox in the deal memo.
[P1.6] The cost of getting it wrong runs the other direction: a performer's voice selling a dubbed line she finds degrading, reading a translation that flattens her choices, or appearing in a version she would have refused.
[P1.7] Opt-in also builds a functioning market. Clear licenses with stated scope and compensation let studios plan and let performers price. Boilerplate invites exactly what the industry just lived through: strikes, lawsuits, and products pulled mid-release.
[P1.8] A voice spoken without permission is ventriloquism. Consent is the only thing that turns it into a performance.

[C1.1] PRO has made a case against hidden consent, not for compulsory opt-in. Those are different propositions. A performer can knowingly agree in advance to AI dubbing, with defined languages, territories, duration, compensation, and prohibited uses. That agreement does not become meaningless merely because it was signed before production rather than repeatedly afterward.
[C1.2] Consent should be judged by what was understood and granted, not by how many signatures were collected.
[C1.3] Nor does AI dubbing automatically manufacture an entirely new performance. Dubbing has always involved translation, altered timing, and vocal choices the original actor never made; a human dubbing artist also creates a performance the actor never gave. The technology matters, but it does not settle the contractual question. If a synthetic voice speaks newly written dialogue, advertises a product, or portrays another character, that is beyond dubbing and should not be sheltered by a dubbing license.
[C1.4] But ordinary localization of an existing performance need not trigger a separate consent regime solely because AI performed the technical work.
[C1.5] PRO treats “all media now known or hereafter devised” as though every contract contained only that phrase and every producer could stretch it indefinitely. Actual disputes depend on the entire agreement, its context, and applicable limits on ambiguity or unfair terms. Where a grant genuinely does not cover synthetic replication, producers should not invent permission. Where the parties clearly granted and priced that right, however, mandatory re-consent adds ceremony rather than protection.
[C1.6] Indeed, PRO’s proposed “tickbox in the deal memo” exposes the weakness. A day player told to tick it before being hired has no magically increased bargaining power; the box can be just as coercive, broad, and underpaid as the boilerplate PRO condemns. Meanwhile, separate permissions across large casts, languages, and catalogue productions create negotiation costs and delays that major stars can turn into leverage but smaller performers cannot.
[C1.7] The safeguard that matters is an enforceable, specific bargain: scope, pay, duration, and remedies, supported by meaningful negotiation. Compulsory opt-in guarantees none of those. It protects a procedure, not necessarily the performer.
