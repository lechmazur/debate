[P1.1] Democracy runs on a shared, rough agreement about what happened. Voters can disagree about taxes or borders, but when they disagree about whether a candidate actually said something, the disagreement stops being political and becomes epistemic. Hyper-realistic political deepfakes attack precisely that foundation, and no democracy has ever survived its citizens losing the ability to distinguish argument from fabrication.
[P1.2] The key word is "hyper-realistic." We're not debating caricature, parody, or obviously exaggerated satire. A politician drawn with an oversized nose on a magazine cover is not what's at issue. What's at issue is synthetic audio or video engineered to be indistinguishable from genuine footage—a candidate appearing to slur a speech, bribe an official, or confess to a crime, two days before an election.
[P1.3] Satire depends on the audience recognizing it as satire. Content designed to defeat that recognition has forfeited its claim to satirical protection, the same way counterfeit money isn't protected speech about economics.
[P1.4] The costs to anonymous speech are real but containable. A narrow ban—targeting false depictions of real political figures doing or saying things they didn't, distributed as authentic—leaves anonymous political commentary, criticism, and whistleblowing untouched. The whistleblower leaking documents and the anonymous blogger skewering a senator lose nothing. What they lose is the ability to fabricate a senator's voice, and that was never speech worth protecting.
[P1.5] The timing problem makes this urgent. Already, real politicians have dismissed genuine embarrassing recordings as "AI fakes," because the existence of the technology provides plausible deniability. Deepfakes don't just create false beliefs—they poison true ones. Every real video becomes contestable.
[P1.6] That corrosion is far more damaging to free political discourse than a ban on synthetic deception could ever be.
[P1.7] We accept cost to speech elsewhere: fraud, defamation, true threats, false advertising. Democracy isn't a suicide pact with perfect liberty of technique. Banning hyper-realistic political deepfakes is a proportionate defense of the only thing that makes free speech meaningful—a public that can tell lies from truths.
