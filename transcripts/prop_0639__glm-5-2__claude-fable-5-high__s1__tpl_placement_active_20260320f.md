[P1.1] Elections don't capture the public's judgment — they capture its fears, partisan loyalties, and reaction to whoever ran the better campaign. A citizens' assembly chosen by sortition captures something different: a cross-section of ordinary people who, given time and structured deliberation, can reason through a policy question and decide it on the merits.
[P1.2] The core case for binding power is simple. Advisory assemblies are structurally toothless. When an assembly issues recommendations, elected officials face no consequence for ignoring them, cherry-picking convenient parts, or burying the report. The deliberation becomes a performance — governments signal virtue while changing nothing.
[P1.3] If citizens can reason about policy well enough to be consulted, their judgment is good enough to hear but not to act on — and that is incoherent.
[P1.4] Binding power changes the equation. It forces the political system to take the assembly's work seriously, because the outcome has legal force. It also changes the assembly itself: participants who know their decision matters invest more in understanding trade-offs. Ireland's citizens' assembly on abortion shows the mechanism — what made it work was that the political system committed in advance to act.
[P1.5] Without that commitment, careful reasoning meets the same fate as every ignored commission report.
[P1.6] The legitimacy objection gets it backward. Sortition produces descriptive representation — a body that mirrors the public on class, gender, ethnicity, and ideology. Elected legislatures systematically over-represent the wealthy, the credentialed, and the professionally political. On specific, well-scoped questions where partisan incentives distort elected decision-making — climate targets, constitutional reform, end-of-life policy — a randomly selected body can claim democratic legitimacy that elections actually fail to provide.
[P1.7] The motion is narrow: binding power on specific questions, not wholesale replacement of Parliament. We're adding a tool for the subset of issues where electoral incentives produce paralysis or distortion, and where genuine public deliberation can do better.

[C1.1] Binding power without accountability is not democracy — it is lottery-appointed rule. When an elected legislature passes a bad law, voters can remove it and its authors. When a citizens' assembly of a hundred randomly chosen people binds the nation and disbands, there is no one to hold responsible, no one to petition, no one to vote out. Accountability is not a decorative feature of democratic legitimacy; it is the mechanism by which power stays answerable.
[C1.2] Sortition severs it completely.
[C1.3] Second, binding power doesn't empower ordinary citizens — it empowers the people who run the assembly. A hundred laypeople deciding climate targets or end-of-life policy depend entirely on curated briefing materials, chosen experts, hired facilitators, and above all the framing of the question itself. Whoever writes the question and selects the witnesses effectively pre-writes the answer. In an advisory system, that risk is contained because elected officials filter the output.
[C1.4] Make the output legally binding, and you've created the most attractive capture target in politics: influence a few dozen deliberators — or the staff who brief them — and you've made law. Lobbyists currently need to move an entire legislature; this hands them a cheaper door.
[C1.5] Third, sortition's statistical promise is weaker than advertised. Participation is voluntary; those who accept a months-long commitment skew toward the retired, the politically engaged, the confident. A body of 100–150 people is a small sample with wide error bars. "Descriptive representation" describes the invitation list, not the room.
[C1.6] Finally, notice what Ireland actually shows. The assembly recommended; the binding decision was made by the entire electorate in a referendum. That is precisely the advisory model working — deliberation informing, the people deciding. The proposition would cut out that final step and let the sample overrule the population.
[C1.7] Assemblies are valuable as advisors precisely because their judgment is filtered through accountable institutions. Remove the filter, and you remove the legitimacy along with it.
