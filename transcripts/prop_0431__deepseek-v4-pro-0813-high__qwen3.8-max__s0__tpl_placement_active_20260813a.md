[P1.1] A school is a shared public trust, not a private parenting service. The moment one family uses teachers to police food, clothing, or social contact that reflects that family’s lifestyle, it stops asking for accommodation and starts conscripting other children into a private code. That is why parents should have less power to make schools enforce highly restrictive personal preferences.
[P1.2] First, such enforcement turns one family’s preference into everyone’s rule. A parent may ban sugar, reject certain clothing, or restrict playmates at home. But when that parent demands the school ban birthday treats for all, impose modesty dress rules on all girls, or resegregate group work and recess, the teacher becomes an enforcer for norms many other families reject. The school is no longer a common institution; it is a tool for private lifestyle control.
[P1.3] Second, it corrupts the teacher’s role. Teachers are hired to teach, assess, and create an inclusive learning environment—not to monitor lunchboxes, hemlines, or cross-gender friendships for a particular household’s comfort. Every minute spent enforcing those rules is a minute lost to instruction and relationship-building. It also puts children in a surveillance relationship with adults who should be educational authorities, not lifestyle police.
[P1.4] Third, the workable default is institutional neutrality. A class of 25 families cannot obey 25 contradictory “highly restrictive” codes. Schools can and should enforce rules grounded in health, safety, harassment prevention, and the basic educational mission. But they should stay out of personal lifestyle orthodoxy.
[P1.5] That gives no parent a veto over common school life, and it protects all families’ equal standing.
[P1.6] Less power here does not mean no parental influence. Parents may still impose their own rules at home, teach their values, and request reasonable individual accommodations. What they should not be able to do is commandeer public schooling to force those preferences on other children. The classroom door is the boundary: inside it, the school must serve all students, not one family’s private enforcement arm.
