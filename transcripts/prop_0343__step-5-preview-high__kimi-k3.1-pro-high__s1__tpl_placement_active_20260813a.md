[P1.1] Universities already live in an AI-saturated world. Students are using these tools daily. The only question is whether universities govern that reality with transparent rules or pretend it away. The proposition wins because disclosure plus accountability converts a hidden behavior into an assessable skill.
[P1.2] Prohibition does not stop AI use; it merely rewards deception. When schools ban these tools outright, they create a black market of secret usage and an atmosphere of suspicion. A student who submits a suspiciously polished paper is suddenly presumed guilty. That dynamic poisons trust and encourages students to hide the very intellectual process instructors should be examining.
[P1.3] Permitting disclosed AI use removes the incentive to lie. It shifts the conversation from “did you cheat?” to “how did you use this tool, and can you defend what it produced?”
[P1.4] The accountability condition is the engine of the proposition. A student may use AI to draft, but they remain responsible for every factual claim and every logical step. That is not a loophole; it is a higher standard. If the AI hallucinates a case citation or a theorem, the student must catch it.
[P1.5] If the argument is weak, the student must repair it. In that environment, the grade measures the same thing it always should: judgment, verification, and reasoning. The tool changes; the intellectual burden does not.
[P1.6] This framework also democratizes access. A non-native English speaker or a student from a weak secondary writing program can use AI to structure a draft while still being graded on the substance of their analysis. Banning the tool freezes existing advantages; permitting disclosed use evens the playing field.
[P1.7] Finally, “most coursework” is deliberately calibrated. In-class exams that test unaided recall may still prohibit AI. But for take-home essays, research papers, and projects, the real world already runs on these tools. Universities should teach students to use them responsibly rather than pretending they do not exist.
[P1.8] Allow the tool, demand the disclosure, enforce the accountability.

[C1.1] Disclosure can tell us that AI was used; it cannot tell us who did the thinking. That is the fatal gap in this proposition. A student may submit an accurate, well-reasoned essay after checking an AI draft while still being unable to formulate its thesis, structure its argument, or repair its reasoning alone. The artifact passes, but the student has not demonstrated the competence the course exists to teach.
[C1.2] “Accountability for accuracy and reasoning” does not solve that problem. It makes verification sound like a safeguard when verification itself requires expertise. Novices are precisely the people least equipped to detect a plausible falsehood, a missing counterargument, or a subtly circular proof. A law student who cannot yet construct a legal argument cannot reliably certify an AI-generated memo merely by checking its citations.
[C1.3] Error-free output is not evidence of learning.
[C1.4] Nor does disclosure make assessment enforceable. Once drafting is permitted across most coursework, instructors cannot determine from a draft whether a student deeply revised it or merely accepted it. Reliable oral defenses for every essay and project would transform the assessment burden; unreliable AI detection would preserve the suspicion PRO claims to eliminate. The rule therefore promises accountability without supplying a practical way to observe it.
[C1.5] PRO presents a false choice between universal permission and prohibition. Universities can teach AI literacy, authorize it for designated tasks, require drafts and oral examinations where appropriate, and still refuse to let it replace the core intellectual work in most coursework. Professional relevance does not change that principle: pilots may use automation, but training must still establish that they can fly.
[C1.6] The equity argument also cuts against PRO. Students with weaker preparation may benefit most from a polished draft while being least able to audit it. Writing support should remove barriers to expressing a student’s thinking, not quietly supply the thinking itself.
[C1.7] A university credential must certify more than responsible supervision of fluent software. Until independent competence is demonstrated, disclosure is documentation—not a substitute for learning.
