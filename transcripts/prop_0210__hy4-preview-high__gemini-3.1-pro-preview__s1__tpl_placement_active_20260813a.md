[P1.1] Consider a drug approved on two positive trials while five negative trials stay buried in a sponsor’s files. Doctors and guideline writers see only the flattering half, and patients unknowingly repeat risks they never accepted. This is not exotic abuse; it is the predictable result of letting companies treat unfavorable evidence as a private asset. Requiring disclosure as a condition of market access removes that incentive.
[P1.2] Clinical research is not an ordinary product test. It depends on volunteers who accept physical risk to produce knowledge that governs others’ treatment. When a sponsor hides unfavorable or inconclusive findings, it takes that contribution and repays it with a distorted evidence base. Conditioning market access on disclosure restores reciprocity: if you want the privileges of the market, you must supply the full record, not just the good news.
[P1.3] The practical stakes are large. Published literature skews positive, so systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and guidelines can overstate benefit and miss harms. Disclosure lets regulators, payers, and independent researchers reanalyze everything, prevents wasted repeat trials, and improves reimbursement decisions that already rest on comparative effectiveness. Voluntary registries have narrowed the problem but not solved it; reporting is often late, incomplete, or gamed.
[P1.4] A market-access precondition is the lever that works because it ties revenue to verified transparency.
[P1.5] Opponents will cite competitiveness and innovation. But this condition need not expose protected intellectual property or personal data; it requires disclosure of efficacy and safety results, with narrow redactions for genuinely confidential information. It does not block a useful drug; it blocks a half-truth. Countries should not have to choose between access to medicines and an honest evidence base.
[P1.6] Market access is the moment of maximum state leverage, so attaching this duty there is the proportionate way to protect patients, respect participants, and keep medical knowledge from becoming merely a marketing byproduct.
