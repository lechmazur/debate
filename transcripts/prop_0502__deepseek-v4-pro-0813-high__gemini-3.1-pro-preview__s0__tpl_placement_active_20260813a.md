[P1.1] The Arctic is no longer a frozen margin. Shrinking ice is opening shipping lanes, seabed minerals, and fisheries while coastal states push overlapping continental shelf claims. The current order—UNCLOS plus the Arctic Council—turns this fragile region into a resource race. A binding Antarctic-style treaty is not an idealistic leap; it is the only structure that treats the Arctic as a shared commons rather than a set of competing national projects.
[P1.2] The Antarctic Treaty System proves the model works. Since 1959 it has demilitarized a continent, frozen territorial assertions, banned commercial mineral exploitation through its later protocol, and built a regime of inspection, scientific cooperation, and collective decision-making. The Arctic needs that same legal ceiling because without it, the strongest coastal states will set the rules for everyone else. Competing claims do not produce stability; they produce escalation by legal filings, coast guard patrols, and infrastructure grabs.
[P1.3] The existing Arctic rules are too thin. UNCLOS gives states economic rights but lacks binding environmental duties, effective dispute resolution for overlapping shelves, and enforceable standards for ice-covered shipping. The Arctic Council is a forum, not a regulator—it cannot compel pollution response, create protected areas, or restrain militarization. That gap is not neutral; it favors those already on the Arctic map and leaves others, including affected Indigenous peoples, with only consultative roles.
[P1.4] The risks are global, not regional. Arctic change drives sea-level rise, disrupted ocean circulation, and methane release. A major oil spill in moving ice would be far harder to contain than any temperate-zone disaster, yet no binding liability or response regime exists. The Arctic should not be governed by five separate strategies and slow-moving diplomacy.
[P1.5] It should be governed by one binding treaty that limits militarism, protects the environment, guarantees free scientific access, and shares decisions beyond the bordering states.

[C1.1] The proposition rests on a fundamental geographic and demographic error: treating the Arctic like Antarctica. Antarctica is an uninhabited, isolated landmass surrounded by ocean. Imposing an Antarctic-style treaty to freeze claims and ban development is not just impractical; it is a direct assault on sovereignty and self-determination.
[C1.2] First, the Antarctic model would disenfranchise the four million people who live in the Arctic, including numerous Indigenous nations. PRO demands we treat the region as a "shared commons" [P1.1], which practically means stripping Arctic states and native populations of their rightful economic zones and resource management to appease non-Arctic states seeking influence. Under the current system, Indigenous peoples have a direct, institutionalized voice through the Arctic Council. A global treaty would dilute their leverage, subordinating their rights to the environmental and economic agendas of nations thousands of miles away.
[C1.3] Second, PRO’s claim that existing rules are "too thin" [P1.3] ignores the reality of how well the current system functions. The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) provides a rigorous, science-based mechanism for delineating continental shelves. Overlapping claims are not escalating "infrastructure grabs" [P1.2]; they are standard, peaceful legal processes. Through the Ilulissat Declaration, Arctic coastal states have already committed to resolving these overlaps peacefully under international law.
[C1.4] Tearing up this functioning legal architecture to negotiate a sweeping, binding treaty from scratch would plunge the region into a legal vacuum, sparking the exact tensions PRO hopes to avoid.
[C1.5] Finally, an Antarctic-style treaty mandating demilitarization and resource bans is functionally impossible here. The international community cannot dictate the demilitarization of sovereign borders like the U.S. coast of Alaska, Norway's northern coast, or Russia’s Kola Peninsula. Coastal states will simply refuse to sign a treaty that compromises their national defense and legitimate economic zones.
[C1.6] The Arctic does not need a utopian global treaty that ignores sovereign reality. It needs the continued, practical enforcement of UNCLOS and regional cooperation.

That gap becomes acute precisely when a seabed mining rush, a major spill, or a military incident occurs.

[P2.4] Demilitarization is not impossible simply because borders exist. The Antarctic model does not abolish coast guards; it bans military measures and weapons testing while creating inspection and transparency. The legal vacuum objection also fails: a negotiated treaty would supplement UNCLOS, not void it.
[P2.5] The real choice is not utopia versus a working order. It is a binding commons regime versus a permissive system in which the largest Arctic states set the rules and everyone else waits outside.
