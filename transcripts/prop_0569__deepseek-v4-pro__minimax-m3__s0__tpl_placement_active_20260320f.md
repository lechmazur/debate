[P1.1] When an AI chatbot is the primary mental health intervention, there is no safety net between a vulnerable person and a system that cannot understand what it reads. Chatbots process text through statistical patterns; they cannot detect the despair behind the words, interpret tone, or notice that a user has stopped sleeping. A fixed-session handoff is not a bureaucratic burden—it is a minimum safeguard to prevent avoidable harm.
[P1.2] The danger grows with repetition. A person can spend weeks in AI-only care, receiving scripted encouragement while their condition worsens. The bot cannot make the clinical leap from “I’m tired” to “this may be a prodromal sign of a suicidal crisis.” It cannot spot the warning signs that a human therapist would flag within the first session. Without a mandatory checkpoint, a user may spiral unnoticed because the system keeps delivering the same optimistic exercises.
[P1.3] The fixed number of sessions acts as a tripwire: after a predetermined point, a licensed professional must review the case, assess risk, and decide whether to continue AI support, begin human therapy, or escalate care. This is not medicine by algorithm; it is medicine with a human decision point.
[P1.4] A fixed limit also closes the regulatory gaps that allow companies to offer “unlimited” AI therapy with no human involvement. A bright-line rule gives regulators an enforceable standard, preventing the corrosive practice of keeping patients in indefinite chatbot loops to cut costs. It mirrors other safety mandates—we require re-evaluations for controlled medications, not because every prescription is dangerous, but because the risk of error accumulates over time. The same logic applies here.
[P1.5] After the handoff, AI can still play a valuable role. The law prohibits only permanent, unsupervised automation of mental health care. That modest requirement—a single human checkpoint after a finite course—is a prudent, life-saving measure that costs far less than the tragedies it prevents.
