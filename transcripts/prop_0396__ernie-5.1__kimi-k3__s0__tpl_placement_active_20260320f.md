[P1.1] Medical debt is the wrong signal in the wrong system. Credit reports exist to predict future repayment risk—whether a borrower will honor future obligations. Illness tells us almost nothing about that. A person diagnosed with cancer, a car accident victim, a parent whose child needs emergency surgery—none of these people become less reliable debtors because they got sick.
[P1.2] Their repayment capacity depends on income, employment, and financial discipline, none of which illness meaningfully predicts. Yet today, a single medical bill can drop a credit score by a hundred points or more, blocking access to housing, employment, and even affordable insurance—the very things needed to recover and eventually pay that debt off.
[P1.3] This isn't a minor statistical inconvenience. Medical debt is the single largest source of collection items on American credit reports, affecting tens of millions of people. Crucially, a significant share of this debt stems from billing errors, surprise out-of-network charges, or insurance denials that patients had no power to prevent. These aren't choices—they're impositions.
[P1.4] Punishing someone's creditworthiness for a billing dispute they never asked for is like docking your credit score because a restaurant overcharged you.
[P1.5] The consequence is a self-reinforcing trap. Damaged credit raises the cost of every financial product a sick person needs to rebuild—higher rent deposits, steeper auto loan rates, pricier utilities. The debt becomes harder to escape, not because the person is irresponsible, but because the system treats their misfortune as a character flaw.
[P1.6] Credit scoring models are tools, and like all tools, they should be evaluated by whether they measure what they claim to measure. Medical debt does not measure repayment intent or capacity. It measures vulnerability. Removing it from credit reports doesn't erase the debt—it simply stops using a person's worst health crisis as a permanent mark against their financial future.
