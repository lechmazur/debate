[P1.1] A commodity windfall is not ordinary income. It is a finite public asset arriving in volatile foreign-currency bursts. The test is not how fast money reaches pockets, but whether a country preserves stability and turns depletion into durable wealth. A sovereign wealth fund is built for that job.
[P1.2] A cash dividend is a receipt for selling the family silver.
[P1.3] Cash mishandles the boom. Pay an oil or copper windfall to households and much of it becomes imports, higher land prices, crowded services, and exchange-rate pressure. The non-resource tradable sector—the jobs needed after the field declines—gets squeezed. A fund can hold foreign assets, keep inflows from flooding the economy, and release money through rule-based budget transfers.
[P1.4] When prices fall, the state can still pay teachers, maintain clinics, and finish roads instead of cutting payrolls in a crash.
[P1.5] Time is the second problem. Equal grants sound fair, but they give this generation the proceeds and the next generation a depleted asset plus the same expectations. Thin universal checks change little; checks large enough to matter can overheat the economy. A fund keeps the principal intact, lets returns finance schools, health, ports, grids, and courts, and forces spending to match the state's ability to appraise projects.
[P1.6] Botswana and Chile illustrate the direction: save in good years, spend by capacity, avoid panic in bad years.
[P1.7] Finally, rules beat auctions. A visible fund with published deposits, withdrawal rules, and audits gives citizens a concrete question: where did this year's royalty go? Cash creates an electoral bidding war over the largest check, with no constituency for tomorrow. Funds can be raided; that calls for tighter mandates, not distributing the principal.
[P1.8] A stolen balance can be investigated; a consumed balance is gone. Cash may relieve one bill. It cannot turn an exhaustible resource into a resilient economy.

[C1.1] Start from the actual setting: young populations, poor households, weak courts, captured auditors, and ruling parties that control extraction. There, a sovereign wealth fund is not a stabilizer; it is a large discretionary pool of public money managed behind closed doors. That is exactly what patronage networks want. Direct cash is the opposite: an equal, visible payment to every citizen, hard to divert without millions noticing.
[C1.2] The fund-first case leans on Botswana and Chile. But those are places where institutions were already strong enough to constrain the fund. The fund did not create accountability; it required it. Transplant that design to a fragile petrostate and you get the familiar result: off-budget spending, borrowing collateralized by resource wealth, and investments that reward connected intermediaries.
[C1.3] A bad rule is not better than a simple rule because it is called a rule.
[C1.4] The macro argument overstates cash’s harm. Poor households spend largely on local food, school fees, transport, and small trade; that expands domestic activity, not merely imports. If overheating is a concern, payments can be phased and a lean stabilization buffer kept. The real Dutch disease risk is public megaprojects and elite consumption, which a sovereign fund can delay—but also conceal.
[C1.5] Intergenerational fairness cuts the other way. A fund holding foreign bonds while children remain malnourished is not protecting the future; it is lending to rich economies. Cash spent on health and schooling builds the asset future generations actually need: productive citizens. And the claim that “a stolen balance can be investigated” assumes the investigators are not the thieves.
[C1.6] In weak states, a stolen fund balance is often buried; a missed universal payment creates millions of complainants. The better default is direct, transparent, universal distribution, with only a lean buffer for volatility. Less elegant, far harder to loot.

In weak states, each is a leak: rulers can understate receipts, steer payment contracts, exclude regions, or consume principal through a popular dividend.
[P2.2] Millions notice a missing check; they cannot see the missing revenue that should have made it larger.
[P2.3] The Botswana/Chile point [C1.2] proves too much. Funds do not manufacture honest courts; they give scarce accountability a definable object—royalty in, rule-based deposit, budget transfer out. Cash dissolves that object into private spending. And “phased” payments plus a “lean stabilization buffer” [C1.4] concede the core: cash alone cannot manage volatility.
[P2.4] A buffer absorbing shocks and releasing foreign exchange over time is fund logic; the remaining dividend is a cash add-on, not an alternative to saving.
[P2.5] The local-spending reply also fails. Food, rent, transport, and school fees are precisely where boom supply is scarce; universal cash bids up prices and imports at the margin. Small checks cannot transform poverty; large checks liquidate a finite asset into current demand. Nor does cash build public systems.
[P2.6] Malnourished children need clinics, teachers, water, and payrolls after prices fall—not one election-cycle payment. A fund warehouses purchasing power for steady budget capacity—even targeted transfers—without flooding the economy.
[P2.7] The cash metric—“far harder to loot” [C1.6]—is too narrow. It counts theft, not depletion, currency pressure, or future budgets. A badly governed fund leaves a stock and paper trail that reformers can contest; a delivered dividend leaves no principal in the bust. Captured auditors do not vanish under cash; discretion moves upstream to extraction contracts and downstream to registries.
[P2.8] Weak institutions argue for tighter deposit and withdrawal rules, not automatic liquidation of the asset.

[C2.1] The administrative objection cuts the wrong way. Universal cash and a sovereign fund both require royalties to be declared and deposited. Cash replaces that with a public formula—declared receipts divided among citizens.
[C2.2] Citizenship rolls and payment rails are easier to audit than fund portfolios; any excluded citizen or smaller check is instantly contestable. When payouts shrink, the shortfall becomes visible in millions of households. [P2.2] assumes citizens cannot see missing revenue, but a smaller check is exactly the signal that sends them looking.
[C2.3] The buffer point is not concession. A lean stabilization reserve is not the proposition’s sovereign wealth fund; it smooths sharp swings while leaving the default as a recurring, rule-based dividend. It does not require open-ended foreign-asset accumulation, intergenerational portfolios, or project pools that become patronage vehicles. If every reserve counts as “fund logic,” the term loses meaning.
[C2.4] The real choice is cash distributes the windfall as a visible entitlement; fund-first retains principal in an institution that, in weak states, rulers can borrow against or invest in connected intermediaries.
[C2.5] On local spending, poor households buying food, school fees, and small trade fuel broad domestic activity; any pressure can be tempered by phasing and supply-side entry. The malnourished child of [P2.6] gains more from cash now than from a future clinic payroll managed by a captured board. Cash does not abandon public systems; it keeps them politically urgent instead of silently deferring them.
[C2.6] Finally, [P2.7]’s wider metric depends on the fund not being looted. In captured states, theft is upstream of every stabilization goal. A stolen balance cannot support budgets, and the paper trail matters only where courts and auditors are independent. Direct payments cannot stop upstream corruption, but they narrow post-royalty chokepoints.
[C2.7] A short check is felt by millions; a bad fund investment is felt by almost no one. That is why cash-first should remain the default.

[C4.1] A1: The principled line is purpose and ceiling, not size. A stabilization reserve is a bounded smoothing account: deposits stop above a published level, assets are short-term, and it does not finance projects or retain multi-generational principal. By contrast, a sovereign wealth fund is an open-ended accumulation vehicle with a discretionary portfolio. That distinction is exactly what matters in weak states.
[C4.2] A capped, formula-driven buffer leaves little room to loot; an ever-growing fund with valuation discretion adds a chokepoint. If PRO says any smoothing reserve is “fund logic” [P4.6], the term expands until it no longer names the proposition, and the real debate should be between a capped reserve plus dividend and a long-horizon state investment fund.
[C4.3] A2: No instrument can make volatile receipts stable; the question is whether the adjustment is visible. Under cash, checks shrink in a bust according to a public formula, and millions immediately notice. Under a fund, the same shortfall is hidden in drawdowns, postponed payrolls, or borrowing against the fund—which is ideal collateral for off-budget loans. The “bust dilemma” is inherent to exhaustible revenue; cash does not create it.
[C4.4] Cash makes any attempt to keep the check constant require an unmistakable political decision to borrow, not an obscure portfolio transaction. A small buffer can soften the fall without pretending to abolish it.
[C4.5] So PRO’s replies still beg the question. They answer capture by promising mechanical rules and audits [P4.1], but in a captured state the same rulers control the rulebook and the billions it protects. Cash does not solve upstream theft, but it ensures the downstream shortfall is felt immediately. A short check creates demand for answers; an invisible fund loss does not [C2.7].
[C4.6] Since stabilization only needs that bounded reserve, cash-first with a lean buffer remains the safer default; the open-ended sovereign wealth fund is the part that is neither necessary nor safe.
