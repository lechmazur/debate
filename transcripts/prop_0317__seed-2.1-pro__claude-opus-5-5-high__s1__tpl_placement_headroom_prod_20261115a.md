[P1.1] Live event ticketing is not an open, competitive commodity market, and the current unregulated resale ecosystem betrays every stakeholder except brokers and the platforms themselves. Major platforms hold exclusive, contractually locked distribution rights for venues and tours, control initial sale access, and collect double fees—once on the original purchase, again on resale—with no accountability for who ends up with seats, while bot-powered brokers scoop up most desirable inventory in seconds to mark up prices by hundreds of percent for fans who saved for months to attend a show, playoff game, or family theater performance.
[P1.2] This is not neutral, efficient market activity; it is engineered rent-seeking that platforms have every incentive to enable, because they earn a cut of every higher resale price without delivering any additional value to artists, venues, or audiences. Requiring platforms to either cap resale markups at a level high enough to cover transaction fees and small, reasonable premiums for fans who truly cannot attend an event, or provide integrated, verified face-value resale channels, fixes this failure without eliminating legitimate resale.
[P1.3] It cuts out the parasitic speculative layer that distorts initial sales dynamics, eliminates the flood of fake tickets that scammers peddle on unvetted secondary markets, and ensures the core purpose of live events—connecting artists and teams to the fans who sustain their work—is not held hostage to extractive toll-taking. Reasonable markup caps do not stop someone who bought a ticket and fell ill, had a work conflict, or changed plans from reselling it without losing money; they stop professional brokers from treating concert, theater, and sports tickets like unregulated speculative assets permanently priced out of reach for ordinary working fans who are the entire reason live events have cultural and financial value in the first place.

[C1.6] Finally, artists who want face-value resale can already demand it, and some do. A mandate removes that choice from everyone else.

There is no thriving black market for a ticket that will not let you through the turnstile; unvetted social media scammers thrive right now, in the unregulated status quo, because platforms have no reason to crack down when they collect fees on every inflated resale anyway.
[P2.2] The argument that open price gouging gives desperate fans a “guaranteed way in” [C1.4] is backwards. That guarantee only exists for fans with the most disposable income, not the most attachment to the event. A teacher who saved $100 for a show cannot outbid a broker charging $500 for the same seat, no matter how badly they want to attend. Caps do not hand tickets to people with fast internet; they take the profit out of bot attacks in the first place, because scooping up hundreds of seats no longer delivers a massive windfall.
[P2.3] The claim that artists can already choose face-value resale [C1.6] ignores lopsided contract power between dominant platforms and every artist who is not a global stadium headliner. Existing anti-bot and disclosure rules [C1.5] have sat on the books for years with almost no meaningful enforcement, because platforms profit too much from the current system to police it. A mandate does not strengthen platform gatekeepers; it strips them of the perverse incentive to prioritize broker markups over the fans who actually make events valuable.
