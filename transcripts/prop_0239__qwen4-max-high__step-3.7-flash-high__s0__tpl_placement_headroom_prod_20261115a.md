[P1.1] Consent answers one question: did this person agree? Ethics asks more: what was agreed to, who else pays, and what is left of the person afterward. Consent is a veto, not a verdict. Its absence condemns a transaction; its presence does not acquit it.
[P1.2] Consider third parties, where consent is undisputed. A landowner and a waste contractor sign a careful, informed, mutually delighted contract to bury solvents above the aquifer that feeds the village downstream. Nobody was coerced. Nobody downstream was asked either.
[P1.3] Consent binds only the consenting; it cannot launder a harm imposed on people who never entered the room. The same logic sinks vote-selling and price-fixing: every signature is genuine, and the injury lands on strangers.
[P1.4] Then there is consent that devours itself. A worker freely signs a lifetime bond he can never exit, or a person sells himself into permanent servitude. Every step is voluntary; the destination is a life with no further choices in it. If consent were sufficient, it would justify its own abolition — an ethics that cannot say no to that has confused a procedural checkpoint with a moral conclusion.
[P1.5] And terms matter even when agreement is real. The only surgeon for a hundred miles tells a father the price is everything he owns and his daughter's next ten years of wages. The father says yes, gratefully, and means it. The desperation was not the surgeon's doing — but the pricing was.
[P1.6] We judge him because ethics evaluates what people do with leverage, not merely whether the other party said yes.
[P1.7] None of this calls anyone stupid or incapable. It only insists that other people exist, that some goods are held in common, and that agreement is the beginning of moral scrutiny, not its end.

[C1.1] Consent is a key safeguard against coercion, but it is a procedural threshold, not a substantive ethical seal. A transaction can meet the bare minimum of individual agreement while still violating core moral commitments, because ethics requires accounting for harms to people who never signed the contract, the inalienability of human dignity, and the difference between formal agreement and meaningful choice.
[C1.2] Consider a logging company that secures consent from low-wage workers to clear old-growth forest that is the sole water source for a remote indigenous village. Every worker agreed because they had no other way to support their families, and no individual threatened them to sign.
[C1.3] But the village, which has held stewardship of the land for centuries, never consented, and the deforestation will cause water contamination and cultural erasure for generations. Consent between the company and its employees cannot wash away the violation of the community’s collective rights, which are not up for individual bargaining.
[C1.4] This also exposes the flaw in treating consent as a blanket ethical waiver. When a transaction eliminates a person’s future capacity to choose for themselves—like a worker consenting to a lifetime indenture contract with no exit clause, or a person selling all their future organs for a one-time payout—the consent undermines the very condition of moral agency that makes consent meaningful. You cannot ethically agree to give up your ability to agree later; that is a logical and moral contradiction no signature can resolve.
[C1.5] Finally, formal consent often masks structural coercion. When a single employer in a company town forces workers to consent to 16-hour shifts in unsafe conditions for pay that barely covers rent, the "agreement" is not truly voluntary: it is extracted by the absence of alternatives. Consent cannot launder exploitation baked into the system, even when no individual exerts direct pressure.

[C2.1] PRO’s claim that my opening “conceded outright” rests on a misreading: I never agreed consent is insufficient, only that invalid consent—obtained via coercion, fraud, or incapacity—fails to meet the threshold of genuine consent in the first place. The core of PRO’s case is that even perfect, valid consent cannot address third-party harms, but this conflates the ethics of a transaction with the broader ethics of social outcomes. A transaction’s ethical status depends on the conduct of the parties who enter it, not every ripple effect on strangers.
[C2.2] The village downstream from the logging site may suffer, but that harm is addressed by environmental regulation, not by declaring the workers’ voluntary contract unethical. PRO also argues that requiring valid consent “packs every ethical requirement into the word consent,” making the claim a tautology. This is a straw man. Valid consent—voluntary, informed, uncoerced—has always been the standard for ethical transactions.
[C2.3] When we say consent alone is sufficient, we mean that if this threshold is met, no further ethical barriers exist between the consenting parties. The company-town worker’s agreement to long shifts is only invalid if he is directly coerced; if he signs freely, even with limited options, his autonomy is respected, and the transaction is ethical. The desperation limiting his options is a social failure, not a flaw in the transaction itself. Similarly, the father who gratefully agrees to the surgeon’s price exercises his agency; condemning the surgeon for leveraging his rare skill violates the surgeon’s own autonomy.
[C2.4] Finally, the claim that consenting to limit future choices is a logical contradiction fails: people make binding commitments constantly—marriage vows, business contracts, military enlistment—all of which restrict future options, and all are ethical when based on valid initial consent.
