[P1.1] Populism is not merely criticism of elites; it is an anti-pluralist ideology that divides society into a morally pure people and a corrupt elite, claiming that only the former possesses legitimate authority. When such movements capture the state, they do not correct elite failure—they dismantle the very checks that prevent any elite from becoming tyrannical, including the ones that would restrain themselves.
[P1.2] Consider the mechanism. Populist leaders routinely attack independent courts, free press, and professional civil service as "enemies of the people," replacing institutional accountability with personal loyalty. In Hungary and Turkey, elected populists systematically weakened judicial independence and media pluralism, converting competitive democracies into electoral autocracies. In Venezuela, a similar trajectory hollowed out legislative and judicial constraints.
[P1.3] These were not corrections of elite capture; they were the capture of the state by new elites wearing anti-establishment costumes, using democratic mandates to entrench permanent power.
[P1.4] The proposition holds because populism treats institutional friction as betrayal rather than protection. A healthy corrective to elite failure works through parliaments, courts, and parties—strengthening these bodies against corruption and capture, expanding participation within the rules. Populism, by contrast, bypasses them, concentrating power in a charismatic leader who claims to embody the popular will exclusively. When institutions block the "will of the people," populists do not negotiate; they abolish or pack the courts, rewrite constitutions, and delegitimize opposition as treason.
[P1.5] This is why populist surges function as warning signs rather than remedies. They reveal that democratic institutions have already lost legitimacy—perhaps through genuine elite failure—but their rise accelerates decay rather than reversing it. The underlying disease is institutional weakness; populism is the opportunistic infection that kills the patient while promising a cure. Democracies need correctives that reinforce the architecture of accountability, not movements that promise to tear it down in the name of the people.

[C1.1] Populism looks dangerous mainly if we define it by its worst leaders. Most populist movements are not a claim to abolish pluralism; they are a claim that pluralism has already narrowed to a managed choice between interchangeable elites. When parties converge, donors matter more than districts, and major grievances are ruled out of respectable debate, naming 'the people' against 'the elite' is a corrective act of representation.
[C1.2] That correction works through ordinary democratic pressure. Populist outsiders raise turnout among citizens who had stopped voting, break policy taboos, and force established parties to compete again. They expose corruption not as individual scandal but as a system of access, push referendums, primaries, and anti-cartel reforms, and create channels for voters who felt spoken for but never listened to. The threat of losing power is often the only language insulated elites understand.
[C1.3] History shows absorption more often than collapse. America's late-nineteenth-century Populists were denounced as extremists, yet many of their demands — railroad regulation, a graduated income tax, more direct democratic selection — were later adopted through normal legislation. In contemporary Europe, populist parties in places such as Denmark and the Netherlands have entered parliaments and coalitions, moderated under responsibility, and pushed mainstream parties to address welfare, immigration, and European integration without dismantling courts or elections.
[C1.4] Hungary, Venezuela, or Turkey prove something narrower: where courts, media, and parties were already fragile and personalized, an authoritarian leader can exploit any majority — populist, nationalist, or technocratic. Blaming populism confuses a weak institution's vulnerability with the movement that exposed it. In stronger democracies, checks check populists too, and that interaction is healthy rather than pathological.
[C1.5] Elite failure is not corrected by deference to the elites who produced it. It is corrected when outsiders make failure electorally expensive. Populism is usually that penalty — messy, sometimes crude, but fundamentally a sign voters still believe democracy can be made to answer them.

Calling opponents part of a distant establishment is harsh campaigning; it is not the same as stripping them of citizenship, banning their parties, or refusing to leave office.
[C2.2] The word the other side cannot carry is “usually.” Hungary, Turkey, and Venezuela are cases of executive capture in already personalized systems. The denominator is far larger: populist parties that win seats, enter coalitions, lose votes, accept defeats, and see their best ideas absorbed while their worst are filtered out. [C2.4] Their treatment of Denmark and the Netherlands gives the game away. They say populists moderated there because institutions constrained them. Exactly. That interaction is how a healthy corrective works: outsiders force neglected issues onto the agenda, institutions block excess, mainstream parties adapt, voters get a response without a rupture.
[C2.5] You cannot credit institutions for every good outcome and blame populism for every bad one. If constraint produces moderation, populism is not inherently a solvent on democracy; the variable is institutional strength, which was our argument from the start.
[C2.6] Criticizing courts and media is also not identical to destroying them. Democracies routinely argue over judicial appointments, press bias, and bureaucratic insulation. The test is compliance and alternation: do they obey rulings, face hostile coverage, and risk losing? Usually, in functioning democracies, they do.
[C2.7] The fever metaphor reverses causation — discontent is often the immune response that forces a complacent body politic to pay attention again.
