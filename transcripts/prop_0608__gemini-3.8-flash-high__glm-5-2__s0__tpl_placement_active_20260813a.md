[P1.1] A corporate “net-zero” claim should signify that an enterprise has actually stopped warming the planet, not that it purchased permission to keep polluting. When fossil fuel producers, airlines, and consumer brands market themselves as climate-neutral while their smokestacks and supply chains operate business-as-usual, net-zero becomes an accounting shell game. Legally capping offsets to a small share of emissions is the only credible way to tether corporate climate claims to atmospheric reality.
[P1.2] First, atmospheric physics demands direct decarbonization. The climate responds to cumulative physical emissions, not financial ledger entries. Offsets suffer from intractable systemic flaws: inflated baselines, carbon leakage, and severe impermanence. Extracting and burning fossil carbon alters the atmosphere for millennia, whereas an offsetting tree plantation can incinerate in a single wildfire season.
[P1.3] Mandating that the vast majority of reductions occur in-value-chain—through efficiency, fleet electrification, and clean production methods—guarantees permanent, irreversible cuts that cannot be undone by external shocks.
[P1.4] Second, unconstrained offsets create a dangerous moral hazard. They offer an artificially cheap escape hatch that delays essential transition investments. As long as firms can purchase paper credits for pennies on the dollar, executive suites will postpone the necessary capital expenditures required to phase out fossil energy and decarbonize suppliers. A strict legal limit forces corporate capital into deep structural change, properly reserving offsets strictly for true residual emissions in hard-to-abate processes.
[P1.5] Finally, market integrity requires binding legal boundaries. Voluntary guidelines have demonstrably failed, fostering a race to the bottom where greenwashing brands mislead consumers and capital markets. Under the status quo, bad actors outcompete genuine innovators who undertake the expensive work of real decarbonization. An enforceable legal cap restores truth in advertising, ensuring that a “net-zero” credential signifies a fundamentally decarbonized business model, not merely an astute public relations strategy.

[C1.1] The proposition treats offsets as a loophole to be closed. That framing gets the economics backwards. Offsets are not an escape from decarbonization—they are a mechanism for financing decarbonization that would not otherwise occur, often in places and sectors where capital is scarcest and emissions are growing fastest.
[C1.2] Consider the core contradiction in PRO's case. It insists the atmosphere responds to cumulative physical emissions, not ledger entries. Fair enough—but then it matters not one bit to the atmosphere whether a ton is avoided inside a company's fence-line or in a Kenyan cookstove program. What matters is whether the ton is real.
[C1.3] A legal cap on offsets does nothing to make in-value-chain reductions more permanent or offsets less permanent; it simply declares that one category of reductions is politically preferred regardless of cost-effectiveness or atmospheric impact.
[C1.4] The moral hazard narrative sounds compelling until you examine the alternative it produces. Forcing a company to chase expensive marginal abatement inside its own operations—retrofitting a facility that might close in five years, or electrifying a fleet before grid infrastructure supports it—can cost multiples more per ton than funding avoided deforestation or methane capture. That capital could achieve far greater cumulative reductions if deployed where tons are cheapest and most abundant. A rigid cap doesn't eliminate moral hazard; it creates a new one: firms will spend more to achieve less, simply to satisfy a legal ratio that has no basis in climate science.
[C1.5] More critically, unconstrained offset markets channel private capital into developing-world climate projects that no domestic regulation will fund. In-value-chain mandates keep corporate climate spending circulating among wealthy supply chains in wealthy economies. That is climate policy as protectionism dressed up as integrity.
[C1.6] The real problem is offset quality, not offset quantity. Standards are tightening; baselines are being scrutinized; permanence requirements are evolving. A legal cap sidesteps that hard work by imposing a crude ratio that punishes high-quality offsets alongside low-quality ones. The proposition's solution is to the wrong problem.
