[P1.1] Precise location data maps every step of a person’s life—home, workplace, clinic visits, protests, places of worship. [P1.2] This is not abstract privacy theory. Continuous GPS trails reveal medical conditions, religious practice, political activity, and intimate relationships with surgical precision. When sold freely, the information arms stalkers, enables discriminatory pricing, and fuels mass surveillance by private actors who operate outside constitutional limits.
[P1.3] Opt-out regimes fail because the default is exploitation and the interfaces are designed for capture, not choice. Explicit opt-in reverses the default and forces genuine notice. Time limits prevent permanent surrender of autonomy; people can reassess as contexts change.
[P1.4] The market does not self-correct. Brokers profit from opacity. Users face high monitoring costs and cannot bargain with every app and advertiser in a chain of invisible transfers. External harms—harassment, chilling of free association—fall on third parties who never consented.
[P1.5] Prohibition on non-consensual sales does not kill useful location services. Apps can still request permission for navigation or weather. First-party uses with clear consent remain viable. What ends is the parasitic secondary market that treats human movement as a free commodity.
[P1.6] The alternative is a world where physical presence becomes commercial property of whoever can scrape a signal. That is incompatible with personal security and democratic dignity. Consent is the minimum safeguard that restores agency over the map of one’s own life. Anything less leaves the individual powerless against industrial-scale tracking.

Data brokers operate across jurisdictions, resell through layers of intermediaries, and mix location signals with other datasets until extraction becomes technically opaque. A ban doesn't stop the trade; it drives it underground where no oversight exists at all. We saw this with earlier attempts to regulate peer-to-peer file sharing—prohibition didn't eliminate it, it just removed legal accountability.
[C1.3] Criminalizing location data sales hands a monopoly to the least scrupulous actors who will operate outside any government's reach.
[C1.4] Second, the proposition treats all location data sales as parasitic, ignoring that they power services people actively choose. Ride-sharing apps, food delivery, emergency dispatch, wildfire tracking, disease outbreak modeling—all depend on aggregated location intelligence that flows through broker ecosystems. Cutting that pipeline doesn't just inconvenience companies; it degrades public safety infrastructure and the everyday conveniences consumers demand. The proposition offers no carve-out for genuinely beneficial secondary uses, so it sacrifices real public goods on the altar of an absolute rule.
[C1.5] Third, the consent mechanism itself is broken. "Explicit, time-limited opt-in" sounds empowering, but in practice it produces consent fatigue. Faced with perpetual renewal prompts, people either reflexively agree to everything or disengage entirely—both outcomes defeat the purpose. The burden falls disproportionately on smaller firms and startups that can't afford sophisticated consent platforms, while entrenched players absorb the cost and consolidate market power.
[C1.6] Better paths exist: enforceable transparency requirements, data minimization standards, meaningful penalties for misuse, and sector-specific rules. The proposition's blunt instrument approach sacrifices nuance, enforceability, and public benefit for a principle that sounds clean but delivers little.
