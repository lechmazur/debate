[P1.1] Pets can damage an apartment, but so can tenants who flood bathrooms or burn countertops. We do not ban cooking or bathing; we charge deposits, enforce leases, and price risk. A blanket pet ban abandons that logic, excluding responsible owners and well-behaved animals because a minority might cause harm.
[P1.2] Default-allow with standardized damage insurance or a deposit aligns cost with the person creating it. The pet owner pays the premium or posts the bond; the landlord gets verified coverage for chewed trim, stained carpet, and extra cleaning without chasing small claims. Standardization replaces arbitrary pet rents and invented fees with one transparent requirement, checked at move-in, that cannot be waived for favorites or weaponized against others.
[P1.3] Bans also fail on their own terms. They produce secret cats behind blinds and dogs who supposedly belong to a visiting friend — unregistered, uninsured, and discovered only after damage. Bringing pets onto the lease reverses that: the animal is identified, rules on vaccination, waste, leashes, and noise are signed before it arrives, and a repeat nuisance can still trigger removal. Regulated permission gives landlords earlier knowledge and stronger enforcement than prohibition.
[P1.4] The payoff is larger than convenience. For an older renter living alone, a remote worker, or a family, a pet is often core to daily wellbeing; forcing a choice between a home and that animal pushes surrenders onto shelters and shrinks the usable housing supply. Insured pet-friendly buildings draw more applicants, fill vacancies faster, and retain tenants who will pay to avoid another pet-restricted search — stability landlords profit from.
[P1.5] Default does not mean unconditional. It means the answer starts at yes when financial risk is covered, then turns on the individual animal's actual behavior. That is fairer, more enforceable, and better insured than banning every pet for what a few might do.

[C1.1] An apartment is not a detached house with a bill attached. It's shared air, thin walls, one hallway, one elevator, one courtyard. A deposit can repaint a door. It cannot unsend a bite, unbreathe allergens from vents, or give a night-shift nurse back sleep lost to a neighbor's barking dog.
[C1.2] That is why the insurance promise fails. The proposition treats pets as purely financial risk — chewed trim, stained carpet. But the costliest pet harms in dense housing are uninsurable: chronic noise, fear in stairwells, feces in shared yards, dander that lingers for the next asthmatic tenant long after cleaning. No standardized policy pays a neighbor for dreading the elevator or for an allergic child who can't use the play area.
[C1.3] Compensation after the fact is not coexistence during.
[C1.4] Second, even on money, the coverage is theater. Many states cap deposits well below a full remediation — tear out carpet, seal subfloor for urine, replace doors, lose two weeks rent while airing out. Insurers dispute causation, exclude breeds, cap payouts, delay. Landlords, often small operators, become claims adjusters chasing receipts while the unit sits empty.
[C1.5] Ordinary tenant damage is different: everyone cooks and bathes; not everyone needs a large dog in a studio. Necessity versus choice matters.
[C1.6] Third, defaults decide buildings. Change the default to yes and pets concentrate where they were once rare. Common-area wear explodes but belongs to no one lease. A landlord seeking quiet, allergy-aware housing must now justify an exception instead of setting a clear rule.
[C1.7] Bans are blunt because shared living requires blunt, legible boundaries. Keep the ban option, and both sides can sort: pet owners to pet buildings, others to pet-free ones. Forcing every building toward yes with a check that doesn't cover what matters most destroys that sorting and calls it fairness.
