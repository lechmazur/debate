[P1.1] Toxicity is not just human nature appearing online; it is human nature curated for profit. When feeds stopped being chronological and became algorithmic, platforms began ranking posts not by when they were made but by what would keep you scrolling. And nothing keeps people scrolling like outrage.
[P1.2] Psychologically, anger holds attention longer than joy or information. A chronological timeline treats an angry insult and a birthday photo equally: newest on top, then both slide away. An engagement-ranked feed treats them oppositely: the insult that draws replies, quote-posts, and hate-watching gets boosted to thousands of strangers, while the birthday photo dies. That selection pressure matters enormously.
[P1.3] First, consider incentives for posters. Under chronology, going viral for cruelty bought you little, because your followers mostly saw your post once in time order, and non-followers rarely saw it at all. Under algorithmic defaults, cruelty scales. The troll, the demagogue, the pile-on entrepreneur learns quickly that calm nuance gets buried while savage dunks and moral condemnation get distribution.
[P1.4] Platforms trained a generation of users to be more extreme to be seen.
[P1.5] Second, consider exposure for readers. Chronology largely shows you people you chose, in context, with natural forgetting. Algorithms import strangers into your feed precisely because you will fight them. The classic toxic experience — a ten-thousand-reply dogpile, a deceptively edited clip ricocheting between hostile tribes, doomscrolling at midnight — is not chronological.
[P1.6] It requires a machine constantly asking: what will provoke this particular user into one more comment?
[P1.7] Keeping chronology as default would not have ended disagreement, but it would have removed the accelerant. Less reward for performing cruelty, less forced contact across hostile contexts, less endless resurrection of yesterday's fight. That is a straightforward recipe for less toxicity.

[C1.2] Worse, chronology has its own selection pressure for abuse. [C1.3] Coordinated raids are trivially effective in a newest-first feed, because a brigade's hundred identical threats land at the top of a target's mentions and stay visible. The single most toxic surface on any platform is the mentions inbox, where harassment actually arrives — and that has always been reverse-chronological. Ranking did not create it, and removing ranking would not defuse it.
[C1.4] The counterfactual also collapses under its own weight. Chronology cannot survive as the default at modern scale: attention is finite, posting volume is not, so users demand filtering, and platforms that refused it lost to those that offered it. More damning, chronology does not remove amplification mechanics. Retweets, screenshots, quote-posts, hashtags, trending lists, and "top replies" all existed to manufacture virality on time-ordered feeds.
[C1.5] A savage dunk spreads through those tools with or without a ranking model; chronology merely means it spreads to whoever happens to be awake.
[C1.6] So PRO needs chronology to stay default, to suppress sharing, and to cool human disinhibition. It does none of these. The toxicity is in the social architecture, not the sort order.
