[P1.1] Dating apps are not neutral mirrors. When artificial intelligence ranks your profile or decides whose photos land in front of you, it is rationing romantic opportunity itself. Who you can meet, date, and eventually build a life with is being filtered before you ever swipe.
[P1.2] That power demands basic disclosure because consent requires knowledge of material facts. You would want to know if a matchmaker deliberately hid your profile from hundreds of people, showed you only accounts likely to keep you scrolling, or scored you as less desirable without ever meeting you. The fact that software does it does not make it less consequential; it makes it more scalable and more invisible. A right to know that AI is shaping your visibility is simply the right not to be steered in your intimate life while being told you are freely choosing.
[P1.3] The need is sharper because the business model profits from dissatisfaction. Apps sell boosts, super-likes, and premium tiers that promise to make you seen. How can a user judge whether to pay when they do not even know an algorithm was suppressing them to create the need? Disclosure does not ban ranking; it prevents manufactured insecurity sold back as a feature.
[P1.4] Transparency is also the precondition for fairness. Automated systems learn from past behavior, which means they can absorb and amplify biases about age, race, body type, or neighborhood, quietly burying some groups. Without notice, a user who is rarely shown will blame themselves, never suspecting the sorting underneath and never able to question, correct, or leave.
[P1.5] And this right is modest. No one asks for source code, weights, or trade secrets — only a clear statement: this app uses AI to rank profiles and personalize who you see. That notice is cheap to give, easy to understand, and essential to autonomy. Intimacy should not be secretly optimized.
