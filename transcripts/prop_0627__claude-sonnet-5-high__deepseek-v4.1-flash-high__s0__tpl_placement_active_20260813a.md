[P1.1] A government that poisons the water its citizens drink has not merely made a policy misjudgment. It has attacked the physical precondition for every other right a person holds. You cannot exercise free speech, due process, or property rights from a hospital bed poisoned by lead, or a lung scarred by industrial particulate matter. That is the core of my case: a minimally clean and healthy environment is not one interest among many competing for legislative attention—it is infrastructural to human agency itself, and courts should treat it accordingly.
[P1.2] Consider Flint. State officials switched a water source to save money, ignored corrosion warnings, and thousands of children absorbed lead that will lower their cognitive function for life. The ordinary political process failed completely: the people harmed were poor, politically weak, and their injury was slow-moving and diffuse, exactly the profile of harm that majoritarian politics systematically underprotects. Courts exist precisely to catch cases where political majorities have no incentive to protect a vulnerable minority.
[P1.3] That is why we already recognize rights to counsel, to travel, to bodily integrity, against majority will—not because legislatures can never be trusted, but because some interests are too foundational to leave to shifting majorities and budget cycles.
[P1.4] This is not a radical constitutional innovation either. Several countries with functioning judiciaries already recognize environmental rights, treating them as judicially enforceable rather than merely aspirational, without collapsing into unworkable litigation or economic paralysis. Courts routinely handle equally complex technical questions in areas like antitrust, patent law, and toxic tort litigation. Judges do not need to become environmental engineers; they need only demand that governments meet a baseline duty—not poison your citizens, not knowingly permit uninhabitable contamination—leaving the technical means of compliance to agencies and legislatures.
[P1.5] The alternative CON must defend is a world where governments can knowingly contaminate a population's water or air, and courts must shrug and call it "not our department." That is not restraint. That is abdication.

Is the limit a lead concentration, a particulate standard, a heat threshold, a tolerable cleanup delay? Every answer redistributes money, risk, and opportunity. A judge who picks one is legislating; a judge who refuses to pick one issues an order no agency can obey consistently.
[C1.3] The Flint example [P1.2] proves the problem, not the solution. Flint had drinking-water statutes, federal oversight, tort remedies, and elected officials. The failure was enforcement and politics. A new right does not replace corroded pipes; it moves the fight into a courtroom where judges still must defer to the same agencies or micromanage them without expertise.
[C1.4] Second, environmental policy is polycentric. A clean-air order can shutter factories, raise housing costs, or divert funds from schools and hospitals. Courts resolve bounded disputes between defined parties. They cannot rationally set society’s risk budget across energy, transport, housing, and public health.
[C1.5] The claim that judges already handle antitrust and patents [P1.4] misses the point: those are statutory regimes with agencies, standards, and reviewable records. A constitutional environmental right supplies none of that. It supplies a banner.
[C1.6] Third, legitimacy. Rights like counsel or bodily integrity protect individual agency against state coercion. A healthy environment is a collective condition. Enforcing it against governments means unelected judges overriding democratic choices about taxes and land use.
[C1.7] That is not countermajoritarian rescue; it is judicial governance. And rights inflation has a cost: when every contested policy becomes constitutional, courts lose the ability to protect the rights that are truly foundational.
[C1.8] Clean water matters. The question is who decides and how. Statutes, agencies, tort suits, elections, and targeted reform can be enforced and revised. A vague super-right cannot.
[C1.9] CON’s alternative is not abdication; it is accountable law.
