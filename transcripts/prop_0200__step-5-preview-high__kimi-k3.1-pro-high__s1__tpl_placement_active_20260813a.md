[P1.1] The moon is not waiting for our treaties. While negotiators pursue a perfect global framework, commercial operators are already planning missions, capital is being raised, and technology is maturing. The question is not whether extraction will happen, but whether it will happen in daylight or in shadow. Recognizing commercial lunar extraction as legal—conditioned on transparency and debris control—converts a dangerous ambiguity into a workable regime.
[P1.2] The Outer Space Treaty already bars national appropriation by sovereignty, but it does not clearly prohibit the extraction and use of lunar materials. States are therefore guessing. This uncertainty does not preserve the moon; it invites the worst outcome. Investors who cannot secure legal title will either stay away, leaving the field to actors who ignore oversight, or pursue extraction under the radar, outside any binding safety regime.
[P1.3] A pre-treaty legal recognition, gated by strict transparency and debris-control obligations, gives responsible operators a reason to operate visibly.
[P1.4] Consider the debris problem directly. Orbital congestion around Earth already demonstrates the cost of ungoverned expansion. Requiring operators to disclose trajectories, extraction sites, and equipment—and to meet debris-control standards—makes them accountable before an accident occurs. A full treaty cannot achieve that if it arrives decades after the first shovel hits regolith.
[P1.5] Waiting for unanimity also mistakes the purpose of law. Treaties codify norms; they do not conjure them from nothing. By establishing a baseline now, states can build the customary practice and technical standards that a future treaty will eventually ratify. The alternative is a vacuum filled by unilateral claims and race-to-the-bottom practices.
[P1.6] The proposition is narrow and pragmatic: legal recognition before perfection, but only for those who submit to transparency and debris control. That is not a reckless shortcut. It is the only path that makes the next decade of lunar activity safer than the last.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes permission for title and visibility for legitimacy. Commercial sale requires more than a state licensing a mission: it requires an internationally defensible right to transfer ownership. Before a global treaty, no licensor can confer that right on behalf of humanity. Domestic recognition would not end uncertainty; it would export competing national answers into a domain where the Outer Space Treaty already rejects national appropriation.
[C1.2] PRO treats extraction as inevitable and legality as a safety device. But recognition changes what investors may claim, not merely what they must disclose. Once resources are sold, governments and companies acquire financial interests in defending sales, sites, and supply. A later treaty would face demands to grandfather operations and compensate investors.
[C1.3] The supposed bridge to agreement becomes a veto over its terms, written first by the few actors capable of reaching the Moon.
[C1.4] Transparency and debris control are valuable but radically incomplete. Publishing coordinates does not decide who may occupy an ice-rich crater, resolve overlapping claims, allocate liability, protect scientifically valuable sites, or share benefits beyond the licensing state. Nor do debris rules answer ownership. A clean, well-documented enclosure of the most accessible resources is still enclosure. “Daylight” describes what observers can see, not whether they consented.
[C1.5] The warning about shadow activity proves too little. Operators willing to evade existing obligations can evade conditions attached to recognition, while a pre-treaty regime cannot bind states that reject it. Responsible missions can already face licensing, disclosure, coordination, and debris requirements without declaring extracted resources legally saleable. The motion adds contested property claims, not the machinery needed to police them.
[C1.6] Waiting has costs, but premature recognition has irreversible ones. Investment uncertainty is a poor reason to allocate rights before the international community has settled the rules. The safer sequence is to build monitoring, liability, environmental, access, and benefit-sharing arrangements first. Law should authorize sales of shared extraterrestrial resources only after legitimacy and enforcement exist—not use those sales to decide who writes the rules.

The Outer Space Treaty bars sovereignty over the Moon, not the extraction and use of its materials. The proposition asks states to recognize a conditional activity, not to convey title on behalf of humanity.
[P2.2] Once that conflation collapses, the rest of CON’s objections lose their foundation.
[P2.3] The claim that recognition becomes a “veto over its terms” gets the timeline backwards. The dangerous fait accompli is not a licensed operator selling regolith under transparency rules; it is an unlicensed actor extracting first and then demanding retroactive legitimacy. Delay does not create a neutral baseline. It fills the field with the least transparent actors, who will present any future treaty with a much harsher version of the very grandfathering problem CON fears.
[P2.4] Conditional recognition is the only mechanism that prevents that outcome by binding operators before positions harden.
[P2.5] Nor is it persuasive to call transparency and debris control “radically incomplete.” No pre-treaty regime is complete. But those obligations generate precisely the information—sites, trajectories, equipment, operations—that later allocation, liability, and benefit-sharing rules require. CON proposes to allocate claims it cannot yet observe and to regulate actors who have no incentive to disclose. That is not caution; it is blindness dressed as principle.
[P2.6] CON’s enclosure metaphor also assumes the motion grants permanent property rights. It does not. It creates a revocable, supervised commercial license. A clean, documented operation under visibility is far easier to constrain than a hidden one; enclosure happens in the dark, not under disclosure obligations.
[P2.7] Finally, licensing alone does not suffice. If sale remains legally unrecognized, operators will structure transactions to avoid the label—selling data, services, or derivatives offshore—while evading the disclosure regime CON claims to support. Recognition conditioned on transparency and debris control is what pulls the activity into the light.
