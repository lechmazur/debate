[P1.1] A dating app that succeeds gets deleted. That one fact explains the whole conflict: revenue grows with time-on-app, while the user's goal—finding a partner—ends the customer relationship. When the product's success metric is engagement and the customer's success metric is exit, engagement-maximizing design is predictably hostile to the customer.
[P1.2] Start with the swipe. It is not a neutral interface but a variable-reward machine, built on the same intermittent-reinforcement logic as a slot pull: unpredictable matches keep people swiping past the point of intent. The consequences are familiar—choice overload, snap judgments on photos, matches treated as disposable because the next one is free, and with them ghosting, dead-end conversations, and burnout. Hours of swiping routinely yield dozens of matches and almost no dates.
[P1.3] That is engagement without matching: the metric the app optimizes, delivered; the outcome the user came for, withheld.
[P1.4] Paid boosts are worse, because visibility is positional. When one user buys a boost, everyone else sinks. When many buy, rankings return roughly to baseline—except the platform has collected rent from all of them, and the unpaid user is now invisible without ever being told the queue was auctioned. That concealed distortion corrodes the trust a matching service runs on: the belief that you are being shown to a fair pool.
[P1.5] The remedy is modest: limits, not bans. Cap daily swipes so choices carry weight. Disclose or restrict paid ranking so visibility is not secretly sold. These mirror consumer protections against engagement-maximizing design elsewhere—loot boxes, gambling mechanics—where we already accept that "the user chose it" is no defense when the design is engineered to override choice.
[P1.6] The likely objection is autonomy and market discipline. Neither holds: users cannot observe the algorithm, so they cannot punish its manipulation, and engagement metrics measure compulsion, not satisfaction. One question decides this: do these features serve the user's goal or the platform's? If the platform's—and the design says so—the motion follows.
