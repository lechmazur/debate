[P1.1] Closed files are not a filing error. They are the last working instrument of the same power that once occupied, interned, tortured, or disappeared people. When colonial tax rolls, wartime execution lists, and secret-police dossiers stay locked after any operation has ended, the state is no longer protecting the living so much as managing the dead. Families cannot bury their own.
[P1.2] Historians cannot test official myths. Affected communities cannot name what was done to them.
[P1.3] The motion already grants the only serious exception: immediate security. Once that expires, leftover secrecy is embarrassment, diplomatic convenience, and the institutional interest of those who wrote the files. None of that outweighs the right of the recorded to know they were recorded. A widow asking for an arrest warrant is not a security threat.
[P1.4] A researcher tracing a famine policy is not a spy. A community asking who signed the land seizures is not requesting a weapons cache.
[P1.5] Broad opening is not reckless dumping. It is a presumption of access for kin, historians, and harmed communities, with narrow, time-limited redaction of still-living private names—not perpetual classification of state wrongdoing. Post-dictatorship openings in Europe showed societies can absorb ugly paper without collapsing. The alternative is a two-tier past: a public story of honor, and a locked basement of the actual orders.
[P1.6] Truth delayed is policy. If the evidence of harm remains the private property of the harming state, propaganda wins by default. After the guns are silent and the networks are gone, the files belong first to those they named, then to the public that still lives with the consequences.

[C1.2] Harm is often delayed—revenge, blackmail, communal reprisal, a border fight reheated by an old dispatch.
[C1.3] Not all leftover secrecy is embarrassment. Some is. Much of it is a duty to people who never held the pen and never agreed to become an exhibit. A widow's claim on an arrest record does not license handing the next file, full of denunciations, to a political association that styles itself an affected community.
[C1.4] Families, historians, and communities are not one claimant. One wants a grave. One wants a footnote. One may want a list.
[C1.5] Broad opening erases that difference, and a file that leaves the reading room cannot be unreleased. Stripping still-living names fails: kinship, workplace, handwriting, and one distinctive raid identify the person the black bar was meant to hide.
[C1.6] These files are not neutral truth. Secret-police paper is thick with coerced statements, invented networks, and gossip promoted to evidence. Colonial files record the occupier's categories, not the occupied's lives. Open them as scripture and you do not correct the official myth; you launder a worse one.
[C1.7] Societies that flung such archives open did not simply absorb ugly paper. They also produced vigilante outings, families wrecked on a handler's word, and fresh political weapons dressed as memory.
[C1.8] Truth is not the state's private property, nor the property of whoever arrives first waving kinship or injury. The workable duty is narrower: a family may see what names its own dead, third parties stay protected, and historians work where living sources and labeled fabrications are not turned loose. That is the difference between reckoning and rearming the past.
