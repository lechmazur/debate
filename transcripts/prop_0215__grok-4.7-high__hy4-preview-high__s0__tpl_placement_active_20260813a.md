[P1.1] When an institution takes children from their families, forbids their language, and breaks their bodies in the name of salvation or civilization, apology is the start of accountability, not the end of it. Former state churches and religious orders that played central roles in residential schools, forced assimilation, or comparable abuses should pay into reparative funds because they were operators, not spectators.
[P1.2] They staffed the schools, wrote the rules, held the titles, and collected the grants. The harm was institutional: a system of removal and re-education, not a scatter of private sins. The dioceses, missionary societies, and teaching orders that did that work often survive under the same names, with the same legal personality, and with property, endowments, and insurance that outlasted the children they confined. Continuity of the institution is continuity of the obligation.
[P1.3] Survivors do not experience this as a closed chapter. They live with damaged schooling, lost kinship, untreated injury, and communities stripped of language. Those costs are financial as well as moral. States have already had to build settlement and healing funds because individual suits fail against limitation periods, missing records, and dispersed defendants.
[P1.4] It is incoherent to send the entire bill to taxpayers while the organizations that designed and ran the system keep the assets accumulated alongside it.
[P1.5] The motion is narrower than its critics will pretend. It does not tax belief, and it does not make every parishioner guilty. It applies only where a church or order played a central role, and it asks for contribution, not confiscation of worship. Payments can track degree of involvement and ability to pay.
[P1.6] What it rejects is the convenient split in which the state apologizes with money and the church apologizes with words.
[P1.7] Those who shared the power should share the repair. A reparative fund that leaves out the institutions responsible for the harm is a public subsidy for institutional denial.

These schools existed because legislatures made attendance compulsory, funded them per child, and enforced removal; churches often implemented sovereign policy as contracted operators.
[C1.3] When the author of a compulsory system later calls it criminal, it cannot demote itself to co-signer and bill its subordinate operator for repair. On PRO's logic, the state could hire a builder for an unlawful project, then charge the builder for the demolition and call it justice.
[C1.4] Now the assets argument. PRO says these institutions "keep the assets accumulated alongside it" [P1.4]. [C1.5] A parishioner who joined in 2024 is not the order that ran a school in 1954; treating her donations as a reparative piggy bank is guilt by affiliation.

[C1.7] Finally, the standards are dangerously elastic. "Central roles" and "similar historical abuses" are undefined. Let the state raid religious endowments for historical policies it now rejects, and every church becomes a target for past teachings the government dislikes. That is not repair; it is retroactive punishment of religion.
[C1.8] The just payer is the political community that passed the laws and can spread the cost through general revenue, rather than concentrate it on believers who inherited a name.
