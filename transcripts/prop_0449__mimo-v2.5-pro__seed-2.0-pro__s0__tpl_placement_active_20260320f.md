[P1.1] Coal is the single most destructive energy source on Earth. It generates a large share of global electricity while producing a disproportionate share of carbon dioxide emissions, and it kills hundreds of thousands of people annually through air pollution. The science is unambiguous: meeting Paris Agreement targets requires coal's near-complete elimination within two decades. A phaseout by 2040 is not radical ambition—it is the minimum timeline physics and public health demand.
[P1.2] The case for enforceable penalties rests on a simple observation: voluntary climate commitments fail. The Kyoto Protocol offered opt-outs. The Paris Agreement relies on nationally determined contributions with no enforcement mechanism. Glasgow's coal language was watered down at the last moment from "phase out" to "phase down."
[P1.3] Each time, diplomatic nicety prevails and emissions continue rising. Trade and finance penalties change this calculus fundamentally. When noncompliance carries real economic cost—restricted access to green finance, carbon border adjustments, exclusion from favorable trade terms—governments face a material incentive to act rather than free-ride.
[P1.4] Critics will invoke sovereignty and development needs. But this proposal does not ask developing nations to sacrifice growth. Renewable energy is now cheaper than new coal construction in most markets globally, and rapidly becoming competitive even against existing plants in many regions. The 2040 target provides a clear runway for transition, and finance mechanisms can direct capital toward clean alternatives in lower-income nations.
[P1.5] The question is not whether countries can afford to phase out coal—they cannot afford not to, given the mounting costs of climate disruption, stranded assets, and health burdens coal imposes on their own populations.
[P1.6] Enforcement through trade and finance also avoids the most common failure mode of climate agreements: good intentions without accountability. Carbon border adjustments already exist in the European Union. Conditioning development finance on coal commitments is already practiced by major multilateral lenders. This proposal simply scales proven mechanisms globally and applies them consistently.
[P1.7] The burden falls on anyone claiming that one more voluntary pledge will finally deliver results. History shows otherwise.
