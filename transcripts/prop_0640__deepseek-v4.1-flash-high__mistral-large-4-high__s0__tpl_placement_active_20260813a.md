[P1.1] Farmland is not just another asset class. It is the physical base of food production, rural livelihoods, and ecological stability. A cap on the share of agricultural land owned by non-farming institutional investors is a guardrail, not a ban: it lets pension funds or asset managers participate as minority owners, while preventing absentee financial control from swallowing the land base.
[P1.2] Ownership determines who farms, at what rent, and whether soil, water, and local food infrastructure are treated as long-term obligations or costs to squeeze. Institutional investors typically lease land to the highest bidder and judge it by portfolio returns. The farmer bears the risk, lacks security, and cannot easily plant perennials, rebuild soil, or diversify. The owner can sell, consolidate, or switch to whatever commodity pays, regardless of local food needs.
[P1.3] That is how you get fragile monocultures, speculative land inflation, and young farmers priced out.
[P1.4] Rural communities lose twice: wealth drains to distant shareholders, and decisions about land are made in boardrooms with no stake in the school, the co-op, or the watershed. A cap preserves a critical mass of independent, place-based ownership. It keeps land in the hands of people who live with the consequences.
[P1.5] The likely objection is that institutional capital brings efficiency and scale. But the motion does not ban capital; it limits control. If institutional investment is genuinely productive, it can work within a cap. If it only works through unchecked accumulation, that is the problem.
[P1.6] A cap is an anti-concentration rule for the food system, much like competition or foreign-ownership limits. The decisive question is whether farmland should be governed by portfolio logic or stewardship logic. A cap does not punish investment; it stops the food system from becoming someone else's exit strategy.

When a young farmer cannot afford to purchase, institutional capital frequently funds the leases and equipment that keep them in business. Rural communities need investment in processing, broadband, and infrastructure—capital often arriving with institutional ownership.
[C1.3] Cutting off that pool does not transfer land to local families; it depresses land values, leaving retirees with smaller nest eggs while entry remains unaffordable, and starves the town of the very investment it needs to survive.
[C1.4] The motion assumes institutional owners are inherently absentee and extractive. In practice, many lease long-term to local farmers, fund conservation, and bring professional management fragmented ownership cannot match. The harms PRO identifies—short-termism, monoculture, wealth extraction—are regulatory failures, not ownership-category failures. If the concern is speculation, impose holding periods or transaction taxes.
[C1.5] If it is tenant insecurity, strengthen lease rights and transparency. If it is consolidation, enforce antitrust. These tools target the harm directly; an ownership cap punishes productive capital alongside speculative capital.
[C1.6] Finally, the cap invites evasion. Define "non-farming institutional investor" narrowly to be enforceable, and capital routes around it through family offices or layered trusts. Define it broadly, and you ensnare the rural credit unions and cooperative investment funds that serve farming communities. [C1.7] Governments should regulate farming practices and market conduct, not ration the ownership of land.

[P2.4] If the only viable model is unchecked land control, that is precisely the concentration problem.

[P2.6] Every tax has evasion; we do not abolish tax law.
