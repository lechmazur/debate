[P1.1] Legal deposit exists because the market does not preserve everything a society needs to remember. Books and films were not deposited because they were easy to store, but because they carry language, norms, politics, and imagination. Today, software is where much of that record lives: games express narrative and design, apps shape daily routines, and digital art uses code as its medium. If we accept the logic of legal deposit, culturally significant software belongs in national archives.
[P1.2] The central problem is fragility. Software depends on layers—operating systems, hardware, servers, DRM, licenses—that disappear. A novel can sit in a climate-controlled room; a game can become unplayable when an online service shuts down, a storefront removes it, or an update breaks compatibility. We already see this in lost Flash works, delisted mobile apps, and online worlds whose private servers vanish.
[P1.3] Voluntary preservation by companies is partial and commercial: it favors what remains profitable, not what is historically important. Future historians should be able to study the software of our era as they study printed pamphlets or newsreels.
[P1.4] A mandate changes three things. First, it creates a comprehensive baseline, so archives receive the work, source materials, and metadata needed to understand it, not just whatever survives on a fan wiki. Second, it gives archives legal authority to preserve and, where necessary, emulate or migrate works, rather than leaving preservation exposed to licence terms and anti-circumvention rules.
[P1.5] Third, it spreads responsibility away from platforms that can change policy overnight.
[P1.6] This is not demanding every trivial update be archived. Like film deposit, the standard is culturally significant software selected under national rules, with scope for smaller developers and exemptions where impractical. But where a work matters, the default should be preservation, not loss. A government mandate turns software heritage from an accident into an obligation.

The alternative—no mandated deposit—leaves preservation to scattered fans and goodwill, where security is weaker and provenance is poorer.
[P2.4] Their strongest point is permission. But permission fixes only part of the problem. [P2.6] On significance, every archive makes choices.
