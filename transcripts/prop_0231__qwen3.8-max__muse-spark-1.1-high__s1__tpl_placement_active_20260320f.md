[P1.1] Supervised consumption sites are not a moral endorsement of drug use; they are an emergency response to preventable death. The decisive test is whether regulated sites save lives and reduce public disorder without increasing drug use. They meet it.
[P1.2] In European cities that have operated them for years—Switzerland, Germany, the Netherlands, Spain—these rooms provide sterile equipment, oxygen, naloxone, and trained staff. An overdose becomes a medical event that can be reversed quickly, not a hidden fatal collapse. They also create contact points: people who avoid hospitals may accept wound care, counseling, and treatment referrals when services come to them. Their experience shows that carefully sited facilities do not become magnets for crime; they make surrounding streets more predictable.
[P1.3] Latin American cities face even sharper stakes. Overdose response is often fragmented, street drug markets are violent, and public injecting is already visible. Where emergency services are stretched and stigma keeps users invisible, a regulated site is a visible point of control. It moves consumption out of doorways, parks, and school routes; reduces discarded syringes; and gives cities a clearer division of labor.
[P1.4] Health staff manage consumption, while public space is restored. This is not legalization; it is managed risk.
[P1.5] The opposition will likely say these sites encourage addiction. But permission does not create new addicts; it changes where and how existing use occurs. The realistic alternative to a supervised site is not recovery. It is a sidewalk, a delayed ambulance, or death.
[P1.6] If a city can keep a person alive long enough to accept help, while making neighborhoods cleaner and safer, it should do so. Regulated supervised consumption sites are a practical, humane tool for reducing overdose deaths and public disorder.
