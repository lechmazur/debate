[P1.1] The cheapest way to stop a shooting is to stop the argument that produces it. Civilian community violence intervention does that work directly. Hiring another officer does not.
[P1.2] Shootings cluster. A modest number of people, groups, and retaliatory chains account for a disproportionate share of the gunfire. Interrupters, hospital responders, and credible messengers are built to live inside that concentration: they learn who is feuding, reach the bedside after a wounding, and broker a pause before the next shot. Those parties will often talk to someone from the neighborhood.
[P1.3] They will often not brief the police. A newly hired officer, however capable, spends most shifts on calls that have nothing to do with the next homicide. The marginal hire is a generalist. The intervener is a specialist pointed at shootings themselves.
[P1.4] Cost follows the design. A sworn officer carries academy time, equipment, overtime, benefits, and a pension. An outreach worker does not. For the fully loaded cost of one additional officer, a city can often field two or three full-time interveners and place them on the corners and in the trauma bays where retaliations actually start.
[P1.5] Speed cuts the same way. Recruiting and training a cop takes many months. A permitted, funded interruption team can be stood up against a live spike.
[P1.6] Permitting is the bottleneck, not the idea. Liability rules, contracting barriers, and the habit of treating non-police mediators as unofficial keep these programs at pilot size. Scale them with clear standards—who is targeted, who supervises, what outcomes are tracked—and a niche becomes a production system. The choice is not community workers instead of all police.
[P1.7] It is the next dollar, scored as shootings prevented—not arrests logged or response times shaved. Spent on an officer, that dollar buys diffuse capacity and a long lag. Spent on civilian intervention, it buys more people, closer to the conflict, sooner. That is the more cost-effective way to reduce shootings.

[C1.1] A shooting is stopped when pulling the trigger looks too costly or becomes impossible. Police change that math everywhere they operate: visible patrol deters the corner confrontation, an arrest incapacitates the serial shooter, a swift clearance signals retaliation will be punished, not rewarded. A civilian mediator can only plead. Pleading is not cost-effectiveness; power is.
[C1.2] The officer is the only specialist shooters actually fear: he can stop, search where lawful, seize illegal guns, hold a crime scene, compel cooperation, and jail the crew driving a feud. An outreach worker may learn who is feuding precisely because he cannot act on it. Calling the unarmed listener the specialist and the armed enforcer the generalist gets the comparison backward. Knowledge without enforcement is just commentary near gunfire.
[C1.3] That is why scaling breaks the model. Credibility is scarce by nature. The effective messenger is rare — reformed, older, still respected on the block, willing to work nights for nonprofit wages while cooperating with City Hall without being branded an informant. You can recruit a hundred officers from academies.
[C1.4] You cannot manufacture a hundred such figures on demand. Permit, professionalize, and standardize them, and you bureaucratize away the street standing that made anyone listen. Expansion turns interrupters into 9-to-5 case managers who clock out before the shooting starts.
[C1.5] Then the cost math collapses. Yes, an outreach salary is lower. But cost-effectiveness is cost per shooting stopped, not salary per hire. Two or three unarmed workers covering a few blocks on limited shifts, with high burnout and constant supervision needs, produce fragile, fleeting pauses.
[C1.6] One additional officer, deployable across hot spots, nights, weekends, and surge operations, deters many attempts and can remove a repeat shooter for years. Cheap inputs that do not stop bullets are expensive.
