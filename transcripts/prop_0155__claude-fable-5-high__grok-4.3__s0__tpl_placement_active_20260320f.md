[P1.1] A camera that recognizes you is not a camera anymore. It is a checkpoint you never agreed to pass through. Real-time face recognition converts every public street into a continuous identity check on millions of innocent people, and that is the line this motion draws: police may use the technology as an investigative tool, with a judge's signature, but never as a live dragnet.
[P1.2] Three reasons this division is exactly right.
[P1.3] First, real-time scanning inverts the foundation of policing in a free society. Investigation is supposed to follow suspicion. Live face surveillance reverses that order—everyone is scanned first, and suspicion is generated by an algorithm. There is no practical way to particularize it, no way to limit it to wrongdoers, because its entire function is to identify everyone who walks by.
[P1.4] A warrant requirement cannot fix that; you cannot get a warrant for an entire crowd.
[P1.5] Second, the failure mode of real-time deployment is uniquely dangerous. When a live match misfires, an armed officer confronts an innocent person within minutes, before any human verification. Documented wrongful arrests from face recognition in the U.S.—disproportionately of Black men—happened even with time to deliberate. Compress that to a street stop in real time and you manufacture violent encounters from software error.
[P1.6] Post-incident use, by contrast, allows analysts to check the match, gather corroboration, and put it before a judge first.
[P1.7] Third, the chilling effect is concrete. People skip protests, union meetings, mosques, and addiction clinics when they know their faces are logged live. Several major cities have already restricted or banned live deployment for precisely this reason, while no one seriously disputes that retrospective, warrant-backed searches helped identify suspects in cases like the January 6th investigations.
[P1.8] The motion gives police the genuine investigative payoff of this technology while denying them only one thing: a permanent, automated lineup of the entire public. That trade is not a compromise. It is the whole point of constitutional policing.

[C1.4] Banning real-time use therefore trades measurable prevention capacity for a procedural safeguard that arrives too late. The distinction the motion offers is not a careful balance; it is a categorical restriction that privileges paperwork over immediate intervention without addressing why the underlying identification errors persist in either setting.
