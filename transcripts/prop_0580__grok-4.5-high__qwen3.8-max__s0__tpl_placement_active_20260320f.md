[P1.1] Expanding monthly child tax credits cuts child poverty more cost-effectively than equivalent childcare subsidies because cash reaches the broadest set of poor children and converts almost dollar-for-dollar into resources that define the poverty line.
[P1.2] Poverty is an income shortfall. A monthly credit deposits money into household budgets immediately, lifting families over the threshold whether parents work formal jobs, care for infants at home, work irregular hours, or live far from licensed centers. Childcare subsidies, by design, only help the subset already using or able to use paid care. Large shares of the poorest children—especially infants, rural kids, and those with stay-at-home or informal caregivers—receive zero benefit from those dollars.
[P1.3] Spending the same total on subsidies therefore leaves more children below the line.
[P1.4] Cash is also administratively leaner and higher-uptake. Families already interact with the tax system; monthly deposits require little new infrastructure and impose almost no matching, copay, or eligibility maze once the credit is expanded. Subsidies demand provider networks, quality rules, slot scarcity, and reapplications. Every friction dollar spent chasing compliance is a dollar that never reaches a child’s household.
[P1.5] Supply constraints compound the problem: new subsidy money often bids up prices or queues rather than instantly expanding usable slots, so the poverty reduction per public dollar shrinks.
[P1.6] The comparison is decisive on margins. Give a low-income parent $300 a month and she can buy food, rent, diapers, or—if she chooses—childcare. Restrict the same $300 to a voucher and many cannot redeem it at all; those who can still face residual costs and limited hours. Equivalent fiscal outlays therefore move far fewer children out of poverty under the subsidy route.
[P1.7] Liquidity and choice matter: predictable monthly cash smooths shocks that push families deeper into poverty, while locked subsidies cannot.
[P1.8] Targeted cash does not pretend to solve every developmental need. It simply does the assigned job—reducing measured child poverty—more efficiently, more universally, and at lower waste than an equivalent sum confined to one service many poor families neither use nor can access. That is the decisive cost-effectiveness edge.

[C1.1] PRO treats child poverty as a temporary accounting gap, so it chooses the cheapest way to add dollars to a household. But durable cost-effective poverty reduction is changing a family’s capacity to generate income and a child’s trajectory. Expanded childcare subsidies do that; monthly cash largely papers over the gap while the payments last.
[C1.2] The binding constraint for many poor families is not simply missing cash; it is missing reliable care. A parent cannot keep a job, take a night shift, attend training, or recover from a health disruption without safe care. Subsidies, especially when the spending is equivalent and used to expand slots, home-based options, rural providers, and extended-hour care, buy a service that unlocks earnings. One public dollar can produce a parent’s paycheck, lower the household’s care costs, and keep a child in a stable learning environment.
[C1.3] That is a multiplier cash cannot match. A monthly deposit may pay rent or food, but it does not create a slot, train a caregiver, or make work possible.
[C1.4] PRO’s reach argument assumes subsidies remain rigid and scarce. But the motion compares expanded childcare subsidies, not the status quo. With equivalent dollars, policymakers can fund supply grants, provider rate increases, contracts with home-based caregivers, transportation, and sliding-scale assistance. That reaches infants and rural families better than PRO admits, while preserving the earnings link.
[C1.5] Cash also has a cost-effectiveness problem: it must be paid forever to keep the poverty line crossed. When payments stop, income falls back. Childcare spending can produce lasting effects: higher employment and wages, better early learning, fewer disruptions. If the state spends once to create access, families can convert it into recurring private income.
[C1.6] Finally, poverty is not only low income; it is instability. Subsidies stabilize work schedules and child routines, reducing future public costs. Judge this motion on durable escapes from poverty, not one-year transfer accounting, and childcare subsidies are the more cost-effective tool.
