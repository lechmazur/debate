[P1.1] Speech is caught; reading is taught. Children learn to talk by being around talkers, but no child has ever learned the alphabetic code by osmosis. Our writing system is an invention, and inventions must be explicitly handed over. That single fact decides this debate.
[P1.2] If reading were natural, autonomy would be harmless, because any reasonable classroom would get there. It isn't natural, so the question becomes: do we guarantee every child receives the explicit, sequenced instruction the evidence supports, or do we leave that guarantee to the luck of which teacher a six-year-old is assigned?
[P1.3] Autonomy sounds like trust in professionals. In practice, for a generation, it meant that tens of thousands of classrooms taught children to guess words from pictures and context, cue systems that collapse the moment texts get harder. Teachers weren't lazy; they were trained in a method that felt humane and didn't work. Autonomy did not protect children from that error.
[P1.4] It protected the error from correction.
[P1.5] Compare what happened when policy chose structure. Mississippi, long near the bottom of national rankings, mandated systematic phonics, trained teachers in it, and moved roughly to the national average on fourth-grade reading within a decade. England's phonics screening check pulled early decoding rates up substantially after introduction. These weren't pilot studies; they were whole systems choosing a floor over a lottery.
[P1.6] And the lottery is not evenly weighted. A child with books at home and parents who sound out words survives weak instruction. The child without that cushion does not. Classroom autonomy quietly converts family resources into reading outcomes.
[P1.7] Structure is the floor, not the ceiling. Teachers keep their judgment over pacing, texts, and the hundred daily decisions that make a classroom human. What policy must not leave to chance is whether the code gets taught at all.

[C1.2] First, the historical lesson cuts the other way. The cueing disaster was not a product of teacher autonomy; it was a product of top-down orthodoxy — a single theory taught in universities and installed through the same kinds of curriculum mandates PRO now recommends. [C1.3] When the mandated method is wrong, every classroom is wrong, and the error survives because fidelity has been made the standard of professional practice.
[C1.4] Second, "prioritize structured reading instruction" is not a neutral floor. It is a budget, a schedule, and a fidelity metric. Minutes accrue to the sequenced program, and everything that makes reading worth doing — read-alouds that build knowledge, writing, discussion, the books that make a six-year-old want to read tomorrow — becomes the residual. Decoding is the mechanism of reading, not its purpose.
[C1.5] A policy that ranks the mechanism above teachers' judgment about the whole enterprise will produce children who can sound out words with nothing worth reading.
[C1.6] Third, structure does not travel well. A sequence that works for one population is not automatically right for a child entering with a different first language, a speech delay, or a year of prior failure. Teachers exist to see the specific child. Curriculum mandates convert that judgment into deviation and document it as infidelity.
[C1.7] None of this denies the alphabetic principle. It denies that the cure for a training failure is to strip teachers of the authority to design.
