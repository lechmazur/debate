[P1.1] A country is not merely a labor market with a flag; it is a shared system for making decisions, protecting rights, and resolving conflict. That system works only when residents can communicate across communities and accept basic civic commitments: equal legal standing, lawful dispute resolution, and freedom from coercion. Expecting newcomers to learn those commitments and the common public language is therefore not cultural snobbery. It is the minimum architecture of equal citizenship.
[P1.2] Language is the decisive practical issue. Parents unable to read school notices, tenants unable to understand leases, and workers unable to report unsafe conditions are formally included but practically dependent. Without a shared language, they must route everyday life through relatives, employers, or community brokers—some helpful, some exploitative. Children interpret; officials struggle to obtain consent; neighbors remain strangers.
[P1.3] Calling integration “optional” does not preserve freedom here. It leaves the least powerful to bear the cost of separation.
[P1.4] The expectation also protects civic equality. A liberal society cannot shrug when intimidation replaces lawful argument, when girls are denied equal schooling, or when religious or ethnic authority is enforced through threats. Private traditions deserve wide room; public coercion does not become acceptable merely because it is traditional. Clear common norms tell every resident, old or new, where cultural freedom ends and another person’s rights begin.
[P1.5] This is an expectation of public participation, not ancestral erasure. Keep your religion, festivals, accent, and home language; use translation during a reasonable transition; receive funded classes and civics instruction. But do not confuse support with indifference. A government that offers language help while refusing to say learning matters sends newcomers toward isolation, and a society that calls shared norms optional abandons the vulnerable first.
[P1.6] Integration should be enabled and expected, with dignity for every heritage—but basic civic duties are not optional.
