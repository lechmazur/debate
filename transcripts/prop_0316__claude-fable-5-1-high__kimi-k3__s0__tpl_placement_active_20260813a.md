[P1.1] A dating app is not a neutral bulletin board. When it decides that your face appears to two hundred people and your neighbor's face appears to twenty, it has made a consequential decision about your life, using your data, in your name, without telling you. The motion asks only this: that you be told the decision is being made. Not how the model is weighted.
[P1.2] Not the source code. Simply that a machine is ranking you and curating who you see.
[P1.3] Start with what users actually believe. Most people think a dating app is a shuffled deck. Swipe enough and everyone eventually passes by. That belief is false on essentially every major platform.
[P1.4] Apps have long used desirability-style scoring, engagement prediction, and models that quietly push some profiles to the top and bury others. A person who goes three months without a match draws a conclusion about themselves. If the real explanation is that a model decided their photos underperform and stopped showing them, that person is entitled to know the difference between "nobody wants me" and "the software shelved me." That is not a technical nicety.
[P1.5] It is the difference between a private humiliation and an accurate understanding of one's own situation.
[P1.6] Second, disclosure is the precondition for every other protection. You cannot consent to what you don't know exists. You cannot choose a competitor with better practices if no one is required to say what their practices are. You cannot even complain.
[P1.7] Markets discipline companies only when customers can see what they are buying; secrecy here is not a neutral default, it is a competitive shield.
[P1.8] Third, the objections collapse on inspection. Trade secrets? Knowing that AI ranks profiles reveals nothing proprietary, any more than a nutrition label reveals a recipe. "Everyone already knows"?
[P1.9] Then a plain-language notice costs the company nothing and the objection is an admission that disclosure is harmless. Gaming the system? You cannot game a fact of existence.
[P1.10] A company making intimate decisions about you owes you, at minimum, an honest sentence saying so.
