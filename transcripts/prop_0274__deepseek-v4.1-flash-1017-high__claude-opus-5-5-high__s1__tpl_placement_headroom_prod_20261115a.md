[P1.1] Every destination has a carrying capacity, and influencer promotion is a machine for exceeding it. A single viral post can turn a quiet trailhead, swimming hole, or residential lane into a weekend traffic jam before any public agency has planned parking, toilets, rescue access, or waste removal. The proposition does not ban tourism or silence critics; it says that when viral traffic is demonstrably damaging resident life and fragile sites, local communities should limit the promotional behavior that keeps feeding the surge.
[P1.2] It is a land-use and safety decision, not a verdict on the speaker’s opinion. That is the right threshold, and it should decide this debate.
[P1.3] The central question is not whether tourism can bring money. It can. The question is who controls the terms when the money arrives with damage attached. The benefit is captured by platforms and a few businesses; the harm is socialized.
[P1.4] Geotagging is not neutral information; it is an attention subsidy. It tells millions of strangers that a fragile place is a must-see, while the costs—eroded trails, trampled vegetation, blocked ambulances, trash, noise, strained water and housing—land on residents and ecosystems. A restaurant can choose to advertise; a public cliff path cannot choose to absorb ten thousand extra visitors.
[P1.5] Limits can be narrowly tailored: remove precise geotags for sensitive sites, pause paid or incentivized promotion during peak damage, require permits for commercial shoots, and publicize alternatives. That preserves general travel information while targeting the amplification that causes the harm. Opponents will say promotion is just speech and tourism is economic development.
[P1.6] But a town can welcome visitors and still refuse to let a viral trend dictate its carrying capacity. If a site is being loved to death, the responsible response is not to keep advertising the funeral.

[C1.5] A town with a jammed residential lane can restrict parking, run a shuttle, cap permits, or close a site to recover. [C1.6] Third, these limits empower the wrong people. "Fragile" and "damaging resident life" are elastic. [C1.7] So the choice is stark: manage crowds where they physically occur, fairly and enforceably, or chase information that cannot be caught while legitimizing control over who may speak about shared places.
