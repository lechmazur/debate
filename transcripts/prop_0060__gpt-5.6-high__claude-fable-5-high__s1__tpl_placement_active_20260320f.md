[C1.2] Start with the measurement trap. Remote workers consistently report feeling more productive—but self-reports track comfort, not output. When researchers have measured objective output rather than surveys, the picture darkens: studies of IT professionals during the pandemic found hours worked went up while output stayed flat or fell, meaning productivity per hour declined. People at home spend more time in meetings and messages just to coordinate what a hallway conversation once settled in thirty seconds.
[C1.3] Longer days masking lower efficiency is not a productivity gain; it is a productivity loss wearing a disguise.
[C1.4] Then there are the compounding costs that never show up in a quarterly dashboard. Junior employees learn by overhearing, by being corrected in real time, by watching seniors handle ambiguity. Remote onboarding is measurably slower, mentoring thins out, and institutional knowledge stops transferring. A firm can coast on pre-existing relationships for a year or two—which is precisely why early remote results looked rosy—but the capital depletes.
[C1.5] New ideas suffer too: serendipitous cross-team contact, the raw material of innovation, cannot be scheduled into a video call.
[C1.6] Finally, the idealized home office is a fiction for most. It assumes a quiet dedicated room, no children, no roommates, no laundry, no television. Many knowledge workers have none of that. Meanwhile the same notifications follow them home, minus the social accountability that keeps focus honest.
[C1.7] The comparison, honestly drawn, is a full workplace system against a fragmented one. For many knowledge workers, on net, dismantling the office dismantles the productivity it quietly generated.
