[P1.1] When terminal disease or degenerative decay strips a person of their physical agency and subjects them to unbearable, irremediable suffering, the law must not force them to endure a prolonged, agonizing death. Allowing competent adults to access medical assistance in dying is a necessary extension of bodily autonomy, human dignity, and basic compassion.
[P1.2] First, bodily autonomy is a foundational principle of modern liberty. Competent adults are trusted to make profound life choices—from refusing life-saving treatment to consenting to risky medical procedures. Denying a competent person the right to choose a peaceful, controlled end to their life, while forcing them to endure protracted physical degradation, is an unjustifiable violation of self-determination.
[P1.3] Second, we must confront the limits of medicine. While palliative care is essential, it cannot always alleviate the worst suffering. For some, pain becomes intractable; for others, degenerative diseases like ALS progressively trap a fully conscious mind inside a failing, suffocating body. Forcing a person to endure this against their explicit will does not preserve life; it merely prolongs the process of dying.
[P1.4] Third, a compassionate society can safely regulate this choice. By implementing strict safeguards—such as requiring multiple independent medical assessments, verifying mental competence, ensuring voluntariness, and mandating a reflection period—we can protect vulnerable populations while honoring the rights of those suffering intolerably. Evidence from regulated jurisdictions shows that strict frameworks successfully prevent abuse while providing immense comfort to those facing their final days.
[P1.5] Ultimately, this debate is about who owns a person's life and death: the individual, or the state. A compassionate, just legal system must choose the individual, offering them mercy, dignity, and control.
