[P1.1] The core purpose of a financial exchange is to provide a fair, transparent, and orderly venue for capital formation. The commercialization of ultra-low-latency co-location directly subverts this purpose. By selling the physical right to place private servers feet away from matching engines, exchanges have engineered a permanent, two-tiered marketplace.
[P1.2] This is not a reward for superior analysis or technological innovation; it is a paid structural privilege. Co-located firms buy a microsecond informational advantage that allows them to observe incoming order flow and adjust their own positions before those same market signals can physically reach the rest of the investing public. This structural latency gap enables a predatory practice known as latency arbitrage. Co-located intermediaries intercept institutional and retail orders, front-running price changes and extracting a risk-free tax on ordinary market participants.
[P1.3] The defense of co-location typically rests on the myth of liquidity provision. But the liquidity generated by co-located high-frequency traders is highly fleeting and toxic. Because these firms operate on microsecond horizons, they do not commit capital to the market in times of stress. Instead, when volatility spikes, their algorithms instantly withdraw, leaving the market hollowed out.
[P1.4] Furthermore, because long-term investors know they are constantly exposed to adverse selection by faster, co-located predatory algorithms, they are forced to widen their spreads to price in this "sniping" tax. Co-location actually increases the true cost of trading for the real economy.
[P1.5] Financial markets must not operate on a "pay-to-win" architecture where the exchange's primary revenue model is selling a speed advantage over its own public users. Prohibiting the sale of co-location is the necessary first step to restoring a level playing field, reducing systemic fragility, and ensuring that exchanges serve their foundational role as fair allocators of capital rather than facilitators of speed-based rent-seeking.

[C1.1] Co-location does not create an unfair two-tier market; it compresses the natural latency differences that would otherwise exist between participants scattered across cities and continents. By placing servers in the same facility, exchanges let any firm that values speed pay to reduce the gap rather than letting geography alone decide who arrives first. The alternative leaves slower participants exposed to exactly the same information lag, only without a transparent price mechanism to equalize access.
[C1.2] The liquidity that results is measurable in narrower spreads and greater depth for the orders that actually matter to long-term investors. When a pension fund or mutual manager executes a block, co-located market makers can adjust inventories in microseconds rather than milliseconds, absorbing the trade without a large price concession. Removing that capability forces wider quotes or forces the same investor to split orders across multiple venues, raising total transaction costs.
[C1.3] Prohibiting the service also ignores substitution. Firms denied exchange co-location simply rent space in nearby buildings or use microwave links, recreating the same speed hierarchy at higher cost and greater operational risk. The exchange loses revenue that could otherwise fund system upgrades while market participants lose the standardized, monitored environment that comes with official co-location.
[C1.4] Finally, the claim that co-located traders merely extract rent overlooks their role in continuous price discovery. Without rapid arbitrage across related instruments, stale quotes persist longer, increasing the chance that retail and institutional orders clear at outdated prices. Banning paid co-location therefore trades an observable, competed-for advantage for a diffuse, harder-to-measure degradation in market quality.
