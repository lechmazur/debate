[P1.1] Climate asylum, properly limited, is not open borders. It is protection for someone whose country can no longer offer a safe place to return. Existing asylum law centers on persecution by human actors for protected reasons. The sea does not persecute; heat has no intention; yet both can make home uninhabitable.
[P1.2] A family whose island water is fouled by saltwater, whose housing is repeatedly washed out, and whose state has no higher ground has a claim rooted in danger, not preference. If law cannot see that, it calls survival “economic migration” and pushes it underground.
[P1.3] That right can be narrow: causation by sea-level rise or extreme heat; severe, lasting harm; no safe internal relocation or return. Its remedy is protection with due process, status, work permission, and non-return while conditions persist—not automatic citizenship or a waiver of security screening. States may use registration, review, regional agreements, and burden-sharing. “Limited” means criteria; discretionary charity can vanish after one election.
[P1.4] That floor serves states too. Unrecognized displacement does not stay put: it breeds irregular crossings, smuggling, and emergency politics. A defined channel lets governments identify arrivals, screen them, plan services, and share responsibility before a crisis peak. The floodgates objection has it backwards: refusing to draw a line makes every case an exception or a test of compassion.
[P1.5] Aid and adaptation cannot answer the hard cases. Seawalls can protect a district, not restore territory already lost. Warnings and cooling centers reduce heat risk, but cannot make every outdoor livelihood safe when ordinary daytime conditions become dangerous. Temporary evacuation helps during one event; it is not a life plan when that event becomes the baseline.
[P1.6] Recognition also allocates responsibility fairly: high-emitting, high-capacity countries should not externalize the human cost onto people least responsible. To oppose even this limited right is to defend return to unlivable conditions or permanent legal limbo. Thresholds, process, and proportionality close that legal gap.

[C1.1] The motion fails at its foundation: asylum is a legal response to persecution, not to habitat loss. It protects a person targeted by a state or identifiable non-state actor. Sea-level rise and extreme heat are diffuse, collective harms with no persecutor and no motive. Converting them into asylum grounds does not expand protection; it dissolves the category until it no longer tells states whom they must admit.
[C1.2] Nor do PRO’s limits solve the problem. Proving that one family’s displacement was caused specifically by sea-level rise or extreme heat is often impossible because salinity, storms, poverty, governance, and global emissions overlap. Deciding whether “safe internal relocation” exists requires courts to judge whether another valley, city, or island is viable. Non-return “while conditions persist” can mean indefinite status based on forecasts.
[C1.3] These are not narrow criteria; they are open-ended climate-risk adjudication.
[C1.4] Scale makes the risk sharper. Coastal exposure and dangerous heat affect very large populations. Once a legal right exists, advocates, governments, and courts will test every boundary, and “limited” will not hold. That threatens domestic consent for protection and invites a backlash against the refugees already protected by asylum law.
[C1.5] The better response is not indifference but correct institutional design: humanitarian visas, temporary labor mobility, planned relocation, adaptation finance, evacuation, and existing complementary protection where climate harm overlaps with persecution or truly prevents return. Those tools can identify arrivals, share burdens, and plan services without pretending every threatened person is an asylum seeker.
[C1.6] PRO’s claim that refusal creates chaos is not an argument for climate asylum. It is an argument for migration governance. A rights category that cannot be administered fairly will not tame chaos; it will institutionalize it.

[P2.1] The issue is not fitting an old persecutor template; it is whether return is unsafe. The claim that “asylum is a legal response to persecution” protects a category while abandoning its function: not sending people back to serious harm when their own state cannot secure a livable place. Non-return law can respond to indiscriminate danger, not only guilty minds. Salinized wells are not harmless because the sea lacks intention.
[P2.2] Mixed causation does not make this “open-ended climate-risk adjudication.” Mixed facts are ordinary in asylum: violence plus poverty, drought plus bad governance, fear plus collapsing services. Courts need not prove climate was the only cause; the threshold is whether return now exposes this person to severe, lasting harm from sea-level rise or extreme heat, with no practically safe internal alternative. A low-island family with no higher ground and saltwater wells differs from someone seeking better wages after one flood. Registration, evidence standards, review, and screening can draw that line; refusal only hides it.
[P2.3] Scale cuts the other way. If exposure may be large, discretionary tools are weaker, not stronger. A temporary visa can expire while the island remains uninhabitable; labor mobility can vanish with a job; adaptation money cannot recreate lost territory; complementary protection keyed to persecution misses the person whose harm has no persecutor. Clear thresholds and burden-sharing preserve consent better than ad hoc exceptions.
[P2.4] Those policies are useful, but permissions a state may withdraw. A limited right is the floor: no return while the danger persists.
[P2.5] The weighing is asymmetric. A false grant, under status, work permission, screening, and review, is an administrative burden that can be planned. A false denial sends someone back to unusable water or dangerous ordinary heat, or leaves them in legal limbo. “Migration governance” without a right is discretionary charity. Recognition turns emergency into a rule: if home cannot safely be returned to, law must see that fact.
