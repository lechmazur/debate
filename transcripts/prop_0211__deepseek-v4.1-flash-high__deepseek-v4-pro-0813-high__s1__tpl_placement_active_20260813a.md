[P1.1] Broad reimbursement of preventive obesity drugs is not a question of whether these medicines work. It is whether health systems can afford to offer them at scale without sacrificing everything else. The proposition answers that with a simple condition: if manufacturers want broad payer coverage, they must accept outcome-based pricing or a spending cap.
[P1.2] The mechanism matters. A spending cap converts an open-ended entitlement into a predictable budget line. Outcome-based pricing ties payment to results payers actually care about: sustained weight loss, fewer diabetes diagnoses, fewer cardiovascular events, better function. If a drug delivers, manufacturers earn.
[P1.3] If real-world adherence collapses or benefits fade after discontinuation, payers are not left paying premium prices for a promise.
[P1.4] Without that condition, broad reimbursement becomes a blank check. Payers face a fixed budget. Every dollar spent on a high-cost obesity drug is a dollar not spent on prenatal care, vaccination, mental health, or obesity prevention itself. A cap prevents the most expensive therapy from crowding out the rest of the system.
[P1.5] Outcome contracts also force manufacturers to generate real-world data, which is exactly what is missing when trials enroll motivated patients and clinics deliver ideal adherence.
[P1.6] Opponents will call this a barrier. It is the opposite: conditionality is what makes broad coverage politically and fiscally durable. If payers cannot control cost, they will ration by prior authorization, high copays, or narrow eligibility—the cruelest rationing. A negotiated cap with outcome terms expands access predictably.
[P1.7] Manufacturers already use rebates and confidential discounts; outcome-based contracts are a more honest version.
[P1.8] The choice is not drugs versus no drugs. It is disciplined broad coverage versus fragile coverage that collapses under its own cost. Require risk-sharing; then broaden access.

[C1.1] The condition is not a guardrail; it is a gate that punishes patients when payers and manufacturers fail to sign a deal. Broad reimbursement should rest on safety, effectiveness, and negotiated price, like any other chronic disease drug. It should not depend on a manufacturer agreeing to absorb the payer’s budget risk. If the manufacturer refuses, the “only when” produces no broad coverage.
[C1.2] Delay and denial become the treatment.
[C1.3] First, outcome-based pricing is a poor fit for prevention. Weight loss can be measured, but the outcomes that matter—diabetes, cardiovascular events, disability—are years away and shaped by diet, smoking, income, genetics, and other medicines. Payers cannot cleanly isolate what the anti-obesity drug contributed. The result is endless argument over risk adjustment and adherence, snagging coverage in actuarial disputes while patients remain untreated.
[C1.4] That is not disciplined access; it is access deferred.
[C1.5] Second, spending caps become rationing by calendar. Once a cap is reached, a health system can exclude otherwise high-need patients until the next budget cycle. That does not control costs; it refuses care. No one stops filling statins or antihypertensives because a quarterly category was exhausted.
[C1.6] Imposing capping on obesity drugs largely because they are expensive creates a separate, inferior climate for patients who need coordinated care and do not benefit from coverage interruptions.
[C1.7] Third, the condition is arbitrary. Health systems do not withhold statins, metformin, insulin, or antidepressants unless the manufacturer accepts an outcome agreement or spending cap. Those are reimbursed because they treat disease. When the evidence shows a drug treats obesity meaningfully, it should enter through the same general formulary and pricing logic.
[C1.8] Requiring organized risk-sharing for this diagnosis alone amounts to raising the bar.
[C1.9] Cost causes can be addressed through normal price negotiation, rebates, generic competition, and tiered formularies. Constraining broad reimbursement to a special pricing signal simply in the access it claims.
