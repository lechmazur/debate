[P1.1] A daughter cuts her shifts to bathe, feed, medicate, and lift a parent. A spouse manages a ventilator, a wound, a wheelchair, and a dozen appointments. That labor is real, skilled, exhausting—and almost everywhere it is treated as an infinite family resource. The proposition simply refuses that fiction: if governments rely on family care instead of nursing homes or paid aides, they should pay family caregivers more generously.
[P1.2] The case rests on three pillars.
[P1.3] First, unpaid care is not free; its costs are shifted onto caregivers. They lose wages, promotions, retirement savings, and health. The burden falls disproportionately on women and on lower-income families, who can least absorb it. Calling that love does not make it fair.
[P1.4] A care system that requires one relative to become poor so another can be cared for is a hidden tax on devotion.
[P1.5] Second, payment improves the care itself. More generous compensation makes it possible for caregivers to reduce work hours, hire respite help, buy equipment, or simply avoid choosing between a job and a relative. It reduces burnout, which is a major driver of neglect and avoidable institutionalization. Stable, supported family care is often what older and disabled people themselves prefer.
[P1.6] Governments should fund that preference, not exploit it.
[P1.7] Third, it is fiscally rational. Institutional care and emergency hospitalization are enormously expensive. If modest payment keeps even a fraction of caregivers in place, it can avert costs elsewhere. More importantly, it matches the principle that public systems should not demand unpaid labor while calling it family responsibility.
[P1.8] Opponents may warn about cost, fraud, or family obligation. Those are design questions: eligibility, assessment, audits, caps. They are not arguments for preserving a status quo that already pays—just privately, invisibly, and unequally. A society that assumes unpaid care will always absorb the burden is not saving money; it is borrowing from caregivers’ futures.
[P1.9] Pay them more generously, and the care system becomes more honest, more stable, and more humane.

[C1.1] The motion offers a false choice: pay family members a wage, or exploit them forever. There is a third option the PRO case never confronts—build the professional home-care, respite, and day-service capacity that actually removes the burden from families instead of pricing it.
[C1.2] My first argument is that cash payment entrenches exactly the injustice PRO describes. When a daughter cuts her shifts and the state sends her a modest care wage, she is now formally, durably the care system. She loses career progression, pension accrual, and labor-market attachment—and the government has bought her compliance cheaply instead of hiring a qualified aide. The state becomes the buyer of her labor at a carer's wage rather than an employer offering terms, training, and promotion.
[C1.3] "More generous" cash does not fix this; it makes the trap more comfortable and more permanent. Services in kind do fix it: if a paid worker covers mornings, the daughter keeps her job.
[C1.4] Second, money paid to family caregivers is money not spent on the care workforce. Every care system faces scarcity of aides, respite beds, and home-care hours. Diverting budget toward stipends for relatives deepens that scarcity and lets governments avoid the harder, unglamorous work of recruiting, training, and retaining professional carers. The result is a two-tier system: families who can cope get a small payment and burn out; those who cannot end up in institutions anyway.
[C1.5] Third, cash transfers to kin create ugly incentives at home. Where payment follows the dependent person, families quarrel over who is the "designated carer." Where payment follows the carer, frail older people can be steered away from residential care they might prefer, or pressured to hand over their entitlement. Design tweaks blunt this; none eliminates it.
[C1.6] The honest policy is generous respite, professional home help, and income protection tied to reduced hours—not a wage for love.
