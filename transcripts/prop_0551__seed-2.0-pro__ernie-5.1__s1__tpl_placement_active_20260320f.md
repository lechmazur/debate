[C1.1] Treating internet as a legally enforceable universal service copies a model that works for water and electricity but fails for a fundamentally different technology. Water and electricity are static, physically delivered commodities with natural‑monopoly characteristics; once the pipe or line is laid, the marginal cost of serving an extra household is low and quality can be defined simply by pressure or voltage. Internet is a rapidly evolving, software‑driven service where “high‑quality” means something different every few years—speed, latency, data caps, platform access—all of which shift with technology.
[C1.2] Imposing a universal‑service obligation freezes that definition in law. Providers must meet a mandated baseline, but the baseline will inevitably lag behind innovation. The result is a floor that drags the whole market down: companies invest in meeting the legal minimum rather than pushing the frontier, and consumers are locked into outdated speeds while the rest of the world moves on.
[C1.3] The cost argument is not abstract. The U.S. Universal Service Fund, created for telephone service, has spent billions yet broadband gaps persist, especially in rural areas. Australia’s National Broadband Network, a government‑mandated rollout, ballooned from an estimated $30 billion to over $70 billion, delivering speeds that still lag behind private alternatives in many regions.
[C1.4] When governments try to guarantee service, they end up subsidising expensive, inflexible infrastructure rather than fostering competition that drives prices down.
[C1.5] Legal enforceability also creates a bureaucracy of compliance and litigation. Providers will spend resources on meeting regulatory check‑boxes instead of improving networks, and citizens will face endless disputes over what “high‑quality” actually means. Targeted subsidies, competition‑friendly policies, and digital‑literacy programs address the digital divide far more efficiently without the rigidity and cost of a universal‑service mandate.
[C1.6] In short, the water‑and‑electricity analogy collapses under the weight of internet’s dynamism. A legally enforceable universal service would entrench outdated standards, inflate costs, stifle innovation, and fail to deliver the connectivity it promises. The better path is market‑driven solutions, not a one‑size‑fits‑all legal guarantee.

[P2.1] The entire opposition case rests on one false assumption: that universal service rules mean freezing a single permanent standard in law. That has never been how this model worked for water, electricity, or telephone service. Baselines are updated regularly as technology advances, just as they were when electricity moved from powering lightbulbs to refrigerators to life support medical equipment. Nobody ever argued we should abandon universal power because the definition of adequate service changed.
[P2.2] The failure cases they cite are not arguments against our position—they are arguments for it. The US fund they mention never granted users an enforceable right to service. It handed billions to private providers with no accountability for actual delivery, exactly the broken market status quo we are seeking to replace. Australia's broadband failure was a badly designed government construction contract, not the legal universal service obligation itself.
[P2.3] They warn of stifling innovation. What actually kills innovation is leaving a quarter of the population entirely offline. There is no incentive to build better public services, new small business tools, or accessible technology for people who cannot even connect at all. The frontier only moves forward when the floor exists for everyone.
[P2.4] Most importantly, they never weighed the tradeoff. They are worried that mandated standards might lag a year or two behind cutting edge speeds. Right now, people are missing cancer telehealth appointments, losing housing assistance, and being locked out of work because they have no connection. That is not an acceptable trade.
[P2.5] We did not build universal water because it was the most agile, innovative market solution. We built it because the cost of exclusion was too high to tolerate.

Australia’s National Broadband Network, a government‑mandated rollout, ballooned from $30 bn to over $70 bn and still lags private alternatives.
[C2.3] Those cost overruns are the direct result of a legal universal‑service obligation, not a design flaw.
[C2.4] Your innovation argument flips the causal chain. When a floor is legally required, firms invest just enough to clear the bar and then stop, because any extra spend yields no competitive advantage. [C2.5] You say the trade‑off is unacceptable: a year‑long lag versus missed telehealth appointments. [C2.6] In short, the water‑and‑electricity analogy collapses under the speed of internet change, the cost evidence you cite actually supports our case, and the innovation penalty you ignore is real.
