[P1.1] Fiscal discipline fails for a political reason, not an intellectual one. Legislators gain votes by delivering benefits now and sending the bill forward. Costs are diffuse, horizons are short, and logrolling makes spending easier to add than to cut. Left to discretion, budgets drift into structural deficit even in expansions, when restraint is cheapest.
[P1.2] A constitutional balanced-budget rule with an automatic-stabilizer exception attacks that failure. It requires structural balance: over the cycle, ongoing spending must be matched by ongoing revenue. It does not demand a crude annual zero. When unemployment rises, revenues fall and transfers rise without a new vote.
[P1.3] Those movements are the stabilizers recessions need. The rule forbids the permanent gap politicians open in good times and later call stimulus.
[P1.4] That distinction answers the stock objection. A rigid cash-balance rule can force cuts in a downturn and deepen the slump. This proposal does not. It keeps countercyclical insurance and closes the loophole by which every pet project becomes an emergency.
[P1.5] Discretion does not protect stabilization; it eats the fiscal space stabilization needs. A government that runs deficits through the boom arrives at the bust already indebted, paying more interest, and less able to respond.
[P1.6] Constitutional form matters because statutory ceilings collapse at the first inconvenience. Creative accounting, off-budget funds, and temporary suspensions let discretion in through the side door. A higher amendment threshold does not make evasion impossible. It makes evasion costly, visible, and slower than an election-year vote.
[P1.7] Constitutions exist to bind today's majority against people who cannot yet vote.
[P1.8] The choice is not rules versus wisdom. It is a constrained default against an unconstrained one that has already produced chronic debt outside wars and pandemics. Lock in structural balance and let stabilizers move. Discretion can always plead an exception.
[P1.9] It almost never restores the surplus.

[C1.2] A finance ministry that wants a permanent program announces that the output gap is worse than thought. Its own forecast makes the deficit cyclical. Who overrules it? A court cannot adjudicate potential GDP.
[C1.3] Either the rule is self-judged, and discretion returns with better paperwork, or we hand macroeconomic forecasting to unelected referees and pretend that's law.
[C1.4] Second, the rule fails precisely where PRO says discretion fails. Spain and Ireland entered 2008 with balanced or surplus budgets and low debt. Their revenues were real; the housing bubble underneath them was not. Structural balance would have certified those booms as sound, because bubble taxes look permanent while they last.
[C1.5] A rule keyed to the output gap cannot see a credit cycle. It restrains the wrong thing.
[C1.6] Third, stabilizers need no constitutional permission, and they are not stabilization. Unemployment insurance cushions households; it does not recapitalize banks, build a vaccine supply chain, or keep schools open through a plague. The responses that mattered in 2008 and 2020 were discretionary, and this constitution would forbid them — or force offsetting cuts during a slump, the exact procyclicality PRO claims to have escaped.
[C1.7] And "over the cycle" means paying back during recovery, when the gap is still negative. That is austerity with a constitutional signature.
[C1.8] The choice is not rules versus wisdom. It is a rule that binds in crises and evaporates in booms. Legislators can be voted out; an output-gap forecast cannot.
