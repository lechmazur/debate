[P1.1] Palm oil has raised incomes, but that does not give governments a license to treat remaining forest and peat as a free input. Conversion still produces haze, species collapse, and carbon release that no later shipment boom can reverse. Land-use rules should therefore tighten even if some smallholders earn less and some export growth slows.
[P1.2] The harms on the other side are public and irreversible. Drained peat and cleared forest feed fires that choke cities, close schools, and send children to clinics across borders. Isolated habitat does not recover on a planting cycle; once a carbon store burns or a corridor is cut, the loss is permanent. Household cash and export volume matter.
[P1.3] They do not outrank a shared atmosphere or the last contiguous stands that still hold orangutans, flood control, and soil.
[P1.4] The income story is also less clean than the opposition will claim. A large share of new clearing is concession-driven and speculative, not a last resort of the poorest growers. Weak maps and weak sanctions let firms externalize fire and flood risk onto the same rural communities later told they cannot afford rules. Aggressive zoning—a hard stop on remaining primary forest and deep peat, plus real penalties for illegal conversion—stops those communities from being used as cover for a land race they do not control.
[P1.5] Markets are already writing forest conversion out of supply chains. A country that keeps expanding into high-conservation land will not keep the premium buyers; it will keep the residual, discounted ones. Flattening some growth now is cheaper than losing access later and then trying to restore landscapes that no longer exist. The motion does not require crushing smallholders.
[P1.6] It requires ending the fiction that every extra hectare is a net public gain. Some income will be lower. Some curves will flatten. That is the price of not selling the last forests for a few more years of volume.

[C1.1] The motion asks the poorest households in rural Southeast Asia to pay, in cash they cannot spare, for environmental gains that tighter rules will not actually deliver. That is the central flaw: not that forests do not matter, but that aggressive land-use regulation fails on its own terms while its costs land precisely on the wrong people.
[C1.2] Consider who pays. Smallholders work roughly two-fifths of palm area in Indonesia and Malaysia—families on two to four hectares. When new zoning locks land, concessions keep lawyers and ministry contacts; the farmer at the forest edge gets a fine and a dead livelihood. PRO concedes weak maps and weak sanctions, then prescribes more of the same instrument.
[C1.3] A state that cannot enforce today's moratoria will not enforce tomorrow's against the powerful. Rules under weak capacity get captured: they bind the compliant while the connected price around them.
[C1.4] Second, leakage. Vegetable-oil demand does not shrink because Jakarta zones harder. Palm yields several times more oil per hectare than soy or rapeseed, so suppressing it pushes the same demand onto more hectares elsewhere—or into illegal clearing that dodges fire rules entirely. A hectare "saved" in Kalimantan can become two or three cleared abroad.
[C1.5] The motion counts only the forest it can see.
[C1.6] Third, the cost itself. "Some smallholder income" means school fees, medicine, a buffer against a failed season. And export earnings fund the fire brigades, mapping agencies, and courts that any conservation regime depends on. Cut that revenue and enforcement gets weaker, not stronger.
[C1.7] The choice is not regulation or nothing. Enforce existing fire and concession law, certify supply chains, raise yields on land already cleared, pay communities for standing forest. Those tools target the actual offenders and keep the revenue conservation needs. The motion reaches for the bluntest instrument available and bills the rural poor for its failures.
