[P1.1] Three facts should organize how we think about this question. First, batteries and clean tech are commodity manufacturing businesses with brutal global overcapacity risk. Second, governments are bad at picking which firms deserve capital, but quite good at being reliable buyers. Third, demand creates durable markets; supply subsidies create dependent firms.
[P1.2] That asymmetry is the whole case for PRO.
[P1.3] Start with the mechanism. A producer subsidy is a bet that a factory, once built, will find buyers. But if nobody wants the product at the price it's made, the factory becomes a subsidized liability — see the solar manufacturing boom-bust cycles of the past fifteen years, where multiple countries poured money into panel plants that went bankrupt the moment global prices fell or policy support dried up. Demand-side policy inverts the bet: governments guarantee the market first — through procurement mandates, EV purchase incentives, charging infrastructure, building codes requiring heat pumps, utility-scale storage requirements — and let competition among producers, domestic or foreign, fight to serve that guaranteed demand.
[P1.4] You get the clean tech deployed either way; the difference is whether the public eats the risk of bad factory bets.
[P1.5] Second, demand-side policy is self-correcting in a way supply-side policy is not. If demand incentives are too generous, you simply overpay consumers — wasteful, but the technology still gets adopted and emissions still fall. If producer subsidies are misallocated, you get stranded assets, zombie firms lobbying for permanent protection, and sunk political capital that makes governments afraid to let failure happen — which is exactly how subsidy regimes become immortal rent extraction schemes rather than temporary bridges.
[P1.6] Third, demand-side policy is technology-neutral and future-proof. Subsidizing "battery factories" bets on today's chemistry — lithium-ion dominance — and can lock out better solutions like solid-state or sodium-ion that a rigid producer-subsidy regime wasn't built to fund. Guaranteed demand lets whichever technology wins on merit actually win, because the market signal is "someone will buy this," not "this specific factory gets money."

[C1.2] China did not dominate battery manufacturing through consumer incentives. It won through sustained producer subsidies, cheap state capital, and deliberate capacity expansion over fifteen years. Today, Chinese firms control the majority of global battery cell production and critical mineral processing. When Western governments offer EV rebates and charging mandates, they are not summoning domestic supply—they are creating demand that Chinese subsidized overcapacity will absorb.
[C1.3] This is not hypothetical: Western automakers rushed to meet incentive-driven demand, only to find their supply chains hollowed out, dependent on foreign cells and materials. Taxpayers finance the transition; foreign industrial policy captures the manufacturing jobs, the supply chains, and the technological learning.
[C1.4] This is the fatal asymmetry. Demand is mobile; supply chains are sticky. A purchase incentive can be spent on a vehicle assembled anywhere. A producer subsidy builds permanent domestic capacity, skilled workforces, and supplier ecosystems that generate learning-by-doing.
[C1.5] Manufacturing costs fall with cumulative production—what analysts call learning curves—and subsidies accelerate that curve by financing the scale that drives costs down. Consumer rebates merely shift demand; they do not compress the cost curve for domestic producers. Batteries are dual-use technologies and supply chain chokepoints are geopolitical weapons. Relying on demand signals to conjure domestic manufacturing is strategic negligence.
[C1.6] The choice is not between markets and subsidies. It is between sequencing and surrender. Producer subsidies build the industrial moat before demand-side policy fills it with water. Without them, you guarantee a market for your rivals' factories while hoping your own emerge organically.
[C1.7] That is not industrial policy; it is industrial charity for foreign competitors.
