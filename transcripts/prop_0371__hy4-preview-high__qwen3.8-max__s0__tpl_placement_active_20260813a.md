[P1.1] The case for the motion is straightforward: an airline ticket is not just a seat. It is passage from A to B with the few belongings a trip requires. One standard roll-aboard that fits the overhead sizer, plus a small personal item, is part of transportation itself, not an optional extra. Yet carriers sell a bare fare allowing only a small personal item while charging for any carry-on, then recover revenue through upcharges for overhead space, gate-check fees, and shifting size rules.
[P1.2] That is not choice; it is a toll charged after purchase, when the traveler is captive and the low fare has hooked them.
[P1.3] Defenders call this unbundling. In practice it makes fares dishonest. At booking, passengers face a maze of baggage distinctions and dynamic fees that defeat comparison shopping. At the airport, the choice vanishes.
[P1.4] Having bought a nonrefundable seat and reached the gate, a traveler who needs clothing, medication, a laptop, or a child's supplies must pay the airline's last-minute price or repack under pressure. A family finding each modest roller bag costs more at the gate than the fare saving has not exercised choice; they have been penalized for needing luggage.
[P1.5] The remedy is a floor, not a ceiling. Requiring every ticket to include a reasonable carry-on baseline does not ban fees for extra, oversized, or checked bags, nor stop competition on comfort, flexibility, or service. It says an airline cannot sell passage while paywalling the ability to carry essentials. Clear size limits and an included allowance reduce gate arguments, stuffed backpacks, overhead-bin races, and delay-causing gate checks.
[P1.6] Prices become comparable and surprises shrink.
[P1.7] The real question is whether a ticket price should tell the truth. Fragmented baggage upcharges let airlines advertise a low fare and monetize necessity. A mandated baseline restores honest pricing and operational order. If travel includes transporting a person, it must include transporting a reasonable amount of what that person needs.

A student traveling with a backpack, a worker flying home for a weekend, or a budget family choosing between driving and flying benefits from the ability to buy the seat alone. If regulators mandate a carry-on baseline, airlines will not absorb the cost; they will build it into the base fare.

[C1.6] A mandate is a blunt solution to a transparency problem.
[C1.7] Finally, including every overhead bag worsens the operational harms PRO worries about. Bins are scarce. If every ticket guarantees roll-aboard space, airlines face more bags, more boarding time, more gate checks, and more delays. Pricing overhead use is a rationing tool.
