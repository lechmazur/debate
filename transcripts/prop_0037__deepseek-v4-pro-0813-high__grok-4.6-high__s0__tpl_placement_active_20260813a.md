[P1.1] Central districts should be governed by a simple rule: private cars do not belong in the densest walkable core of a major city. Congestion pricing is not a serious substitute; it is a toll that lets the harm continue for anyone willing to pay. The point is not to make driving more expensive but to stop private cars from consuming space, polluting air, and endangering people where walking, cycling, and public transport should have priority.
[P1.2] A ban changes the physical reality immediately. With private cars gone, lanes can be reallocated to wider sidewalks, outdoor seating, and protected cycling; buses run without blockage, and crossings become safe. Congestion pricing only shaves traffic. It preserves an environment where a child stepping off a curb still faces fast-moving private vehicles, and exhaust remains.
[P1.3] Wealthy drivers or expensed ride-hail trips can treat the charge as a cost of convenience. The result is not a people-first center; it is a paid car lane.
[P1.4] The policy is also simpler and fairer. Congestion pricing requires complex payment systems, exemption battles, and ongoing enforcement. A private-car ban can be enforced with clear permits for deliveries, emergency access, disabled residents, and public transport. Rather than charging working people out of the core, it removes a minority of private vehicles that cause disproportionate harm.
[P1.5] This is a choice about how scarce central road space is used. Buses, trams, cycles, deliveries, and accessible transport move more people and goods per square meter than private cars. Where private cars have been removed, foot traffic and street life generally improve because transit and walking become reliable. The right metric is not whether car speed improves under a toll.
[P1.6] The right metric is whether a ten-year-old can cross safely, a bus can cross without delay, and tailpipe emissions are gone. On that metric, congestion pricing alone loses; a ban wins.

[C1.2] A private car in the core is not always a luxury. It is a night nurse finishing a shift after the last train. It is a caregiver moving a relative who cannot board a crowded bus. It is a tradesperson carrying tools no tram will take.
[C1.3] Pricing forces those trips to justify themselves. A ban simply forbids them, then spends years inventing exceptions that recreate the same congestion under another name.
[C1.4] That is prohibition's fatal simplicity. PRO promises "clear permits" for deliveries, emergencies, and disabled residents. The list never stays short: hotels, residents, contractors, taxis, anyone who can argue necessity. Enforcement becomes a sticker war.
[C1.5] Pricing is cleaner: one price, public revenue, fewer carve-outs.
[C1.6] The fairness charge against pricing also runs the wrong way. A charge can be recycled into cheaper fares and better buses. A ban is free only for people already well served by transit or lucky enough to live inside the line. Everyone else pays in time, lost customers, and jobs they can no longer reach.
[C1.7] Nor does street life require erasure. Pedestrian streets, bus lanes, and protected cycling can sit beside a priced residual of cars. Charge the core and traffic falls. Ban it entirely and you shift the queue to the ring, dump cars onto nearby streets, and punish regional visitors who keep shops and theaters alive.
[C1.8] The right test is not whether private cars vanish from twenty blocks. It is whether the whole city moves more people, more safely, with less pollution, without locking out those transit cannot serve. On that test, pricing wins. A ban loses.
