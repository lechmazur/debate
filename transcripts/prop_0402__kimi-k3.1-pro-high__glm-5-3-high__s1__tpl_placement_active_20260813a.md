[P1.1] This rule protects a simple distinction: landlords may judge whether an applicant can reliably pay the rent, but not whether they dislike the applicant’s source of lawful income. The words “otherwise qualified” and “solely” matter. Owners can still use neutral, consistently applied standards concerning rental history, credit, references, and the tenant’s actual financial obligations. They simply cannot turn a passing application into a failing one because a voucher or public assistance appears on it.
[P1.2] The present system lets discrimination defeat the purpose of assistance. Government may calculate that a household can afford housing with support, issue the benefit, and yet leave that household trapped in shelters, overcrowded rooms, or distant neighborhoods because landlords refuse even to consider it. The family has resources on paper but no meaningful access to the market. A benefit that cannot be used where suitable housing exists is an unreliable bridge to stability.
[P1.3] Consider two applicants with identical rental histories and equal ability to cover their respective shares of the rent. One receives wages; the other combines earnings with a housing voucher, often with the assisted portion paid through a program rather than at the landlord’s discretion. Rejecting the second applicant automatically does not assess risk more carefully. It substitutes a stereotype about poverty for an evaluation of this tenant.
[P1.4] Nor does the motion force owners to accept destructive tenants, waive lawful requirements, or ignore genuine administrative incompatibilities. It prohibits rejection *solely* because payment comes through lawful assistance. That is a narrow constraint with a large return: more households can compete for homes, children face fewer forced moves, and public funds purchase actual housing rather than repeated searches and temporary accommodation.
[P1.5] Property rights include wide freedom to select tenants, but not an unlimited entitlement to frustrate lawful support and exclude qualified people by categorical proxy. The landlord’s remaining screening tools protect the investment; the rule protects equal access. When both sides’ legitimate interests can be preserved, blanket refusal is not prudent business judgment—it is unnecessary exclusion.

[C1.1] The motion's key word is "solely," and that word is where it collapses. A voucher never arrives alone. It arrives attached to a housing authority that inspects the unit, dictates lease language, sets rent ceilings, delays approvals, and pays on its own schedule. When a landlord declines a voucher, they are not rejecting the tenant's character—they are declining a different contract, with a government bureaucracy inserted as a co-counterparty.
[C1.2] Calling that rejection discrimination "solely" because of income source is like calling a refusal to accept a corporate lease "discrimination against employees."
[C1.3] Second, payment through a third party is a legitimate, predictive business signal, not a proxy for poverty. The landlord's real question is: who do I chase if rent falls short, and how fast do payments arrive? A tenant whose subsidy can be suspended after a paperwork error—a missed recertification deadline, a disputed inspection—presents a genuine default risk that has nothing to do with how responsible they are. Landlords screen tenants, but they cannot screen the housing authority.
[C1.4] Declining the arrangement is risk management, and it is no more irrational than declining any other payment plan with uncertain timing and compliance strings.
[C1.5] Third, the mandate operates asymmetrically and does nothing to fix the underlying problem. If voucher holders struggle to find units, the causes are subsidy levels set below market rents, slow inspections, and chronic underfunding of local programs. Forcing every landlord into the program does not raise the subsidy or speed the bureaucracy; it converts a public-policy failure into a private legal liability, enforced through complaints, penalties, and litigation, with small landlords—who own much of the affordable stock—bearing the compliance burden least able to absorb it.
[C1.6] Property rights here are not a loophole. The freedom to choose one's contractual counterparties is what makes private rental housing work at all. The motion takes a real social problem and answers it by stripping owners of a genuine business judgment. That is the wrong tool, aimed at the wrong party, and it should be rejected.

[P2.1] The opposition’s case depends on blurring the very distinction the motion draws. If inspections, required lease terms, or approval procedures create a genuine incompatibility in a particular case, a landlord may consider them. What the rule prohibits is rejecting an otherwise qualified applicant *solely* because lawful assistance pays the rent. CON therefore cannot defend categorical refusals by pointing to reasons that would independently survive the motion.
[P2.2] Their “different contract” argument also proves far too much. A corporate lease changes who occupies the unit and who bears legal responsibility; assistance does not necessarily do either. The tenant can remain the same counterparty, subject to the same screening and obligations. And inspections or rent ceilings cannot explain a blanket rule covering “other lawful public assistance,” which need not carry those features.
[P2.3] CON has described possible burdens of some programs, not a justification for excluding every assisted applicant.
[P2.4] Nor is third-party payment automatically a meaningful default signal. Wages can disappear, hours can fall, and any tenant’s circumstances can change. Landlords remain entitled to examine rental history, references, arrears, and the applicant’s actual share of the rent. If a subsidy’s conditions create a concrete problem, evaluate that problem; do not infer this household’s unreliability from the existence of assistance.
[P2.5] Saying “we cannot screen the housing authority” does not excuse refusing to screen the tenant fairly.
[P2.6] The argument about underfunded programs commits a similar evasion. If assistance is inadequate, the applicant may fail a neutral affordability standard, and the motion compels no acceptance. But where assistance is sufficient and the applicant otherwise qualifies, low subsidy levels elsewhere and slow bureaucracy are not reasons to preserve an additional, purely categorical barrier. Government should improve program administration; landlords should stop defeating assistance that already works.
[P2.7] Finally, enforcement costs do not vindicate the conduct being enforced against. The basic obligation—apply consistent, relevant criteria rather than an automatic exclusion—is not an impossible burden on small owners. CON defends individualized business judgment, which this motion preserves, in order to save categorical business avoidance, which it rightly removes.
