[P1.1] When a downturn hits, the fiscal state's job is not to freeze. Automatic stabilizers—unemployment insurance, progressive tax revenue shifts, means-tested transfers—exist precisely to expand when private demand collapses and to withdraw on their own when recovery arrives. Elevated debt ratios change the arithmetic; they do not reverse the logic.
[P1.2] The core reason is what happens to the debt ratio itself. Debt sustainability depends on the path of GDP relative to interest costs, not on the current ratio alone. A recession that is allowed to deepen lowers the denominator: tax bases shrink, unemployment spending climbs for reasons unrelated to policy generosity, and productive capacity atrophies as workers lose skills and firms scrap investment. Countries that front-load consolidation in slumps routinely end up with higher, not lower, debt ratios than countries that let stabilizers run.
[P1.3] Cutting spending to protect a debt ratio during a demand shortfall is a way of protecting the number while destroying the thing the number is supposed to measure: solvency and economic capacity.
[P1.4] Second, automatic stabilizers are the cheapest form of support we have. They are targeted by construction—money flows to people who have lost income—and they are temporary by construction, unwinding without a legislative fight once unemployment falls and incomes recover. That self-reversing property is exactly what we want when debt is high: no permanent new programs, no open-ended commitments, just the existing code doing its work. Discretionary austerity, by contrast, is slow, politically contentious, and hard to reverse.
[P1.5] Third, the macroeconomic multiplier on transfers and unemployment benefits during a slump is high, because recipients spend them quickly at exactly the moment monetary policy is constrained. At the effective lower bound, fiscal withdrawal is not offset by cheaper money; it lands almost fully on output.
[P1.6] The opposition's burden is to show that elevated debt ratios justify overriding a rule-like, self-limiting stabilizer. I will argue they cannot: suspension of stabilizers is not fiscal discipline. It is a fiscal cliff built into the worst possible moment.
