[P1.1] The core of this debate is simple: countries should not export the toxic consequences of their consumption to communities least able to refuse them. Electronic waste is not ordinary scrap. It contains leaded glass, mercury, cadmium, brominated flame retardants, and circuit boards that yield valuable metals only through dangerous processing. When high-income countries ship old phones, computers, and televisions to lower-income countries, they often export pollution under the label of “used goods.” Much of that material ends up in informal yards where workers burn insulation off copper wire, strip components by hand, or use acid to recover gold.
[P1.2] The result is preventable exposure to neurotoxins and heavy metals in neighborhoods with weak healthcare and little protective equipment.
[P1.3] A ban is the necessary policy trigger because voluntary standards and export consent have not solved the problem. If the receiving country is poorly equipped, consent is often formal rather than meaningful, and enforcement across borders is hard. The proposition replaces that leakage with a clear duty: the country that generated the waste manages it domestically, or through certified regional facilities that meet audited environmental and labor standards. This is not isolationism; it is responsibility.
[P1.4] It also allows certified regional processing, so smaller countries can pool compliant capacity rather than forcing every state to build redundant plants.
[P1.5] Domestic responsibility changes incentives in ways export does not. When governments and producers must manage their own e-waste, they have stronger reasons to design longer-lived devices, modular batteries, standardized chargers, and easier recovery systems. It also builds formal recycling jobs at home instead of subsidizing dangerous informal work abroad. The alternative—continued export—lets wealthy consumers enjoy cheap electronics while shifting the health and environmental bill to lower-income communities.
[P1.6] That is not trade; it is dumping by another name. The right rule is: if you consume it, you recycle it.
