[P1.1] The permanent member veto is not a design quirk of the UN Security Council—it is the central flaw that defeats the institution’s entire reason for existing. The Council was built to stop mass atrocity, prevent catastrophic cross-border war, and enforce shared global norms, but the veto lets a single permanent member paralyze collective action whenever its own interests, or those of a close ally, are at stake. We have seen this play out repeatedly: years of blocked action to stop civilian slaughter in Syria, repeated vetoes of even modest humanitarian ceasefires, and blanket protection for allied governments credibly accused of war crimes, no matter how overwhelming global support for action.
[P1.2] The threat of major power withdrawal does not justify preserving this broken system. A Security Council that cannot act in the face of mass atrocity because one member chooses to shield aggression has already lost the legitimacy it needs to matter; it is a hollow debating society, not a body for collective security. Abolishing the veto does not destroy the Council—it reclaims it. Major powers do not permanently walk away from functional, legitimate global bodies that set the terms of sanctions, peacekeeping, and post-conflict recovery; they only lose the ability to hold those bodies hostage for narrow, parochial gain.
[P1.3] When major powers choose to act unilaterally today, they already do so, often hiding behind veto-driven gridlock to justify their actions; the veto does not prevent unilateralism, it launders it.
[P1.4] Caving to withdrawal threats to preserve the veto locks in permanent, predictable failure: a Council that will never act when action matters most. Abolishing the veto calls the great power bluff, and builds an institution that delivers on its core promise rather than offering permanent impunity to its most powerful members.

[C1.1] The veto is not an obstacle to collective security; it is the minimum condition for collective security that can actually be enforced. A Security Council that outvotes a permanent member on an existential issue will not thereby acquire power. It will lose it. Sanctions bite only when major economies cooperate; peacekeepers deploy only when major states provide troops, logistics, and political cover; enforcement commands respect only when the great powers accept the Council as their own.
[C1.2] The veto is the price of that acceptance.
[C1.3] PRO measures success by the number of times the Council acts. That is the wrong metric. The relevant test is whether Council decisions prevent or contain threats without triggering great-power defiance. On that test, abolishing the veto fails.
[C1.4] If China, Russia, or the United States is overruled on war, sanctions, or intervention, it can ignore resolutions, block implementation informally, or withdraw and act outside the UN. Great powers do not usually remain in bodies that permanently subordinate their security interests; the United States stayed out of the League of Nations, and modern states have left or defunded institutions when they believed those institutions worked against them. Treating withdrawal as a bluff is not realism; it is wishful thinking.
[C1.5] The veto also has a protective function. It prevents a transient majority from branding a great-power opponent an aggressor and authorizing coercion that the defeated power will resist. That path does not produce peacekeeping; it produces contested interventions and escalation. Syria is a genuine indictment of Council paralysis, but replacing paralysis with formal authority and no capacity would be worse: more resolutions, less security.
[C1.6] Reform should tighten veto use through pledges not to block credible atrocity responses, clearer thresholds, and broader regional input. Abolition under threat would not reclaim the Council. It would hollow it out.

[P2.1] The case for keeping the veto rests on a false choice between a gridlocked Council great powers stay in, and an empowered Council they abandon. That tradeoff collapses the moment you look at how the status quo actually works. Great powers already act outside the Council when it suits them; the veto does not stop unilateral aggression, it gives them a tool to prevent anyone else from acting collectively against it. When Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine, it did not wait for Council approval to attack—it simply vetoed every meaningful response, turning the institution built to check cross-border aggression into a diplomatic shield for it.
[P2.2] The claim that the veto prevents great power defiance gets the dynamic backwards: the veto normalizes great power defiance from inside the institution, because a permanent member never has to choose between openly defying global consensus and getting its way—it can just kill the resolution before it passes. The League of Nations warning misreads history: the League failed not because it lacked a veto, but because it could not deliver collective action even when most of the world supported it, exactly the flaw the permanent veto locks in today.
[P2.3] Abolishing the veto does not strip permanent members of their privileged seats, their agenda-setting power, or their ability to shape outcomes through negotiation and coalition-building; it only removes their unilateral right to kill broad, supermajority-backed action. Their preferred alternative—voluntary pledges to limit veto use on atrocities—has been tried for years, and has done nothing to stop repeated vetoes of humanitarian ceasefires, because non-binding pledges are abandoned the moment a powerful state finds them inconvenient. A Council that passes a consensus resolution demanding action, even when a permanent member drags its feet on implementation, is still stronger than a Council that cannot even speak to mass atrocity because one member objects.
[P2.4] Formal global condemnation shifts norms, isolates aggressors, and creates accountability even when enforcement is imperfect; the current system offers guaranteed, permanent impunity instead.

[C2.1] The case depends on a category error: it confuses the ability to pass a resolution with the ability to stop aggression. Ukraine proves the point. Russia vetoed Council action, yet states that possessed the relevant leverage imposed sanctions, supplied weapons, and pursued accountability outside the UN. A Council majority overruling Moscow would not by itself have made China, India, or much of the Global South join an oil embargo, contribute troops, or risk escalation with a nuclear power.
[C2.2] It would have produced a stronger-sounding resolution with weaker implementation.
[C2.3] That is the decisive comparison. With the veto, deadlock is visible; without it, paralysis moves from the chamber to the battlefield, where enforcement depends on the defeated permanent member's cooperation. PRO concedes this when it says a permanent member may "drag its feet on implementation." Withdrawal need not even occur: withholding troops, enforcement, or funding suffices.
[C2.4] That is not a bug; it is the real constraint. A Council that commands sanctions, peacekeepers, and coercive measures a major power refuses to fund, staff, or obey will lose credibility faster than one that sometimes fails to speak.
[C2.5] Nor does the League analogy help PRO. The League's core failure was the absence of decisive power and enforcement capacity, not the absence of a veto; reproducing that condition by overruling a P5 member is not learning from history. Voluntary pledges may be imperfect, but their failure shows only that pledges cannot change interests. Abolition cannot change interests either.
[C2.6] The veto is a damage-control device. It prevents a transient majority from branding a great power an aggressor and authorizing coercion that power will resist, converting contested interventions into escalation. Reform can raise the cost of blocking atrocity responses; abolition simply bets that the institutions that set war and peace can survive being commanded to act against the material interests of their most powerful members. That is not realism.
[C2.7] It is hollowing out the Council while claiming to reclaim it.
