[P1.1] A child who stars in a family channel is working. She performs on schedule, retakes tantrums for the camera, smiles for sponsors, generates ad dollars that pay the mortgage. But unlike a child actor on a set, she often has no trust account, no limit on hours, no advocate checking that school and sleep come first. That gap is indefensible.
[P1.2] Parents are asked to play two incompatible roles at once: loving guardian and profit-maximizing manager. When rent depends on views, the incentive is to film the fever, the meltdown, the first period, the custody fight. Good parents feel that pull too, because the algorithm rewards exposure and constancy. Without outside rules on earnings, the child's pay can simply disappear into household spending, leaving her with nothing at eighteen for years of labor that built the brand.
[P1.3] Scale changes the nature of the harm. A photo shared with grandparents is not the same as a childhood catalogued for millions, searchable forever. A seven-year-old cannot consent to having her embarrassments and body and friendships monetized for strangers. And she cannot meaningfully opt out when home is the set and refusing to perform feels like disappointing Mom and Dad.
[P1.4] That is why removal matters as much as money. A teenager should be able to demand that platforms and parents take down commercial content featuring her as a child, and have that request enforced. Childhood should not be an irrevocable contract signed by someone else.
[P1.5] No one is banning parents from sharing family life. The restriction falls only on large commercial operations built on a child's image. If the business is valuable enough to sustain a family, it is valuable enough to set aside the child's share and respect her future veto. Freedom to parent does not include freedom to sell a childhood without safeguards.

If enforcement is thin, the answer is to extend and enforce those tools—counting hours, requiring trusts, licensing the work—not to forbid the business. [C1.5] Third, the "right to later removal" is the most dangerous part of the package. It hands one teenager an eraser over family speech, over siblings' childhoods, over a parent's account of family life, and over platforms' records. [C1.6] It is a censorship veto dressed as a right, and PRO owes us a theory of why family memory uniquely deserves it.
[C1.7] PRO's instinct is sympathy. Its policy is a vague ban plus a deletion right. Sympathy does not carry that burden.
