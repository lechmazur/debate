[P1.1] A monthly check is a claim on a market. A service is the thing itself. That distinction is decisive, because in the four sectors named, the market is precisely what fails.
[P1.2] Start with supply. Hand a parent in a childcare desert five hundred dollars and you have not created a single licensed slot. You have created demand chasing a fixed number of places, and the price of those places rises until the subsidy is absorbed by providers. Money is fungible; a bus route, a fiber line, a neonatal unit are not.
[P1.3] No household, however empowered, can purchase a transit network — it is indivisible, and its value comes from everyone being on it. Cash cannot buy public goods; only provision can.
[P1.4] Second, risk. Unconditional cash is a smooth, predictable stream. Illness is the opposite: rare, sudden, ruinous. No stipend lets a family self-insure a cancer diagnosis.
[P1.5] Pooling risk across a whole population is a technology cash transfers structurally cannot reproduce, and it is why every wealthy country that covers everyone does it through a system, not a stipend.
[P1.6] Third, bargaining power. One public buyer negotiating drug prices or broadband buildout extracts terms a million individual shoppers never will. The same dollar buys more healthcare when spent collectively. That is not ideology; it is arithmetic about who faces the seller.
[P1.7] The opposition will say people know their own needs best. Usually true — for groceries. But the question is not who chooses; it is whether the choice exists. Freedom that cannot be exercised is a receipt, not a liberty.
[P1.8] And universality is durable: a bus people ride every morning has constituents; a transfer has a line item, and line items get cut.
[P1.9] Spend the marginal dollar where it becomes capacity, not where it becomes someone else's rent.
