[P1.1] Every few months a new app becomes the villain — a game, a short-video feed, a chatbot — and parents get fresh instructions for fighting it. This is an arms race against an industry of engineers paid to hold children's attention, and it is a race parents will always lose. The app-by-app model doesn't just exhaust families; it misdiagnoses the problem.
[P1.2] The core harm of heavy screen use is displacement: hours on a device are hours not asleep, not outside, not staring out a window inventing something to do. If that's the mechanism, the cure is not better trench warfare over content but restoring what got crowded out. Sleep, boredom, and free play belong at the center of digital-wellbeing guidance.
[P1.3] Each is foundational. Sleep underwrites attention, mood, and learning; a child whose phone leaves the bedroom at night gains more than any content filter delivers. Boredom is the training ground for self-generated engagement: the child who survives a boring car ride without a screen is building the self-regulation that matters at fifteen, when no parent is watching. Free play teaches negotiation, risk, and imagination — the social software no app ships with.
[P1.4] This framing also wins practically. Goals like "protect sleep" or "an hour of unstructured play" survive every product cycle, work from age four to seventeen, and can be checked by any parent or pediatrician. "Block this app" expires the moment a friend shows up with a different phone. And it changes the parent's role from cop to coach: not policing an endless queue of apps, but building a life full enough that the phone loses by default.
[P1.5] The opposition will say rules still matter. They do — guardrails have their place. But the question is where the center of gravity sits. Rules manage symptoms; sleep, boredom, and play are the underlying health.
[P1.6] Advice that builds a child who no longer needs the fight beats advice that schedules the next one.
