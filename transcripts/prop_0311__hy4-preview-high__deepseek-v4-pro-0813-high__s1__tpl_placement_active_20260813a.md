[P1.1] We judge cohabitation by the wrong test. Commitment is not measured by how fast a couple signs a marriage license; it is measured by what they are willing to build, risk, and learn together. Living together before marriage is a reasonable trial of a shared life, not evidence of hesitation.
[P1.2] Marriage changes legal and economic stakes, not just feelings. Cohabitation tests the unglamorous variables that predict durability: money, chores, conflict, illness, sleep, and routines. A couple that has never shared a kitchen can vow forever while knowing almost nothing about the person they see at 7 a.m. after a bad night. Finding irreconcilable habits while renting is disappointing; finding them after a wedding, a mortgage, and legal knots is devastating.
[P1.3] Calling the earlier exit "weak commitment" confuses prudence with timidity.
[P1.4] Opponents will cite the old link between premarital cohabitation and divorce. That link is mostly selection, not causation. People who are younger, poorer, or skeptical of marriage are more likely to cohabit and more likely to divorce anyway. Once researchers distinguish couples who merely slid into sharing housing from those who made a deliberate plan—often engagement or clear intent to marry—the gap narrows sharply and often disappears.
[P1.5] The risk lies in drifting, not in the address.
[P1.6] Cohabitation is also already ordinary and economically rational: two incomes, one lease, shared utilities. In a housing market where many young adults cannot comfortably live alone, treating lovers who share rent as deviant punishes necessity.
[P1.7] A marriage entered after cohabitation is not a lesser marriage; it is a more informed one. The alternative—marry first, investigate later—turns a wedding into an experiment and divorce into the only exit. We should treat premarital cohabitation as a normal, prudent stage: a deliberate test of compatibility that protects love and marriage. Commitment is shown by choosing someone every day, not by refusing to learn whether the choice is wise.

That is a weaker standard of commitment, not a neutral one.
[C1.2] PRO’s own case rests on a distinction the motion does not preserve: deliberate cohabitation with clear intent to marry. If the protective factor is clarity and intent, then socially normalizing all premarital cohabitation is the wrong policy. Ordinary cohabitation is often not a deliberate trial; it is a slide. One lease ends, rent rises, convenience suggests moving in, and the couple acquires joint property, pets, and routines without ever making a mutual commitment.
[C1.3] The easy exit PRO praises becomes harder every month, while the harder question—will we marry?—can remain unanswered.
[C1.4] That entanglement before commitment is costly. Couples may stay not because they decisively choose one another, but because leaving now requires a move, a broken lease, and financial loss. Cohabitation can therefore create inertia that carries uncertain relationships into marriage, not prudence that tests them. The proposition treats an ambiguous private arrangement as if it were a reliable trial.
[C1.5] Economic necessity does not make cohabitation a reasonable relationship step either. Roommates can share rent without pretending their housing contract signals romantic commitment. When lovers move in mainly to save money, that is exactly the weak-commitment, low-clarity arrangement the motion would sanitize.
[C1.6] The better social signal remains: if you intend marriage, say so and marry; if you are merely sharing life while keeping exits easy, do not call that ordinary commitment-building. Cohabitation before marriage may sometimes be prudent, but as a social default it erodes clarity, delays decision, and substitutes convenience for commitment. That is why it should still be treated as a sign of weak commitment, not as an ordinary reasonable step.
