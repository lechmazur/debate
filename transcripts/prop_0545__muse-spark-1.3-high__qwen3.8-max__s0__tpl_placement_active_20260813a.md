[C1.1] The proposition asks you to assume that changing a legal default safely converts silence into consent. It does not. Organ donation does not hinge on paperwork alone; it hinges on trust, family cooperation, and a small eligible donor pool. Presumed consent strains all three.
[C1.2] First, the promised measurable increase is unreliable. Most deaths do not create usable organs; only a tiny subset occur in hospitals with ventilated, viable donors. Even where opt-out systems see consent rise, the step from consent to recovered organs depends on intensive coordination, staff training, and family support. Spain’s high donation rate is best explained by that infrastructure and public trust, not by presumption alone.
[C1.3] If a jurisdiction lacks those conditions, a default change may register more nominal donors but produce few extra transplants. Worse, if families feel bypassed, they may resist, reducing effective donation.
[C1.4] Second, presumed consent creates ethical violations precisely where it is supposed to prevent them. Silence is not informed agreement. Many people never register objections because of disability, language barriers, limited literacy, distrust of the state, or simple lack of access—not because they consent. The policy lets the state claim organs from the dead by leveraging inertia.
[C1.5] For communities with legitimate medical mistrust, that is not a harmless nudge; it is an appropriation risk.
[C1.6] Third, it can increase family distress. PRO imagines families are relieved, but in crisis they are told the law already decided. If relatives object, they must rebut the state, often while grieving and under time pressure. If hospitals override them to meet targets, distress rises; if they defer to families, the presumed-consent benefit collapses.
[C1.7] The motion demands both more transplants and no added ethical harm. PRO cannot have both: either families retain veto power and gains are small, or they lose it and violations and distress grow.
