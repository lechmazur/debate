[P1.1] Your voice is not content. It is your identity, your livelihood, and the way people who trust you know it is you.
[P1.2] When a platform or media company makes money distributing an AI clone that sounds like an identifiable person, it is selling that person without paying them and without letting them say no. That is wrong on its face, and dangerous in practice. A familiar voice bypasses skepticism in a way text never does. That is why scammers now use cloned voices of children to panic parents, why viral tracks imitating famous singers have racked up millions of plays the artists never approved, and why fake political messages in a candidate's voice can mislead voters before truth catches up.
[P1.3] Takedowns after the fact do not fix this. Once a convincing clone circulates, listeners cannot unhear it, and victims cannot chase every copy. The only point where control is possible is before commercial distribution. Affirmative consent — a clear yes, not silence treated as permission, not a buried opt-out — places the decision with the person whose identity is at stake.
[P1.4] That mirrors how we already treat faces in advertising, samples in music, and personal data in decent privacy regimes. No company can put your face on a billboard and apologize later; it must ask first.
[P1.5] This rule is narrow and workable. It does not ban building voice tools, private experimentation, or genuine commentary. It says: if you want to profit by circulating a synthetic voice identifiable as a real person, get permission and share the terms. Voice actors, musicians, and ordinary people keep bargaining power instead of begging for removal.
[P1.6] Platforms already license music, flag impersonation, and manage rights at scale. Adding voice likeness to that pipeline is a choice, not an impossibility.
[P1.7] The alternative is a market where identity belongs to whoever clones it first.

[C1.7] The real danger is asymmetric enforcement.
