[P1.1] The juvenile justice system rests on a promise the digital age has quietly broken. The promise was this: a child who errs will be corrected, not branded. Juvenile courts were built separate from adult courts precisely so that a fifteen-year-old's worst night would not define a forty-year-old's life. Paper records honored that promise by default—they sat in a courthouse basement, obscure, effectively forgotten.
[P1.2] Digital records honor nothing. They are indexed, scraped, resold by data brokers, and surfaced by a background-check algorithm in a landlord's office twenty years later. The question is not whether we want juvenile mistakes to follow people forever. Almost no one does.
[P1.3] The question is who carries the burden when technology makes forever the default.
[P1.4] The motion answers: the state should. "Presumptively" means the record closes at adulthood unless a prosecutor or judge articulates a specific reason it should not—an ongoing violent-offense pattern, a sex-offense registration requirement, a pending case. That preserves every legitimate exception. What it eliminates is the current arrangement in most places, where a young adult must know sealing exists, afford a lawyer, file a petition, and wait, while the record keeps working against them.
[P1.5] Predictably, the people who navigate that maze are the ones with resources, and the people who don't are the ones the record hurts most.
[P1.6] Consider the concrete stakes. A seventeen-year-old shoplifts, completes diversion, graduates, applies for a nursing program at twenty-three. A digital juvenile adjudication appears. She is screened out before anyone asks what happened.
[P1.7] No public safety was served. A future taxpayer, caregiver, and stable tenant was lost.
[P1.8] The adolescent brain is still developing well into the twenties; recidivism among juvenile offenders drops sharply once they hold stable work and housing. An open record attacks exactly those stabilizers. So sealing is not leniency—it is the safety measure. Anyone opposing this motion must explain why the burden of proof belongs on the twenty-year-old rather than on the state that promised her a second chance.

[C1.2] The real question is not second chances versus permanent branding, but who must justify the exception: the state or the person asking to hide a public record.
[C1.3] Begin with serious cases. PRO's exceptions cover only "an ongoing violent-offense pattern, a sex-offense registration requirement, a pending case" [P1.4]. That leaves out the seventeen-year-old who commits a single armed robbery or aggravated battery—a serious violent felony, no pattern, maybe no registration duty. Under presumptive sealing, his digital record closes automatically at adulthood unless the state affirmatively proves an exception.
[C1.4] In the real world, overloaded prosecutors and clerks let defaults happen; there is no hearing, no victim notice, no finding of rehabilitation. The juvenile court's promise was confidentiality and a chance at reform, not automatic erasure of violent adjudications at eighteen.
[C1.5] The remedy is also mistargeted. The villain in [P1.2] is the data broker, the scraper, the background-check algorithm—not the courthouse docket. Sealing the official file does not un-scrape a broker's copy or delete a cached mugshot. What it deletes is the one authoritative record proving the youth finished diversion and paid the debt.
[C1.6] If the harm is algorithmic permanence, regulate the private data market; don't destroy the official source that supplies accurate context.
[C1.7] Finally, PRO's burden question in [P1.8] inverts responsibility. Sealing a judicial record is an extraordinary remedy. The state cannot prove future dangerousness in every case; the individual is the one who knows and can document rehabilitation. Shifting the burden to the state turns sealing from a reward for demonstrated reform into a birthday windfall—automatic amnesia for everyone, including those who have earned no second chance.
