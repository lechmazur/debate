[P1.1] AI excels at the exact type of iterative, rule-bound practice that defines mastery learning. Mastery practice requires high-frequency, low-latency, personalized repetition of well-defined skills, whether that is solving quadratic equations, memorizing biology taxonomy, or drilling verb conjugations. AI can generate infinite, leveled practice sets, deliver immediate correction, and adjust difficulty in real time without fatigue. This frees secondary teachers from grading rote drills to focus on higher-order instruction, directly improving outcomes for all students.
[P1.2] For students who need extra repetition to master foundational skills, AI provides 24/7 access to practice that human tutors cannot match, eliminating scheduling barriers that often leave struggling learners behind. AI can also track long-term progress across entire cohorts, identifying common gaps in mastery that teachers can address in whole-class instruction, a task that would take humans hours of manual grading to complete. This use case aligns perfectly with AI’s core strengths, making it an indispensable tool for mastery practice.
[P1.3] Secondary writing feedback, by contrast, demands human judgment that AI cannot replicate, so it must never serve as the main source of that feedback. Writing instruction in secondary school centers on developing individual voice, rhetorical reasoning, and context-specific argumentation—skills that rely on nuance, not pattern-matching. AI feedback defaults to generic, formulaic suggestions that prioritize surface-level compliance over creative or critical growth. A student drafting a persuasive essay on local zoning laws, for example, gains far more from a teacher who can link their draft to prior class debates on community impact than an AI that flags passive voice and suggests synonyms.
[P1.4] Over time, main AI writing feedback trains students to optimize for algorithmic approval rather than authentic expression. This erodes student agency in writing, as they learn to prioritize algorithmic rules over their own intended meaning, a habit that persists long after secondary school, which undermines the core goals of secondary writing curricula.
