[P1.1] When the only two hospital systems in a region merge, regulators ask whether patient bills will rise by a few percent. They should ask with the same urgency whether nurses will lose the only realistic alternative employer who would bid up their pay, offer safer staffing, or let them move shifts. That second harm is not a side effect. It is the same market failure, viewed from the other side of the market.
[P1.2] A monopolist is a seller with power over price. A monopsonist is a buyer with power over price — here, the price of labor. Merge competing employers and workers face fewer bids, so wages, bonuses, training, schedules and non-wage conditions can be pushed below competitive levels without losing everyone to a rival. People work fewer hours, leave occupations they trained for, commute further, or exit the workforce.
[P1.3] That is deadweight loss, not a harmless transfer: labor is misallocated, output falls, service quality deteriorates, and eventually patients, students and customers pay for understaffed hospitals, schools and plants.
[P1.4] Treating this as secondary also creates a perverse loophole. A merger can be waved through because it promises lower consumer prices precisely by squeezing workers harder — consolidating payroll, closing duplicate sites, and ending wage competition between plants. Counting the saving while ignoring how it was extracted makes merger review systematically biased toward consolidation. The gains are not efficiencies if they are simply the exercise of new buying power.
[P1.5] Equal seriousness is administrable. Labor markets can be defined as product markets are: by occupation, skills and realistic commuting alternatives. Where a merger leaves teachers, drivers, meatpackers or coders with only one or two credible employers, concentration screens, wage effects and internal documents about retention and pay bands deserve the same weight as pricing models. That does not mean blocking every deal with any employment overlap.
[P1.6] It means the same presumptions, evidence, and willingness to require divestitures or block when competition for workers is materially destroyed.

[C1.1] Mergers should be judged first by what they do to consumers. Putting labor monopsony on the same plane sounds fair, but it would make merger review slower, more speculative, and hostile to deals that lower prices for millions.
[C1.2] Consumer price effects are observable and testable. Charge more for the same cereal, lose shoppers tomorrow. Labor markets have no such clarity. Is the market nurses, or all health workers?
[C1.3] A thirty-minute commute, or sixty with remote scheduling? Baristas who could tend bar, drive delivery, or go back to school? Define it narrowly and every hospital, warehouse, or tech deal looks like a monopsony. Give that guess equal weight and enforcers will sacrifice certain savings for millions to modeled wage risks for a few.
[C1.4] Workers also have escapes consumers lack. A shopper facing the only grocery chain in town must pay or go hungry. A meatpacker, driver, or coder facing a merged employer can commute farther, switch industries, bargain collectively, travel for work, or go remote — options that have only grown. That mobility does not make wage pressure harmless, but it makes it less durable and less suitable for a one-shot veto than consumer monopoly.
[C1.5] Wages are the wrong target for merger courts anyway. Pay is shaped by minimum-wage laws, unions, benefits rules, and workplace safety regulation — tools that act continuously and can be tuned. Merger review is blunt: block, condition, or clear. Turning enforcers into roving wage regulators invites politicized, unpredictable decisions far outside their competence.
[C1.6] Most damning is the tradeoff. Eliminating duplicate shifts and consolidating plants is usually efficiency, not exploitation. Yet under equal seriousness, the lower grocery bill counts for no more than a flattened pay band. Blocking a deal that helps millions of consumers, including those same workers as shoppers, to insure against speculative wage harm is not evenhanded.
[C1.7] It sacrifices the certain and broad for the conjectural and narrow.

[P2.1] The supposed clarity of consumer-price review is a myth. Defining cereal versus breakfast foods versus all groceries, predicting post-merger pricing, and modeling pass-through are also judgments. Courts manage that uncertainty; they do not demote consumer harm because substitution is messy. Labor markets deserve the same discipline, not perfection.
[P2.2] Nor are labor markets unusually opaque. Employers map the competition themselves: licensing separates nurses from orderlies, pay bands track rival hospitals, and retention plans name employers workers actually leave for. If a firm can tell its board whose offers it must match to keep teachers or meatpackers, a reviewer can use that map. Overly narrow definitions are a risk to police, not a reason to ignore power where skills and family ties make alternatives nominal.
[P2.3] Mobility is likewise overstated. Shoppers can travel, substitute, buy online, or go without; we do not excuse a grocery monopoly because a determined customer could drive an hour. For workers, switching industries means abandoning training, losing seniority and benefits, moving children, or accepting a longer commute. Remote work helps some coders, not nurses, drivers, or plant workers whose jobs are physical and local.
[P2.4] If escape were easy, merged employers could not profitably hold pay down.
[P2.5] The other-tools argument confuses a floor with competition. A minimum wage does not restore a second hospital bidding for experienced nurses, and a union cannot recreate the outside offer that gives bargaining leverage. Safety rules set minimums; only rival employers push pay, staffing, and scheduling above them. Merger review is the one moment to prevent that rival's elimination.
[P2.6] Finally, paying the same nurse less for the same shift is not an efficiency; producing care with genuinely fewer resources is. Equal seriousness preserves that distinction: count output-enhancing savings, reject savings extracted through buying power. Otherwise review blesses lower prices purchased by hollowing out the incomes of the very consumers it claims to protect.

[C2.1] Equal uncertainty does not mean equal administrability [P2.1]. Yes, defining cereal versus breakfast foods requires judgment, but enforcers then test it against scanner data, actual purchases, diversion ratios week by week. A labor-market guess about nurses versus all health workers, a 30-minute versus 60-minute commute, plus the value of schedules, safety, training and status, has no single price to check it against. Stacking four unobservables does not create the same discipline; it compounds error, and giving that compound equal veto power multiplies false blocks.
[C2.2] Employers tracking rivals does not solve it [P2.2]. An HR deck naming hospitals whose offers it matches to keep staff is anxiety, not market definition. Retention plans overstate threats because managers would rather overpay than be short-staffed on a night shift. Import that slide as a market map, add an equal presumption, and virtually every hospital, warehouse or school-district deal becomes presumptively illegal.
[C2.3] The mobility parallel also fails [P2.3]. The claim that shoppers can drive an hour so workers should get no discount ignores who can arbitrage. A grocery monopolist in a food desert faces no temp agency to undercut it tomorrow. A merged hospital that pushes nursing pay below market faces staffing agencies, rival clinics, outpatient centers and travel contracts that move within months to capture the gap.
[C2.4] Workers quit, retrain, and bargain; shoppers just pay.
[C2.5] Finally, the neat line between real efficiency and mere extraction collapses in practice [P2.6]. Closing a duplicate night shift, consolidating purchasing, standardizing staffing lowers cost per patient. Is that producing care with fewer resources, or paying the same nurse less for the same shift? Reviewers cannot tell in real time, so equal seriousness means treating plausible savings as suspect.
[C2.6] Labor has continuous regulators — wage floors, safety rules, unions that countervail. Consumers have no daily repricing regulator. Merger law should stay where its one-shot veto does the most certain good, not sacrifice lower bills for millions to insure modeled pay bands for a few.

[P5.1] The debate ends on a concession that decides it. When the only two hospitals cut experienced nurses' pay for the same shifts, that saving 'deserves zero credit' [C4.1]. Why? Because competition has been destroyed on the buying side, and a price lowered by power is not a benefit created by efficiency.
[P5.2] But that judgment cannot survive a consumer-first hierarchy. If labor harm is secondary, speculative, or someone else's job, the merged firm presents the same cut as lower costs, claims pass-through to bills, and asks for clearance. Reviewers must then either credit the extraction — and bless the loophole — or interrogate wages, staffing, and outside offers with the same seriousness they give prices. There is no middle position where the harm counts enough to deny the benefit but never enough to block the merger that causes it.
[P5.3] Equal seriousness is that interrogation, nothing more radical. It does not guarantee workers win over shoppers. It guarantees one rule for both sides of the market: gains earned by producing more with less count; gains extracted by eliminating the rival who would bid for people do not.
[P5.4] A system that polices only the price shoppers pay, while discounting the price workers receive, does not protect competition. It protects one half of it.
