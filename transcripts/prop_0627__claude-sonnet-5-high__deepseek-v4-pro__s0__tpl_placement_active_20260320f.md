[P1.1] A government that can poison the water its citizens drink, foul the air they breathe, and leave contaminated land in their neighborhoods, and face no binding constitutional check for doing so, has failed at the most basic function of governance: keeping people alive to exercise every other right. That is the case for recognizing a fundamental right to a minimally clean and healthy environment.
[P1.2] Start with the logic of rights we already accept. The right to life is meaningless if a state can lawfully permit lead in the water supply, as happened in Flint, Michigan, for years while regulators shuffled paperwork. The right to due process is hollow if a family's home becomes uninhabitable because of unregulated industrial dumping and they have no court to go to. Courts already infer unenumerated rights from the structure and purpose of constitutions, privacy, bodily autonomy, travel, because those rights are preconditions for a functioning free society.
[P1.3] A liveable environment is at least as foundational. You cannot litigate, vote, work, or raise children if the air is unbreathable or the water is toxic.
[P1.4] Second, look at what happens without this right: environmental harms fall disproportionately on the politically weakest, poor communities, minorities, rural populations with no lobbying power. Ordinary politics systematically underprotects these groups because polluters are concentrated and well-organized while victims are diffuse. Courts exist precisely to correct majoritarian failures that harm minorities. A constitutional right creates a floor that legislatures cannot bargain away when industry lobbying wins the day.
[P1.5] Third, this isn't speculative. Countries and states that have adopted constitutional environmental rights, from India's judicially recognized right to a healthy environment to provisions in countries like Colombia and France, have used them to force cleanups, block toxic projects, and mandate government action, without collapsing their economies or their courts.
[P1.6] The proposition is simple: when government action or inaction leaves people breathing poison or drinking contaminated water, that should be a constitutional violation, not a policy preference. That's the case CON must answer.
