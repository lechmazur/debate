[C1.1] The proposition turns a sociological complaint into a new public obligation: not merely maintaining parks or libraries, but actively funding and zoning for “revival” of third places. That inference fails. Decline is not disappearance, past distortion is not proof of a targeted remedy, and loneliness is not a blank check for subsidized sociability.
[C1.2] PRO’s collapse story selects nostalgic forms and calls them the baseline. Union halls, bowling leagues, and corner cafés have thinned, but social life moved into gyms, houses of worship, dog parks, amateur sports, coworking rooms, hobby groups that begin online, and private homes. A bowling league closing while a climbing gym opens is churn, not market failure. Commercial, suburban, chain, or digitally organized substitutes are simply counted as loss.
[C1.3] The externality argument in [P1.4] proves too much. A café owner may not invoice cohesion, but owners charge for coffee, seats, events, memberships, and atmosphere; landlords charge rent; clubs charge dues. Gyms, churches, schools, and laundromats create weak ties too. If every unpriced spillover justified subsidy, any meeting venue could claim public money.
[C1.4] Positive externalities are common; targeted subsidies require a measurable spillover, a causal supply shortfall, and a mechanism government can run without capture. Third places usually fail that test.
[C1.5] The remedy is worse than the disease. If “zone for revival” means repealing bans on mixed use, that is ordinary legalization, not a third-place program. If it means preferences, grants, quotas, or mandates, officials choose approved fellowship. Grants favor professional nonprofits and chains; quotas capitalize into land values.
[C1.6] A subsidized “community café” can raise rents and displace the cheap informal room where regulars belonged. Empty civic plazas show that space is not community.
[C1.7] Governments should remove barriers and keep basic facilities safe, not curate leisure with scarce funds. Bad zoning justifies correction, not an industrial policy for friendship. A third place works because people freely choose it repeatedly; subsidy can buy a building, but not belonging.
