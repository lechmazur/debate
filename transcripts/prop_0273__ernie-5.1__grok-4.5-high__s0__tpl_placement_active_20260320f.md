[P1.1] The airline industry's pricing model is built on a bait-and-switch: advertise a low fare, then stack on baggage fees, seat selection charges, and service costs until the final bill is unrecognizable. For families—who need multiple bags, adjacent seating, and sometimes extra services like priority boarding—this deception is especially damaging. The proposition forces transparency at the point of comparison, and that single change restores real market competition.
[P1.2] Here is why this matters fundamentally. When you see a flight priced at two hundred dollars per person, you cannot evaluate it against an alternative at two-fifty if the first carrier will charge you eighty dollars per checked bag and thirty dollars to sit together. The displayed price is fiction. Requiring full practical cost disclosure means consumers finally see apples-to-apples comparisons, and airlines compete on genuine value rather than who can hide more fees in the checkout flow.
[P1.3] Consider a family of four flying round-trip with two checked bags each. Under current rules, one airline might advertise a total fare that looks two hundred dollars cheaper than a competitor—only to lose that advantage once eight bags and four seat assignments are priced in. The family ends up overspending, frustrated, and distrustful. Transparency eliminates that trap.
[P1.4] Opponents will call this burdensome regulation that drives up displayed prices and scares customers away. But that fear is precisely the point: if a price looks scary only because hidden fees were propping up the headline number, the market was already broken. Businesses should not profit from confusing customers; they should profit from being genuinely competitive.
[P1.5] The practical cost is not a technicality—it is the cost families actually pay. Mandating its display before comparison is the simplest, most direct fix to a system that currently punishes the people least able to absorb surprise charges.
