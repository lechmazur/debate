[P1.1] The fairest rule treats an unavoidable charge as part of the price. A mandatory restaurant service charge, ticketing fee, or delivery-app service fee is not a tip or optional upgrade. It is money the customer must pay to get the exact thing advertised. If the seller can compute a total at payment, it can show that same total one screen earlier; the customer cannot decline the charge.
[P1.2] This rule does not cap prices or forbid service charges; it requires sellers to compete on the real price.
[P1.3] That timing is the whole harm. People choose a restaurant from a menu, select seats from a ticket list, and decide whether to order delivery before the final checkout screen. Once they have browsed, entered an address, built a cart, or sat through a meal, the late fee is no longer part of a free comparison. It is a penalty for switching.
[P1.4] A diner who sees a $25 entrée and learns only at payment that an unavoidable service charge applies cannot un-order the meal. A fan comparing two $60 tickets may discover one carries a large fee after seat selection; the apparently cheaper option won by being less honest.
[P1.5] Early disclosure also makes markets work. When all mandatory service charges appear in the headline price or in a clear adjacent line before checkout, customers can compare restaurants, venues, and apps on like terms. Honest firms no longer lose to rivals advertising a shaved-down base price and recovering it at the end. The rule rewards transparent pricing without telling any firm what it may charge.
[P1.6] The likely objection is that a total is eventually shown, so consent exists. But consent after sunk time and commitment is not choice among real offers. A payment-screen revelation is an ambush dressed as fine print. If a charge is mandatory, the customer should see it while still able to say no.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds modest, but it smuggles in a false premise: that every mandatory charge can be known, and meaningfully displayed, before the final checkout screen. Often it cannot—and when it can, mandating the placement achieves little that the checkout total itself doesn't already accomplish.
[C1.2] Start with how these charges actually work. A delivery app's fee legitimately varies with distance, demand, order size, and courier availability. A restaurant's service charge often depends on party size, which may change between reservation and arrival. A ticketing fee can depend on delivery method or seat tier selected mid-flow.
[C1.3] Demanding early disclosure of a charge that is not yet determined forces merchants either to display a guess—which invites its own deception claims—or to flatten pricing into blunt surcharges that punish the customers whose orders were cheap to serve. The rule doesn't produce honesty; it produces worse prices for most people to protect a hypothetical shopper who never reaches the payment screen anyway.
[C1.4] Second, the checkout screen is where the contract actually forms. That is where every consumer protection framework from ancient caveat emptor to modern e-commerce law places the duty to present a full, itemized, assentable price. The customer there sees the total and can walk away with one tap. My opponent calls this an "ambush," but a fee disclosed on the screen where you press "pay" is not an ambush; it is the ordinary moment of offer and acceptance.
[C1.5] Diners who ate the meal and claim they were trapped chose to skip asking about surcharges—a common practice worldwide that no one hides.
[C1.6] Third, the compliance burden falls hardest on small restaurants and independent venues, which lack the engineering teams to rearchitect menus and seat maps. The rule rewards platform incumbents and lawyers, not consumers.
[C1.7] Where a charge is genuinely deceptive or undisclosed at the moment of payment, existing false-advertising and consumer-protection law already reaches it. What the proposition adds is not protection but a new timing mandate—costly, rigid, and aimed at the wrong screen.
