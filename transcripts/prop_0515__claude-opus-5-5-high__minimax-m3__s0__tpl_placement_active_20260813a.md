[P1.1] Learning to read is not like learning to talk. No child picks up the alphabetic code by immersion the way they pick up speech. Writing is a human invention a few thousand years old, and the brain has to be taught, deliberately, that these squiggles map to those sounds. That single fact drives this debate.
[P1.2] If reading must be explicitly taught, the question is whether we guarantee that teaching or leave it to chance. Structured instruction guarantees it. Classroom autonomy over curriculum leaves it to chance.
[P1.3] Here is what chance has looked like. For decades, many classrooms used "balanced literacy" and three-cueing methods, which taught children to guess words from pictures and context instead of decoding them. Those methods spread through teacher choice, not malice. They felt intuitive, and they looked fine for the kids who would have learned anyway.
[P1.4] But they quietly failed the children who needed systematic phonics, and those failures often surfaced years later, when a fourth grader could not read a science textbook. Autonomy did not protect those children. It produced the problem.
[P1.5] Contrast the places that chose structure. Mississippi, long near the bottom nationally, adopted a statewide push for phonics-based instruction, teacher training, and early screening. [P1.6] The common thread is not a magic program. It is a shared, evidence-based sequence every child receives.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds reasonable only because it has been skillfully framed. "Structured instruction" is presented as the cure, autonomy as the disease. Once we accept that frame, the debate is over. I want to dispute it.
[C1.2] First, the PRO case commits a category error. The history of failed reading instruction is not a history of teacher autonomy. Three-cueing and "balanced literacy" did not spread because teachers freely rejected phonics. They spread because they were packaged, marketed, and adopted *as* the structured approach of their era, sold with the same confident certainty that synthetic phonics is sold today.
[C1.3] When that pedagogy failed, the failure was pedagogical, not political. Pulling the lever toward a new mandate will not immunize us against the next fad. It will centralize who gets to inflict it on every classroom.
[C1.4] Second, policy should not be in the business of choosing among competing methods of teaching. Teachers read their own students every day. They see which five-year-old shows signs of dyslexia risk, which English learner needs oral vocabulary work before decoding will land, which child was read to nightly and which arrived holding a book for the first time. A uniform mandate cannot honor those differences.
[C1.5] An autonomous professional can.
[C1.6] Third, the evidence is not as clean as the opening suggested. Mississippi's gains coincided with multiple reforms pursued simultaneously, and England's phonics check did not by itself resolve that country's broader literacy challenges. The real lesson is that *any* serious, sustained reform effort can move the needle, not that mandates are uniquely virtuous.
[C1.7] Finally, equity cuts both ways. A scripted phonics regime that produces technically capable decoders who hate reading has not delivered equity either. A child who can sound out words but cannot comprehend a paragraph about frogs has been failed in a different way.
[C1.8] Policy should empower teachers with evidence, training, and accountability, not with a single mandated method. The PRO side is selling centralization as virtue. The CON side is defending professional judgment, on behalf of every child the next top-down fad will overshoot.
