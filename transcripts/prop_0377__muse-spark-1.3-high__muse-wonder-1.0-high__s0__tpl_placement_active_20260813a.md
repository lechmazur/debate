[P1.1] Picture two 25-year-olds. One becomes an emergency physician after a decade of brutal training. The other inherits a $40 million portfolio because he picked the right grandparents. Only the first pays substantial tax on what makes her advantaged.
[P1.2] That is backwards if we care about equal opportunity.
[P1.3] High annual earnings, even large ones, mostly track something the earner did: years of schooling, long hours, scarce skills, entrepreneurial risk. The advantages they buy are real but limited and must be re-earned each year. Dynastic wealth is different. It arrives unearned, in a lump sum large enough to permanently detach its holder from labor.
[P1.4] It compounds on its own through investments, real estate appreciation, and business ownership.
[P1.5] That capital then buys every gateway to opportunity at once: houses in the best school districts, debt-free elite degrees, unpaid internships in expensive cities, seed money and connections for startups, the safety net to fail and try again. A salaried professional, however well paid, cannot compete with someone whose starting capital earns more while they sleep than a salary pays. Over generations this hardens into a separate class that transmits status by birth, not merit.
[P1.6] Taxing that transfer is also far less damaging than punitively taxing work. The heir did nothing to deserve lower taxes, and the bequest motive weakens little when the estate is already enormous; no billionaire stops building a company because the 50th million left to children will be taxed. We should tax windfall luck at least as heavily as effort, not the reverse.
[P1.7] Large inheritances should therefore face steep rates with minimal loopholes, with revenue used to broaden real opportunity: schools, health, access to capital for those without family fortunes. A society that taxes the surgeon more fiercely than the trust fund is not rewarding merit. It is protecting aristocracy.

[C1.1] That 25-year-old trust-fund heir is the exception sold as the rule. Most inheritances, even large ones, arrive when parents die and children are in their forties or fifties — after schooling, first jobs, networks, and most opportunity gaps are already set. A steep tax at death cannot rewind the childhood that actually shaped those chances.
[C1.2] What shapes them is income repeated through childhood. High-earning parents buy the house in the prized district at six, tutoring at twelve, test prep and debt-free choices at eighteen. They do so across millions of professional households, outbidding others for elite schools and safe neighborhoods. If opportunity is hoarded, the steady high salary is the broader engine, not a portfolio inherited in middle age.
[C1.3] Inherited wealth is also not a windfall from nowhere. It is usually income already taxed, then saved rather than consumed, often by building a business whose value is illiquid until sold. Tax that transfer far more aggressively and you penalize the parent who worked and saved, force sales of family firms and farms, and reward those rich enough to give early, use trusts, move assets, or spend lavishly before death.
[C1.4] That is why steep estate taxes tend to raise little revenue for schools or opportunity programs: the truly dynastic plan around them, while less mobile savers get caught. Fortunes also fragment among heirs, spouses, charities, taxes, and bad decisions. Advantage must still be remade each generation through parenting, education, and earnings; it does not simply compound untouched forever.
[C1.5] Equality of opportunity means open competition and strong public foundations, not confiscating a family's after-tax life project because money left behind is easier to resent than the time, connections, and cultural capital other parents lavish at home. Tax inheritances far beyond work and you tax thrift, invite evasion, and miss the income-driven advantages that actually decide who gets ahead.

[P2.1] The late-inheritance excuse misunderstands how dynasties work. Great fortunes do not wait politely until the heir is fifty. They flow early and continuously — private school at six, debt-free elite college at eighteen, down payment in the expensive zip code at twenty-five, unpaid internship and startup seed money in between. The formal bequest is merely the final settlement of advantages already delivered.
[P2.2] Taxing that transfer reaches the source that funded the whole childhood the other side worries about.
[P2.3] Nor does steady salary rival that power in scale. Millions of professionals do bid up good neighborhoods, and we already tax their salaries every year. But a salary stops when work stops and cannot reproduce itself overnight. Capital does.
[P2.4] A portfolio large enough to earn more in appreciation while its owner sleeps than a surgeon earns in a year buys every gate at once, then buys insulation from failure, then buys influence over the rules themselves. That is a difference in kind, not degree.
[P2.5] The double-tax and family-farm alarm confuses the saver with the recipient. A steep tax on large transfers leaves the builder free to build, spend, and enjoy every dollar in life. It falls on the heir who did nothing, precisely because thrift by one generation cannot morally entitle the next to permanent advantage. And protecting operating businesses is a design detail — high exemptions, payment over years — not a reason to let $40 million portfolios pass lightly.
[P2.6] Finally, that the rich dodge death taxes is an argument to close trusts and valuation games, as the proposition demands, not to surrender to them. Fragmentation among two or three heirs still leaves dynastic chunks when returns compound. If a fortune dissolves through charity, taxes, or bad choices, it has stopped threatening opportunity. Only the enduring, compounding lump does — and that is exactly what should be taxed much more aggressively.

[C2.4] Separating saver from heir is a legal trick, not a family reality. Many parents build and defer consumption to launch children or keep a business intact. Take much more if they save for family than if they spend on themselves, and you subsidize consumption, early gifting by the sophisticated, and capital flight by those who can plan decades ahead.
[C2.5] Closing every trust and valuation route is asserted, not shown. Steeper rates raise the payoff for avoidance, control-preserving charitable vehicles, and moving assets abroad. Installment payments do not save an illiquid firm whose value is premises and equipment, not cash to send the Treasury for a decade.
[C2.6] Tax the residue harder if you like. Do not pretend it reopens childhoods already bought with income, or that dynasties are the broad threat while professional earnings do the daily hoarding.
