[P1.1] A city that lets its small venues die is not neutral. It has made a choice, usually by accident, through a noise complaint, a parking-minimum, or a licensing officer who treats a 150-capacity room like a nail salon. The question is whether cities should recognise what they are actually deciding.
[P1.2] Infrastructure is the word for things whose value flows mostly to people who don't own them. A bridge doesn't profit from the businesses on the other side; a library doesn't capture the earnings of the kid who learned to read there. Small venues are exactly this. The 200-person room is where musicians learn to hold a crowd, where sound engineers and promoters train, where a neighborhood acquires the identity that later shows up in its rents.
[P1.3] The arena tour, the festival, the tourism campaign all draw from a pipeline they never paid for. When a venue closes, the owner loses a business; the city loses a training ground it cannot rebuild by decree.
[P1.4] That is the first reason for special treatment: irreversibility. A noise complaint is fixable with insulation, curfews, or sound limiters. A closure is not. Once the lease ends and the building becomes flats, the license lapses and the network scatters.
[P1.5] Any sane decision rule weighs a reversible harm against an irreversible one differently.
[P1.6] Second, the fixes are cheap and already exist. The agent-of-change principle, adopted in British planning after London lost a large share of its grassroots venues, simply says the newcomer bears the cost of adapting to an existing use. Build condos next to a club, you soundproof the condos. Development proceeds; the venue survives.
[P1.7] Night-mayor offices in Amsterdam and elsewhere mediate between venues and police before a raid becomes a revocation. None of this is subsidy or immunity. It is proportionality.
[P1.8] Cities already grant this kind of weight to churches, historic façades, and parks. The only question left is whether the places where a city's culture is actually made deserve the same consideration as the places where it is merely preserved.

[C1.2] First, the category is unprincipled. Culture is not made only where guitars are plugged in. It is made in immigrant restaurants, barbershops, comedy basements, bookstores, church halls. The moment a city declares one commercial format "infrastructure," every other struggling business has an equal claim — and no principled way to refuse it.
[C1.3] What follows is not cultural policy but lobbying: whoever hires the better consultant gets the carve-out. The bridge and library analogy fails precisely here — cities build bridges by neutral criteria; they would designate venues by political pull.
[C1.4] Second, the costs land on people with no seat at the table. Agent-of-change sounds elegant until you price it: mandatory soundproofing is a tax on new apartments next to venues, in cities where housing scarcity is the actual emergency. Weighting noise complaints against residents tells the night-shift nurse her sleep ranks below someone's Thursday gig. And "special consideration" in policing is the most dangerous ask of all — fire codes, capacity limits, and safety enforcement are exactly where deference kills.
[C1.5] Overcrowded rooms with tolerant inspectors are how tragedies happen.
[C1.6] Third, the irreversibility claim inverts reality. Individual venues close constantly and scenes survive, migrating to cheaper neighborhoods — that migration is how new scenes are born. What special protection actually does is freeze incumbents in expensive districts, subsidizing yesterday's rooms while starving tomorrow's.
[C1.7] If the fixes are genuinely cheap and sensible — clearer noise standards, mediation before revocation, fewer parking minimums — apply them to every business, neutrally. That solves the real problems without inventing a privileged caste. The motion isn't asking for good governance; it's asking for an exemption from it, dressed in the language of culture.
