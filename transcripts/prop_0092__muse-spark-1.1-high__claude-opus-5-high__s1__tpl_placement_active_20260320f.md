[P1.1] City streets are not free. Every foot of curb, every ton on asphalt, every blind corner is a cost someone bears. Right now that someone is not the driver of a six-foot-high, three-ton pickup used to commute alone. Higher fees for oversized vehicles are simply making the price match the footprint.
[P1.2] First, safety. Physics does not negotiate. More mass at higher speed is more force in a crash. A taller front end hits a pedestrian in the chest and head, not the legs.
[P1.3] A longer hood and higher beltline create blind zones big enough to hide a child or a wheelchair. When cities count pedestrian deaths, they find the risk climbs sharply with vehicle height and weight, and the victim is almost never inside the big vehicle.
[P1.4] Second, street use. Road damage rises steeply with weight. A pickup that weighs twice a Civic does multiples more damage. Geometry matters too: a crew-cab long-bed takes 20% more curb than a compact, meaning one oversized SUV in dense parking erases space, sight lines, and bike lane buffer for everyone else.
[P1.5] Flat parking meters turn that extra consumption into a discount.
[P1.6] A tiered fee fixes a broken incentive. Federal rules let automakers classify big SUVs and pickups as light trucks, loosening fuel rules, so they built bigger. Drivers don't pay that cost at purchase. A city curb fee or weight-based registration brings it back to where the cost lands.
[P1.7] This is not a ban. It is pricing we already use for commercial trucks, applied to private vehicles by weight, length, or height, with carve-outs for tradespeople and accessibility needs. The fair default is: if you take more street and impose more danger, you pay more to use it.
