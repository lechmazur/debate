[P1.1] The proposition is not a rejection of BCI innovation, but a demand that we treat direct neural intervention with the seriousness it deserves. Consumer-grade BCIs that write to or stimulate the brain are fundamentally different from read-only neural wearables: they alter the biological substrate of thought, emotion, and behavior, not just measure it. Unregulated consumer sales will expose the public to irreversible, poorly understood harm. Unlike medical devices subject to years of clinical trials, consumer products prioritize speed to market and profit.
[P1.2] Off-the-shelf tDCS kits already marketed for "cognitive enhancement" have been linked to scalp burns, seizures, and unanticipated mood dysregulation in users with no underlying medical need—harms that would never be approved for general use under medical regulatory review. Chronic non-medical neural stimulation can also produce persistent changes to neural circuitry: a user who buys a headset to boost focus may end up with reduced ability to concentrate without the device, a permanent alteration they never meaningfully consented to. Consumer markets also create coercion that medical regulation is explicitly designed to prevent.
[P1.3] If these devices are sold as ordinary products, employers will mandate their use for productivity, schools will require them for test performance, and social pressure will push people to use them to "keep up." Medical use, by contrast, is governed by informed consent and clinical need, not market pressure. We do not sell implantable cardiac devices or deep brain stimulators for recreational use, because the risk of harm and coercion is too high. The same standard must apply to any device that can write directly to the human brain.

[C1.1] The motion asks us to choose between two extremes: either lock this technology behind a medical prescription, or leave it a lawless free-for-all. That is a false choice, and the debate should turn on rejecting it. There is a well-established third option: consumer sale under a strict regulatory tier — safety testing, dosage limits, mandatory disclosures, post-market surveillance — the same model already used for TENS units, over-the-counter hearing aids, and wellness-grade tDCS devices. None of PRO's harms require full medicalization; they require good consumer-safety regulation, which is compatible with keeping these products on the open market.
[C1.2] Consider what "restrict to medical use" actually does. It means a healthy adult who wants a non-invasive, reversible stimulation headset to help with focus, sleep, or anxiety must first acquire a diagnosis, then a prescription, then navigate a system priced and gatekept for disease treatment, not personal wellness. That is not caution — it is paternalism dressed as safety. It also guarantees a worse outcome than PRO admits: people who cannot legally buy a tested, dosage-limited device do not simply stop wanting neural enhancement.
[C1.3] They build their own. The DIY tDCS community already exists, wiring batteries and sponges together with no engineering review, no current limits, no burn protection — precisely the harms PRO cites, but worse, because there was no regulated product absorbing that demand safely.
[C1.4] PRO's own evidence undercuts medicalization: scalp burns and mood dysregulation from off-the-shelf kits are arguments for mandatory safety engineering standards, not for banning consumer sale outright. A defibrillator analogy fails too — DBS is invasive brain surgery; external stimulation headsets are reversible and noninvasive, a difference in kind, not degree.
[C1.5] And the coercion worry — employers, schools — is a labor-law and anti-discrimination problem. We solve workplace drug testing coercion with employment law, not by banning caffeine pills. The same logic applies here: regulate misuse, don't foreclose the market.
