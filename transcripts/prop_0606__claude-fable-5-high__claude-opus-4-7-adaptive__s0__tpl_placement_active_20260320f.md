[P1.1] A patent is a state-granted monopoly, and no state grants monopolies so that its own citizens can die of treatable diseases. When the price of an essential medicine exceeds what a country's people can actually afford, the patent has stopped serving its purpose and started obstructing it. The proposition simply says: at that point, the state should reclaim what it granted, license generic production, and pay the patent holder a reasonable royalty.
[P1.2] Three pillars carry this case.
[P1.3] First, the moral and legal foundation already exists. The TRIPS agreement and the Doha Declaration explicitly recognize compulsory licensing for public health. What is missing is a predictable trigger. Today, invoking it requires a political brawl in which pharmaceutical companies and their home governments threaten trade retaliation.
[P1.4] A defined affordability threshold converts an arbitrary, intimidation-prone process into a transparent rule both sides can plan around.
[P1.5] Second, the threshold itself is the policy's genius, not its flaw. A clear line disciplines pricing before any license is ever issued. When Brazil and Thailand credibly threatened compulsory licenses on HIV antiretrovirals, prices collapsed by enormous margins almost overnight—proof that monopoly prices for essential drugs reflect bargaining power, not cost. A standing threshold makes that leverage permanent: most companies will price under the line voluntarily, and the license becomes the rarely-used backstop.
[P1.6] Third, innovation survives. Patent holders receive royalties under any license. The countries most likely to invoke this—where a hepatitis C cure priced at tens of thousands of dollars meets average incomes of a few thousand—contribute a sliver of global pharmaceutical revenue. Forgoing sales that were never going to happen at monopoly prices destroys no meaningful R&D incentive; it only converts dead-weight loss into saved lives.
[P1.7] The real choice is between rationing by wealth, enforced ad hoc by political muscle, and a rule-based system where affordability is the line. Lives should not depend on which country can withstand trade pressure.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds humane in the abstract and collapses on contact with the actual mechanism it proposes. Three problems make it indefensible as written.
[C1.2] First, the trigger is incoherent. "Whenever prices exceed a defined affordability threshold" treats affordability as a single dial, but affordability is plural: affordable to whom, paid by whom, against which alternative? A breakthrough oncology drug priced at $50,000 is "unaffordable" to almost every individual on Earth, yet routinely funded through insurance pools, tiered pricing, or aid procurement. An automatic threshold flattens those distinctions and licenses generic copying even where a negotiated discount or differential price would have delivered the medicine more cheaply and more reliably.
[C1.3] The rule overshoots the problem it claims to target.
[C1.4] Second, compulsory licensing solves the wrong half of access. Patents are not the binding constraint on most "essential medicines." Cold-chain logistics, diagnostic capacity, trained prescribers, regulatory approval, and procurement financing are. Insulin has been off-patent for a century and is still rationed in poor countries.
[C1.5] A threshold-triggered license does nothing about any of that, while creating the comforting illusion that the access problem is solved—diverting political energy from the harder fixes that actually save lives.
[C1.6] Third, the policy poisons the pipeline precisely where it matters most. PRO waves this away by pointing to poor countries' small revenue share, but a rule "whenever prices exceed a threshold" is not limited to poor countries—it is the entire architecture of the proposition. Middle-income and even wealthy states would face domestic pressure to set the threshold low. Tiered pricing—which today lets companies charge Norway more and Nigeria less—only survives if the high-price markets stay high-price.
[C1.7] Make every country a potential licenser the moment a price stings, and the rational response is one global price, set high enough to hedge expropriation risk. The poor lose the discount they currently get.
[C1.8] The choice is not rule versus muscle. It is a blunt automatic rule versus targeted tools—tiered pricing, pooled procurement, targeted licensing—that actually fit the disease.
