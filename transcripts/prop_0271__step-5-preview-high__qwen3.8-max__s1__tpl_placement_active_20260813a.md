[P1.1] The fight over children's attention is being lost in the settings menu. Parents today can already restrict screen time or block specific apps, but the moment a child opens a video feed, a social platform, or a streaming service, an algorithm takes the wheel. Recommendation engines decide what comes next, and they are engineered for one goal: maximum engagement. Parents are left with blunt instruments while the child is pulled through a tunnel of autoplay videos, infinite scrolls, and personalized content streams designed by specialists in behavioral psychology.
[P1.2] The core issue is a structural power imbalance. App makers possess the technical sophistication to build meaningful parental overrides—hard stops on algorithmic feeds, delays before recommended content, or age-gated recommendation modes—but they face commercial incentives to keep those features weak. Engagement metrics drive revenue. When a child stays online for an extra hour because the algorithm found the perfect next video, the platform profits.
[P1.3] The parent, meanwhile, is handed a checkbox labeled "restricted mode" that does little to stop the momentum of a recommendation engine.
[P1.4] This is not an argument against technology itself. It is an argument about who holds the steering wheel during childhood. Children lack the cognitive maturity to recognize when they are being manipulated by variable reward schedules and infinite feeds designed to erode natural stopping cues. Parents, not product teams, should hold default authority to delay or block those features until a child is old enough to understand the tradeoff.
[P1.5] The inconvenience to platforms is trivial compared to the developmental stakes: sleep, attention spans, exposure to harmful content, and family conflict.
[P1.6] The motion asks for a specific shift: stronger, enforceable parental power over algorithmic recommendations. That shift is justified because current design treats children as engagement units rather than developing people. Give parents the ability to hit pause on the algorithm, and you restore a boundary that technology has quietly erased.

[C1.1] The proposition assumes every recommendation feed is a trap and every parental veto makes children safer. That is false. Algorithms are not only engagement accelerators; they are also curation systems that steer children away from the open web’s worst corners and toward age-appropriate lessons, tutorials, and communities. A child who finishes a math video may be guided to another explanation at the right level; a child whose recommendations are blocked may be pushed into manual search, external browsers, or knock-off apps with weaker safeguards.
[C1.2] The real weakness in the PRO case is its control fantasy. Giving parents sweeping new power to block or delay recommendation engines does not create careful mediation; it creates blunt switches that vary wildly with each parent’s time, technical skill, and patience. Highly technical households may configure safe alternatives. Many families will not.
[C1.3] Children in those homes will either face broken, confusing services or migrate to spaces with fewer protections. The policy would therefore increase inequality in child safety rather than reduce it.
[C1.4] App makers are also the actors able to distinguish dangerous recommendation patterns from beneficial ones. They can rank content, apply age bands, suppress harmful loops, and provide supervised accounts. A broad parental veto over “algorithmic recommendations” treats a reading app’s next-book suggestion like an endless doomscroll. It invites designers to build crude off-ramps instead of better guardrails, and it may force invasive age checks or data collection to enforce age-gated modes.
[C1.5] Parents deserve transparent controls and strong defaults. But the motion asks for more power to shut down recommendation systems than companies now allow. The decisive question is not whether parents want control, but whether this added veto improves child welfare. It does not.
[C1.6] That is not protection; it is a crude kill-switch that can make children’s digital spaces less educational, less safe, and less equitable. Vote CON.
