[P1.1] When a tenant can pay the rent, meet every screening standard a landlord sets, and still be turned away solely because part of the payment comes from a housing voucher or other lawful public assistance, the rejection is not about risk. It is about stigma. That practice should be prohibited.
[P1.2] Public assistance exists to make housing affordable for people who would otherwise be shut out. A voucher does not make a tenant less qualified; it often makes the rent more reliable, because the public share is typically paid directly and on time. The tenant remains responsible for their portion, the lease, and the unit. If income is adequate once the subsidy is counted, rental history is clean, and every other lawful criterion is met, source of payment is a label, not a qualification.
[P1.3] Landlords keep every legitimate tool. They may reject for insufficient income even after assistance, poor credit, prior evictions, property damage, or occupancy rules. The prohibition bars only one thing: using the lawful source of funds as the sole disqualifier. It is not a mandate to accept unqualified tenants or to waive inspections and lease terms.
[P1.4] The harm is concentrated. Families, disabled people, and seniors who rely on assistance are steered toward a shrinking set of willing landlords, often in higher-poverty areas. A blanket refusal does not select better tenants; it selects people who do not need help. That hardens segregation and wastes the subsidy already funded by the public.
[P1.5] Administrative friction is manageable and is no justification for categorical exclusion. Consider the applicant who works, pays bills, and has solid references, yet hears "we don't take vouchers" before any real screening. That refusal protects a preference, not the property. Housing decisions should turn on whether a tenant can meet the lease, not on whether lawful assistance helped them do so.

[C1.1] A voucher is not the same as cash rent. It is a conditional subsidy that requires a landlord to enter a contract with a housing agency, accept agency inspections, keep rent within agency standards, comply with recertification timelines, and risk payment delay or recapture. A tenant is not “otherwise qualified” if their payment method imposes those costs; those costs are part of the application. The proposition would force owners to take the state as a third party, not merely a tenant.
[C1.2] That compulsion is different from banning discrimination. Race, disability, or family status do not rewrite an owner’s contractual obligations; source of funds can. A private landlord may legitimately decline to manage government program compliance, especially a small landlord with one unit and no staff. Prohibiting that refusal is compelled contracting and a serious intrusion on property rights.
[C1.3] The reliability claim is one-sided. The public share may be paid directly, but it can be delayed at initial lease-up, suspended when household income changes, cut after inspection failure, or clawed back. The tenant portion can also shift with recertification. A landlord rationally worries about procedural default, not poverty.
[C1.4] If a tenant loses eligibility, the landlord inherits an underfunded lease or an eviction.
[C1.5] Mandates also reduce supply. An owner who cannot bear inspection and payment risk will avoid voucher-eligible units or leave them vacant rather than take legal risk. That shrinks the very stock voucher holders need. Voluntary participation plus incentives—quicker inspections, damage funds, reliable direct deposit—would do more for access than making refusal illegal.
[C1.6] Finally, “solely because” is unworkable. Once refusal is forbidden, landlords face pretext claims whenever they cite credit or income near the margin. The law would punish owners for not wanting a government contract, not for actual tenant misconduct. That violates freedom to choose one’s counterparty and misdiagnoses bureaucratic friction as bigotry.
