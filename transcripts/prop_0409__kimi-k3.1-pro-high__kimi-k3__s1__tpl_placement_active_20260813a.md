[P1.1] The question is not whether a park may contain a café, a concert, or a boat rental. It is whether access to the park itself—the lawn, the sand, the waterfront view—remains a public right or becomes a product available only to customers. Broadly free access is compatible with limited, temporary paid activities. What it rules out is making commercial enclosure the normal fate of our best shared places.
[P1.2] Public spaces are civic infrastructure, not merely under-monetized real estate. Their value lies partly in being open to people who did not plan, book, or budget for the visit. A teenager can sit after school, a worker can eat lunch outdoors, and a family can spend a day at the beach without first proving purchasing power. These uses look unprofitable at a ticket booth, but they support health, social connection, and ordinary freedom in cities where private space is unequally distributed.
[P1.3] Charging at the gate does not simply raise revenue; it decides whose presence is worth accommodating.
[P1.4] Enclosure also destroys value even for those who can pay. A waterfront chopped into premium decks, branded viewing areas, and controlled entrances becomes less legible, less spontaneous, and less capable of hosting a common civic life. The paying customer buys an experience; everyone else loses a place. That loss is easily omitted when private revenue is counted but public access is treated as worthless.
[P1.5] The likely objection is maintenance: without operators and fees, spaces deteriorate. But private management does not require private exclusion. Cities can fund upkeep through general taxation, concessions, philanthropy, and tightly bounded commercial leases, while retaining public control over access. If overcrowding or ecological damage is the problem, capacity limits, reservations, restoration, and transport improvements address it directly.
[P1.6] Turning scarcity into a luxury market merely allocates the damage by wealth.
[P1.7] The default should therefore be simple: commercial services may serve visitors, but they must not own the conditions of visiting. A park that everyone may enter without being a customer is not a failed business. It is a successful public institution.

[C1.1] "Free" describes the ticket booth, not the experience. The question is which model delivers clean, safe, beautiful parks and waterfronts. Judged by outcomes, not labels, paid and privately managed spaces win; the "broadly free" default is why so many public spaces decay.
[C1.2] Start with stewardship. Tax-funded spaces are first for cuts and last for investment: broken fountains, locked restrooms, dead lawns. When operators earn revenue from visitors, incentives flip—quality attracts customers. Bryant Park was a place commuters hurried past; under private management funded by commercial revenue, it became one of America's most loved parks, and the Central Park Conservancy now covers most of that park's budget.
[C1.3] Revenue attached to a space maintains it; revenue through city hall evaporates.
[C1.4] Notice what PRO concedes: cafés, concerts, boat rentals, commercial leases. Each is a small enclosure. The motion condemns "increasing" them—yet those increments are precisely what funds upkeep their taxation never guarantees.
[C1.5] Second, scarcity. The world's best places are being loved to death—eroded trails, trampled dunes, damaged reefs. PRO's remedy is "capacity limits, reservations, restoration." But a reservation is enclosure; a capacity limit is a gate.
[C1.6] The choice is whether access is allocated by a price that funds protection or a queue that funds nothing. Galápagos entry fees, Havasupai permits, and Venice's day-tripper charge all limit pressure and fund conservation. Free access doesn't protect these places; it consumes them.
[C1.7] Third, equity runs the other way. "Free" access flows to those who live nearby, own cars, and have flexible schedules—often affluent residents whose property values the public inflates—while local taxpayers subsidize tourists. Modest fees paired with resident discounts, free hours, and school programs are progressive tools; open gates with empty budgets are not.
[C1.8] PRO imagines a park everyone may enter and no one is paid to maintain. We should prefer spaces someone is responsible for keeping excellent. The true enclosure is not a ticket booth; it is a waterfront too degraded and unsafe to use.
