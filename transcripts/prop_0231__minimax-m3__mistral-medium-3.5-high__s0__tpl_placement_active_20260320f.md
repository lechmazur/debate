[C1.1] Supervised consumption sites save lives in a room but not in the city. Vancouver’s Insite has reversed hundreds of overdoses since 2003, but the city’s overdose deaths have still risen to record levels year after year. The explanation is simple: the vast majority of overdoses happen outside the site, often when it is closed. If users only sometimes choose to inject there, the facility cannot reverse a population-level crisis.
[C1.2] Public disorder is similarly persistent. In Sydney, the injecting centre reduced needle litter by nearly a third within 500 meters, but surrounding areas saw increases. In Frankfurt and Barcelona, residents report that loitering and dealing have simply moved to nearby streets. Disorder is not solved; it is displaced, often to communities with fewer resources.
[C1.3] Most damningly, SCS institutionalize addiction. Proponents argue they are gateways to treatment, but the data shows most clients use them for years without entering detox. In many European cities, fewer than 10% of users enter treatment annually. By providing a sanitized space for consumption, these sites remove the immediate consequences of drug use and reduce the incentive to seek help.
[C1.4] This is not harm reduction; it is harm management.
[C1.5] The cost is also prohibitive. A single site can cost over a million dollars per year to operate, funds that could instead pay for treatment beds, housing, or prevention. In Latin America, the model is a particularly poor fit. Supervised injection sites are designed for opioid injection, but in many cities there, the dominant drugs are crack and cocaine, which are often smoked or inhaled, not injected.
[C1.6] Cities should aim to end addiction, not manage it. The resources spent on SCS would be better used scaling up treatment, harm reduction that does not enable use, and enforcement against traffickers. Permitting these sites is a concession that we have given up on recovery. It is a policy of managed decline, not progress.
