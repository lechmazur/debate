[P1.1] When a blockbuster opens on a flood of IMAX, Dolby, and other premium large‑format auditoriums, the ordinary moviegoer—someone who cannot or will not pay a twenty‑dollar surcharge—faces a shrinking menu of affordable showtimes. The proposition is simple: that dominance must be curtailed, because it threatens cinema’s role as a broadly accessible public experience.
[P1.2] First, access is the lifeblood of the theatrical market. Standard‑price screens provide the low‑cost entry point that draws families, students, and lower‑income patrons. When studios and exhibitors allocate the majority of prime slots to premium formats, the regular‑price schedule collapses to off‑peak hours or disappears entirely. The result is a de‑facto two‑tier system where only those who can afford the surcharge enjoy the most convenient times, while everyone else is forced to wait, travel farther, or stay home.
[P1.3] This is not a marginal inconvenience; it is a structural barrier that erodes the cultural commons cinema has traditionally offered.
[P1.4] Second, the economics of volume trump the allure of per‑ticket premiums. Premium screens generate higher revenue per seat, but they also occupy a disproportionate share of auditorium capacity. Industry observations show that when a blockbuster is over‑programmed on large‑format venues, overall attendance on regular screens drops sharply, and total box‑office receipts can fall because the higher price deters a large segment of the audience. Theaters that rely on steady foot traffic from standard showings risk long‑term viability if they cede those slots to a niche that cannot sustain volume.
[P1.5] Third, the social equity dimension cannot be ignored. Cinema is one of the few shared spaces where people of different backgrounds experience the same story at the same time. Allowing premium screens to dominate scheduling fragments that shared experience, turning movies into a luxury good rather than a communal event.
[P1.6] Limiting the share of premium large‑format screenings preserves affordable options, sustains overall attendance, and protects cinema’s democratic cultural function. That is the core of the case we will defend.
