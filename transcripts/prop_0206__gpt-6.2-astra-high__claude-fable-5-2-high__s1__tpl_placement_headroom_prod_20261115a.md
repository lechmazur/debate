[P1.1] A retirement age that never changes is not a neutral policy. If people live longer after reaching it, each new generation can expect more years of pension than the last. Keeping that age fixed and raising payroll taxes makes a choice: the pension covers all the extra years, and the bill goes to people earning wages.
[P1.2] Share the gain instead. Tie the normal retirement age to life expectancy near retirement, not at birth, and give workers ample notice of changes. Suppose a cohort can expect roughly two more years of life than its predecessors. Moving the normal age up by one year would still leave that cohort roughly one more year of retirement.
[P1.3] The system pays benefits for less time than under a frozen age, and people who stay employed contribute for longer. That works without assuming every extra year of life is a year fit for work.
[P1.4] A payroll tax increase collects revenue, but it does nothing to change the number of pension years that revenue must cover. Calling it an employer contribution does not make it free: some of the cost can come back to workers in lower wages. A young worker paying rent has as much claim to consideration as a healthy professional who would prefer not to postpone retirement.
[P1.5] The hard case is the worker whose body gives out early, or who started arduous work as a teenager. Protect that worker directly with accessible disability benefits and an earlier pension for long contribution histories. Use additional revenue where those protections require it. Unequal capacity is a reason to distinguish between workers, not to tax every paycheck more so that even those best able to work longer need not do so.
