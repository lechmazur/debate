[P1.1] A memorial is never just stone and glass; it is the state's answer to the question "who are we now?" After civil conflict, the gravest danger is not forgetting but remembering in hostile silos. Fully separate ethnic narratives freeze the war's categories: they teach the next generation that the violence was a clash of tribes, that only our side suffered, and that justice means vindicating our martyrs. Public commemoration then becomes recruitment.
[P1.2] A shared civic account breaks that cycle. It names atrocities as crimes against civilians and citizenship, condemns violations by any armed actor, and honors victims irrespective of ethnicity. It does not erase private grief or ban communal mourning; it insists that state-sponsored memory apply one standard: every citizen's life is equally grievable.
[P1.3] The stakes are concrete. Post-conflict societies are starved of trust. When official memorials are partitioned, each group reads the other's monument as denial, even glorification. Separate commemorations can keep grief tied to mobilisation, as where rival murals and martyr narratives in divided cities continue to mark territory.
[P1.4] A shared civic memorial lowers that threat. It establishes a baseline of facts and values: no community's dead are invisible, no perpetrator is shielded, and the conflict's central lesson is the collapse of institutions that should have protected everyone. That turns memory from a weapon into a warning.
[P1.5] The defence of separate narratives confuses recognition with official endorsement. Communities may keep their own rituals and histories; the state should not consecrate rival nationalisms. Preserving fully separate competing ethnic narratives in public projects uses public authority to certify each side's self-justifying story, locking politics into a permanent tribunal of victimhood. A shared civic account is harder because it demands acknowledgment across lines.
[P1.6] But only that foundation can make commemoration an instrument of coexistence rather than a rehearsal for the next war.
