[P1.1] Gene-editing therapies are not incremental treatments; they are one-time, lifelong cures for devastating conditions like sickle cell disease and inherited blindness. Yet under unchecked patent monopolies, pharmaceutical companies routinely price these breakthroughs in the millions of dollars per patient. At that scale, even wealthy health systems must ration care, while developing nations—where the vast majority of patients with conditions like sickle cell actually live—are locked out entirely. A cure priced out of reach is not a medical triumph; it is a rationing crisis by design.
[P1.2] Requiring a public-interest licensing pathway breaks this deadlock. When a manufacturer refuses to offer an essential gene therapy at a sustainable price, the government can compel a license, authorizing qualified regional manufacturers or academic medical centers to produce the treatment in exchange for fair royalties.
[P1.3] Crucially, this pathway works primarily through leverage: when drugmakers know a state has a credible legal mechanism to bypass price-gouging, they negotiate affordable, volume-based pricing up front.
[P1.4] The opposition will claim that compelling access kills innovation or that gene therapies are too complex to copy. Both excuses fail. First, the foundational science behind gene editing was heavily financed by public grants and universities. Taxpayers should not pay twice—first to fund the discovery, and again to bankrupt public budgets for the product.
[P1.5] Moreover, licensing is not confiscation; paying guaranteed royalties across a vastly expanded patient pool still rewards R&D without relying on extortionate markups. Second, biological complexity is the exact reason we need a dedicated modern pathway rather than outdated rules built for chemical pills. By compelling access to not just patents, but regulatory data and viral vector protocols, countries can empower capable hospital networks and regional biologics hubs to manufacture cures locally.
[P1.6] When science can rewrite a fatal genetic diagnosis, artificial monopolies must not stand in the way.

[C1.1] Compulsory licensing is a seductive shortcut, but the motion mistakes the central bottleneck. Gene-editing therapies are not chemical pills that can be reverse-engineered in a generic factory. They are autologous cell products, viral-vector biologics, and complex clinical procedures. A license to patents does not hand over the validated manufacturing process, the quality systems, the trained staff, or the hospital capacity to deliver them.
[C1.2] A government can compel a license and still have no affordable therapy: it holds a legal right and an empty clean room.
[C1.3] The proposition’s leverage story also cuts the other way. If countries require a public-interest pathway with politically determined “affordable” prices, companies will not reliably respond by cutting global prices. For small patient populations, the rational response may be to delay launch, limit supply, or avoid those markets altogether—especially when the pathway threatens their pricing model everywhere else. And once “essential” and “affordable” are defined by budget officials, the category expands.
[C1.4] That is not access; it is a standoff.
[C1.5] Worse, the motion treats today’s gene therapies as the only stakes. The real prize is the next generation of cures for cancers, metabolic disorders, and rare diseases. Those depend on investors accepting enormous scientific and financial risk. A compulsory pathway that turns every successful therapy into a public bargaining chip weakens that bargain.
[C1.6] Public funding of basic science does not erase the cost of translational trials, manufacturing scale-up, and long-term safety monitoring. Royalties on copied products rarely replace that.
[C1.7] Better tools exist: voluntary licenses, tiered pricing, advance purchase commitments, public manufacturing investment, and conditions attached to public funding. They target affordability without pretending patents are the whole disease. The motion offers a vivid slogan, but it cannot manufacture the cure—and it risks fewer cures for the future.
