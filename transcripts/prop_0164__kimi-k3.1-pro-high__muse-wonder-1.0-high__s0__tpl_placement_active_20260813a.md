[P1.1] A public research budget is not a prize for producing the most surprising papers. It is an investment in knowledge that doctors, engineers, policymakers, and other scientists can safely build upon. A discovery that cannot be reproduced is not an asset; it is a liability whose cost is passed to everyone who trusts it.
[P1.2] The case for a reserved share begins with a structural market failure. Novel results bring publications, prestige, and further grants. Replication usually brings little credit, even when it prevents an entire field from chasing a false lead. Researchers therefore rationally underproduce it, while funders repeatedly pay for the downstream consequences: failed follow-on studies, misleading evidence reviews, and wasted laboratory time.
[P1.3] Leaving replication to compete case by case against exciting new proposals preserves exactly the bias that creates the problem. A fixed share is the credible correction.
[P1.4] This is not spending money to avoid discovery. It is protecting discovery from becoming an expensive accumulation of unverified claims. Consider a striking preliminary finding that attracts years of related research. One well-designed early replication can either strengthen the foundation for that work or reveal that the foundation is sand.
[P1.5] In both outcomes, the public gains. Even failed replications can identify fragile methods, hidden boundary conditions, or measurements that need improvement.
[P1.6] The opportunity cost is real: some novel projects will go unfunded. [C1.1] A fixed replication quota mistakes an accounting rule for a scientific judgment. The value of checking a result depends on the claim, the field, and the moment: how influential it is, how fragile its methods look, whether rivals are already testing it, and whether anyone will act on it. That value rises and falls; a share reserved in advance does not. In quiet periods the quota pays for easy, low-value reruns to spend the pot.
[C1.2] In urgent ones it can cap the response. Merit-based funding can surge where doubt matters; a percentage cannot tell a landmark finding from an inconsequential one.
[C1.3] That rigidity carries a distinctive opportunity cost. Public funders are often the only backers for the unproven first step — a new instrument, a neglected mechanism, an untested theory — with no near-term payoff. Once a result points toward a drug, product, or policy, firms, charities, and rival labs have reasons to rerun or build on it, which checks it in practice. Cut the novel budget by a fixed slice every year and science becomes safer and more incremental, with fewer major discoveries left worth verifying.
[C1.4] Replication also does not reliably buy certainty. If a second study disagrees with the first, science has not learned which is wrong: samples, settings, reagents, or skill may explain the gap, inviting a third and fourth study. The public pays for an unresolved argument. Stronger original grants — adequate samples, preregistered analysis, shared materials, and multi-site validation for major claims — prevent many errors before a literature forms around them, at lower cost than cleaning up afterward.
[C1.5] The alternative is not indifference to false findings. Fund replications when they beat novel proposals on expected value, target claims already guiding treatment or spending, and require originals to be verifiable by design. That treats replication as a scientific priority, not a budgetary entitlement, and directs scarce public money in proportion to need rather than quota.

[P2.1] The opposition treats a reserved share as a command to fund repetitions regardless of circumstances. It is not. It is a protected budget floor, not a ceiling: expert panels can still rank influential, fragile, decision-relevant claims, and funders can support further replication or urgent novel work outside it. What the reserve removes is the recurring temptation to sacrifice verification whenever something more exciting appears.
[P2.2] The “quiet period” objection assumes away the backlog created by the very incentives CON ignores: consequential findings accumulate faster than independent checks.
[P2.3] Nor does building on a result reliably check it. A team can extend a mistaken mechanism, use the same vulnerable assay, or inherit the same selection bias while producing apparently impressive progress. Commercial follow-up is especially uneven: it concentrates where payoff is near, not necessarily where public evidence needs independent scrutiny. Public funders’ unique support for risky first steps is a reason to keep funding novelty—not a reason to leave the reliability of the resulting public knowledge to whoever happens to have a private interest.
[P2.4] CON is right that a disagreeing replication does not instantly identify which study is wrong. But that is an argument against expecting certainty from any single study, including the original. Disagreement is itself useful information: it can expose sensitivity to samples, settings, reagents, or skill before those conditions are mistaken for settled knowledge. Better originals and independent replication are complements.
[P2.5] Preregistration and data sharing make checks more informative; multi-site validation simply demonstrates why independent testing belongs inside the funding design.
[P2.6] Finally, “fund replication whenever it beats novelty on expected value” sounds neutral but recreates the structural failure. Novel proposals offer visible upside; replication’s gains often appear as disasters avoided, portfolios redirected, and confidence justified—benefits easily discounted in head-to-head competition. The choice is therefore not flexible wisdom versus bureaucratic repetition. It is dependable capacity to audit consequential public knowledge versus a standing promise to audit only when novelty leaves room.

[C2.1] The floor defense renames rigidity without removing it. If the reserve must be spent, panels only choose the best available replication; they never ask whether it beats the novel project displaced. If unspent money can return to general funding, the reserve is not the credible commitment promised, just discretionary funding with a label. Either it binds when value is low, or it fails to protect when protection is supposedly essential.
[C2.2] A backlog of unchecked papers does not prove a backlog worth rerunning at a constant rate. Some claims attract scrutiny because other labs must make them work; others stay unchecked because they are obscure, superseded, or unlikely to guide action. Influence and fragility do not arrive on a budget schedule, yet PRO needs high-value targets to match a preset percentage across fields and years.
[C2.3] Building on a result is an imperfect check, but replication is imperfect too. Extension has one advantage: failure matters to the team relying on it, so scrutiny intensifies as stakes rise. Where private follow-up is absent yet a claim guides treatment or spending, targeted public funding is justified. Uneven private checking supports filling selected gaps, not confiscating a fixed slice from areas already being tested.
[C2.4] Disagreement can be informative, but information is not worth any price. A non-replication may launch costly disputes over samples, reagents, and skill, with no verdict. Making originals adequately powered, transparent, and—for major claims—multi-site from the start attacks error before follow-on work multiplies it. Calling prevention and replication complements evades the trade-off: rerunning avoidably weak studies consumes money that could design stronger ones.
[C2.5] Protecting replication from head-to-head comparison does not correct biased valuation; it abolishes valuation at the margin. Funders can use strategic calls and explicit criteria for avoided harm without guaranteeing income to one study type. Dependable auditing funds the check whose value is high now, rather than reserving payment before the target is known.

[P4.1] A1: The motion does not require one universal percentage for every field and year. “Meaningful” means large enough to sustain independent checks of consequential claims, not a token fund; “fixed” means protected when budgets are allocated, not eternally immutable. Funders can set and periodically review different shares using the same evidence about backlogs, influence, and research volume that CON’s strategic calls would require. Demanding a number before accepting the principle is like rejecting quality-control budgeting because its exact share varies across laboratories.
[P4.2] A2: Reserve the money, impose scientific quality thresholds, and permit worthwhile multi-year studies or carry-forward within the replication program; unused money does not revert to novelty. Panels therefore need not fund a weak rerun merely to empty an annual pot. When the reserve is spent, its justification is portfolio-wide protection, not a pretence that every replication individually outranks every displaced discovery. The floor binds against novelty raids, while targeting and carry-forward discipline bind against low-value spending.
[P4.3] This answers the alleged dilemma: protection concerns who controls the budget, not whether panels may exercise judgment within it. CON’s own “strategic calls and explicit criteria” supply the targeting machinery. What they lack is protection against the predictable next cycle, when visible breakthroughs again outbid benefits such as avoided waste and redirected research portfolios.
[P4.4] Nor does reserving a share “abolish valuation.” It changes the comparison to the correct level. A modest novel-grant reduction is distributed across many uncertain proposals; the reserve creates dependable capacity to test claims on which numerous projects, treatments, or spending decisions may depend. Prevention requirements can still improve those original proposals—they are not an alternative budget that makes independent scrutiny unnecessary.
[P4.5] CON’s strongest risk, occasional weak replication, is manageable through selection and carry-forward. CON’s alternative leaves the structural failure intact: replication receives money only after novelty’s claims on it are satisfied. Public funders should guarantee not merely exciting beginnings, but a continuing capacity to discover whether publicly supported knowledge deserves the confidence placed in it.

[C4.1] A1: Those tools can select targets inside a floor; selection was never the dispute. The dispute is whether they should operate only after a guaranteed transfer from novelty. Prestige bias can be restrained without that transfer: dedicated calls judged on decision relevance, explicit scoring for avoided harm, and public reporting of replication outcomes. That fixes how value is judged instead of exempting one study type from judgment — and prestige would still operate inside a protected pot, steering money toward fashionable targets and teams.
[C4.2] A2: No single replication must ceremonially beat one named novel grant. But the reserve as a bundle must beat the novel bundle it displaces at the margin. Portfolio benefits are not free-floating; they are the sum of individual checks that detect or deter error. Novel portfolios also compound, through findings, instruments, and questions later work depends on.
[C4.3] If replication's portfolio case is strong in a field and year, it can win a larger allocation then. Fixing it in advance assumes the conclusion before targets are known.
[C4.4] Those answers expose the central instability. The reserve is now described as varying by field, periodically reviewed, and carried forward rather than spent on weak work. The more adjustable it becomes, the closer it is to needs-based strategic funding without a fixed share. The more fixed it remains, the worse the costs: public money parked while a time-sensitive novel proposal lapses, or spent to protect next year's allocation.
[C4.5] Carry-forward does not cure low-value spending; it turns it into idle funds. Portfolio language does not abolish opportunity cost; it obscures the discoveries whose absence also impoverishes future research. The responsive alternative remains stronger: strengthen originals, fund replications aggressively when a consequential claim is fragile or load-bearing, and let the share rise or fall with demonstrated need. That protects truth without making verification a permanent tax on discovery.

[C5.1] The decisive choice is not verification versus novelty. It is whether funders must commit money to replication before knowing which claims need checking, at what scale, in which field, and at what cost to discovery.
[C5.2] That advance commitment is what the motion requires, and it is what the defense cannot sustain. Once the share may vary by field, be revised, and sit carried forward rather than fund the work available, it is no longer a meaningful fixed protection; it is strategic, needs-based funding under another name. Keep it fixed, and the opposite failure returns: money is parked while urgent novel proposals lapse, or panels spend it on the best available rerun rather than the best available science.
[C5.3] Targeted calls, credit for avoided harm, and stronger original designs do not make verification optional. They make its scale follow evidence of need. That matters because both unchecked error and foregone discovery compound. A false foundation wastes future work, but so does the missing instrument, theory, or first finding that would have made future work possible.
[C5.4] Public funders should be free to expand replication sharply when consequential doubt appears. They should not tax every future discovery in advance to guarantee it.
