[P1.1] No one books a vacation to get ambushed at the last step. Right now, a user can scroll for an hour, find a mountain cabin advertised at $110 a night, click request to book, and only then discover the $170 non-refundable cleaning fee, $60 mandatory resort charge, and a rule banning even adult guests after 7pm. This is not an oversight—it is a deliberate dark pattern. Platforms hide these details because they know by the time a user reaches checkout, they have already invested time, narrowed down options, and committed mentally to the trip.
[P1.2] Most will reluctantly overpay rather than restart their entire search. This extracts extra revenue by exploiting decision fatigue, not by delivering better value. Opponents will claim this rule burdens hosts or clogs listing pages. That argument collapses under even light scrutiny.
[P1.3] We do not require posting the entire 3-page house manual up front, only hard dealbreakers: non-negotiable mandatory fees, and high-stakes rules that would immediately disqualify the property for most searchers. Everything else can stay behind the booking click. This is basic honest commerce, not heavy regulation. Grocery stores do not hide half the price of milk until you reach the register.
[P1.4] Car rental companies are already required to show all mandatory fees before you select a vehicle. There is no good reason vacation rentals should get a special exception to lie by omission. Honest hosts win here too. Right now, transparent hosts regularly lose bookings to competitors who advertise artificially low nightly rates then hide enormous fees at checkout.
[P1.5] This rule stops that race to the bottom. The only parties harmed are the small number of hosts and platforms who profit from tricking their own customers. Everyone else gets a fairer, faster, less frustrating market.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds intuitive until you examine what it actually demands. Then it falls apart on three distinct levels: it solves a problem the market is already fixing, it creates new harms its sponsors haven't accounted for, and the phrase "house rules" makes the mandate practically unworkable.
[C1.2] Start with the market. Major platforms have moved substantially toward upfront fee disclosure under competitive and regulatory pressure precisely because users prefer it. When a platform loses bookings because searchers feel deceived at checkout, it has a direct financial incentive to fix the problem without a mandate. The trend toward total-price display is already underway.
[C1.3] Legislating the moment of disclosure doesn't accelerate transparency—it freezes one particular design choice into law, blocking the better solutions platforms are currently developing.
[C1.4] Now consider the harm the proposition ignores. Search results are a compression problem. Users are already processing dozens of signals—location, photos, ratings, price range, amenities. Mandatory pre-click disclosure of fees and rules doesn't just add information; it adds noise at the wrong moment.
[C1.5] A user comparing twenty listings doesn't want twenty cleaning-fee disclosures and twenty rule sets in the search view. They want a shortlist, then detail. The proposition mistakes information volume for informed decision-making.
[C1.6] Most critically, "house rules" is a category the proposition cannot define precisely. Is a no-smoking rule a dealbreaker that must appear before the click? What about check-in windows, noise curfews, restrictions on visitors? Every host's rules differ.
[C1.7] Any regulation that requires "house rules" to be shown pre-click either mandates displaying everything—creating unusable listings—or requires regulators to specify which rules qualify, which is an ongoing, expensive bureaucratic exercise with no natural stopping point.
[C1.8] The PRO case rests on a real frustration but points to the wrong solution. Requiring total price display by default addresses the genuine problem cleanly. The proposition as written is an overreach that trades one inconvenience for a different, less visible one.
