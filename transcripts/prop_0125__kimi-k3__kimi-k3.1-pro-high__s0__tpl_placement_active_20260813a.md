[P1.1] Nearly a century ago, private utilities looked at rural America and delivered the same verdict carriers deliver today: not commercially viable. Too few customers, too many miles of wire, no business case. Had government accepted that spreadsheet logic, farms would have stayed dark for decades.
[P1.2] Instead, rural electrification turned the lights on — and agricultural productivity exploded. Broadband is the electricity of this century, and the case against subsidizing it is the same argument in newer clothes.
[P1.3] That history exposes the central flaw in the opposing position: "not commercially viable" is not a rebuttal of the motion — it is the motion's operating condition. Markets price only what carriers can capture: subscriber fees. They cannot capture the stroke patient in a county with no neurologist who gets a telehealth consult, the ranch kid who takes advanced courses online, the farmer whose soil sensors cut fertilizer and water costs, the volunteer fire department whose dispatch works. When social returns dwarf private returns, private underinvestment is not an outcome to respect; it is a failure to correct.
[P1.4] That is textbook economics, not sentiment.
[P1.5] This is also about whether rural communities survive. Every school that cannot stream, every business that cannot sell online, every young family that leaves for a connected town deepens the hollowing-out of rural regions. Keeping a county connected costs a fraction of what decline costs — lost tax base, concentrated poverty, emergency services stretched across dying towns.
[P1.6] Nor does subsidy mean waste. Competitive grants, build-out milestones, and clawback provisions discipline spending. And we should not wait on satellite promises to rescue a business model that has failed on the ground; low-orbit service remains costlier and more capacity-limited than terrestrial networks where people actually live and work. The real choice is not subsidy versus efficiency; it is connection versus abandonment.
[P1.7] Carriers measure revenue per mile. This motion asks what a community is worth. Governments answer correctly when they build the on-ramp and let rural America drive.

[C1.1] Rural connectivity is valuable; a standing obligation to subsidize commercially unviable construction is not. The motion requires public spending precisely where revenues cannot cover costs, yet demands no showing that social benefits exceed total costs or that subsidy is the least expensive way to deliver service. That is not correcting market failure. It is assuming market failure whenever a carrier declines to build.
[C1.2] Rural electrification proves that some infrastructure deserves public support, not that every broadband project does. Electricity faced a different technological environment; broadband can be delivered through fiber, fixed wireless, and satellite, with costs and performance varying by terrain and density. For a handful of homes beyond miles of road, avoiding new cable may matter more than maximizing theoretical capacity. Dismissing satellite because it can be costlier or capacity-limited elsewhere does not establish that trenching fiber is worthwhile everywhere.
[C1.3] Nor do evocative examples settle the accounting. Telehealth, online classes, and precision agriculture can create benefits carriers do not capture, but governments must still compare those benefits with construction, maintenance, subscription, and adoption costs. Sparse settlement can mean both high expense and few beneficiaries. Competitive grants, milestones, and clawbacks may discipline contractors; they cannot demonstrate that the project deserved funding.
[C1.4] A milestone can certify an expensive mistake completed on time.
[C1.5] The opportunity cost is borne by the same rural communities invoked to justify spending. Money committed to an underused network cannot improve a clinic, provide affordable service, or connect several other communities through cheaper technologies. And coverage is not use: a line passing a farmhouse does not by itself make service affordable, teach digital skills, or keep a young family from leaving.
[C1.6] The decisive test is therefore not “connection versus abandonment,” but demonstrated public value per dollar against realistic alternatives. This motion supplies no such test and no limiting principle beyond carriers saying a project is unprofitable. Rejecting it does not devalue rural communities; it refuses to turn their needs into a blank check for infrastructure whose strongest argument is that someone else should pay.
