[P1.1] In high-demand neighborhoods, housing is already rationed by price. Convert a long-term apartment into a revolving tourist unit and you do not add hospitality; you subtract a home. Owners do it because a week of visitors can beat a month of rent. The result is mechanical: fewer units for the people who actually live there, higher bids among remaining tenants, and displacement that is not metaphorical.
[P1.2] A family leaves the school catchment. A nurse commutes an hour farther. The block loses the residents who staff its shops and watch its kids.
[P1.3] Restriction reverses that subtraction. Units come back onto the residential market. Extra supply against the same local demand eases rent pressure where it is most political and most personal. That is the dominant effect in exactly the places the motion names—tight, high-demand cores—because those are the places where conversion is most profitable and therefore most common.
[P1.4] The offsetting harms do not match that scale. Host income is not a general local wage; it is concentrated among people who already control scarce property. They keep the asset. They can lease long-term at still-elevated city rents, or sell.
[P1.5] Visitor spending does not evaporate. Travelers use hotels, licensed rentals, or less strained districts. Tourism work continues. What is lost is a premium on turning homes into inventory, not the visitor economy itself.
[P1.6] Weigh the losses correctly. A rent spike that forces a household out is large, localized, and hard to reverse: new schools, lost networks, longer commutes, weaker local demand from people who used to spend every month on the same streets. A host earning less from weekends, or a visitor shifting spend by a few blocks, is smaller and more substitutable. Housing is the binding constraint.
[P1.7] Restriction relaxes it more than it taxes a flexible flow of guests. That is why the net is lower rents and less displacement, not a tourism collapse.

[C1.1] The motion asks you to believe a subtraction story: tourist stays steal apartments, so banning them restores homes and lowers rents. But that story only works for whole-home commercial conversions in neighborhoods where such units are a material share of housing. In most high-demand neighborhoods, short-term rentals are a thin slice of stock, and many are spare rooms, basement units, or primary residences that were never available to a long-term tenant. Removing them cannot produce the promised flood of returned homes.
[C1.2] Even where whole units do leave the tourist market, they do not automatically become affordable long-term rentals. Owners may sell to high-income buyers, hold them vacant or for family use, convert to mid-length executive rentals, or simply stop listing while prices remain strong. High demand is not created by a few listings; it is created by jobs, transit, wages, and years of underbuilding. A cap on visitors does not build housing or loosen the broader shortage.
[C1.3] The harms, by contrast, are immediate and concentrated. Neighborhood visitor spending pays housekeepers, cleaners, maintenance workers, restaurants, grocers, corner cafés, drivers, and local services. Guests do not simply disappear into hotels; they shift to corporate hotel districts or less strained areas, draining spending from exactly the blocks the policy claims to protect. Many hosts are not idle landlords.
[C1.4] They are residents using a spare room or a temporary absence to cover the mortgage, taxes, or a family emergency—income that lets them keep living in an expensive city. Cutting that income can push marginal owners toward displacement.
[C1.5] So the proposition gets the tradeoff backwards. It promises a rent reduction that is small and uncertain, while sacrificing a local income stream that is visible and real. The stronger response is enforcement against large-scale illegal hotel operations, not broad restrictions that tax the neighborhood economy and fail to meet the housing need.
