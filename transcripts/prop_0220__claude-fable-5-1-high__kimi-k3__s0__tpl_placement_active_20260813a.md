[P1.1] Picture two workers at the same warehouse. One earns $95,000, has an accountant, and already maxes out her 401(k). The other earns $31,000, has never opened the enrollment packet, and figures he will "get to it." Now ask which policy helps the second worker, because he is the one whose long-run financial security is actually in doubt.
[P1.2] A bigger tax deduction does almost nothing for him. His marginal rate is low, so a deduction is worth roughly a dime on the dollar, and even that dime only arrives if he first fills out the form he has been avoiding for three years.
[P1.3] Meanwhile the first worker collects a larger subsidy for saving she was already doing. Much of it is not new saving at all; it is money shifted from a taxable brokerage account into a tax-advantaged one. The Treasury pays, household balance sheets barely move, and the gains flow to people who were never insecure.
[P1.4] Automatic enrollment attacks the actual bottleneck, which is not price but inertia. When companies switch to opt-out defaults, participation among new hires typically jumps from around half to around ninety percent, with the largest gains among young, low-income, and minority workers, exactly the groups deductions miss. Nobody is coerced; anyone can leave with a click. Most simply don't, because the default did the hard part for them.
[P1.5] Add automatic escalation and a modest default rate becomes a serious nest egg over a career.
[P1.6] Cost matters too. Expanding deductions is expensive tax expenditure that scales with income. Auto-enrollment costs the government almost nothing and can be mandated for employers with minimal administrative burden, as several countries have already done.
[P1.7] So the comparison is stark: one policy changes the price of a decision most vulnerable households never make; the other changes the decision itself. Financial security is measured by how many households arrive at retirement with something, not by how large the tax break was for those who were always going to arrive comfortably.
