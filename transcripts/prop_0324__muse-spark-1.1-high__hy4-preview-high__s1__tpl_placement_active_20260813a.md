[P1.1] Place identity isn't manufactured in a press release. It's what a community repeats, owns, and recognizes as its own. Public institutions that want to build it should invest in practices, not spectacles.
[P1.2] One-off prestige mega-events get the logic backwards. A city paying to host a Formula One race, an international summit concert, or a week-long expo isn't expressing identity; it's renting someone else's. The format is standardized by global rights holders, the sponsors are transnational, the audience is flown in. Residents are spectators in their own streets, often literally displaced by security zones and price surges.
[P1.3] When the circus leaves, what remains? An empty stadium, debt, and photos of a place that could have been anywhere. It teaches the world you can host, not who you are.
[P1.4] Creator-led local festivals do the opposite. When a city funds its musicians, cooks, storytellers and artists to make a recurring event on their terms, it funds distinctiveness itself. A neighborhood marigold parade that returns every November, a riverfront makers market every summer, a local sounds festival curated by local producers — these accumulate ritual, memory, and craft. Because they recur, families grow into them.
[P1.5] Because creators lead, they stay specific: New Orleans doesn't need an Olympics to be itself; its identity lives in second lines and Mardi Gras Indian practices built over generations.
[P1.6] They also distribute power. Local festivals make residents participants, not extras, building skills, businesses and pride year over year. Mega-events concentrate risk and reward. For a public institution with limited money and a duty to its people, the choice is clear: build identity from the ground up, week after week, not buy it for a weekend.

[C1.1] PRO defines place identity as "what a community repeats, owns, and recognizes as its own" [P1.1]. That describes identity after it exists, not while it is built. Recurrence maintains identity; it rarely remakes it. When institutions try to build or reposition a place, the task is not repeating rituals for insiders but making the place legible to outsiders and confident to itself.
[C1.2] For that, one-off prestige mega-events are often the sharper instrument.
[C1.3] Mega-events solve a coordination problem. A neighborhood market or local sounds festival builds craft, but its audience is mostly the already-converted; it deepens a scene, not a city. A mega-event forces a deadline, concentrates capital, and produces one shareable "before and after" story. Barcelona's 1992 Games illustrate this: they accelerated waterfront regeneration, transit, and a design-forward image, helping turn an industrial, inward-looking city into an open Mediterranean capital—results scattered annual grants would not have matched.
[C1.4] The value was not the fortnight; it was the rupture—new transit, districts, and a reordered narrative, not just debt.
[C1.5] PRO says a mega-event is "renting someone else's" identity [P1.2]. But the format is a vessel; meaning is host-authored. The same Olympics reads differently because the host writes the narrative, commissions local artists for ceremonies, and attaches the event to its landmarks. Mega-events do not crowd out creators; they give the biggest stage and often the largest commission budget to them.
[C1.6] Local recurring festivals also have a dark side. They can become an exclusive loop of regulars, resistant to newcomers, and captured by a cultural niche; they conserve identity rather than expand it. They are excellent at maintenance, poor at transformation. If public money is scarce, preferring them risks subsidizing intimacy while forfeiting the chance to change a city's trajectory.
[C1.7] Institutions should choose the tool that fits the goal. To sustain identity, fund recurring local work. To build or reset identity at scale, prefer the mega-event. The motion's default preference confuses depth with reach, and process with impact.
