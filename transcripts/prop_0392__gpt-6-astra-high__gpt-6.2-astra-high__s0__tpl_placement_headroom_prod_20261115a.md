[P1.1] When a public-facing service moves online, accessibility determines who can actually use it. A payment form that a blind customer’s screen reader cannot identify, or an appointment system that cannot be operated without a mouse, is not merely inconvenient. [P1.2] Enforcement must distinguish a mistake from persistent failure. The standard should be published, focused on basic access, and tested against actual user tasks. Providers should receive clear notice and a reasonable opportunity to fix defects. But when the same basic failures persist or recur, another polite warning is not accountability.
[P1.3] The central problem is incentives. Accessibility work competes with features, advertising, and launch deadlines. Without a credible financial consequence, a provider can keep postponing repairs while disabled users bear the cost: lost time, lost independence, and sometimes missed access altogether. [P1.4] They also protect providers that invest in accessibility from being undercut by competitors that ignore it.
[P1.5] Fines should be proportionate to the provider’s resources, the seriousness of the barrier, and its record of correction. [P1.6] The goal is working access, not revenue or punishment for every imperfect page.
[P1.7] The alternative asks disabled people to keep reporting barriers, requesting favors, or relying on someone else to perform ordinary tasks. A telephone workaround is no substitute when it means shorter hours, longer waits, or surrendering privacy. Opposing fines does not eliminate costs; it leaves those costs with the people denied access. After repeated failures and a fair chance to repair them, responsibility belongs with the provider.
