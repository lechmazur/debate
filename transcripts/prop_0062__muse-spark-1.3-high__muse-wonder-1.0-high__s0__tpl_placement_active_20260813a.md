[P1.1] White-collar work was supposed to be safe because it required judgment, language, and credentials. Generative AI breaks that bargain directly. Previous waves automated muscle or rigid clerical steps; this wave automates drafting, summarizing, coding, triage, and analysis — the core of office work. That makes large-scale displacement not a distant fear but the business model.
[P1.2] Consider the mechanism inside firms. A bank does not need to fire its whole legal or compliance team to cut jobs. It needs one analyst with AI to review hundreds of contracts, flag risks, and draft memos that once took five juniors. A software team ships with fewer testers and documenters.
[P1.3] Insurers, consultancies, publishers, and call centers use the same pattern: keep the senior approver, remove the ladder beneath them. The savings are immediate, the software scales instantly, and executives are rewarded for headcount reduction. Job creation from AI maintenance, prompting, or oversight will exist, but one oversight role cannot replace ten entry roles.
[P1.4] Nor will cheaper services automatically create enough new white-collar demand to compensate. People do not need ten times more audits, marketing emails, or quarterly reports because they got cheaper. Much new value flows to owners of models, cloud infrastructure, and a narrow band of elite builders and integrators, not to displaced paralegals, copywriters, junior analysts, or support agents. Early-career pipelines hollow out first, which is especially damaging because those rungs teach the judgment senior roles require.
[P1.5] That asymmetry is why early policy preparation is rational insurance. Retraining, portable benefits, wage insurance, stronger income floors, and attention to credentialing take years to build. Waiting until unemployment spikes means reacting after skills atrophy and hiring ladders collapse. Even if optimists hope new occupations eventually appear, hope is not a plan.
[P1.6] Prepare for net loss now; if abundance arrives, the safety net simply costs less.

[C1.1] Generative AI automates tasks, not entire white-collar jobs. An office job is a bundle: drafting and summarizing, yes, but also gathering messy context, dealing with clients, exercising accountable judgment, and knowing which unusual detail changes the answer. When AI makes the first part cheaper, it often raises the value of the rest. A consultant can serve more clients, a lawyer can spend more time on strategy, and a manager can escape reports for the human work they crowded out.
[C1.2] Productivity also expands demand rather than simply shrinking headcount. Spreadsheets did not end accounting; they made analysis cheap enough that businesses wanted more forecasting and advice. Cheaper code means not just fewer programmers for the same software, but software for small clinics and retailers that never could afford developers — plus people to specify, integrate, test, sell, and support it. Cheaper drafting similarly brings legal, marketing, and financial help to firms previously priced out.
[C1.3] New work appears around the technology across implementation: governing data, evaluating outputs, redesigning workflows, managing risk, customizing tools, and helping clients use them. As AI output becomes abundant, bottlenecks also move to verification, editing, distribution, trust, and relationships — scarcer skills that command more human labor, not less.
[C1.4] Adoption will be uneven enough for markets to adapt. Liability, privacy, regulation, unreliable outputs, and fragmented data slow full substitution in the high-stakes fields where displacement is most feared. Some jobs will disappear, but the likely path is churn and recomposition, not net white-collar collapse.
[C1.5] Preparing now for mass displacement would therefore insure against the wrong future. Large AI-specific retraining schemes, wage subsidies for exit, or new income floors built on a collapse forecast risk training people away from durable work, discouraging hiring, and slowing the adoption that creates new demand. Policy should keep labor markets flexible, competitive, and mobile — not pre-commit to decline that the mechanism does not support.

When drafting gets cheap, they don't pay five juniors to do more strategy — they keep one senior to sign off and cut the rest. The higher-value human work is real, but one approver can cover what used to require a team.
[P2.2] That is net loss by design.
[P2.3] Spreadsheets are the wrong analogy. Spreadsheets made numbers cheaper but still needed humans to interpret and advise; final judgment stayed human. Generative AI sells the final memo, brief, code draft, and pitch itself. Cheaper accounting created more advisory demand because advice remained scarce.
[P2.4] When the machine writes the advice draft too, demand doesn't multiply the same way. A small firm buying a cheap subscription does not rehire the juniors a big firm fired.
[P2.5] Implementation and verification jobs won't close the gap either. Customizing tools, cleaning data, and checking outputs are real, but they are temporary and top-heavy. An integration project that once employed dozens becomes a product update that employs a few. And verification scales cruelly: one editor can now review output that once took ten writers to produce.
[P2.6] Regulation and unreliability only stagger the blow, they don't stop it. A law firm worried about liability still lets AI do the research and keeps one partner to take responsibility — which still eliminates juniors. Fragmented data slows outsiders, but inside the firm the incentive is relentless: software scales instantly, salaries don't.
[P2.7] Finally, early preparation is not betting against adaptation; it enables it. Portable benefits, retraining capacity, and income supports make workers more mobile, exactly what flexible markets need. If disruption proves milder, that insurance costs little. If it proves severe, waiting until ladders collapse leaves a generation with no rungs to climb back on.
[P2.8] Delay is the risky gamble, not preparation.

[C2.1] The unbundling story proves too much. A senior who signs off is not a rubber stamp who can scale infinitely; she is legally responsible for what she approves. If AI lets her office produce ten times more memos and code, her review, client calls, and liability become the bottleneck. Firms then need more reviewers, client-facing staff, and exception-handlers — or they cap output.
[C2.2] One approver replacing a team only works if demand stays fixed and accountability is free. Neither is true.
[C2.3] That is why dismissing spreadsheets misses the point. Generative AI does not sell accountable advice; it sells a plausible draft. Clients do not pay lawyers, consultants, or analysts mainly for sentences in order. They pay for someone who knows their business, spots the fact that should stay out of the memo, and stands behind the recommendation.
[C2.4] Cheaper drafts make that judgment usable for more clients and problems, just as cheaper calculation made analysis usable more widely. Output multiplies; responsibility does not automate away.
[C2.5] Verification cuts the same way. If one editor can check work that once took ten writers, firms need not stop at the old volume. They can test more campaigns, personalize more support, review contracts they once ignored, and build features they once deferred. Work that was uneconomic becomes worth doing — especially for small firms that never employed those ten writers.
[C2.6] Implementation is also not a one-off project: models, data, rules, and client needs keep changing, so integration, evaluation, and governance recur.
[C2.7] Finally, preparation for net collapse is not free insurance. Retraining people out of supposedly doomed occupations, subsidizing exit, and building income systems around permanent displacement tell employers and workers that office work is ending. That can discourage hiring and investment today, while training for yesterday's forecast. Flexible, general mobility support is sensible precisely because the future is recomposition, not disappearance.
[C2.8] Policy should not lock in the pessimistic premise.
