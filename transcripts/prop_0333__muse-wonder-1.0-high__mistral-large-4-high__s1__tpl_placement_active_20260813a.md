[P1.1] Public broadcasters are losing the audience that will decide whether they exist in twenty years. A teenager who never encounters public-service reporting, drama, or science on the phone in her hand will not magically start watching a scheduled channel at thirty, and she will not defend its funding as an adult. Relevance is therefore not about adding more places to broadcast the same offer. It is about showing up where attention already lives.
[P1.2] That is why short-form video and creator partnerships must be the priority. Short-form is how young people now sample the world: news explainers between classes, comedy sketches shared in group chats, sixty-second history or health clips that lead to a fuller documentary. Treating that format as a marketing trailer wastes its power. Done well, it is itself public-service content — accurate, accessible, and designed for completion and sharing rather than passive viewing.
[P1.3] Creator partnerships solve the other barrier: trust. Young audiences often distrust institutions but follow individual presenters who speak plainly, show their workings, and reply in comments. When a broadcaster co-produces with a science educator, a local comedian, or a youth journalist who already commands that loyalty, it borrows reach without surrendering standards. The broadcaster brings verification, safeguarding, and production support; the creator brings language, community, and discoverability algorithms actually reward.
[P1.4] Traditional channel expansion does the opposite. Launching or protecting another linear outlet spreads thin budgets across schedules that need filling twenty-four hours a day, duplicates genres already abundant on commercial TV, and still misses cord-cutting households. Every pound, dollar, or euro locked into transmission, listings, and filler is money not spent learning the formats that will carry public value forward.
[P1.5] Prioritizing youth short-form is not chasing trends. It is rebuilding the habit, trust, and public legitimacy broadcasters need to survive — one encountered, shared, credible clip at a time.

Public service requires context: explaining why a policy matters, holding power to account over months rather than seconds, and presenting complex science or history with the nuance citizens need to govern themselves. A sixty-second clip can spark curiosity, but it cannot deliver the investigation, the documentary, or the live debate that justifies public funding. When broadcasters prioritize the format, they train audiences to expect snackable content and erode the appetite for the serious journalism that democracies require.
[C1.3] The result is a race to the bottom where accuracy becomes secondary to velocity.
[C1.4] Creator partnerships compound this damage by compromising editorial independence. Public broadcasters derive their authority from impartiality and rigorous standards. [C1.5] The audience senses this contamination; trust evaporates, and with it, the broadcaster's license to operate.
[C1.6] Meanwhile, the proposition ignores universal service. Not every citizen owns a smartphone or trusts social platforms. The elderly, the rural poor, and the digitally excluded rely on traditional channels as their primary window to the world. Abandoning channel investment to fund short-form gambles betrays the broadcaster's founding mandate: to reach everyone, regardless of platform preference or technological access.
[C1.7] True relevance is not measured by trending hashtags or follower counts. It is measured by whether citizens still turn to the broadcaster when democracy itself is under pressure—whether they trust the source when misinformation floods their feeds. That trust is built through sustained, independent journalism delivered across channels that reach the whole population, not through borrowed algorithms and ephemeral clips.
