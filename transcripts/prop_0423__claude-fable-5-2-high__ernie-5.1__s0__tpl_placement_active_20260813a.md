[C1.1] Reserving a slice of tickets for locals or members before a dynamic general sale is a well‑intentioned idea that, in practice, creates more harm than good. The core problem is that it pits a blunt quota against a price signal that already does the heavy lifting of matching scarce seats with willing payers. When organisers carve out a “meaningful share” for residents, they inevitably leave a chunk of capacity that is priced below market and therefore under‑filled, because many locals simply lack the time, money, or desire to attend a marquee event.
[C1.2] The result is empty seats that could have generated revenue—revenue that would fund the very public services, policing, and infrastructure the pro side claims to protect.
[C1.3] Consider a large‑scale music festival. If twenty percent of tickets are earmarked for zip‑code verification, a significant portion may end up in the hands of people who never plan to use them, while genuine fans from farther afield are priced out. Those unused tickets often circulate on resale platforms at a premium, defeating the purpose of the reservation and re‑introducing the very market distortions the proposal seeks to avoid.
[C1.4] The proposal also smuggles in a vague, administratively costly definition of “local.” Commuters, tourists staying in hotels, even people who work in the city but live just across the boundary all become either unfairly excluded or arbitrarily included. This invites litigation, fraud, and a bureaucratic apparatus that dwarfs the modest benefit of a few extra community seats.
[C1.5] Finally, the pro side’s claim that dynamic pricing “exports benefits” ignores that ticket revenue, taxes, and ancillary spending flow back into the host city regardless of who holds the ticket. Loyalty is already rewarded through existing membership schemes; forcing a pre‑sale window adds little value and merely distorts the efficient allocation of scarce entertainment. The true cost of the reservation is not a loss of community spirit—it is a measurable loss of revenue, a surge in administrative burden, and a market that works less well for everyone.
