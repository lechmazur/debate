[P1.1] Professional esports is not a hobby; it is labor performed mostly by young people under intense commercial pressure. The proposition asks for stronger protections in three areas where the market has repeatedly failed: abusive contracts, underage exploitation, and schedules that grind players down faster than they can develop. The winning path is simple: when the competitors are teenagers, careers are short, and organizations hold the scouting, salary, and roster power, voluntary fairness is not enough.
[P1.2] First, contracts. Players often sign long, one-sided agreements while barely adult, with buyouts, streaming rights, substitution clauses, and vague conduct standards that can trap talent or end careers over disputes. Stronger baseline review, clear termination rights, and independent representation do not destroy teams; they prevent coercion and make the league credible.
[P1.3] Second, minors. A sixteen-year-old prodigy is not a free market actor in any meaningful sense. Guardians, coaches, and sponsors all profit from the child’s reflexes and brand. Protections such as education guarantees, income trusts, limits on streaming and practice hours, and mandatory advocacy treat these players as children with rights, not disposable assets.
[P1.4] Third, schedules. Elite play already demands thousands of hours of repetitive motion and constant travel. Without rest rules, injury reporting, and off-seasons, organizations externalize the cost of burnout onto players. A wrist injury at twenty-one can end a career; stronger scheduling standards preserve health and competitive integrity.
[P1.5] The opposition will likely say regulation is too rigid or that players can walk away. That mistakes leverage for consent. The question is not whether esports should be commercial, but whether commercial esports should be allowed to profit from exploitable youth without basic labor safeguards. It should not.
