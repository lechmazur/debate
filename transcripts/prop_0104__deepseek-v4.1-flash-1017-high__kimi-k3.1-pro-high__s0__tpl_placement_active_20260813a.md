[P1.1] The comparison is asymmetric from the start. A safe-storage law asks gun owners to lock a firearm when it is not under their control—not to surrender it, register it, or stop carrying it. It buys a measurable reduction in the shootings that begin with a child’s hand on a gun.
[P1.2] The mechanism is concrete. Adolescents in crisis are impulsive; a loaded gun in a nightstand converts a temporary despair into a permanent death. Young children find guns and shoot themselves or playmates. Teenagers take a parent’s unlocked firearm to school or into a dispute.
[P1.3] Locking a gun does not make suicide or violence impossible, but it inserts time, distance, and a responsible adult between impulse and tragedy. Storage laws also change norms: when locked storage is expected, the default household practice shifts, and fewer guns circulate to kids.
[P1.4] The burden side is real but modest. Compliance means a lockbox, cable lock, or quick-access safe, and most laws leave the firearm available when the owner is carrying it or keeping it within immediate control. A parent can still defend the home; they simply cannot leave a loaded pistol where a curious eight-year-old can reach it. The cost is dollars and seconds; the benefit is a child’s life.
[P1.5] The usual objection—criminals will ignore the law—misses the target. Safe storage is not aimed primarily at the burglar; it is aimed at the fourteen-year-old in the house and the toddler in the next room. And many stolen guns begin as unlocked guns in lawful homes, so secure storage narrows the pipeline to illegal use too.
[P1.6] The proposition does not demand perfection. It demands that the reduction in youth firearm deaths and injuries outweigh the burden on lawful ownership. When the burden is a locked box and the benefit is fewer dead children, that comparison is not close.
