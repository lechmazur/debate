[P1.1] A ban pretends the tool does not exist. Students already carry chatbots, grammar assistants, and search engines that draft prose. The real choice is not whether generative AI enters homework. It is whether schools force that use underground or require students to show how the work was made.
[P1.2] The purpose of school is not abstinence from available tools. It is the production of people who can think, verify, and take responsibility. We did not treat calculators as cheating forever; we taught when calculation was the skill and when interpretation was. The same split applies now.
[P1.3] A student may use a model to list counterarguments, then must cite the tool, keep the prompt trail, and mark what they accepted, rejected, or rewrote. That process file is a better window into learning than a polished page of unknown origin.
[P1.4] Picture two essays of equal quality. Under a ban, both look clean and the teacher guesses. Under transparency, one student attaches a messy trail of queries, corrections, and a note that the thesis survived three model suggestions they refused. That is assessment of judgment.
[P1.5] The ban cannot see it.
[P1.6] Broad bans fail because they are unenforceable, inequitable, and pedagogically backward. Detection is an arms race. It flags non-native writing and formulaic prose while missing anyone who paraphrases. Punishment then falls on the clumsy, not the dishonest.
[P1.7] Teachers spend hours on suspicion instead of feedback. The professional world will not reward secret use; it will demand accountable use. Default permission with citation and process transparency matches that world. It still allows targeted bans: in-class cold drafts, oral exams, handwritten proofs.
[P1.8] The default simply puts the burden on the school to justify a restriction, rather than on the student to pretend the tool never arrived.
[P1.9] Integrity is disclosure, not purity. If the goal is students who can defend their claims, require them to show the making of the claim.
