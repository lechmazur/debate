[P1.1] Requiring college degrees for jobs where they aren’t necessary is a costly anachronism that harms employers, workers, and society. Degrees have become a proxy for class rather than competence. A coding bootcamp graduate can often outperform a computer science degree holder in practical programming; a skilled electrician with vocational training doesn’t need a liberal arts degree to excel at facility management. These requirements systematically exclude capable candidates—especially first-generation professionals and those priced out of higher education—while failing to guarantee performance.
[P1.2] They also force individuals into unnecessary debt for credentials they don’t need.
[P1.3] Major employers like Google, IBM, and Bank of America have moved toward skills-based hiring for many roles after finding degree status often fails to predict job performance. This approach expands the talent pool and improves quality by focusing on actual ability rather than financial privilege. Removing these barriers opens opportunities to millions without sacrificing competence.
[P1.4] This change isn’t just about efficiency—it’s about fairness. Degree requirements perpetuate a cycle where opportunity flows to those already privileged by background. They create artificial scarcity, drive up wages without productivity gains, and waste human potential. The solution is straightforward: evaluate candidates on demonstrated skills and knowledge, not paper credentials.
[P1.5] Anything else is gatekeeping disguised as quality control.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes 'can do the immediate tasks' for 'will perform reliably in the job.' A degree is rarely required because every course will be used daily. It is required because completion is a cheap, verifiable signal of things employers cannot test in an interview: sustained effort over years, meeting deadlines for supervisors, writing clearly, tolerating setbacks, and learning unfamiliar material on command. Those traits predict who can be trained, promoted, trusted with clients, and left unsupervised.
[C1.2] Remove that filter and hiring does not become a contest of skill. It becomes more expensive and more subjective. A midsize firm posting an operations, sales, or project coordinator role without a degree line can be flooded with applications it has no cheap way to sort. It will reach for cruder substitutes: referrals, polished portfolios, unpaid trial work, personality screens, and gut feel.
[C1.3] Each favors people with connections, free time, coaching, and cultural familiarity more than a degree from a community college or public university does.
[C1.4] That is why the fairness argument cuts the other way. A degree requirement is at least a public standard anyone can understand in advance. Skills-based hiring too often shifts costs onto applicants — build a portfolio, pass bespoke assessments, complete sample projects, network your way in — while employer discretion stays hidden. The well-off can buy preparation for that too, without the financial aid, transfer pathways, and standardized credential that make college legible to first-generation applicants.
[C1.5] Employers also bear the downside when a hire fails: lost customers, retraining, team disruption. If degrees were useless gatekeeping, competitors dropping them would win cheaper, better talent and force the rest to follow. Their persistence in competitive industries suggests managers are buying risk reduction, not indulging snobbery. Judged over a career, not a day-one work sample, completion often is tied to performance — and employers should not abandon a useful signal for methods that are costlier, murkier, and easier to game.

[C2.1] That IBM and Bank of America announced they would drop some degree lines proves far less than claimed. Announcing skills-based hiring is not the same as hiring, training, and promoting non-degree workers at scale and succeeding. Where firms do hire without degrees, they often substitute years of prior experience, certifications, or internal referrals — filters that are harder for a young first-generation applicant to meet than enrolling in college. A press release is not proof that a four-year signal has no value.
[C2.2] The proposed replacement, a work sample, tests a snapshot, not performance. It shows who can produce one polished deliverable on one day, with coaching, templates, generative tools, and rehearsal at home. It does not show who will meet deadlines in month six, learn a second system after the first changes, write clearly to an angry client, or persist when work is tedious. That is what completing a degree has already forced a candidate to demonstrate repeatedly, under different instructors, over years.
[C2.3] The cost argument also reverses reality. A degree is verified once and carried from employer to employer. Bespoke assessments mean applicants repeat tests, portfolios, and trial projects for every posting — unpaid — while each employer pays to build, validate, update, and legally defend its own version. For a midsize firm without IBM's human-resources department, keeping a legible degree line is not inertia; it is the cheapest way to avoid drowning in applications or inventing a worse test.
[C2.4] Calling that standard a class filter ignores working adults who used public colleges precisely to become legible to employers. Nor does a degree make bad hires uniquely expensive; failure costs customers and retraining whoever holds the job, and the salary reflects lower expected variance. Typewriters vanished quickly once a cheaper, better substitute arrived. Degree requirements endure in competitive hiring because the substitutes keep proving narrower, costlier to run, and easier for the already-advantaged to game.
