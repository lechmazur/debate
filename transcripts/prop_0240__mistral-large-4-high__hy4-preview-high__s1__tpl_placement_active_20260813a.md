[P1.1] Technical competence is necessary but never sufficient for legitimate governance. A bridge built by the finest engineers collapses into disuse if the community rejects its necessity; a pandemic response designed by the best epidemiologists fails when citizens refuse compliance because they were excluded from the decision. This is not an argument against expertise, but a recognition that expertise serves legitimacy, not the reverse. Technical skill without democratic mandate is merely force wearing a lab coat.
[P1.2] Public decision-making involves making binding choices for a collective. Authority to bind others derives from democratic consent, not from credentials or algorithms. When policymakers substitute technocratic optimization for democratic deliberation, they sever the vital connection between decision and the governed. The result is not merely unpopularity but illegitimacy: decisions lack moral standing and face resistance, evasion, or reversal at the first opportunity.
[P1.3] Stable governance requires that citizens recognize the right to decide, however brilliant the design. Democracy transforms subjects into citizens; technocracy reduces citizens to data points.
[P1.4] Consider austerity programs imposed by international financial institutions during debt crises, or public health restrictions implemented without legislative debate. Technically sophisticated models produced precise recommendations, yet where democratic processes were bypassed, implementation faltered badly. Compliance depends on perceived legitimacy. Citizens follow rules they helped shape; they resist edicts imposed from above, however mathematically optimal.
[P1.5] The failure is not technical but political: the absence of consent destroys the capacity to govern, turning correct policies into conflict.
[P1.6] Democratic legitimacy provides essential feedback mechanisms that competence lacks. Elections, deliberation, and public justification force policymakers to confront painful trade-offs, correct errors, and maintain trust across diverse populations. Technocracy promises efficiency but delivers brittle systems that fracture under stress because they lack the social capital to weather disagreement. Expertise can optimize outcomes within frameworks, but only democracy can legitimately set the framework itself.
[P1.7] Competence answers "how to do X well." Legitimacy answers "why we should do X at all." In public decision-making, the second question is logically prior. Without democratic authority, technical excellence is power without right.
