[P1.1] Most fans are not loyal to sport in general. They are loyal to one club, one hometown team, or one competition, and they structure their weeks and identities around watching that team play. A streaming model that respects how fandom actually works should let them buy what they came for.
[P1.2] Today’s broad bundle does the opposite. To watch a single Premier League club, an NBA team, or their college side, fans are pushed into a package stuffed with other leagues and sports they will never open. The result is not choice; it is a toll. When the only legal route costs many times the value a fan actually uses, younger fans, lower-income households, students who have moved away, and diaspora supporters are priced out of their own community.
[P1.3] A cheaper team-specific or competition-specific pass fixes that mismatch without abolishing the bundle. Keep the all-access subscription for the omnivore who wants everything. Add a narrower pass for the supporter who wants every match from one team, or one tournament, at a proportionate price. Lower the entry price, let people pay for what they actually love, and casual interest becomes habit.
[P1.4] That conversion is also good business. Every priced-out fan today is either lost to highlights, driven to unreliable illegal streams, or never formed as a paying customer at all. A team pass brings them into the legal ecosystem, where broadcasters gain subscribers, viewing data, advertising reach, and future upsell opportunities. A smaller payment from a fan who would otherwise pay nothing is not cannibalization; it is market expansion.
[P1.5] There is no technical excuse left. Streaming already personalizes recommendations, logins, and feeds for each user; restricting access by team or competition is trivial by comparison. What remains is a business habit built for cable scarcity, now imposed on internet abundance. Fans should not have to buy the whole stadium to watch their own team.

[C1.1] The PRO case treats the bundle as a toll booth on a natural product. In truth, the bundle is the financing mechanism. Sports broadcasting carries huge fixed costs; its dominant expense is the rights fee paid up front for an entire package. Leagues sell all clubs together because the aggregate is worth more than the sum of team parts, and the broad bundle lets a broadcaster bid enormous sums and spread that cost.
[C1.2] PRO calls it a toll [P1.2], but the fan most eager for a team pass is the loyal, high-value fan whose surplus funds production trucks, analysts, women’s sport, and rights fees that keep smaller clubs solvent. A cheaper pass does not mainly recruit strangers; it lets that fan trade a full-price bundle for a discounted one-team pass. That is cannibalization, not market expansion [P1.4]. With fixed costs unchanged, the gap closes only through higher prices for those left behind, thinner production, or lower rights fees that squeeze clubs and players.
[C1.3] The fans PRO champions get worse coverage, not cheaper access.
[C1.4] PRO’s “keep the bundle, add a pass” compromise [P1.3] is unstable. Bundles survive only if cherry-pickers stay. Once they leave, remaining prices rise, pushing more fans out.
[C1.5] The “no technical excuse” claim [P1.5] mistakes the barrier. The obstacle is contractual. One club’s season spans league, cup, and continental matches held by different rightsholders, so no single broadcaster can lawfully assemble a team-specific pass without every owner agreeing. Leagues refuse, because fragmentation destroys the aggregate value they just sold.
[C1.6] Competition-specific passes already exist where rights allow, but the motion’s bite is team-specific, and there it breaks.
[C1.7] So the question is whether unbundling creates new fans or merely shifts income from broadcasters to the already loyal while shrinking the pot that funds the sport. The bundle is not access denied; it is access subsidized.
