[P1.1] Central banks ignore housing bubbles at their peril. The 2008 crisis proved that asset-price instability, especially in housing, can devastate the real economy. When central banks focus solely on consumer prices, they overlook the financial accelerants building beneath the surface. Housing is not just another asset—it is the largest store of household wealth, the collateral underpinning most credit, and the key transmission belt between finance and the real economy.
[P1.2] In many advanced economies, residential real estate exceeds GDP in value, making its stability foundational to economic health. Let it inflate uncontrollably, and you seed inequality, distort consumption, and set the stage for a crash that pulls down jobs and growth with it.
[P1.3] Targeting asset prices is not a radical departure but a necessary evolution. Central banks already pull levers that ripple through housing: interest rates shape mortgage costs, and macroprudential tools can curb risky lending. Making stability an explicit goal would force earlier, more calibrated responses. Consider Canada and New Zealand: as house prices surged, their central banks’ hands were tied by narrow inflation mandates, even as household debt hit record highs and financial vulnerabilities mounted.
[P1.4] An explicit asset-price target would have justified earlier action to moderate the boom before the bust, using tools like loan-to-value ratios or countercyclical capital buffers.
[P1.5] The alternative—waiting for CPI to signal trouble—is dangerously slow. By the time consumer prices reflect housing pressures, the bubble may already be popping. Asset prices are leading indicators; ignoring them is like driving by looking only in the rearview mirror. Stability in housing markets also promotes social stability, reducing the wealth gap between property owners and renters that fuels political and economic tensions.
[P1.6] Rising home values primarily benefit older, wealthier cohorts, while locking younger generations out of ownership.
[P1.7] Opponents will warn of mission creep, but the greater risk is repeating 2008. Central banks exist to safeguard prosperity, and that requires watching where the greatest systemic risks gather. Central bankers cannot distinguish a fundamental boom from a bubble in real time, and forcing them to cap home values plunges unelected technocrats into toxic distributional politics between leveraged homeowners and renters.

[P2.4] Central banks already make continuous real-time assessments of financial stability risks; this proposal merely formalizes and focuses that responsibility where it is most needed.
