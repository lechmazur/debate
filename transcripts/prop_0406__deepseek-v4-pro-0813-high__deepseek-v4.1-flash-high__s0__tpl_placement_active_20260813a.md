[P1.1] Someone sleeping in a car because no safe shelter exists is not choosing to cause a nuisance; they are choosing the least harmful survival option. Fining them, towing their vehicle, or waking them with police lights does not create a single bed. It removes the last private, lockable, weatherproof place they have and turns a housing shortage into a cycle of debt and arrest.
[P1.2] The qualifier “no safe alternative” is decisive. Shelters are often full, unsafe, or inaccessible to people with disabilities, couples, pets, possessions, or jobs that do not fit curfew hours. A car can be safer than a crowded shelter or a street encampment; for someone fleeing violence, a locked door is protection. Routine fines fall on people with no ability to pay, becoming warrants and license suspensions that make it harder to work or exit homelessness.
[P1.3] Towing seizes not just a vehicle but a home, medication, documents, tools, and the only means of reaching work. Nightly police contact destroys sleep and stability without producing housing.
[P1.4] The predictable objection is sanitation and neighborhood impact. But actual misconduct—blocking traffic, dumping waste, violence—can be addressed through conduct-based rules. The motion does not require unlimited parking anywhere; it prohibits using fines, towing, and harassment as the routine response to the status of having no alternative. When cities pair safe parking with bathrooms and trash service, the same concerns are managed without destroying the one shelter people have.
[P1.5] The correct test is simple: if enforcement punishes the bed, not the behavior, it punishes homelessness itself.

[C1.1] The motion sounds humane because it hides a larger claim: that sleeping in a vehicle must be exempt from ordinary rules governing public space. It should not be. The debate is not whether homeless people deserve punishment—they do not. It is whether a city may use neutral, conduct-based enforcement to prevent vehicles from becoming permanent, unsafe encampments.
[C1.2] First, “no safe alternative” is not something an officer can reliably verify at 2 a.m. Shelter beds fluctuate; safety and accessibility are contested; the claim can be asserted by anyone with a car. A rule built on that phrase will be ignored or applied arbitrarily. The likely result is not a few desperate people resting, but clusters of RVs and broken cars, blocked streets, waste, generators, and fire risk.
[C1.3] Second, a vehicle is not housing. It can be a stopgap, but it is also a place where people suffocate from carbon monoxide, freeze, or live without toilets or water. Treating car sleeping as an untouchable right makes it easier for cities to leave people there rather than offer real shelter. Routine contact—outreach, health checks, time limits, towing only when blocking—can be the bridge to services.
[C1.4] The humane answer is not to normalize a dangerous sleeping arrangement as a right.
[C1.5] Third, the motion converts hardship into a special exemption. Yes, a fine does not create a bed; but neither does a blanket exemption. Once enforcement is off the table, residents and other unhoused people pay: blocked sidewalks, occupied parks, lost business access, neighborhoods absorbing costs. And the people most harmed are often the most vulnerable.
[C1.6] The better rule is safe parking with bathrooms, trash service, and caseworkers, paired with enforcement against genuine hazards and obstruction. If the choice is a bed or a car, build more beds—don't tell every city it can never manage its streets.
