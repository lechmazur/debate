[P1.1] You cannot justly punish a person for an act they have no realistic ability to avoid. That is the most basic guardrail of fair law: if there is no legal, accessible place for an unhoused person to go, no bed they can actually use that night, no safe place to keep the medications, IDs, and personal belongings they cannot carry on their person, no accessible path to connect to housing and health services, then anti-encampment laws do nothing but criminalize the status of being too poor to afford rent.
[P1.2] Genuinely available shelter cannot be the kind that turns people away for having a pet, a partner, unmanaged addiction symptoms, or no government ID; those barriers make a bed functionally nonexistent for the people most likely to be in encampments. Sweep-only, no-option encampment bans do not deliver public order, either. They do not end unsheltered homelessness; they simply displace people from visible sidewalks to isolated riverbanks, under remote highway overpasses, and into alleys where they face sharply higher risk of fatal exposure, overdose, and violent victimization.
[P1.3] Sweeps routinely destroy or confiscate tents, work tools, family mementos, and life-sustaining medications, because people targeted have no safe storage option for those belongings when forced to move. They waste police resources on endless, revolving-door arrests and citations that saddle people with warrants and fines that make qualifying for housing, jobs, or public benefits even harder, creating a self-defeating cycle where enforcement makes the underlying problem worse. This is not a blanket rejection of encampment rules. When cities deliver on the actual, low-barrier shelter, storage, and services the proposition describes, reasonable encampment limits connect people to support instead of punishing vulnerability, and serve a legitimate public interest for all residents.
[P1.4] Without those genuinely available options, though, these laws are arbitrary, cruel, and counterproductive, punishing a choice no one in their position is actually free to make.

Clearing dry hillside brush during fire season, keeping hospital entrances and transit platforms passable, or preventing a sprawling camp from entrenching over months actively reduces total danger, even if residents relocate to less hazardous spots nearby. Duration and location matter: entrenched sites accumulate biohazards, makeshift fuel lines, and predatory crime far deadlier than dispersed, temporary presence.
[C2.2] Stopping site-specific disasters is not futile shuffling; it is urgent harm reduction for housed and unhoused neighbors alike.
[C2.3] Realizing indoor beds take years, PRO pivots to "temporary designated safe camping areas" [P2.3] with on-site clinicians handling "unmanaged addiction symptoms" [P2.2]. [C2.4] To protect sober guests and vulnerable families, every shelter or sanctioned site must still exclude individuals whose active behavior threatens others. [C2.5] PRO also conflates civil limits with unpaid fines that turn into arrest warrants [P2.3] or trashed belongings [P1.3]. Cities need no fines, warrants, or confiscation to enforce reasonable boundaries. Non-punitive time-and-place rules, like requiring daytime tent takedowns on busy sidewalks or clearing hazardous structures after advance notice, abate acute obstructions without imposing a cent in penalties or a criminal record. By declaring anti-encampment laws justified "only when" universal shelter, storage, and services already exist, PRO erases non-criminal hazard management, paralyzing cities from protecting shared space while long-term capacity is built.
