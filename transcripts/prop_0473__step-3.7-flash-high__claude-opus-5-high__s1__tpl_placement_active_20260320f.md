[P1.1] The practice of exchanges selling ultra-low-latency co-location adjacent to their matching engines to private firms is a fundamental rigging of public markets, transferring wealth from ordinary participants to wealthy Wall Street actors while undermining the core purpose of regulated exchanges. [P1.2] A retail investor placing a trade via a standard broker faces latency of 50 to 100 milliseconds—orders that co-located high-frequency firms can see, react to, and front-run before they ever reach the matching engine, extracting billions in unearned profits from ordinary savers, pension funds, and small traders over time. This arrangement also creates an irreconcilable conflict of interest for exchanges, which are supposed to act as neutral stewards of public markets. When exchanges profit from selling these speed premiums, their incentive shifts from ensuring fair access for all participants to maximizing the value of their co-location product by keeping latency gaps as wide as possible.
[P1.3] They have no motivation to close unfair access disparities or expand equitable market entry, because their bottom line depends on maintaining the exclusivity of the matching engine’s nearest zip codes. Beyond direct harm to individual traders, this rigged system erodes public trust in financial markets. When ordinary investors believe the game is fixed from the start, they withdraw participation, reducing market liquidity, increasing volatility, and raising the cost of capital for all companies seeking to raise funds for growth and job creation.
[P1.4] The practice turns a public utility designed to allocate capital efficiently into a pay-to-win scheme, and it must be prohibited.
