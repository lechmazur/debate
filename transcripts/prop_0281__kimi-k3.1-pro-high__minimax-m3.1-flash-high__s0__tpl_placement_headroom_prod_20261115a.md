[P1.1] A subscription is continuing consent to pay, not a permanent surrender of control. When a service can take a card number and begin billing in moments, but stopping requires a phone call, retention screens, or buried account menus, the extra friction is not neutral administration. It changes the consumer’s decision: “I no longer want this” becomes “I will try again after being charged once more.” Regulation should restore a basic symmetry: withdrawal must be no harder than enrollment.
[P1.2] The welfare loss is larger than irritation. Unwanted renewals transfer money after the consumer has stopped valuing the service; obstructive journeys also tax time, attention, and confidence. Those costs multiply across services and trial conversions. A small monthly charge may seem trivial to a firm, yet several forgotten charges matter greatly to a household watching its balance.
[P1.3] The burden falls hardest on people with limited time or confidence navigating digital accounts.
[P1.4] Easy cancellation also improves competition. Firms should retain customers by offering value, not by exploiting forgetfulness and delay. If exit is costly, weak services keep revenue while better rivals struggle to win switching customers. If exit is easy, cancellation becomes prompt feedback: improve the product, adjust the price, or lose the sale.
[P1.5] Profits depending on consumers failing to escape are not evidence of innovation.
[P1.6] The remedy is practical, not a demand for identical seconds or clicks. Require cancellation through the same available channel, with comparable steps and no obstructive detours, while allowing proportionate identity and security checks. A clear, dismissible pause offer can remain; a mandatory negotiation cannot. Businesses can redesign forms more easily than millions of consumers can fight them.
[P1.7] Continuing billing requires continuing consent, and consent that is easy to give but hard to withdraw is what regulators should fix.

[C1.1] Dark patterns are a genuine problem, but the proposed cure rests on a false equivalence: enrolling and withdrawing are not mirror-image acts. Signing up is usually optional; cancellation can involve notice-period charges, credits, shared accounts, or identity verification. Click counts cannot establish informed consent. “As easy as sign-up” is unclear when one route takes two clicks and another requires authentication.
[C1.2] The rule also creates perverse incentives. If cancellation must match enrollment friction, firms can make sign-up needlessly cumbersome—or make both flows one-click while hiding renewal terms elsewhere. That rewards superficial compliance and may worsen competition by making services harder to try. A proxy that can be gamed is a weak regulatory standard.
[C1.3] Nor does equal effort address the most serious failures. A one-click cancellation button may be useless if the merchant substitutes another paid service, continues charging, or makes refunds difficult. Better rules directly prohibit hidden renewals, misleading consent, mandatory retention paths, and deliberate cancellation barriers, while preserving proportionate authentication and dispute handling. The core losses are money, time, and uninformed continuation, not merely extra mouse movements.
[C1.4] Rules should therefore target conduct rather than count interface steps.
[C1.5] Uniform simplicity also creates risks. For shared accounts and services involving minors or vulnerable dependents, unauthorized cancellation could expose a household to continued charges or deny someone essential access. Verification and account ownership can protect consumers rather than obstruct them.
[C1.6] Thus the regulatory question is not whether exit should feel effortless, but whether firms deliberately prevent informed, timely withdrawal. Precise duties—conspicuous renewal notice, accessible cancellation, no mandatory negotiation, prompt cessation and refunds—address the harm directly. Subscription fatigue warrants anti-dark-pattern rules, not universal parity. Cancellation should be straightforward; “as easy as sign-up” is an unsupported legal standard that can be gamed.

A comparator does not make a legal standard precise when “same channel,” “comparable steps,” and “no obstructive detours” permit subjective judgments. [C2.2] Parity also mistakes the relevant transaction. Sign-up is a consumer’s request to begin something; cancellation is a request to end an existing relationship with possible contractual consequences. The price of entry may be low because consumers can compare many sellers and reject a poor offer. Exit may involve recovering prepaid value, resolving a billing dispute, or changing access for a shared account.
[C2.3] Applying entry friction to those problems confuses ease of trying with fairness of termination.
[C2.4] [P2.3]’s examples actually strengthen this point rather than defeat it: an ineffective “cancel” button violates any legitimate cancellation duty, including parity, so parity adds little to those cases. The cases parity uniquely addresses are extra screens, calls, or retention scripts. Yet it does not explain why the remedy must duplicate sign-up rather than prohibit a clearly defined barrier, such as requiring a retention conversation before processing a timely request. Such a duty gives firms certainty and consumers a real exit.
[C2.5] Nor should consumers’ recovery depend on firms having optimized acquisition. [P2.3] calls making enrollment cumbersome a cost in lost sign-ups, but businesses routinely use low-friction trials because they expect many users to decline or forget. If cancellation must mirror that deliberately streamlined enrollment, the law codifies acquisition tactics rather than consumer control. That can preserve subscription fatigue: a one-click trial conversion paired with a one-click cancellation still forces consumers to remember and act before renewal.
[C2.6] The better benchmark is effective, accessible, and timely cancellation, with objective duties: visible cancellation, no mandatory negotiation, prompt cessation and refunds. This catches the exploitative journey directly. “As easy as sign-up” is broader, less predictable, and less focused than the harm; it should not become the statutory rule.
