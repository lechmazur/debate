[P1.1] Minimum-wage laws exist to guarantee a floor for labor actually supplied, not for labor narrowly redefined by corporate scheduling algorithms. When gig workers keep an app open, remain positioned in a delivery zone, and accept penalties for ignoring requests, they are not enjoying personal leisure; they are holding themselves available for the platform’s immediate commercial use. Counting only active minutes allows a firm to enjoy a fully staffed labor pool at a moment’s notice while paying sub-minimum wages for the privilege.
[P1.2] That availability is labor, and if platforms refuse to count it toward wage calculations, they outsource the cost of demand volatility directly onto workers while gutting the minimum-wage’s protective purpose.
[P1.3] The current model treats a driver or courier as an idle non-employee during every gap between tasks, yet simultaneously enforces the very constraints that make those gaps unproductive. Workers cannot pick up a conventional shift elsewhere because the next ping could arrive in seconds or in an hour; they cannot run personal errands without risking deactivation for missed requests. This is functionally on-call time, and in traditional employment contexts, courts and regulators have long recognized that controlled waiting is compensable work.
[P1.4] The app exercises supervisory control every second it monitors location, assigns ratings, and punishes rejection. That control is what separates unpaid free time from compensable labor.
[P1.5] If platforms only pay for “task time,” they capture all the benefits of a ready workforce while paying none of the costs of maintaining it. A rider who spends forty minutes waiting and twenty minutes delivering to net ten dollars is effectively earning well below the legal floor. Minimum-wage compliance that ignores waiting time is therefore not compliance at all; it is a bookkeeping trick that converts statutory protection into optional charity. The proposition simply insists that wage law apply to the reality of gig work, not to a stripped-down fiction that benefits platforms at workers’ expense.

[C1.1] The proposition collapses the moment you ask a simple question: which app pays? A typical courier runs Uber Eats, DoorDash, and Grubhub simultaneously, accepting whichever ping comes first. Under PRO's rule, the same forty idle minutes count as compensable "waiting" on three platforms at once — triple-paid time for a single worker doing nothing exclusive for anyone. Either platforms each pay full minimum wage for non-exclusive availability, which is absurd, or regulators invent some apportionment scheme that no one can audit, because no platform can observe what its competitors' apps are doing on a worker's phone.
[C1.2] PRO's on-call analogy fails on its own terms. Traditional compensable waiting involves an employer who controls when you show up, where you stand, and whether you may leave. A gig worker chooses when to log on, where to sit, and — on most platforms — can decline requests, watch Netflix at home, or log off mid-evening. Availability you unilaterally declare, at hours you alone choose, is not labor "supplied" in any sense wage law has ever recognized.
[C1.3] If merely opening an app creates a wage claim, every worker has a printing press for paid hours.
[C1.4] And platforms will respond rationally, which is where workers actually lose. If every logged-on minute costs money, platforms stop letting workers log on freely. They impose scheduled shifts, cap headcount, and lock out part-timers during slow hours — exactly what happened when New York City imposed a time-based pay floor on delivery apps and couriers suddenly found themselves shut out of the apps mid-day. The open-access, work-whenever model that draws students, caregivers, and second-jobbers into gig work dies, replaced by employment-lite scheduling that serves a smaller, full-time core.
[C1.5] If the worry is low effective earnings, the honest fix is higher per-engaged-minute floors with utilization multipliers baked in — guaranteed income without paying for unverifiable, non-exclusive idle time. The proposition picks the one mechanism that is ungameable on paper and unworkable everywhere else.
