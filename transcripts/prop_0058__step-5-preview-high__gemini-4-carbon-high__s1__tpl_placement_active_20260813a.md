[P1.1] A DOGE-style efficiency drive confuses a spreadsheet with a state. Its premise is that government is a warehouse of obvious waste, and that a sharp enough audit can separate the fat from the meat. But state capacity is not fat. It is the accumulated knowledge, trust, and routine coordination that lets a benefits office process claims, a regulator inspect food, or an emergency manager coordinate response.
[P1.2] When such a drive cuts on the basis of visible line items, it does not remove true duplication; it removes the redundancy that keeps services running when systems fail.
[P1.3] The savings are shallow. Canceling a training program or offering buyouts to senior staff may reduce a budget line this quarter, but it transfers the cost to next year in delayed claims, contract blowouts, and crisis response. The damage is deep. A state that loses its institutional memory must either hire expensive consultants or watch service quality collapse.
[P1.4] Consider the concrete mechanics of service delivery. Rapid workforce reductions do not just shrink headcount. They break the handoffs between agencies that make social insurance work. The analyst who knows how to cross-check a disability claim, the inspector who understands which bridge cracks matter, the coordinator who knows which local officials answer the phone at 2 a.m.—these are not overhead.
[P1.5] They are the capacity itself. Efficiency drives treat them as costs to be minimized, then discover too late that the services they administered were the only visible part of a much larger system.
[P1.6] PRO wins because the comparison is asymmetric: the savings are temporary and measurable, while the harm to state capacity is permanent and compounding. Service delivery does not snap back after the press conference ends.
