[P1.1] Planetary defense should be judged by a practical question: if an object is found on a collision course, can governments change its path before impact? Research papers and one-off demonstrations do not answer yes; a paper plan is not a deflection. A deployed system does: surveys, orbit tracking, characterization, ready launch contracts, maintained impactor designs, command authority, exercises, and post-deflection tracking. That capability is what permanent funding buys.
[P1.2] This is insurance against ruin. Most years pass quietly; the bad year can mean a shattered city, a devastated region, or global crop failure. Chelyabinsk arrived with essentially no useful warning; Tunguska flattened a vast forest area. Warning is not guaranteed.
[P1.3] Low probability is not a reason to wait: the harm is catastrophic, irreversible, and poorly handled by markets or annual budget cycles that underbuy rare emergencies. Protection whose value appears only on the worst day is a core government responsibility.
[P1.4] Deflection is time-sensitive. A small nudge years ahead can turn catastrophe into navigation; a hurried mission after discovery may need far more energy, approval, integration, and luck. If spacecraft, sky coverage, tracking pipelines, launch slots, and decision chains must be invented after the alarm, warning time is squandered. A kinetic-impact test showed the method is plausible; it did not leave a standing inventory and trained operators.
[P1.5] Maintaining that chain is infrastructure, like fire stations: quiet until the moment delay itself becomes fatal.
[P1.6] The research-niche model confuses knowledge with readiness. Grants expire, teams disperse, software decays, and a successful demonstration becomes history. Permanent funding creates replacement cycles, rehearsals, transparent civilian control, and international notification routines. The objection from low odds or competing budgets uses the wrong metric: it discounts ruin and ignores latency.
[P1.7] A bridge is not wasteful because few people fall into the river; it is justified because having no bridge, on the day it is needed, is intolerable. Before the sky supplies the deadline, governments should maintain the capability.
