[P1.1] When a patient's settled wish about her own body collides with someone else's account of what is "dignified" for her, one value must yield. Dignity should lose that comparison, for three reasons.
[P1.2] First, determinacy. Dignity has no fixed content at the bedside — it is invoked on both sides of the same case. Opponents of assisted dying say dignity means never choosing death; supporters say dignity means choosing how one dies. Same word, opposite prescriptions.
[P1.3] Autonomy has a clear owner and a clear test: what does the person whose body it is actually want? When values conflict, the one that settles cases without smuggling in the judge's own morality carries more weight.
[P1.4] Second, the track record. Detached from the patient's will, dignity appeals have done paternalism's dirty work: forced sterilizations of women deemed unfit, contraception and abortion refused "for her own dignity," court-ordered cesareans over a laboring woman's refusal, transfusions forced on Jehovah's Witnesses who decline blood. Each time, dignity served as a vessel into which physicians, churches, and legislatures poured their preferences, and the patient became a passenger in her own body. Autonomy's record is modern medicine's great corrective: informed consent and the right to refuse treatment.
[P1.5] Third, error asymmetry. Override autonomy in dignity's name and the harm is physical and often irreversible: a body penetrated against refusal, a pregnancy continued against will, a dying prolonged against pleading. Override dignity in autonomy's name and the harm is that onlookers must tolerate choices they dislike. One error violates a person; the other offends a sensibility.
[P1.6] Finally, dignity in medical choice is mostly downstream of autonomy anyway. A dignified death is dignified because chosen; dignified care is care the patient accepts. Remove the choice and what remains is not dignity but staging. The value that grounds the other outweighs the one it grounds.
[P1.7] That value is autonomy.

[C1.5] Dignity is inalienable. It does not require cognitive capacity or a "settled wish" to be invoked. It demands we provide compassionate, respectful care to the non-autonomous patient, protecting them from neglect, indignity, or abuse when their own voice is gone.
[C1.6] Autonomy is a vital tool for the capable, but dignity is the ultimate shield for the human. When the two conflict, the foundational value must outweigh the procedural one, because a choice stripped of dignity is no longer a human right.
