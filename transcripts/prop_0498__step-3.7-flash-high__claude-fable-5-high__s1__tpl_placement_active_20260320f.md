[P1.1] The central problem with letting a single entity control an app store and compete as a first-party developer on it is simple: that entity has both the motive and the means to rig the ecosystem in its own favor, at the expense of rivals, small developers, and consumers. Behavioral fixes like public promises not to self-preference have failed repeatedly, because the platform operator controls every lever of the store—search rankings, featured placements, rule enforcement, fee structures—and can adjust those levers in subtle, hard-to-detect ways to boost its own apps.
[P1.2] We see this play out constantly in dominant app ecosystems. Apple has repeatedly been found to prioritize its own first-party apps, including Apple Arcade and Apple TV+, in App Store search and featured slots, while burying rival game and video streaming services that refuse to pay its 30% cut. Google, for its part, has repeatedly been caught tweaking Play Store search algorithms to demote rivals like Spotify, Yelp, and small independent email clients to promote its own Gmail, YouTube Music, and Google Maps equivalents.
[P1.3] The harm falls hardest on the smallest players: an independent developer can spend years building a niche, high-quality app, only to have it buried in search results the moment the platform decides to launch a copycat first-party version. Consumers lose too, stuck with the platform’s often-inferior first-party apps because rivals were pushed out before they could gain traction. Structural separation is the only durable fix for this dynamic. It does not bar Apple or Google from building first-party apps—it only bars them from using their control of the store to give those apps an unfair leg up.
[P1.4] First-party apps would have to compete on quality and price, rather than rigged rules, to the benefit of every user and developer in the ecosystem.

[C1.1] Structural separation is the corporate death penalty, and the proposition asks us to impose it for an offense that targeted conduct rules can handle at a fraction of the cost. That mismatch between remedy and harm is the core flaw in the case, and everything else follows from it.
[C1.2] Start with what separation actually destroys. The reason an iPhone or Android phone works out of the box—with a camera app, maps, mail, and messaging that integrate with the hardware, security architecture, and accessibility features—is that the platform operator builds first-party apps in deep coordination with the OS and the store. Sever them into separate companies and you don't get a fairer market; you get worse defaults, slower security patching, fragmented privacy standards, and two firms that must now negotiate at arm's length over APIs they used to share freely.
[C1.3] Consumers pay for that friction. Small developers do too, since the billions platforms invest in SDKs, developer tools, and free global distribution are funded precisely by the integrated business model the proposition would dismantle.
[C1.4] Second, the remedy doesn't even fix the alleged disease. A spun-off app store still controls rankings, featured slots, and fee structures—and now it can sell preferential placement to the highest bidder, including its former parent. Gatekeeping power is a property of running any dominant marketplace, not of vertical integration. Self-preferencing concerns are answerable with proportionate tools: nondiscrimination mandates, ranking transparency, algorithmic audits, sideloading and alternative payment requirements—the approach Europe's Digital Markets Act takes without blowing up a single company.
[C1.5] Finally, notice what the proposition demands as a trigger: merely running a store and competing on it. No finding of dominance, no proof of harm, no chance to comply. That's punishment for a business structure, not for conduct. When a scalpel exists and works, choosing the chainsaw isn't toughness—it's recklessness, and consumers are the ones standing closest to the blade.
