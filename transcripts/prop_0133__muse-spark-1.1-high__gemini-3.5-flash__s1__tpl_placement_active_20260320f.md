[P1.1] There are lines a decent society does not cross even to save a few pennies. Keeping a hen in a space smaller than a sheet of paper for her entire life, unable to spread her wings. Locking a breeding sow in a crate so tight she cannot turn around for month after month. Keeping a veal calf isolated in darkness unable to walk.
[P1.2] These most restrictive systems are not about efficiency, they are about squeezing the last drop of biology from a sentient animal at the cost of constant suffering.
[P1.3] That suffering persists only because the price is hidden. In a supermarket you cannot see the crate. Producers who want to do better get undercut by those who don't. This is a textbook market failure that only government can fix by setting a floor.
[P1.4] And we know the floor works: a dozen states and the entire European Union have already phased out battery cages and gestation crates. Shelves stayed full. Farmers adapted to enriched colonies, cage-free barns, and group housing.
[P1.5] The price of that adaptation is exactly what the motion says: modest. When every producer plays by the same better rules, the bump spreads across millions of consumers — pennies per dozen eggs, cents per pound of pork. That is affordable because it reflects the true cost of producing food without torture.
[P1.6] Meanwhile extreme confinement brings its own public costs: disease spreads faster when animals are immobile and stressed, routine antibiotics to keep them alive fuels resistance, and workers endure horrific conditions inside those sheds.
[P1.7] If food were about to double, the trade-off would be hard. It isn't. The question is whether a civilization that can afford alternatives should knowingly mandate lifelong immobilization to save a dime. Governments exist to answer yes, some efficiencies are not worth it.
[P1.8] Phasing out the worst confinement aligns our laws with the values most people already hold and ends an indefensible bargain.
