[P1.1] A democracy's legitimacy rests on a simple claim: that those who govern speak for the governed. But when only half the electorate votes—and the half that stays home is disproportionately poor, young, and marginalized—that claim collapses. We end up governed by the preferences of the comfortable and the organized, while the absent are not neutral; they are unrepresented. Compulsory turnout is how we close that gap.
[P1.2] My case rests on three pillars.
[P1.3] First, representativeness. Voluntary systems systematically skew toward the affluent and the elderly, because those groups vote most reliably. Mandating participation flattens that distortion, producing electorates that actually mirror the population. Australia, with decades of enforced compulsory voting and turnout consistently above 90%, demonstrates that a stable democracy can do this without authoritarian drift.
[P1.4] The result isn't tyranny—it's parliaments that must court everyone, not just habitual voters.
[P1.5] Second, incentive realignment. When politicians know the poor and disengaged stay home, they ignore them and campaign on wedge issues that mobilize their base. When everyone votes, the rational strategy shifts toward broad, median appeals. Universal turnout pushes parties toward the center and toward issues that affect the whole citizenry—a structural moderating force that voluntary systems lack.
[P1.6] Third, the penalty is modest and proportional, exactly as the motion specifies. We are talking about a small civil fine, comparable to a minor traffic infraction, with exemptions for genuine hardship and the option to spoil one's ballot. This is not forced speech; it is a duty to show up, no different in kind from jury duty or census participation—obligations stable democracies already impose without controversy.
[P1.7] The burden CON must meet is steep. They must show that a low-turnout, skewed electorate produces more legitimate representation than a near-universal one—and that a small fine is a graver imposition than systematically governing people who never had their preferences counted.
[P1.8] Voting is not merely a private right to be exercised at whim. In a self-governing society, participation is the price of legitimacy. Mandating it makes the outcome genuinely ours.
