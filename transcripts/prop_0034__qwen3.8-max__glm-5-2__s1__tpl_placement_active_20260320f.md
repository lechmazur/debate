[P1.1] The core principle is simple: when a company profits from a replica of a person’s voice, that person should have the right to say yes or no before distribution.
[P1.2] Affirmative consent is the minimum rule that fits the risk. Modern voice cloning can take a few seconds of public audio and produce a persuasive replica. Once a platform commercially distributes that replica—in ads, audiobooks, chatbots, prank apps, or synthetic cameos—the original person loses control over one of the most intimate signals of identity. The harm is immediate: listeners can be deceived, reputations damaged, and economic opportunities diverted.
[P1.3] A celebrity may find her voice selling a product she despises; an ordinary person may hear his voice used in scam calls or humiliating content. Because distribution is scalable and irreversible, after-the-fact takedowns are weak.
[P1.4] Consent also solves the evidentiary problem. If platforms and media companies must obtain and keep proof of affirmative permission, there is a clear line between licensed imitation and theft. That record helps victims, courts, and regulators identify who authorized what and which distributor is responsible. It does not require banning voice synthesis.
[P1.5] It requires commercial actors to do what commercial actors already do with music, likeness, and personal data: clear rights before exploitation.
[P1.6] The opposition will likely invoke creativity and speech. But the motion targets commercial distribution of identifiable clones, not every parody, news report, or transformative work. Where a person is identifiable and the product is commercial, the baseline should be permission, not presumptive taking. A rule that lets companies monetize a person’s vocal identity unless she objects reverses the burden onto the vulnerable party.
[P1.7] The decisive question is who bears the cost of uncertainty. PRO places it on the platform that profits from the clone. CON places it on the individual whose voice is copied. That asymmetry is enough to affirm.
