[P1.1] Pay secrecy is not a neutral default. It is a policy choice that quietly transfers power from workers to whoever controls the information. When large employers hide pay bands and promotion rates, employees negotiate blind, discrimination hides in the gaps, and "managerial flexibility" too often just means the freedom to pay two people differently for the same work and never explain why. My case rests on three claims.
[P1.2] First, disclosure corrects a market failure. Wages are supposed to reflect the value of work, but that only happens when both sides have information. Right now one side—the employer—knows the whole distribution, and the worker knows almost nothing. Publishing pay bands by role lets people see whether they're inside the range or being lowballed.
[P1.3] That's not a distortion of the market; it's what a functioning market requires.
[P1.4] Second, disclosure is the most reliable tool we have against pay discrimination. Gaps by gender and race persist not mainly because managers are villains, but because bias thrives in the dark. When a woman can see that her role's band tops out higher than her salary, or that men in her role get promoted at twice her rate, she can act—and, crucially, the employer knows she can, which changes behavior before a complaint is ever filed. Sunlight disciplines conduct that lawsuits catch only years too late.
[P1.5] Third, promotion-rate disclosure targets the exact place where flexibility becomes favoritism. Pay bands alone can be gamed if advancement stays a black box. Publishing promotion rates by role reveals whether "we promote the best people" is true or whether the same demographic keeps rising. If a company's numbers are fair, disclosure vindicates them.
[P1.6] If they aren't, that is precisely the flexibility we should want reduced.
[P1.7] The motion concedes the cost—less managerial discretion—and I accept it head-on. Discretion that cannot survive being seen is discretion we should be suspicious of. The question is not whether managers lose some latitude, but whether that latitude was ever legitimate. Vote PRO.
