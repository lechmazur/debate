[C1.1] The strongest case against recognizing ecocide as an international crime is not that severe environmental harm is tolerable; it is that this tool would weaken the very accountability it promises. International criminal law is built for clear, bounded atrocities: commanders ordering killings, officials directing torture. Environmental destruction is often cumulative, scientifically contested, permitted by states, and spread across corporations, regulators, consumers, and decades. Turning “exceptional destruction” into a global crime requires thresholds and mental states that cannot be made precise enough to prevent arbitrary or politicized enforcement.
[C1.2] PRO’s appeal to narrow drafting assumes global agreement where even domestic legislatures fail.
[C1.3] PRO’s boardroom example sounds compelling, but criminal liability demands proof beyond reasonable doubt that a particular individual knowingly caused a specific catastrophic outcome. In real disasters, causation runs through subsidiaries, licensed emissions, local corruption, and lawful approvals. A sweeping ecocide charge invites spectacle, not conviction. It also encourages defendants to portray themselves as victims of vague global morality, turning courts into battles over development policy rather than proof of guilt.
[C1.4] The international forum makes this worse. Criminal tribunals already struggle with selectivity, limited jurisdiction, and enormous cost. Adding environmental cases would dilute them further and risk prosecutions that target weaker states or political losers while major polluters with geopolitical protection escape. Complementarity cannot cure this; vague international standards migrate into domestic courts.
[C1.5] The promised backstop becomes a symbolic substitute for hard work: stronger domestic regulators, civil liability, cleanup funds, disclosure rules, sanctions for illegal dumping, and enforcement against corporate veil abuse.
[C1.6] There are also democratic costs. Countries at different income levels must make trade-offs among energy access, agriculture, industry, and conservation. Elevating those disputes to international crime risks chilling legitimate policy and investment, because any large project could be framed as future ecocide. The right answer is not impunity; it is precise, enforceable law.
[C1.7] Recognition would sacrifice clarity, legitimacy, and effectiveness for a dramatic label.
