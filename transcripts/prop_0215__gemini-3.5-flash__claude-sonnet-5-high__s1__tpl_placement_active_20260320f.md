[C1.1] The proposition asks us to endorse a blanket rule: that former state churches and religious orders, wherever they played "central roles," owe financial contributions to reparative funds. That is a much stronger claim than "some settlements are just." It demands an institutional duty divorced from consent, capacity, or context—and that is exactly where it collapses.
[C1.2] First, primary liability sits with the state, not its instrument. Residential schools existed because governments legislated compulsory attendance, funded per-child subsidies, and set policy. Churches operated schools as contracted agents of a state program they did not design and could not refuse without losing standing. Joint enterprise, as PRO invokes it, actually cuts against them: in joint enterprise law, liability tracks control and benefit, and here the state held both the legislative power and the purse.
[C1.3] Treating the operational agent as equally or primarily liable lets the actual architect—the state—dilute its own reckoning behind the church's wallet.
[C1.4] Second, "the church" is not a stable legal person across a century. Dioceses, orders, and parishes today are financially and often legally distinct from the entities that ran these schools; many of their assets fund hospitals, shelters, and schools serving the very communities harmed, including Indigenous congregants. A mandatory extraction rule doesn't reach into some hidden vault of ill-gotten gold—it cuts operating budgets for present-day social services, punishing today's vulnerable to satisfy yesterday's ledger.
[C1.5] Third, forced financial levies invite a moral-hazard trade: government points to church payments as "justice done," while declining to fund its own share of truth commissions, land return, or language revitalization—the things survivors actually say they want. Compare Canada's residential school settlements: government funding dwarfed church contributions, yet public attention fixated on the churches, letting the state's far larger culpability recede from view.
[C1.6] Better justice comes from negotiated, capacity-sensitive agreements tied to actual historical involvement—not an abstract "should" that treats institutions as infinite piggy banks for state-created harm.
