[P1.5] Quality matters because a bare connection that cannot sustain telehealth, remote work, or online exams is not modern utility service. Universal service does not mean the service must be free; it means it must be available and affordable under public accountability, as water and electricity are. The question is not whether governments can afford to enforce internet access; it is whether they can afford to leave something this fundamental to uneven private choice.

[C1.1] Water is molecules; electricity is electrons. Both deliver a uniform, unchanging commodity through static grids. The internet is fundamentally different: a rapidly evolving, multi-layered ecosystem driven by constant technological churn. Forcing it into the rigid legal framework of twentieth-century public utilities is a profound category error that will stifle innovation, trigger wasteful litigation, and distort public budgets.
[C1.2] First, "high-quality internet" is a moving target that defies static statutory definition. Potable water is chemically verifiable; electricity maintains a standardized voltage. Broadband, however, evolves continuously across speed, latency, and architecture—from copper and cable to fiber, 5G, and satellite constellations. Codifying an enforceable legal standard either locks in yesterday’s technical specifications or sparks endless lawsuits whenever network congestion or severe weather causes performance to dip below arbitrary statutory baselines.
[C1.3] Innovation is replaced by compliance.
[C1.4] Second, legal enforceability creates catastrophic fiscal distortions. A universal service entitlement obligates the state to ensure qualifying connections to every single dwelling, regardless of cost. Trenching physical infrastructure to an isolated mountain cabin can easily cost tens of thousands of dollars for a single household. Forcing capital into extreme last-mile builds to satisfy legal mandates starves funding for smarter, highly adaptable alternatives—such as low-Earth-orbit satellite arrays and fixed wireless—that bridge digital divides without bankrupting the state.
[C1.5] Third, utility-style mandates destroy the private investment that builds modern networks. Unlike power lines that last for decades, digital networks require relentless capital reinvestment to handle exponential data growth. Imposing universal service obligations, along with the rate regulations that inevitably follow, compresses margins and drives away private capital. The continuous, multi-billion-dollar cost of network modernization is thus shifted entirely onto taxpayers.
[C1.6] Universal connectivity is an admirable goal, but legal enforceability is the wrong instrument. Real inclusion is delivered through targeted vouchers, spectrum deregulation, and competitive private-public partnerships—not by cramming a dynamic technological frontier into the litigious straightjacket of municipal plumbing.

[P2.4] The fiscal warning [C1.4] exaggerates by equating enforcement with trenching fiber to every isolated cabin. The far larger distortion is households without the connection needed to work, learn, or receive care.
[P2.5] Nor does enforceability require rate regulation or public takeover. Universal-service funds and build-out conditions can give providers predictable revenue and guaranteed demand, not shrinking investment. Vouchers and deregulation may help at the margin, but they leave the decisive gap: no provider has an obligation to serve the people left behind. The real difference is between a legal right and a policy hope.
