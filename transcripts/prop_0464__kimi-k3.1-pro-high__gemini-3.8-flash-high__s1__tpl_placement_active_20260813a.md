[P1.6] They would also retain practical reasons to use the UN for coordination, legitimacy, and burden-sharing.

[C1.1] The veto is not an oversight or an arbitrary privilege; it is the structural foundation that keeps the United Nations alive. It was forged from the catastrophic collapse of the League of Nations, which disintegrated precisely because it operated on the fiction that sovereign great powers could be outvoted and coerced into compliance. The veto reflects an unyielding reality: you cannot enforce collective security against a nuclear-armed power without precipitating global war.
[C1.2] The proposition asks us to abolish this mechanism "even if major powers threaten to withdraw." That condition concedes the entire debate. PRO imagines that removing the veto creates an agile, principled Council. In truth, it destroys the institution.
[C1.3] International law possesses no standing army; Council resolutions carry weight only because the states capable of destroying the globe are bound inside the same institutional tent.
[C1.4] If the United States, China, or Russia exits, the Security Council does not become an engine of justice—it becomes an irrelevant rump assembly. An unenforceable resolution passed against an absent nuclear power does not rescue vulnerable populations; it merely demonstrates the Council’s impotence, or worse, transforms the UN into a partisan alliance that escalates regional clashes into systemic conflict.
[C1.5] PRO decries the veto as paralysis [P1.1], but institutional gridlock is vastly preferable to great-power war. The veto was never intended to guarantee moral outcomes in every crisis; it was built as an emergency brake to ensure the UN could never be weaponized by one faction of superpowers to mandate conflict against another.
[C1.6] Abolishing the veto does not eliminate great-power unilateralism; it simply drives it outside diplomatic guardrails, replacing imperfect multilateral containment with unrestrained brinkmanship. An institution that produces frustrating deadlock while keeping rival nuclear states at the same table fulfills its most vital existential mandate. We cannot burn down the table in pursuit of an unenforceable ideal.

Condemnation, investigations, targeted sanctions, humanitarian measures, peacekeeping mandates, and authoritative findings do not automatically become war. Abolition changes who can block those decisions; it does not make the Council capable of compelling Washington, Moscow, or Beijing to fight.
[P2.2] Their “emergency brake” [C1.5] therefore stops far more than the catastrophic scenario they invoke.
[P2.3] Nor does gridlock reliably prevent great-power war. A permanent member can still invade, arm proxies, or act unilaterally; the veto merely ensures that its victims cannot obtain a collective response through the Council. CON calls this “containment,” but containment of what? Not the aggressor—only the institution’s ability to respond.
[P2.4] Keeping rivals seated at a table where one can nullify everyone else is not meaningful diplomacy if the seating itself becomes the achievement.
[P2.5] The withdrawal argument also proves too much. If any threat to leave requires surrendering the rules, every major power possesses a second veto over reform. The motion’s “even if” clause does not concede that abolition destroys the UN; it requires us to judge whether membership purchased through permanent impunity is worth preserving. A Council retained on those terms may survive organizationally while failing exactly when collective security matters.
[P2.6] Departure is not costless for the departing power either. It forfeits agenda-setting, legitimacy, coordination, and influence over measures others may pursue. Even if withdrawal occurs, resolutions supported by remaining members are not inherently impotent: sanctions and diplomatic pressure derive from participating states, not from the absent state’s consent.
[P2.7] The real comparison is not deadlock versus nuclear war. It is possible collective action, still constrained by political realities, versus guaranteed legal obstruction at a permanent member’s discretion. We are not burning down the table. We are ending the rule that lets one guest overturn it whenever the discussion concerns their own conduct.
