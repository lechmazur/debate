[P1.1] When the state can strip your Medicaid, deport your family, or add years to your sentence, you should be entitled to a human who decides, who can explain why, and who can be held to account. That is what a legal right to a human decision-maker guarantees.
[P1.2] First, accountability. Government power is different from private convenience. It must come with reason-giving and reviewability. You cannot cross-examine a risk score, you cannot ask an algorithm to reconsider its weighing of your evidence, and you cannot hold code responsible when it is wrong.
[P1.3] A human decision-maker restores the basic due process chain: someone must own the decision, put reasons on the record, and face appeal.
[P1.4] Second, these decisions demand judgment, not just classification. Benefit eligibility, asylum, sentencing - all involve discretion, hardship, intent, credibility, and mercy. An automated system can pattern-match past cases, but it cannot exercise equity to say this case, though similar on paper, deserves a different outcome. Only a human can temper a rule with conscience where the law explicitly allows it.
[P1.5] Third, without this right, errors become catastrophes at scale. We have seen state systems auto-flag unemployment recipients as fraudsters, auto-cut home-care hours for disabled people - thousands wrongly punished before anyone noticed. One human clerk might make one mistake. One bad model makes the same mistake ten thousand times at once, with no one to stop it.
[P1.6] A right to a human is the circuit breaker. That difference alone justifies a legal right.
[P1.7] This does not ban AI assistance. It says when life-altering state power is used, you can demand a person at the wheel. Efficiency cannot outweigh that. In high-stakes government action, legitimacy is not measured by speed or average accuracy, but by your right to be seen and heard as a person, by a person.
