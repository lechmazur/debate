[P1.1] Unlimited PTO is a promise; a four-day week is a schedule. That difference decides this debate.
[P1.2] Start with how each policy actually delivers rest. Under unlimited PTO, every day off is an individual request, negotiated against a manager's mood, a team's deadlines, and the quiet fear of looking less committed than the colleague who never leaves. With no stated norm, people anchor on the most cautious person in the room. This is why it is widely observed that employees under "unlimited" plans often take about the same time off as before, or less.
[P1.3] Because nothing accrues, the company also no longer owes them a payout when they leave. The benefit exists on paper and evaporates in practice, most of all for junior staff, caregivers, and anyone too anxious to ask.
[P1.4] A four-day week flips the default. Nobody has to ask, and nobody is singled out. Rest arrives every week for everyone, whether or not they are confident enough to claim it. And because the whole team is off together, work doesn't pile up while you're gone.
[P1.5] Anyone who has returned from vacation to four hundred emails knows that time off you pay for later isn't recovery. A recurring, shared day is.
[P1.6] That is why the well-known multi-company trials in the UK and elsewhere reported lower burnout, better sleep, and fewer resignations, and why most participating firms chose to keep the schedule afterward. People stay at a job that gives them fifty extra days of life a year. They don't stay for a policy they are afraid to use.
[P1.7] The word "pilot" matters as well. A pilot is time-bound, measured, and reversible. It forces the organization to cut bloated meetings and redesign the work, and it tracks attrition and burnout against a baseline. If it fails, you stop.
[P1.8] Unlimited PTO has no measurement and no redesign: the workload is identical, and the employee is left to manage the guilt.
[P1.9] So the question is which policy changes what people actually experience. One changes the week itself. The other changes only the handbook. [clipped]
