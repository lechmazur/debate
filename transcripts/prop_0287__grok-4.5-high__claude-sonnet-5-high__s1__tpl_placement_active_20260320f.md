[P1.1] Fixed terms restore the balance that life tenure has broken. Courts are not meant to freeze the values of one election for a generation or more. When justices serve for decades after the president or parliament that chose them has left the scene, the court becomes a holdover aristocracy. [P1.2] Fixed, staggered, non-renewable terms reverse that: each democratic cycle gains predictable influence over the court’s composition without turning judges into short-term politicians.
[P1.3] Independence does not require eternal tenure. It requires insulation during the exercise of power. A single eighteen-year term with no reappointment possible does exactly that. The justice has no incentive to curry favor with future governments; the end date is known and non-negotiable.
[P1.4] Rulings against the sitting majority carry no career penalty. By contrast, life tenure itself creates independence problems—strategic retirements timed for preferred successors, late-career decline, or legacy grandstanding that political actors then exploit. The exit becomes as politicized as the entrance.
[P1.5] The weighing is straightforward. The democratic deficit of life tenure is large and chronic: a court majority can outlast repeated popular repudiations of the coalition that created it, breeding distrust that corrodes the institution from outside. Any independence risk from fixed terms is modest and design-dependent; non-renewability and length close the reappointment channel that truly endangers impartiality. Public trust in the judiciary rises when citizens see the court regularly refreshed by democratic agents yet shielded while it decides.
[P1.6] That legitimacy dividend outweighs residual independence concerns, because a court perceived as permanently unaccountable cannot remain independent for long.

[C1.2] Look at what fixed terms actually change. Appointment power still sits with presidents and legislatures. All fixed terms do is guarantee that every few years, without fail, there is a fresh high-stakes battle over who fills the seat. That is not a democratic dividend—it is a recurring incentive for court-packing.
[C1.3] A party that wins two consecutive election cycles can capture a majority of an eighteen-year staggered bench outright, especially if health or resignation timing among incumbents clusters unfavorably. Life tenure at least makes vacancies unpredictable and unsynchronized with any single coalition's run of luck; fixed terms make capture calculable and plannable.
[C1.4] Non-renewability doesn't insulate justices the way PRO claims either. A justice with a known exit date still has a career after the bench—law firms, foundations, diplomatic posts, future political appointments. Knowing exactly when they'll need those doors to open is worse for independence than an open-ended tenure with no forced departure at all. The incentive to please future patrons doesn't disappear at a fixed date; it just becomes schedulable, which makes it easier, not harder, for outside actors to time pressure and rewards around it.
[C1.5] And the diagnosed "holdover aristocracy" problem is overstated: real courts already turn over through death, retirement, and health, giving successive majorities recurring appointment chances without needing to convert the judiciary into a clock-driven institution.
[C1.6] So the trade is not modest independence risk against large legitimacy gain. It's structural, calculable capture risk against a legitimacy gain that assumes political actors won't exploit the very calendar being handed to them.
