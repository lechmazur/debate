[P1.2] The first principle is carrying capacity. When demand exceeds what a site can safely hold, some rationing is unavoidable. The real choice is not between preference and neutrality; it is between rationing deliberately and rationing through whatever system currently wins: fastest clicks, flexible work schedules, packaged tour allocations, or greater purchasing power. A visitor able to pay peak prices and plan months ahead is not inherently more deserving than a resident whose school, family, or work life surrounds the site all year.

[C1.1] The motion gets the diagnosis right and the prescription backwards. A fragile site is damaged by total footfall, handling, humidity, crowding—not by the postcode of the person making the footprint. Resident priority booking does not reduce a single visitor’s impact; it only changes who holds the ticket. A local discount can even increase peak demand among residents, precisely when the site can least absorb it.
[C1.2] If the problem is carrying capacity, the remedy must ration capacity directly: timed entries, hard caps, closures of sensitive rooms, tourist levies, and better dispersal. Residency is not a conservation tool.
[C1.3] Nor is it a fair one. A museum is a public institution, not a municipal club. “Residents” are not a homogeneous deserving group. A wealthy local homeowner and a low-income nearby worker may be treated differently by an arbitrary municipal boundary; commuters, domestic taxpayers, and people whose families have ties to the place but live a mile outside it are excluded. The motion replaces one crude rationing mechanism—ability to pay or plan—with another crude mechanism—a passport check—and calls it justice.
[C1.4] Worse, it inverts the relevant scarcity. Local people can visit across the year, on quiet mornings, in low season, through school programs and civic partnerships. Many tourists have one trip, fixed dates, and no second chance. At the exact moment capacity is most constrained, the motion gives priority to the more flexible group and pushes out the less flexible one.
[C1.5] That is not correcting bias; it is manufacturing it.
[C1.6] There are better ways to preserve the civic relationship: free or discounted resident days outside peak, local school access, community programming, and direct congestion management. Those protect heritage and residents without turning fragile peak-season access into a residency entitlement. The proposition offers symbolism at the expense of conservation and equal access.
