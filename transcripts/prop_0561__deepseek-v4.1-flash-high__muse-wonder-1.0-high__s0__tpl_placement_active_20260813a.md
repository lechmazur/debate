[P1.1] Platform gig work has turned a simple principle upside down: hours of labor can produce revenue and risk, but zero health coverage, zero paid leave, zero retirement. A driver who logs thirty hours a week for a ride-hail app can be injured on the job and find that the platform's algorithm counted her minutes only when paying her, not when protecting her. Government should end that asymmetry by requiring prorated benefits tied to hours worked.
[P1.2] This is not about forcing every gig worker into a traditional employee box. “Prorated” is the key: log ten hours, accrue ten hours of health subsidy, leave, and retirement credit. Platforms remit into portable accounts, so workers can carry protection across apps and jobs. That preserves the flexibility gig workers value. Social Security and Medicare already tie contributions to earnings.
[P1.3] If an app can calculate a fare by the minute, it can calculate a benefit contribution by the hour.
[P1.4] CON will call this a job-killer. But the costs don't vanish if platforms skip them; they are dumped onto workers, hospitals, and taxpayers through unpaid medical bills, public coverage, and old-age poverty. A per-hour contribution makes the price of gig labor honest and stops platforms from undercutting competitors that do provide benefits.
[P1.5] Tying benefits to hours also prevents overreach: someone who opens an app once doesn't get a full-time entitlement. Protection scales with work—part-time to full-time, single-app to multi-app. Government sets a floor, not a schedule.
[P1.6] The real choice is not flexibility versus security. It is whether flexibility can be financed by denying security. Prorated benefits let workers keep the hours they want while ensuring those hours build a safety net, not just a platform's valuation. When a worker gives an hour, the law should make that hour earn wages and build protection.

[C1.3] The only way to police that is invasive tracking, rigid definitions, and constant disputes over minutes — turning independent, drop-in work into monitored shift work.
[C1.4] Platforms will not simply absorb a new per-hour tax on top of pay. The first people priced out will not be full-time drivers the proposal imagines. They will be the student delivering five hours a week, the parent working school hours, and the retiree earning supplemental income — people for whom low-barrier access matters precisely because they cannot take a conventional job.
[C1.5] Proration also sounds precise until it meets the benefits themselves. Health coverage is not divisible like a fare: a few dollars per hour scattered across three apps does not buy a usable insurance premium, yet administering micro-accounts and pooling risk eats much of it in friction. Paid leave assumes a schedule to be absent from. A forced retirement slice is worse than cash for a worker with urgent rent, debt, or coverage already provided through a spouse or a main job.
[C1.6] The mandate takes flexible earnings and compulsorily converts them into locked, fragmented benefits many workers would not choose.
[C1.7] That is not security. It is fewer trips, lower take-home pay, and fewer doors open to casual earners, in exchange for accounts too small to protect anyone.

[P2.6] CON's real claim is that workers prefer no protection to prorated protection. But a choice made when benefits are absent and rent is due is not proof of preference; it is proof of bargaining power. The proposition sets a floor. It leaves hours flexible and benefits proportional.
