[P1.1] European and Latin American cities already have open drug use. The question is not whether it happens, but where and with what consequences. Right now it happens alone in alleys, stairwells, public toilets. That is where people die and where disorder is created.
[P1.2] A regulated supervised consumption site reverses that geography. A person uses their own substance, under observation by health workers with naloxone, oxygen and a direct line to emergency care. Alone, a respiratory depression becomes death. Observed, it becomes a reversible medical event.
[P1.3] You cannot die of an overdose when someone trained is ready to breathe for you. Across decades of sites in Switzerland, the Netherlands, Germany and Spain, the record is consistent: thousands of overdoses reversed, zero deaths inside the facility. That alone satisfies the motion's first goal.
[P1.4] The second goal follows from the same shift indoors. Public injecting generates public disorder precisely because it is public: discarded syringes in playgrounds, blood in metro stations, residents calling police, emergency crews scattered across neighborhoods to respond to chaotic overdoses in the street. A permitted, regulated site concentrates that activity into a clinical room with safe disposal, hygiene rules, and no dealing allowed. Police in surrounding blocks report fewer public injecting sightings and less syringe litter because the alternative is no longer the street.
[P1.5] It is order, not chaos. It is the city reclaiming control of a problem it already pays for.
[P1.6] Regulation is the key word. Permit means cities can set location, hours, security, staffing. It is not liberalization of drugs; no drugs are sold there. It is a choke point that connects people who otherwise avoid services to wound care, HIV and hepatitis testing, and treatment referrals.
[P1.7] In contexts from Frankfurt to Bogotá, where punitive sweeps have only moved the problem one street over and where use of crack and basuco is highly visible, this health foothold reduces both bodies lost and cities made unlivable.

[C1.1] The proposition fails because it measures success at the wrong place: the moment of injection. A supervised room may prevent death inside its walls, but cities should be judged on whether they reduce total overdose mortality, dependency, and street-level disorder. Permitting consumption sites does not reliably do either.
[C1.2] First, the safety benefit is narrower than PRO suggests. If a site reverses overdoses on-site, that does not prove it prevents more deaths than outreach, naloxone distribution, and rapid treatment would. People who use drugs are not evenly distributed near facilities; many die alone at home or in places far from a clinic. A prevented death inside a room is real, but it is not the same as fewer deaths across the city.
[C1.3] A fixed site can also attract higher-risk use to its surroundings, creating queues, open intoxication, and discarded paraphernalia just outside the door. The disorder is not erased; it is relocated and often made more visible.
[C1.4] Second, public order is not only about syringes. Residents experience disorder as fear, noise, aggressive panhandling, and drug dealing near a known consumption point. Even if no dealing is allowed inside, markets follow users. In Latin American cities where municipal control is uneven, a permitted site can become a territorial prize for gangs or a permanent encampment anchor.
[C1.5] In European cities, it can concentrate politically volatile misery in one neighborhood, provoking backlash that destroys broader harm-reduction coalitions.
[C1.6] Third, the stronger policy is treatment-first: low-threshold methadone and buprenorphine, psychiatric care, housing, and assertive street outreach. These interventions reduce the demand for dangerous use rather than supervising it. Finite budgets make this choice concrete. The PRO’s model tells cities to budget for rooms where people can inject; the better answer is to budget for pathways out.
[C1.7] If the goal is fewer deaths and calmer streets, permission is not enough—cities should choose interventions that reduce consumption, not institutionalize it.
