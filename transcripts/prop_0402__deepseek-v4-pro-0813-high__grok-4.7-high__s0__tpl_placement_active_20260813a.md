[P1.1] A tenant’s rent source is only as good as the tenant’s ability to pay and maintain the unit. Rejecting an otherwise qualified applicant solely because rent comes from a voucher or other lawful public assistance punishes people for receiving help, not for being bad tenants.
[P1.2] The right test is whether the applicant is likely to pay and comply with the lease. Credit, income adequacy, rental history, references, and background checks answer that. Voucher status answers none. A family can pass every neutral screen and still be denied because a landlord dislikes program paperwork or the tenants the program serves.
[P1.3] That is a categorical penalty on lawful assistance, not a judgment about risk.
[P1.4] Voucher-assisted rent is not unreliable rent. The public agency contracts to pay its share directly on schedule; that portion is often steadier than a private paycheck. The landlord still collects the tenant share, can require a deposit, and can evict for nonpayment or lease violations. This rule would not force anyone to accept a bad tenant.
[P1.5] It only removes payment source as a proxy for bad tenancy.
[P1.6] If rejection remains legal, voucher programs cannot fulfill their purpose. Many families have a limited time to use a voucher; each source-based denial burns that time and traps children in high-poverty, segregated neighborhoods. Because voucher holders disproportionately include Black, disabled, and female-headed households, refusing vouchers functions as a backdoor for status discrimination. It also steers public aid away from safer, higher-opportunity areas.
[P1.7] The line is clear: landlords may screen strictly; they may not make the source of lawful rent the sole disqualifier. That protects property interests without letting stigma or convenience block stable housing.

[C1.1] A voucher is not another paycheck. It is a government contract laid over a private lease: mandatory inspections, rent ceilings, payment holds when a unit fails a checklist, agency addenda, and a bureaucracy that decides when money moves. Barring landlords from declining that package solely because of its source pretends the source is irrelevant to cost, control, and risk. It is not.
[C1.2] Payment reliability is only one variable, and even that is overstated. An agency can pay on schedule one month and freeze the next over a failed inspection or an unresolved repair. The tenant’s share can still go unpaid. Eviction, already slow, slows further when a third party must be noticed or appeased.
[C1.3] Deposits do not cover months of vacancy, legal fees, or a unit held empty while an inspector reschedules. Credit checks and rental history cannot capture costs the program creates after move-in.
[C1.4] The rule also turns a voluntary subsidy into compulsory dealing with the state. Ownership includes choosing counterparties and payment terms. A landlord with two units cannot staff compliance or swallow a rent cap below what the unit earns on the open market. Rational owners will not become more welcoming.
[C1.5] They will sell, convert to owner-occupancy, tighten every other screen, or exit renting. The families this ban claims to help then face fewer units, not more.
[C1.6] A ban will not end segregation. Owners in higher-opportunity areas who can avoid the program’s burdens will avoid landlording rather than accept vouchers. Fights over whether a denial was solely source-based punish caution and reward pretext, without making inspections faster or payments certain.
[C1.7] The right question is not whether stigma is ugly. It is whether the state may force private owners to accept a payment system that changes the deal. It may not. Make vouchers timely, rents realistic, and inspections less punitive, and landlords will take them without a ban.
[C1.8] Coercion substitutes for fixing the program. That is the wrong bargain.

[P2.5] On coercion: property rights are not absolute. Landlords already operate under fair-housing, habitability, and deposit rules. They retain broad screening.
