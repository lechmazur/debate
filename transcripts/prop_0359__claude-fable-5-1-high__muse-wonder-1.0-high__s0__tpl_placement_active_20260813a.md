[P1.1] Every ride-hail trip is a three-party transaction in which two of the parties are blindfolded. The rider sees what she paid. The driver sees what he earned. Only the platform sees both numbers, and it has every incentive to keep it that way, because the gap between them is its revenue.
[P1.2] Picture an ordinary airport run. The rider pays about forty dollars and assumes most of it reaches the person who drove her. The driver receives about eighteen and assumes the rider must have paid something close to that, perhaps with a surge he never saw. Neither suspects the platform kept more than half.
[P1.3] That is not a hypothetical shape; drivers who compare screenshots with their passengers routinely find per-trip take rates swinging from modest to enormous with no explanation. Opacity is the precondition for this. Once pricing was "upfront" and decoupled from what drivers are paid, the platform could price each side at whatever it would tolerate, separately, and nobody could check.
[P1.4] Markets discipline prices only when prices can be seen. The old taxi meter was crude, but everyone in the car watched the same number tick. Finance, airlines, and restaurant bills all learned the same lesson: a mandatory itemized breakdown costs almost nothing and changes behavior at the margin. A driver who sees he is getting thirty percent of a fare can decline that trip; a rider who sees the platform pocketing most of a surge can switch apps.
[P1.5] That friction is the point. It is how a competitive market is supposed to work.
[P1.6] The motion asks for a default, not a ban on algorithms. The platform may keep its pricing logic; it must simply print the result: rider paid X, driver received Y, platform kept Z. Trade secrets do not include the final receipt. Confusion is a design problem solved with one line of text.
[P1.7] And if showing the split would embarrass the company, that is the strongest argument for showing it.

[C1.1] The rider price and the driver payment are not two slices of one pie waiting to be revealed. They are two separate prices for two separate services, deliberately decoupled — and forcing them into a single split by default would mislead both sides while making both worse off.
[C1.2] A rider buys a guaranteed upfront price, insurance, payment processing, refunds, support, and a car that arrives. A driver accepts a trip after seeing his own pay, distance, and duration. The platform bridging them also pays city taxes and fees, rider promotions, driver bonuses, and the empty miles and unmatched trips that keep the network liquid. None of that appears in "rider paid X, driver got Y, platform kept Z."
[C1.3] Z is gross retention before those costs, not profit pocketed, yet the receipt would teach everyone to read it as profit. On a discounted trip a driver can earn more than the rider paid; on another, the margin funds that discount. A per-trip snapshot hides that portfolio.
[C1.4] Make that percentage the public test of fairness and platforms will stop using it flexibly. They will be punished for charging a high-willingness airport rider more while paying a stable rate, even when that margin subsidizes off-peak guarantees, short-trip bonuses, or service in thin neighborhoods. To keep each trip looking defensible, they will tie pay tighter to the individual fare, making earnings more volatile, guarantees thinner, and rider prices higher. Drivers do not pay rent with a percentage of a stranger's coupon-adjusted fare; they pay it with predictable earnings per hour, which decoupling was designed to protect.
[C1.5] The proper transparency is clear: tell each side, before it agrees, exactly what it will pay or receive and what fees apply to it. Mandating the other side's number adds conflict in the car, invites rivals to reconstruct pricing patterns, and substitutes a shaming percentage for useful information.
