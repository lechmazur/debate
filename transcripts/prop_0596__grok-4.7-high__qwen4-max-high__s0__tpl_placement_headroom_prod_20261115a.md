[P1.1] When a treatment can rewrite a disease out of a child’s body, price should not decide whether that child lives well. Essential gene-editing therapies now arrive as one-time interventions for conditions that once meant lifelong pain, disability, or early death. Launch prices measured in the millions place them beyond most patients, most insurers, and nearly every public budget. A patent system built to reward invention has, in these cases, become a lock on the medicine itself.
[P1.2] A public-interest licensing pathway is the proportionate answer. It would not seize every gene-editing product or abolish patents. It would give governments a defined power, triggered only where a therapy is medically essential, access is inadequate, and the price is unreasonable, to compel a license at an affordable rate with fair compensation to the rights holder. Societies already use this logic when monopoly power collides with public necessity.
[P1.3] HIV medicines showed the pattern: public-interest and compulsory licenses expanded treatment without ending private research.
[P1.4] Three reasons make the case decisive. First, delay is not abstract. A person with sickle cell disease or a fatal genetic disorder does not get a second chance at the patent term. Second, much of the underlying science was built with public money, university labs, and shared biological knowledge.
[P1.5] Guaranteeing access is reciprocity, not theft. Third, innovation survives compensation. Developers can still earn substantial returns through royalties, volume, and products that never meet the essentiality threshold. What they should not earn is the right to price a cure as if only the wealthy were entitled to be cured.
[P1.6] The pathway is narrow by design. Courts or independent bodies can test essentiality, set remuneration, and prevent political abuse. What countries cannot defend is a rule that treats monopoly silence as a health policy. Refusing this power chooses scarcity by statute.
[P1.7] Adopting it keeps the reward for invention and restores the purpose of medicine: a therapy that can end a disease should be within reach of the people who have it.

[C1.1] The HIV analogy is the load-bearing wall, and it does not hold. Those were small molecules: read the patent, synthesize the compound, press pills. Gene-editing therapies are not that. The patent is the smallest part of what separates a patient from a cure.
[C1.2] The hard assets are cell lines, vector manufacturing, validated assays, and process know-how developers deliberately keep as trade secrets rather than publish in patents. Compel a license tomorrow and a government still has no right to that process, no qualified facility, and no regulator who will approve a biologic made by an unfamiliar producer. Compulsion without capacity is a press release, not a prescription.
[C1.3] Nor is monopoly rent the main reason these therapies cost millions. You are paying for conditioning chemotherapy, weeks in a specialist centre, and years of mandated follow-up — plus recovering a development programme across a few thousand patients. Drive the price to "affordable" by fiat and you may fall below the cost of safely delivering the therapy at all. No licensee steps forward, and the child gets nothing while the statute congratulates itself.
[C1.4] And the risk lands precisely where PRO claims to help. Investors can absorb a licensing threat on a blockbuster; they will not fund a bespoke sickle-cell or ultra-rare programme whose market is small and whose essentiality designation guarantees confiscation the moment it succeeds. Public money funded discovery; private money funds the trials and the factories, and that is the money this pathway frightens.
[C1.5] Real access levers exist: outcome-based contracts, instalment payments, pooled procurement, regional manufacturing capacity. Existing compulsory and march-in powers sit unused — not from cowardice, but because they do not produce doses. PRO must show a compelled licence becoming an administered cure, somewhere. Until then this is symbolic expropriation with a waiting list attached.

[P2.1] The manufacturing objection answers a pathway nobody proposed. [C1.1] imagines a stranger reading a patent and synthesizing a cure. Compel access means ordering the rights holder — already in possession of the cell lines, vectors, and validated process — to supply at a remunerated price, or to license a manufacturer that already meets regulatory standards. Trade secrets are not a veto against their owner.
[P2.2] They are a reason to write the order as a supply-and-transfer duty, not a photocopy of a claim. Complexity raises the design of the remedy. It does not restore a permanent seller's veto.
[P2.3] Delivery costs do not sanctify the launch price. Conditioning, specialist beds, and follow-up are health-system costs, not patent rights. Folding them into a multi-million figure lets the monopolist bill the public for infrastructure it did not invent. Remuneration set by an independent body can cover safe delivery and a real return.
[P2.4] "Affordable" is not a synonym for "below cost." Volume and instalments can sit inside that price. What voluntary contracts cannot do is override a seller who refuses.
[P2.5] The investment claim inverts the trigger. Designation is not confiscation on success. It fires only where a therapy is essential, access is inadequate, and the price is unreasonable. Price within public reach and the pathway never opens.
[P2.6] Investors already live with reimbursement refusal; a compensated, reviewable rule is clearer than a political standoff. Frightening only the unreasonable deal is the point.
[P2.7] Unused powers do not prove doses are impossible. A credible threat often yields the supply agreement before any formal order. Outcome contracts and pooled procurement work until the rights holder walks away — and then the waiting list is the policy. The pathway keeps the reward and removes that veto by binding the firm that can already administer the cure.

[C2.1] Notice what just happened to the motion. PRO's remedy is no longer a licence that lets someone else make the cure; it is an order telling the originator to keep making it, cheaper. That is price control wearing licensing's clothes — and it abandons the HIV precedent entirely, because the whole point there was new producers, new capacity, new doses. A supply mandate creates none of those.
[C2.2] Vector and cell-processing capacity is the bottleneck; telling one firm to serve your market at a dictated price reallocates a queue, it does not lengthen the line. Firms respond by launching later, or not at all, where terms are dictated — and reference pricing spreads that discount across borders. The child PRO invoked waits longer, not shorter.
[C2.3] PRO calls delivery "health-system costs, not patent rights." But the developer builds the qualified-centre network, the cryopreservation chain, and the long-term follow-up regulators demand for integrating therapies. Those are its costs. Strip them out to declare the residual "unreasonable," and the trigger is manufactured by accounting, not by greed.
[C2.4] And the safe harbour is illusory. "Unreasonable" is judged by the payer who cannot afford the therapy; a two-million-dollar cure for a few thousand patients is unreasonable to every budget by construction. Investors price that judgment years before any tribunal sits. PRO's own defence — "a credible threat often yields the supply agreement" [P2.7] — concedes the work is done by bargaining leverage governments already hold.
[C2.5] So the marginal gain is a slightly better negotiation; the marginal cost is a permanent, statute-written discount risk hanging over the smallest, frailest programmes. That is not proportionate. You cannot make a fragile market attractive by legislating away its upside. It moves prices on paper and pipelines in the wrong direction.
