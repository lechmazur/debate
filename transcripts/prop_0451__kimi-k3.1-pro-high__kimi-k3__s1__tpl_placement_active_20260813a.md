[P1.1] The decisive distinction is between buying emissions reductions and building the capacity to sustain them. Treating Chinese EVs and clean-tech imports primarily as an industrial threat does not mean rejecting decarbonization or banning trade. It means recognizing that Europe’s transition depends on strategic productive capacity, not merely on this year’s cheapest sticker price.
[P1.2] First, import-led decarbonization can destroy the industrial base on which the next stage of decarbonization depends. Vehicles, batteries, solar components, and grid equipment are not isolated consumer purchases; they are interconnected technologies requiring suppliers, engineering skills, and sustained investment. If heavily subsidized imports make European production unviable, capital and expertise leave. Rebuilding them later is far slower than switching suppliers of ordinary goods.
[P1.3] Europe’s difficulty retaining solar manufacturing illustrates the danger: once an industrial ecosystem contracts, low prices do not automatically bring it back.
[P1.4] Second, concentration creates vulnerability precisely when clean technology becomes essential infrastructure. A Europe dependent on one dominant external supplier for batteries or critical components inherits exposure to export restrictions, geopolitical retaliation, and supply disruption. The green transition would then replace one strategic dependency with another. CON may celebrate short-term consumer savings, but a cheaper vehicle is a poor bargain if its supply chain weakens Europe’s ability to electrify transport on its own terms.
[P1.5] Third, the climate case itself requires political durability. Decarbonization cannot survive as a project associated with factory closures, weakened regions, and permanent dependence. Domestic industrial capability gives workers and communities a stake in the transition. Treating imports as an opportunity first sacrifices that coalition for temporary price relief.
[P1.6] The appropriate response is disciplined, not autarkic: enforce trade rules, apply remedies where distortion is demonstrated, diversify supply, attach resilience conditions to public support, and invest in European production and innovation. Chinese products can still compete, but they should not set the terms of Europe’s industrial future.
