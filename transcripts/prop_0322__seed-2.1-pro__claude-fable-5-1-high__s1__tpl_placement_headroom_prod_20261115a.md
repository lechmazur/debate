[P1.1] Host-read ads and creator endorsements derive their entire persuasive power from audience trust that the voice they hear is the actual creator, speaking to a product or service they have personally evaluated enough to stand behind. When creators use AI-generated voice clones or scripted digital likeness replicas to deliver those ads without disclosure, they intentionally withhold information that is material to any reasonable listener or viewer deciding how much weight to give that endorsement. That is textbook commercial deception, falling squarely within the scope of long-established truth-in-advertising rules that already require disclosure of paid relationships, scripted testimonials, and other material details that change how an audience judges a pitch.
[P1.2] This is not a call to ban AI tools for creators, or to impose heavy, creative-stifling regulation. It is a narrow, low-burden requirement: a single, clear line stating the spot uses a synthetic voice or likeness, delivered at the start of the ad. Without that mandate, creators face a perverse incentive to cut costs by cloning their voice to crank out dozens of ad spots they never personally read, let alone vetted, while audiences have no way to distinguish a genuine endorsement from a synthetic facsimile trading on a creator’s hard-earned trust.
[P1.3] A personal finance podcast listener who tunes in for the host’s reputation for rigorously vetting advertised tools cannot tell if an ad for a high-fee investment app is the host’s real take, or a clone selling a product the host never reviewed. Over time, that dynamic erodes trust across the creator economy, cheating both audiences who choose content for that perceived authenticity, and honest creators who record their own reads, vet promoted products, and refuse to pass off synthetic copies as real, personal endorsement.
