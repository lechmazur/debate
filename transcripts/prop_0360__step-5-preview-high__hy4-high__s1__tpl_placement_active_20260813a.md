[P1.1] Tighter enforcement for luxury and performance cars is not class warfare; it is proportionality. The proposition follows from a simple premise: when external costs are unusually concentrated, regulation should be unusually focused. Ordinary vehicles move people and goods as a byproduct of daily life, imposing diffuse, largely unavoidable urban noise. Performance machines concentrate those same harms deliberately, in the places and hours where quiet and safety are most scarce.
[P1.2] Consider the mechanism. A quiet residential street at midnight does not suffer from the aggregate hum of commuter sedans traveling at lawful speeds. It suffers from the concentrated blast of a high-output exhaust, or the squeal of acceleration used as spectacle rather than transport. The driver internalizes the thrill; the neighborhood absorbs the cost.
[P1.3] That asymmetry is the entire justification for differentiated enforcement.
[P1.4] Concrete examples make the stakes vivid. A single supercar doing a standing launch outside an apartment block can momentarily produce noise levels comparable to sustained construction equipment, yet the ordinary car in the next lane is doing nothing more than obeying the posted speed limit. The marginal social cost of the performance vehicle’s discretionary use is vastly higher than the ordinary vehicle’s necessary use.
[P1.5] Enforcement should track that difference. Speed cameras, decibel monitors, and acceleration sensors are blunt instruments against a uniform problem, but they can be calibrated to distinguish lawful transit from concentrated disturbance. This is not about banning luxury; it is about ensuring that those who amplify urban externalities face the corresponding duty of care.
[P1.6] The motion wins because it aligns burden with benefit. Ordinary drivers gain mobility at a manageable social price. Enthusiasts seeking urban performance can still enjoy it, but they must absorb the concentrated costs they currently export onto others. That is fairness, not punishment.

[C1.3] The claim that concentrated costs justify focused regulation [P1.1] is right only if “focused” means precise instruments, not privileged suspects. Night decibel limits, bans on standing starts or tire squeal near homes, and heavier penalties for repeated or extreme violations target the harm directly. If a supercar produces “the concentrated blast” [P1.2], the noise rule catches it. If it does not, tighter treatment is punishment without the claimed harm.
[C1.4] The apartment-block example [P1.4] proves the case for conduct rules, not category rules.

Louder urban disturbance often comes from cheap modified exhausts, motorcycles, or aggressive ordinary cars. Necessary-versus-discretionary use is no sturdier: cities cannot adjudicate trips by moral seriousness.
[C1.6] Acceleration is also a poor proxy: a quick merge or avoidance move may be safe. Sensors aimed at a car class invite false positives. [C1.7] The proposition discounts identical harm from an ordinary vehicle and invents harm from status when no offense occurs—the opposite of aligning burden with cost [P1.6].
