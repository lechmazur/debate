[C1.1] Universal fuel subsidies are a blunt instrument, yes. But the proposition asks us to trade that blunt instrument for something far more fragile: targeted cash transfers that, in most of the countries where this matters, simply do not work at scale.
[C1.2] Start with the hardest problem. Targeting requires identifying the poor accurately, then delivering money reliably, then trusting that cash reaches the right hands. In countries with large informal economies, weak state registries, and patchy banking—precisely the countries drowning in fuel subsidies—every step breaks down. Exclusion error is the silent killer: studies of welfare programs across South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa routinely find that a quarter or more of intended recipients are missed, while better-off households slip through.
[C1.3] A farmer without a bank account, a migrant worker without papers, a woman in a household not registered under a male name—these are the people the proposition promises to protect, and they are exactly the people targeted systems fail first.
[C1.4] Now consider the transition. Phasing out subsidies means raising pump prices before the transfer system is bulletproof. During that gap, the poor face higher costs with no compensation yet landing. Egypt learned this painfully: when subsidies were cut and cash transfers lagged, street protests followed within weeks.
[C1.5] Political backlash can reverse reforms entirely, leaving countries worse off than before—still subsidizing, but with shattered credibility.
[C1.6] Universal subsidies, for all their inefficiency, are simple. Every liter sold is subsidized; no one needs to prove poverty to benefit. That administrative ease is not a trivial virtue—it is what makes the policy survivable across political cycles. Replacing it with a targeted apparatus demands institutional capacity that most fuel-subsidizing nations do not yet have, and building that capacity takes years, not the gradual phase-out the motion naively assumes.
[C1.7] The proposition treats targeting as a solved problem. It is not. And betting the poor on an unsolved problem while removing their existing protection is a gamble they cannot afford.
