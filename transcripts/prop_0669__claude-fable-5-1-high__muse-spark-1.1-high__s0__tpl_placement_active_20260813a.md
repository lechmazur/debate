[P1.1] A public university is the state. When it tells a chemist that her job, her tenure, or her grant depends on affirming a political creed, the government is conditioning public employment on belief. That is the thing the loyalty-oath era taught us to reject. The 1950s versions demanded that professors disavow communism; courts eventually struck them down not because anticommunism was wrong, but because the state may not make orthodoxy a hiring criterion.
[P1.2] The principle was never about which orthodoxy. It was about the test itself.
[P1.3] That is the frame this debate should turn on: not whether any particular ideology is good, but whether a state institution should hold a job hostage to a professed belief. Three reasons it should not.
[P1.4] First, loyalty statements measure the wrong thing. A required statement of ideological commitment cannot distinguish a sincere believer from a fluent performer. It filters for people willing to say the words—and quietly filters out the honest dissenter who would have been an excellent teacher. An institution built on inquiry ends up rewarding the one skill inquiry should punish: telling authority what it wants to hear.
[P1.5] Second, the sword has no fixed handle. Whoever controls the appointing power controls the creed. A campus comfortable requiring one political affirmation today should picture a new governor and board requiring a patriotism pledge or a statement against "divisive concepts" tomorrow. The only durable protection for every faction is a rule that no faction may impose a test.
[P1.6] Neutral prohibition is the compromise everyone should want behind the veil.
[P1.7] Third, prohibition costs the university nothing it legitimately needs. It can still evaluate teaching, mentoring, and service by conduct and record—did you actually help first-generation students, did you actually run a fair classroom. What it may not do is substitute a recitation for evidence. Conduct is assessable; creeds are compelled.
[P1.8] So the question for the other side is simple: which required political affirmation, imposed by a state employer as the price of a paycheck, are you prepared to defend—and would you still defend it once [clipped]
