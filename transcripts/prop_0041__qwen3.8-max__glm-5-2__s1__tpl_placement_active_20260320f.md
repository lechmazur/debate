[P1.1] The proposition bans one specific practice: letting algorithms set different prices for the same item by profiling an individual customer’s likely desperation, loyalty, wealth, or urgency. It does not ban sales, seasonal pricing, volume discounts, or price changes driven by supply and cost. The line is simple: prices may respond to market conditions, but not to a secret estimate of how much a particular person can be squeezed.
[P1.2] This is wrong first because it is a hidden tax on vulnerability. If a retailer infers that a shopper is comparison-averse, needs medicine quickly, uses an expensive device, lives in a wealthy postcode, or has repeatedly viewed a product, the “market price” becomes a personal surcharge. The customer sees one number; the basis is concealed. That is not efficient exchange, it is extraction.
[P1.3] A traveler booking a last-minute flight may be charged not because seats are scarcer, but because the algorithm knows she cannot wait.
[P1.4] Second, the practice is structurally discriminatory even when no protected class is explicitly used. Willingness and ability proxies correlate with income, race, age, disability, and geography. Personalized pricing can therefore reproduce redlining with cleaner variables: not “charge this group more,” but “charge people with these data footprints more.” A ban prevents retailers from laundering bias through device type, browsing history, or payment method.
[P1.5] Third, this destroys the trust markets need. Ordinary consumers cannot shop if the sticker price is merely an opening guess calibrated to their psychological profile. They will hesitate, compare obsessively, use VPNs, or avoid purchases altogether. That is worse for honest retailers than clear, uniform pricing.
[P1.6] The obvious objection is that personalized pricing can offer discounts to price-sensitive shoppers. But a ban still allows coupons, student discounts, loyalty programs, and markdowns offered openly to all. The only thing lost is the right to secretly charge each shopper the maximum the algorithm thinks they will tolerate. That is exactly what should be banned.

Every price a retailer sets reflects an estimate of what customers will tolerate. A gas station near an airport charges more because it knows travelers will pay.
[C1.2] Umbrella prices rise when rain starts. Surge pricing charges more precisely when riders' urgency peaks. These are not exceptions to the market — they are the market. Algorithmic personalization makes the same logic more precise, not more sinister.
[C1.3] The claim that only extraction is lost hides who actually benefits. Personalized pricing routes discounts to price-sensitive customers who would otherwise be priced out. A student browsing on a budget device, comparison-shopping across four tabs, signals price sensitivity — and an algorithm can respond with a lower offer. A retailer forced to set one uniform price sets it at the average: the student pays more, the affluent impulse-buyer pays less.
[C1.4] The ban redistributes upward.
[C1.5] The carve-out proves the point. Coupons, loyalty programs, and student discounts are all willingness-to-pay pricing with different labels. A student discount charges non-students more based on assumed ability to pay. A loyalty program charges new customers more to capture switchers.
[C1.6] If these are acceptable, the principle is already conceded. What remains is an arbitrary ban on the algorithmic method — which happens to be the most efficient version of a practice everyone accepts.
[C1.7] The discrimination argument also collapses. Supermarkets already charge different prices in different neighborhoods. Insurance, credit, and tuition are already personalized. The motion bans the algorithm, not the practice it claims to condemn — and algorithms are often what make pricing fairer by replacing coarse proxies like zip code with granular, behavior-based signals.
[C1.8] A ban doesn't remove discrimination from pricing. It removes the tool most capable of correcting it.

[P2.2] Surge pricing, whatever its flaws, applies a visible multiplier at a given time. Personalized algorithmic pricing does something else: it looks at your device, browsing trail, purchase history, location, and inferred urgency, then decides how much you in particular can be made to pay. That is not “the market” becoming more precise; it is the market becoming a private negotiation in which only one side knows the rules.
[P2.3] The “discounts for students” argument proves this distinction. If a retailer wants to help price-sensitive shoppers, it can publish a coupon, student rate, income-based discount, or flash sale. Those are open, repeatable, and contestable. [P2.4] A shopper does not need to know she is being priced as affluent, anxious, or locked in; she only sees a higher checkout number. The alleged benefit to the student is not a reason to allow the opposite practice: secretly charging someone else the maximum. The redistribution claim assumes a false choice: the ban stops secret surcharges, not public discounts.
[P2.5] CON also says algorithms are fairer than zip codes. But granular behavior is not neutral. Device type, payment method, browsing speed, and address history are proxies for income and class, often for race and age. At least a zip-code difference can be observed and challenged.
[P2.6] A real-time personal price cannot be audited by the customer, compared with a neighbor, or corrected by regulators. The remedy for biased pricing is not a black box; it is a clear rule: do not set prices by profiling individual need or wealth.
