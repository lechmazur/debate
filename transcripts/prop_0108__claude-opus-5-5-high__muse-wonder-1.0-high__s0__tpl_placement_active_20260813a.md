[P1.1] The defect in American legislative elections is structural, not personal. Winner-take-all districts hand all representation to whoever clears the plurality line and give nothing to everyone else. That one rule produces three failures that reinforce each other, and ranked-choice or proportional voting attacks all three.
[P1.2] First, safe seats move the real election into the primary. When a district reliably leans one way, the November race barely matters; the decisive contest is a low-turnout primary dominated by the most activated partisans. Representatives learn that the only threat to their job comes from their own flank, so compromise becomes career suicide. Ranked-choice voting changes that incentive: a candidate needs a majority, including second and third preferences, so appealing beyond the base pays off.
[P1.3] Proportional multi-member districts go further, because nearly every district becomes competitive for at least one seat.
[P1.4] Second, winner-take-all wastes votes on a massive scale. A Republican in Massachusetts or a Democrat in rural Oklahoma can vote for decades and never help elect anyone. Under proportional rules, roughly thirty percent of the vote earns roughly thirty percent of the seats. Gerrymandering also loses its teeth, because drawing lines can't erase a party's share when seats are allocated by vote totals.
[P1.5] Third, winner-take-all locks in exactly two parties. Voting for a third option risks splitting your side and electing your worst choice, so voters retreat to the lesser evil. The result is a binary in which every issue gets sorted into team identity. Ranked ballots remove the spoiler problem; proportional seats let new coalitions actually win representation.
[P1.6] This is not utopian. Maine and Alaska already use ranked-choice voting, and most established democracies elect legislatures through some form of proportional representation. Mixed systems like Germany's keep local representatives while making the overall chamber reflect how people voted, with vote thresholds to prevent fragmentation.
[P1.7] So the burden on the other side is heavy: they must defend a system that makes most votes meaningless, rewards extremism, and lets politicians choose their voters. That is the status quo they are asking you to keep.

[C1.1] The proposition bundles two different fixes because neither solves polarization on its own, and together they trade a clear, accountable system for a more confusing, more fragmented one.
[C1.2] Winner-take-all has a democratic virtue proportional and ranked systems weaken: accountability. One district elects one representative who must face those voters again by name. If she panders, compromises badly, or ignores the district, she can be removed. Under proportional lists, politicians answer first to party leaders who control their ranking, and policy emerges after election day in coalition deals no voter voted for.
[C1.3] Under ranked-choice, the link survives but the mandate blurs: after exhausted ballots are discarded and preferences transferred, the winner may be nobody's first choice.
[C1.4] Second, fragmentation is not representation perfected; it is leverage for extremes. Winner-take-all forces parties to build broad coalitions before the election, where fringe demands must be moderated to win a majority of a district. Proportional representation reverses that. A small party with a narrow, intense base can win a few seats, then become the indispensable partner that extracts concessions out of all proportion to its support.
[C1.5] Ranked-choice does not escape this trade-off — in single-member districts it preserves two-party dominance, as Maine and Alaska still show, while adding recount rounds and center-squeeze outcomes where the most broadly acceptable candidate can be eliminated early.
[C1.6] Third, polarization is not mainly manufactured by ballot format. Americans are sorted by geography, culture, media, and primaries; a Democrat in rural Oklahoma and a Republican in Massachusetts disagree with their neighbors before any counting rule applies. Changing how votes are tallied will not unsort the country, but it will make counting slower, less intuitive, and harder for voters to verify — a real cost to trust when trust is scarce.
[C1.7] A system that forces broad appeals before elections, gives every district a representative, and lets voters deliver an unmistakable verdict is not broken entrenchment. It is majority rule with accountability.
