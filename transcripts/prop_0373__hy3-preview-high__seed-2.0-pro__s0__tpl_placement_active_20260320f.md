[P1.1] The current wave of book challenges and removals in public schools and libraries has steamrolled past any reasonable line of protecting students, even when challengers’ claims about age-inappropriateness hold partial water. When a title is deemed unsuitable for 8-year-olds but yanked from high school shelves serving 17-year-olds, that is not tailored protection—it is blanket censorship that harms older students who can engage with complex, even uncomfortable, material.
[P1.2] This overreach is not limited to rare edge cases. Most removals target books discussing race, gender, or historical trauma, framed as "age-inappropriate" for the youngest students, but pulled from all grade levels and public library stacks. For example, challenges to books about LGBTQ+ identities often cite a single passage about puberty as inappropriate for elementary students, but lead to full removal of the title from high school health classes where that content is directly relevant. A memoir about growing up Black in America might include language a parent deems unfit for 3rd graders, but removing it from a 12th grade social studies class erases critical context for students preparing to enter adulthood.
[P1.3] That is the core harm: removals punish all patrons to address a narrow, often unproven risk to a tiny subset of users.
[P1.4] Preemptive self-censorship makes the problem far worse. Librarians now pull books before challenges even arise, fearing retaliation from school boards or activist groups, so even titles with clear educational value for teens vanish. The result is a hollowed-out collection that avoids all controversy, depriving students of the chance to encounter diverse perspectives or practice critical thinking. Even if a handful of challenged books truly are wrong for some ages, the systemic removal of hundreds of titles across districts goes far beyond any defensible boundary.
