[P1.1] National loyalty can be honorable, but honor is not moral priority. The proposition is comparative: when allegiance to one’s nation conflicts with duties owed to people simply because they are human, national loyalty is the weaker claim. It may guide our choices; it may not excuse sacrificing outsiders’ fundamental interests for insiders’ advantage.
[P1.2] The decisive reason is equal moral worth. A child’s need for food, medicine, or protection does not diminish because the child was born across a border. Nationality can determine which government has jurisdiction, but it cannot determine whose suffering matters. If two people face identical dangers, and the only relevant difference is their passport, preferring one requires a justification beyond “they are ours.” Otherwise, national loyalty is not a moral reason at all—it is inherited partiality wearing moral language.
[P1.3] This does not abolish special duties. I may owe my family particular care, and citizens may owe their political community taxes, service, or support for just institutions. But such duties are limited by the rights and welfare of others. Caring especially for my child does not permit me to injure yours.
[P1.4] Likewise, pursuing national prosperity cannot justify aggressive war, colonial exploitation, or knowingly shifting lethal harms onto foreigners. The stronger duty is the side constraint: national loyalty operates within universal morality, not above it.
[P1.5] Universal welfare also explains why national loyalty can have value. Stable communities, trust, and public institutions often help people fulfill duties effectively. A citizen may best serve humanity by improving their own country rather than pretending to assist everyone equally. But that justification is instrumental and conditional.
[P1.6] When loyalty protects institutions that serve people, it deserves respect; when it demands harm to outsiders merely for national prestige or advantage, its moral warrant collapses.

[C1.1] A child's suffering matters wherever it occurs, but moral worth is not the same as a moral claim on me. A claim becomes strong when a specific person can rightly demand something from a specific agent because of what they have built, promised, and relied upon together. National loyalty has that structure; an abstract duty to maximize universal welfare does not.
[C1.2] Citizens are not strangers sharing a passport. They submit to the same laws, fund the same hospitals and pensions, accept taxation, and live with collective decisions. That reciprocity creates legitimate expectations: if I pay into and obey a common scheme on the understanding it will protect us when vulnerable, my community owes me priority, and I owe it the same. Diverting that scheme whenever a calculation finds greater need elsewhere is not impartiality; it is betrayal of reliance.
[C1.3] Test the opposite rule. A government that taxed its poorest citizens, then sent the money abroad because each dollar could do marginally more good there, while its own schools, flood defences, and clinics decayed, would not be heroic. It would violate the basis on which it was entrusted with power. The same intuition governed vaccine allocation in a pandemic: a state protecting others before its own vulnerable, who had funded its health system and obeyed its restrictions, would rightly face condemnation, not praise.
[C1.4] Universal welfare duties are real but thin and indeterminate. They forbid cruelty and require some emergency aid, yet pick out no particular bearer, amount, or recipient, and taken impartially they would swallow every family, friendship, and local obligation. A duty that demands everything in principle reliably secures nothing in practice.
[C1.5] National loyalty is therefore not inherited prejudice. It is the prioritized responsibility that makes large-scale welfare, accountability, and sacrifice possible. Borders do not create worth, but shared institutions create obligations — and obligations to identifiable people who depend on you are stronger than a diffuse duty to everyone in general.

[P2.2] The taxation example therefore attacks a position I do not hold. Universal welfare is not an instruction to send away every dollar that could do marginally more good elsewhere while one’s own clinics decay. Basic duties to citizens can be especially urgent and institutionally assigned.
[P2.3] But reliance cannot sanctify any priority: contributors to a scheme do not acquire a moral license to shift grave harms onto non-contributors.
[P2.4] The same answer dissolves the vaccine argument. Protecting one’s own vulnerable people first may sometimes be justified by responsibility, reliance, and delivery capacity. That is a conditional priority in the service of welfare, not proof that foreign lives count for less. [P2.5] A reason to act first is not a reason to inflict anything.
[P2.6] CON calls universal duties “thin and indeterminate,” yet some of the most determinate duties are universal: do not torture, do not wage aggressive war, do not knowingly poison people across a border. [P2.7] Nor would universal morality swallow families or nations. It constrains partiality rather than erasing it: I may give my child special care, but not injure yours to fund it. Likewise, national loyalty is legitimate when it sustains institutions serving people, and illegitimate when “ours first” becomes “theirs do not count.” CON has shown that nations can be useful agents of welfare, not that loyalty to them is the stronger moral claim.

[P4.1] A1: No numerical threshold is needed. A state may prioritize dependents where coercion, reliance, and unique capacity make it responsible for their basic security, and where priority preserves institutions capable of helping others. It may not convert that trust into minor compatriot gains at the cost of grave, preventable outsider losses. Vaccines and flood defences therefore show conditional responsibility, not national priority “winning”; the standard authorizing and limiting that priority is human welfare.
[P4.2] A2: Capable individuals and institutions owe people in severe need assistance proportionate to capacity, urgency, and other responsibilities: rescue at reasonable cost, and a fair institutional contribution to lifesaving relief rather than unlimited donation. The exact budget is contextual, just as the exact duty to citizens is contextual. Indeterminacy at the margin does not make avoidable deaths morally weightless. Nor can CON set aside negative duties: prohibiting torture, aggression, and poisoning directly protects human welfare—precisely what this motion compares.
[P4.3] The deeper problem remains CON’s substitution. Taxation, coercion, and reliance can establish a state’s trustee obligations; they do not establish the superior moral force of national loyalty. Such obligations may cover non-citizen residents, while excluding emigrant compatriots who no longer depend on the scheme. Dependence explains why an agent must act, not why outsiders’ suffering counts less.
[P4.4] That distinction defeats the family analogy. Special care can structure my service to human welfare without licensing harm or proving that unrelated people matter less. CON’s ordinary examples—clinics, vaccines, flood defences—concern securing basic interests, not sacrificing greater outsider welfare for national advantage. Once basic security and institutional capacity are protected, loyalty supplies no independent moral scale on which flags outweigh lives.
[P4.5] Universal welfare is stronger because it explains when national obligations deserve priority, when they must yield, and why every person remains within the moral community. National loyalty is accountable to that standard; it is not the standard itself.

[P5.1] The decisive distinction is between a duty’s administrative precision and its moral weight. A citizen may be able to demand a clinic while a distant stranger cannot demand a particular payment; that identifies who is accountable to whom. It does not make the stranger’s welfare matter less, any more than a clearly written contract makes its purpose morally supreme.
[P5.2] CON’s own reliance argument proves this. The claim grows for resident non-citizens and fades for emigrant compatriots because it tracks dependence on an institution, not national attachment. Calling that dependence “loyalty made institutional” cannot change its justification: people have been made vulnerable through coercion and reliance, so their welfare must be protected.
[P5.3] Nor does “a limit is not a reversal” answer the motion. Strength is not measured by how often a consideration wins routine budget choices, but by what ultimately justifies and constrains it. Universal welfare explains why basic citizen security may come first, why dependable institutions deserve support, and why grave preventable suffering can override collective improvements. National loyalty supplies no independent scale on which national advantage outweighs human lives.
[P5.4] Loyalty can therefore be valuable, enforceable, and sometimes decisive—while remaining the weaker moral claim. It is accountable to human welfare; human welfare is not accountable to the flag.
