[P1.1] Cephalopods are the only invertebrates European research law protects as animals capable of suffering, and British sentience legislation was extended to them after an evidence review. Regulators have already conceded the premise of this motion. Farming is the one setting where that concession buys an octopus nothing.
[P1.2] Consider what is being confined. An animal that opens jars from the inside, carries shells as portable shelter, squirts water at lights it dislikes, learns mazes, shows stable individual personalities, and recognises particular keepers. Much of its nervous system sits in the arms themselves: a mind built for touching, tasting, digging, hunting, and exploring a complex reef.
[P1.3] Industrial confinement offers that animal two options. Crowd it, and solitary, territorial cannibals injure and eat each other. Separate it — which is what growers actually propose — and you get a curious predator alone in a bare tank: nothing to dig, nothing to hunt, nothing to solve, no escape from the same walls for its whole short life. The welfare fix and the welfare harm are the same design choice.
[P1.4] That is not a problem better engineering solves.
[P1.5] Add the biology: these are carnivores fed on wild-caught fish, and early life stages die in enormous numbers in captivity. An industry that loses most of its animals before maturity is not husbandry with rough edges; it is a system whose baseline is suffering.
[P1.6] "We need more science" is an argument for not starting, not for licensing a new industry while we study it. Nor does the cruelty of existing livestock farming excuse this: we can still stop a harm before tanks, supply chains, and investors lock it in. Banning this costs us a luxury protein we do not need; refusing to ban it costs a problem-solving animal its entire life.

[C1.2] The motion needs something far stronger. It must show that confinement is cruel in principle, everywhere, under any standard. That claim fails.
[C1.3] First, consider the real alternative. A ban does not end octopus eating; demand across the Mediterranean, East Asia and Latin America stays. It shifts supply onto wild capture: pots, traps and trawls, animals crushed in nets, left to suffocate on deck, or killed by methods no regulator inspects. Wild fisheries are where cruelty is invisible and unaccountable.
[C1.4] A farm is a fixed site that can be required to stun humanely, enrich tanks, cap densities, and open its doors to auditors. Banning the only controllable setting protects no octopus; it protects our consciences.
[C1.5] Second, PRO's dilemma is false. Octopuses are solitary in the wild, so housing them separately mirrors their nature rather than violating it. "Bare tank" is a design choice, not a law of biology. Aquaria and laboratories already keep octopuses with dens, substrate and puzzle feeders.
[C1.6] Where intelligence creates welfare needs, the answer is to write those needs into licensing conditions and refuse permits to anyone who won't meet them.
[C1.7] Third, PRO's mortality point misreads the animal. Octopuses produce vast numbers of tiny larvae, and in the sea nearly all die quickly. High early losses reflect the species' reproductive strategy. They are not proof of farm cruelty.
[C1.8] Finally, consistency. Pigs solve puzzles and recognise individuals too. We answer that with welfare law, not with claims that every pen everywhere is indefensible. The motion skips that step and leaps straight to prohibition.
[C1.9] That leap is where PRO loses.
