[C1.1] The proposition asks central banks to treat a modest rise in unemployment as an acceptable price for hitting an inflation target. That framing hides the real risk: unemployment is not a clean, reversible lever the way the word "modest" implies. It scars.
[C1.2] First, hysteresis is real and asymmetric. A worker laid off for a year loses skills, networks, and bargaining position. Research on recessions consistently shows that unemployment spells—especially among younger and lower-income workers—translate into years of reduced lifetime earnings, not a temporary dip that snaps back. Inflation, by contrast, is often self-correcting: once supply chains normalize or energy prices stabilize, price growth falls on its own, as we saw inflation cool substantially in recent years without needing a severe employment shock.
[C1.3] Treating these two costs as symmetric trade-offs misstates the comparison PRO wants you to make.
[C1.4] Second, monetary policy is a blunt instrument operating with long, uncertain lags. A central bank that "prioritizes" inflation can easily overshoot, because by the time rate hikes bite, inflation may already be falling for other reasons—leaving the bank hiking into a slowdown that becomes far more than modest. History offers real examples: aggressive disinflation campaigns have repeatedly produced recessions deeper than intended, because policymakers can't fine-tune unemployment the way the motion assumes.
[C1.5] Third, who actually bears the unemployment cost? Not the economists setting policy, but the last-hired, first-fired: younger workers, minorities, those without a financial cushion. Meanwhile, moderate above-target inflation is manageable through wage adjustment, especially when labor markets are tight enough that workers can bargain for raises—something that disappears exactly when unemployment rises.
[C1.6] The better path over three years is not rigid prioritization but calibrated balance: tolerate moderate overshoot, protect employment, and let supply-side normalization do more of the work than demand destruction. That is not recklessness—it's recognizing that unemployment's costs are deeper, longer-lasting, and less equitably distributed than the inflation PRO wants us to fear.
