The proposition is not a ban on travel, photography, or talking about a region. It is a duty to stop turning load-bearing places into unpriced stadiums.
[P1.2] Exact geotags and “secret spot” reels collapse the old friction of finding a place. What used to be a rumor among hikers becomes a pin that delivers thousands who never intended to share the cost of the damage they leave. Paths widen into scars. Dunes and moss die under footprints.
[P1.3] Narrow streets lose ambulances, parking, and quiet. Neighborhoods become sets; residents become extras in someone else’s feed. That is not cultural exchange. It is a shock to carrying capacity.
[P1.4] Local governments then face an ugly choice: let the site degrade, or close it after the damage is done. Limits intervene before that binary.
[P1.5] Influencer-style promotion is the mechanism that makes the shock unique. A handful of accounts optimize for novelty, then broadcast coordinates as content. Ordinary visitors still find coastlines, museums, and cities. What fails is pinpoint marketing of micro-sites after harm is visible.
[P1.6] Destinations already close trails, ration parking, and fence dunes. Extending that logic to the attention layer—blurring pins, withholding exact locations, requiring permits for commercial shoots—is how you stop the last quiet corner from being sacrificed for engagement.
[P1.7] Without limits, the incentive is perverse: the more fragile and photogenic the place, the faster it is consumed. Creators capture attention; platforms capture ads; the destination inherits the cleanup, the closures, and the resentment. Residents pay in housing pressure, lost access, and safety. Ecosystems pay in recovery time they do not have.
[P1.8] Tourism that destroys its product is not liberty; it is extraction. Limits keep places visitable rather than viral and then gone.
