[P1.1] Mandatory national service supplies the only realistic scale for credible defense in an era when professional armies alone cannot meet sudden territorial threats. Finland’s system, which draws every able cohort into structured training, produces reserve forces large enough to deter immediate neighbors without relying on distant allies for the first critical weeks. Most European states now field active forces too small to hold ground against sustained pressure, and recruitment shortfalls have only widened that gap. Conscription restores depth quickly because it does not wait for economic incentives to align.
[P1.2] The same mechanism also builds social cohesion that fragmented societies otherwise lack. Young adults from different regions, income levels, and immigrant backgrounds share barracks, duties, and risk for a fixed period. That enforced proximity reduces the distance between parallel communities more effectively than voluntary programs that self-select similar participants. Data from existing conscript systems show measurable drops in later residential segregation and higher cross-group trust metrics compared with purely professional forces.
[P1.3] Critics who claim costs outweigh gains ignore the alternative expense of under-prepared borders and persistent internal divisions. Professional armies already consume large budgets yet still fall short on numbers; adding mandatory service spreads the burden across the entire cohort and multiplies reserve capacity at marginal extra outlay. Social integration gains come at no added fiscal price once the training infrastructure exists.
[P1.4] Countries that abandoned conscription after 1990 have not replaced its integrative effect with any comparable institution. Expanding or restoring service therefore addresses two simultaneous shortfalls—military mass and civic trust—that no narrower policy currently tackles.

[C1.1] Two problems, one instrument—that is the proposition's pitch, and it is where the case breaks. Mandatory service is asked to repair advanced-warfare readiness and rebuild social trust simultaneously, in societies that have not shared a national project for generations. Treating a draft as a cure-all is exactly why the policy keeps getting suspended across Europe.
[C1.2] Mandatory service produces numerical mass, not combat mass. Russia discovered this in September 2022, when partial mobilization of reservists with minimal refresher training collapsed into a logistics and morale disaster within weeks. A cohort that returns home and rarely refreshes is a roster, not a force. Finland's reserve works because it rests on a professional cadre, continuous training cycles, and a live border threat.
[C1.3] Transplant that template to Portugal, Romania, or Ireland and the ingredients do not exist; what remains is a budget line producing low-readiness personnel that cannot actually be mobilized at speed.
[C1.4] The economic case is worse than the motion admits. Removing several hundred thousand young adults from labor markets, universities, and apprenticeships for six to twelve months imposes an opportunity cost in the billions—far above the "marginal extra outlay" claimed in [P1.3]. Germany suspended conscription in 2011 partly because skilled employers objected. Modern defense needs cyber operators, drone specialists, and intelligence linguists; conscripts cannot be meaningfully trained in these roles during basic service, so they fill mess halls while critical tasks still go to a small professional core.
[C1.5] Finally, the cohesion claim reverses the causal arrow. Finland's high cross-group trust predated and enabled its conscription system; the country did not become cohesive by drafting itself. In societies with weaker baseline trust, enforced barracks proximity more often reproduces the hierarchies it is meant to dissolve—repeated hazing and gender-integration scandals in Sweden and Norway illustrate the pattern. Targeted reserves, professional incentives, and pooled EU capability address both shortfalls without this coercion.
