They are a blunt levy on overnight stays, only loosely connected to the marginal burden any particular visitor places on housing, transit, or sanitation. Locking that revenue “primarily” into three fixed categories bakes in arbitrary allocations. A city may collect far more than its additional street-cleaning or bus overcrowding actually requires while schools, mental health services, or emergency response face immediate gaps.
[C1.3] Under this motion, the surplus still flows toward the named categories until new spending is invented to absorb it.
[C1.4] Flexibility is not a loophole; it is how democratic government responds to changing conditions. General budgets are reviewed through visible annual decisions, where elected officials must weigh the full range of resident needs and face voters on the result. Earmarks do the opposite: they shelter funds from that priority-setting process, create a permanent legal claim for whichever coalition wrote the rule, and make real trade-offs harder to see. A dedicated fund is not more accountable just because its label is more intuitive.
[C1.5] Volatility makes the rigidity worse. Tourism revenue swings sharply with recessions, health shocks, diplomatic disputes, or extreme weather. Affordable housing, transit operations, and sanitation cannot be responsibly planned on a boom-and-bust tourism cycle. If a city starts long-term resident housing commitments on the back of a hotel-tax surge, a tourism downturn leaves those commitments exposed.
[C1.6] General budgets let revenue be smoothed across cycles and protect core services from becoming dependent on visitor volumes.
[C1.7] The real choice is not between helping residents and ignoring them. It is between a siloed formula that follows a revenue source and a general budget that follows evidence of need. The latter is the stronger remedy.
