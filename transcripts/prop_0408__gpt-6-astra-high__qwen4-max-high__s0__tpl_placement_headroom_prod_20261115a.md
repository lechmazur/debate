[P1.1] A diploma should mean students have been taught how to navigate the basic obligations awaiting them. Schools should therefore require a serious financial-literacy course for graduation: not a motivational assembly about saving, but structured instruction in debt, taxes, and budgeting, with practical assignments and support for students who struggle.
[P1.2] The strongest reason is fairness. Leaving this knowledge to families makes preparedness depend on whether parents have the time, experience, and confidence to explain it. A requirement establishes a common educational floor without requiring a common financial background.
[P1.3] The content should be concrete. Students should compare loans by total repayment, not merely the advertised monthly payment; read a pay stub; complete a sample tax return; and build a budget that survives an unexpected expense. Knowing percentages is not the same as recognizing how fees, repayment periods, and interest interact in an actual contract. Application deserves teaching, not assumption.
[P1.4] This does not claim that budgeting cures poverty or that informed consumers never make mistakes. It claims that students deserve tools to identify avoidable costs and seek reliable help before signing consequential agreements. Financial education complements fair lending rules and adequate incomes; neither regulation nor income makes understanding your own obligations unnecessary.
[P1.5] Curriculum time is scarce, but that supports prioritizing skills almost every graduate will use. A focused semester, taught through realistic exercises rather than financial-product promotion, is a proportionate investment. [P1.6] They can guarantee that preparation for ordinary financial life is not reserved for students lucky enough to receive it elsewhere.
