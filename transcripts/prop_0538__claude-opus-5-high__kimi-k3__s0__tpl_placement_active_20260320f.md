[P1.1] Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire together grow most of the world's cocoa. They capture a sliver of a chocolate industry worth on the order of a hundred billion dollars a year. The beans leave; the grinding, the branding, the confectionery jobs, the profit margin — all of that happens in Zurich, Amsterdam and Chicago. That single picture is the whole motion.
[P1.2] The question is not whether Africa should trade. It is whether Africa should keep specialising in the one part of the value chain that pays worst, employs fewest people, and is most violently exposed to price swings decided in other people's markets.
[P1.3] Three reasons the priority should shift.
[P1.4] First, jobs. Africa's working-age population is growing faster than anywhere on earth. Mines and plantations are capital-intensive enclaves; a modern copper mine can generate billions in exports and employ a few thousand people. Garment factories, food processing, cement, assembly — these absorb labour at scale.
[P1.5] Only manufacturing does the arithmetic of Africa's demography.
[P1.6] Second, the composition of trade already proves the point. Intra-African trade is a small share of the continent's total, but the goods Africans sell each other are far more manufactured and processed than what they ship overseas. A Kenyan firm exports machinery parts to Uganda and raw tea to London. Regional markets are the ones that actually buy African industrial output.
[P1.7] Strengthening them is not sentiment; it is the only demand base that rewards making things.
[P1.8] Third, volatility. A country whose budget rides on one commodity is a country whose schools and clinics ride on a futures curve. Diversification into manufacturing is macroeconomic insurance.
[P1.9] The old objection is that African markets are too small and protection failed before. That failure was national — thirty tiny walled gardens. The continental free trade area changes the denominator: over a billion consumers, a market with the scale that made East Asian industrialisation work.
[P1.10] Prioritising means directing infrastructure, credit and trade policy toward making and selling to each other. The alternative is exporting beans forever.

[C1.2] First, commodities pay for industrialisation. Raw exports earn the foreign exchange that buys machinery, turbines, port cranes and medicine. Diverting credit and infrastructure away from the one sector that earns hard currency today, toward factories that may take decades to compete, is cutting the engine to build a glider. The real choice is never commodities or industry; it is whether rents are invested or squandered — a governance problem, not a product problem.
[C1.3] Botswana prospered on diamonds, Norway on oil, Chile on copper. Nobody told them to stop digging.
[C1.4] Second, the success stories PRO invokes refute the motion. Korea, Taiwan and China industrialised by exporting manufactures to distant rich markets — exactly the trade this motion ranks second. And "a billion consumers" is a headcount, not a market: the continent's combined purchasing power is a fraction of the American or European consumer economy. There is also simple geometry: Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire both grow cocoa, so naturally they do not buy each other's.
[C1.5] Low intra-African trade reflects similar endowments, not a policy failure.
[C1.6] Third, we have run this experiment. Import substitution consumed the decades after independence; Nigeria's Ajaokuta steel complex swallowed billions and never produced at commercial scale. PRO replies that the free trade area "changes the denominator" [P1.9] — but a treaty does not pave roads, harmonise customs, or keep borders open when politics closes them.
[C1.7] Manufacturing will grow — but from commodity strength, on power and skills bought with export revenue. Prioritising factories over the export base is a gamble with school budgets. Africa should climb the value chain standing on the beans, not kick them away.
