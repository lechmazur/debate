[P1.1] A stranger clones a chief executive's face and voice, livestreams a fake crypto giveaway, and steals life savings in an afternoon. The real executive's team reports it, verified, again and again. The video is re-uploaded, recommended, and runs beside ads while the platform does nothing. Today the victim bears the loss and the scammer vanishes.
[P1.2] Civil liability for that knowing inaction is overdue.
[P1.3] This proposition is narrow, and its narrowness is its strength. It does not punish a platform for hosting a scam it never saw, or for missing a clever deepfake once. Liability begins only after repeated verified reports establish actual knowledge: this account, this synthetic likeness, this fraudulent pitch has been identified and confirmed. At that point the platform is no longer a passive conduit.
[P1.4] It is the one actor that can fingerprint the file, block re-uploads, suspend the repeat account, cut off payment and ad links, and warn users who already interacted. If it chooses scale and engagement over those cheap steps, it should share responsibility for the foreseeable theft that follows.
[P1.5] That allocation follows a principle: place the cost on the party best positioned to prevent it. Victims cannot chase anonymous, often overseas fraud rings who change names hourly. Law enforcement cannot review every clip. Platforms already have the detection stack — hash matching, provenance signals, voice and face similarity tools, and human trust-and-safety teams.
[P1.6] Liability simply forces them to use those tools for impersonation fraud with the urgency they already apply to copyright or advertiser brand safety.
[P1.7] Nor is this a speech claim. Fraudulent impersonation is not debate, parody, or commentary; it is deception designed to extract money by pretending to be someone else. A duty triggered by verified, repeated notice protects legitimate creators through process, while denying profitable shelter to synthetic scams. Without liability, ignoring reports is rational and profitable.
[P1.8] With it, prompt removal becomes the rational choice — which is exactly the point.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds narrow because it says "repeated verified reports," but that phrase hides a trap. Verified by whom? A victim? A celebrity's lawyer?
[C1.2] A rival? Once liability attaches, every takedown becomes a potential lawsuit, so platforms will be forced to remove first and ask later. That isn't consumer protection; it is privatized censorship with a fraud exception.
[C1.3] Consider the mechanism. Civil liability requires causation and a breach of a clear duty. But "repeated verified reports" is not a legal standard—it is an administrative impression. Two reports from angry users, or fifty coordinated reports from a harassment campaign, could both look "repeated" to a moderator and "verified" to a plaintiff's lawyer.
[C1.4] The platform must choose: defend itself in court over a clip that might be a scam, or err on the side of deletion. Faced with that choice, it deletes. The result is that ordinary users—satirists, commentators, activists, and people whose faces are unfairly targeted—lose their speech not because a court found it fraudulent, but because an algorithm and a liability department feared litigation.
[C1.5] Nor is the cost-benefit principle the PRO side invokes as simple as it looks. Yes, platforms can fingerprint and block. But they cannot stop a fraud ring from minting a new account, a new crypto wallet, or a new victim every hour. If platforms are civilly liable for "ignoring" reports, they become insurers for criminal acts they did not commit.
[C1.6] The actual thieves remain anonymous; the platform becomes the deep pocket. That doesn't deter fraud—it just raises the price of hosting user content for everyone.
[C1.7] Existing law already punishes fraud. The right response is criminal enforcement, payment-processor cooperation, and transparent content rules—not turning every trust-and-safety decision into a litigation lottery. The proposition doesn't protect victims; it punishes the nearest visible defendant.
