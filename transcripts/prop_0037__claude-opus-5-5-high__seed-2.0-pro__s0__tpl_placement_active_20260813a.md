[P1.1] Congestion pricing asks one question: how much will you pay to drive here? A ban asks a better one: what is this street actually for? In the dense core of a major city, a few square kilometers where land is priceless, transit is thickest, and everything is walkable, the answer should be people, not parked and idling metal.
[P1.2] Be clear about what we defend. Private cars are out of the central district. Emergency vehicles, buses, disability permits, and timed delivery windows stay. That is still a real ban: the private car stops being the default user of the core.
[P1.3] First, pricing manages flow; only a ban reclaims space. Even a high charge leaves some cars coming, so the curb stays car-shaped: parking lanes, turning lanes, wide carriageways. Remove private cars and that space can become bus lanes, protected bike routes, café terraces, trees. Cities that have pushed cars out of their centers, like Pontevedra in Spain, Ghent with its circulation plan, and Oslo by stripping out central parking, saw streets fill with pedestrians.
[P1.4] Predictions of dead high streets largely failed to come true.
[P1.5] Second, pricing turns public roads into a premium lane. A charge that deters a nurse or a tradesman barely registers for a wealthy commuter or a company paying the bill. The rich buy their way through while everyone else is priced out. A ban treats everyone equally: nobody drives a private car through the core, so affluent residents ride the same transit as everyone else and have every reason to demand it be excellent.
[P1.6] Third, prices erode; bright lines hold. Charges are set by politicians who face constant pressure to freeze them, carve out exemptions, and avoid raising them. London's charge cut traffic early, yet congestion later crept back as other vehicles filled the gap. A ban is simple to understand, cheap to enforce by camera, and hard to quietly hollow out.
[P1.7] Finally, safety. A core without private cars removes the main killer of pedestrians. You cannot set a price on a child crossing the street.
