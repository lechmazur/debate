[P1.1] Recessions are not solved by legislation at the speed legislators move. By the time a discretionary package is drafted, negotiated, and passed, months of job losses have already burned through household savings. Automatic stabilizers solve the timing problem: benefits expand the moment unemployment rises, with no vote, no lobbying, no partisan deadlock. That speed is the core of the case, and it is why the debt objection, real as it is, cannot carry the day.
[P1.2] The mechanism is straightforward. Unemployment is not just a statistic; it is a collapse in the incomes of people with the highest propensity to spend. A laid-off worker doesn't save their transfer — they pay rent, buy groceries, fix the car they need for the next job interview. That spending is someone else's revenue, someone else's paycheck.
[P1.3] Without that floor, the multiplier runs in reverse: falling demand produces more layoffs, which produce more falling demand. Cash transfers to the jobless are among the most demand-effective tools governments have precisely because the money moves fast and gets spent completely.
[P1.4] The debt question deserves a direct answer, not a dodge. Debt ratios rise during downturns partly because the denominator — national income — is shrinking. Refusing to borrow when private credit is collapsing and interest costs are low is a policy that makes the ratio worse in the long run by making the recession deeper. The alternative to increased debt in a downturn is not fiscal virtue; it is procyclical austerity, which history has punished repeatedly.
[P1.5] Countries that cut during slumps watched tax receipts fall and deficits widen anyway.
[P1.6] Crucially, automatic stabilizers are self-reversing. When unemployment falls, payouts contract on their own. Discretionary stimulus is the ratchet: programs get created and persist. A pre-committed rule expands debt temporarily and predictably, then unwinds — which is exactly the profile of borrowing that markets can absorb.

[C1.1] PRO's core is timing. But timing is not the only fiscal virtue, and the motion sacrifices targeting, discipline, and sustainability for speed. Governments already have automatic stabilizers: unemployment insurance, means-tested welfare, progressive taxes. The proposition adds a new ratchet: cash transfers that expand whenever unemployment rises, regardless of why, and even when debt is already high.
[C1.2] Unemployment is a noisy, ambiguous signal. It rises in demand recessions, but also in supply shocks, sectoral shifts, strikes, demographic change, and statistical revisions. An automatic trigger cannot distinguish a laid-off construction worker who will spend every dollar from a household with savings, a second earner, or a worker between jobs. So a large share of the transfer leaks to saving, debt repayment, or imports.
[C1.3] Meanwhile, the households truly in crisis may still be missed if they are outside the unemployment category—gig workers, discouraged workers, small business owners.
[C1.4] The debt clause is the decisive flaw. "Even if it increases public debt" is not a minor concession; it is the design. Downturns are exactly when tax revenues fall and borrowing needs spike. Additional automatic cash transfers can raise yields, crowd out private investment, and invite monetary offset: if central banks tighten to control inflation, the stimulus is neutralized while the debt remains.
[C1.5] For vulnerable countries, debt-financed transfers risk a sovereign-spread spiral, forcing procyclical cuts later—the very austerity PRO condemns.
[C1.6] PRO calls these stabilizers self-reversing. Politics rarely reverses benefits. Formulas become floors, constituencies defend them, and temporary expansions become permanent baselines. Discretionary policy can target, condition, and sunset aid; a formula cannot.
[C1.7] The better approach is to preserve existing automatic stabilizers, let monetary policy and targeted emergency programs respond, and save debt capacity for genuine catastrophes. Speed without aim is not stabilization; it is an expensive entitlement triggered by imperfect data. That is why governments should not adopt the proposition.
