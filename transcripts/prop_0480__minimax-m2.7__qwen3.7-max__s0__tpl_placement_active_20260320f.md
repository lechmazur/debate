[P1.1] Universal basic services—reliable transit, broadband, childcare, and healthcare—share one crucial feature that cash transfers cannot replicate: they solve coordination problems that individual money cannot. When a city builds a subway, everyone benefits simultaneously. When a government guarantees childcare, parents can work, children develop better, and the economy grows—all at once. Cash does none of this.
[P1.2] Consider what actually happens when governments hand out money without strings. Some recipients pay rent. Others do not. Some buy nutritious food.
[P1.3] Others do not. The guarantee is uniformity of transfer, not uniformity of outcome. A family in a transit desert cannot spend their way to a commute that does not exist. A child without healthcare cannot use cash to conjure a pediatrician into their neighborhood.
[P1.4] Cash assumes a functioning private market for every need, and in too many domains—healthcare, early childhood education, reliable internet—that market either does not exist, charges monopoly prices, or delivers catastrophic inequality.
[P1.5] Universal services sidestep this. Medicare-for-all or national childcare systems negotiate prices, standardize quality, and guarantee access regardless of ZIP code or credit score. They also avoid the administrative humiliation of proving poverty, which punishes recipients and creates bureaucratic drag. More fundamentally, they treat essential human needs as infrastructure rights rather than charity cases.
[P1.6] A child with guaranteed healthcare and childcare does not experience their childhood differently based on whether their parents are prudent or reckless with a monthly check. The service itself is the guarantee.
[P1.7] Opponents will argue that cash gives people freedom. But freedom to choose between bad options is not freedom at all. A parent choosing between childcare and groceries faces a false choice that well-designed public systems eliminate entirely. The real choice is this: do we build the scaffolding that lets everyone participate in society, or do we hand out vouchers and hope markets behave?
[P1.8] History suggests we should build the scaffolding.
