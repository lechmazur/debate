[P1.1] Automated video and personality assessments are not neutral shortcuts; they are pre-employment discrimination at scale. Governments should ban or heavily restrict them because they fail the basic tests that justify any hiring tool: validity, transparency, and fairness.
[P1.2] First, these tools measure the wrong thing. A video assessment may score whether a candidate smiles, maintains eye contact, or speaks at an expected pace, but it cannot measure whether she can do the job. That means it systematically penalizes disabled applicants, neurodivergent people, non-native speakers, and candidates from cultural backgrounds where direct eye contact or performative enthusiasm is less common. A personality test that screens for a narrow ideal of extroversion or “emotional stability” can quietly weed out people with depression, anxiety, or simply an introverted style, even when they would perform well.
[P1.3] The supposed objectivity is manufactured.
[P1.4] Second, the process is opaque and unaccountable. A rejected candidate usually cannot learn which facial movement, pause, or trait lowered the score, cannot meaningfully contest it, and may not even know a machine made the judgment. When bias occurs, the vendor’s algorithm hides it. Governments already regulate hiring to prevent discrimination, but these systems evade that regime by turning subjective judgments into proprietary scores.
[P1.5] Third, the harm is not hypothetical or small. One flawed tool can filter thousands of applicants before a human ever looks at them, screening out the very candidates who might challenge groupthink or bring different experiences. The employer’s claimed benefits—speed, cost, consistency—do not justify automating exclusion. Consistent unfairness is not better than occasional unfairness.
[P1.6] A heavy restriction is the minimum response: prohibit these tools unless the employer can prove the assessment measures job-relevant skills, has been validated on the relevant population, and allows meaningful human review. In practice, most would not survive. If they cannot, they should not decide who gets a livelihood.

[C1.1] The motion mistakes a bad implementation for a bad idea. Automated video and personality assessments are not inherently discriminatory; they are among the few tools capable of forcing employers to apply the same standard to every applicant. A government ban or heavy restriction would not purify hiring—it would restore the unaccountable, informal judgments these tools were built to discipline.
[C1.2] Consider the status quo PRO leaves in place: a recruiter in a live interview, weighing “culture fit,” shared background, or a firm handshake. That process is opaque, inconsistent, and notoriously biased in favor of conventional appearance and social proximity. An automated assessment asks every candidate the same questions, scores against a documented rubric, and leaves a record that can be audited. For a parent applying at midnight, a candidate with speech anxiety, or a disabled applicant who cannot travel to an office, an asynchronous video interview is not exclusion—it is access.
[C1.3] PRO says these tools “measure the wrong thing.” Sometimes they do. But the remedy for weak validity is a validity requirement, not prohibition. Structured job-sample videos and properly normed personality instruments have genuine predictive value when built for the role and validated on relevant populations. Banning the category because some vendors sell snake oil is like banning interviews because some interviewers ask illegal questions.
[C1.4] Heavy restriction also has a hidden cost. If employers face liability for using auditable algorithms, many will retreat to referrals and unstructured conversations—channels no regulator can inspect and no rejected candidate can appeal. PRO’s “prove it or lose it” regime would entrench incumbents and chill the very improvements that could make screening fairer. A heavy default rule makes the safe choice the no-choice: hire through the old boys’ network and hope no one asks for the data.
[C1.5] The right policy is regulatory, not prohibitory: require job-related validation, independent bias audits, clear disclosure, and meaningful human review. Do not amputate the instrument. Fix the calibration.
