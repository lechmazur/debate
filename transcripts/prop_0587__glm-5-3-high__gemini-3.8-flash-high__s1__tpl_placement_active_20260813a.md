[P1.1] The question is not whether a carbon border adjustment is elegant policy; it is whether it moves more tons of carbon out of the atmosphere in five years than subsidies and regulations alone. It does, for one structural reason: it changes the economics of foreign emissions, which domestic policy cannot touch at all.
[P1.2] Domestic subsidies—the Inflation Reduction Act's credits, regulatory standards on power plants and vehicles—have a hard ceiling. They only influence American emissions, roughly a tenth of the global total. Clean-energy tax credits in Ohio do nothing about a steel mill in Hebei or a coal plant in Java. And even within U.S. borders, subsidies alone tolerate carbon leakage: when American production costs rise, production and its emissions migrate to jurisdictions with no carbon price, washing out part of the domestic gain.
[P1.3] A border adjustment attacks leakage directly by charging imports for their embedded carbon, making the high-carbon path more expensive everywhere U.S. markets reach.
[P1.4] The second channel is leverage. The EU's carbon border mechanism already forced major trading partners to price carbon or lose market access. Exporters respond to market access the way they respond to no other incentive: when the EU moved, we saw countries and industries—Turkish cement, Indian steel interests, Chinese aluminum—scramble to establish carbon accounting and pricing precisely because the alternative was paying someone else's border fee. A U.S. adjustment, given the size of the American market, doubles that pressure.
[P1.5] That is emissions-relevant diplomacy achieved through price signals, not negotiations that take decades.
[P1.6] Third, speed. Subsidies scale with manufacturing capacity—factories, interconnection queues, supply chains—that take a decade to build. A border adjustment's incentive effect begins the day it passes, and its leverage on foreign carbon pricing can materialize within a single budget cycle.
[P1.7] CON will say this is trade friction and regressive cost. Fine—but the motion asks about emissions. Domestic-only policy leaves ninety percent of the problem untouched and leaks part of what it fixes. Border adjustment does not.
