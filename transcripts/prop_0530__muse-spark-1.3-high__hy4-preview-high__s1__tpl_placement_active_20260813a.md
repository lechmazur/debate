[P1.1] A mass grave is not just an archaeological site. It is someone's grandfather, grandmother, child, left where killers dumped them. No university, museum, or tourism board inherits a stronger claim to those bones than the families and communities who still mourn them. Substantial control is not special treatment; it is the basic recognition that the dead belong to their kin.
[P1.2] Only descendant communities can make these sites truthful. Outsiders see artifacts and skeletons. Descendants carry names, languages, burial rites, songs, and the oral memory of what happened and why it matters. When archaeologists or officials control the story alone, they flatten atrocity into neutral science: dates, soil layers, body counts.
[P1.3] That is how forced labor camps become exhibits about industrial architecture and how extermination sites become lessons in military logistics. Community control forces excavation and explanation to answer the right question: what was done to our people, and what does justice require now?
[P1.4] Without that control, harm repeats itself. Bones are photographed, stacked in glass cases, handled by strangers. Sacred ground is opened for research careers, headlines, or dark tourism. Worse, governments that inherit guilt have every incentive to sanitize: to call murder "excesses," slavery "labor migration," genocide "tragedy."
[P1.5] Giving curators and state agencies final say invites a second erasure — this time under the banner of education.
[P1.6] Control also makes memory work. Grief needs authority over whether to dig or rebury, what to show and what to veil, which words are intolerable. When communities lead, sites become places of mourning and warning that the public actually trusts. When they are overruled, families boycott, protest, withdraw testimony — and the public learns nothing real.
[P1.7] Who should decide how atrocity is shown? Those who live with its consequences.

[C1.1] The dead are not artifacts — the PRO is right. But a mass grave or forced-labor site is not only a grave; it is a crime scene and a public archive. The fatal flaw in the proposition is that it merges the right to mourn with the power to decide what evidence exists and what the public is told. Grief deserves a seat at the table; it cannot hold the gavel over forensic science and public history.
[C1.2] Excavation is evidentiary, not merely ceremonial. In Srebrenica, turning anonymous graves into named victims and courtroom proof required professional control: chain of custody, autopsy, DNA, years of methodical recovery. If a descendant faction could dictate "dig or don't dig" or "publish or veil," a veto could destroy that evidence — from relatives wanting prompt reburial before documentation, or politicians speaking for the community. Independent stewardship balances reverence with proof; control sacrifices proof to whoever wins an internal struggle.
[C1.3] And the struggle is real, because "descendant community" is not one voice. Atrocity sites usually hold plural victims — Jews, Roma, Poles, Soviet POWs in one forced-labor camp; students, Indigenous peasants, and unionists in one dictatorship-era grave. Descendants are divided between diaspora activists, local survivors, families demanding exhumation, and families wanting the past left sealed. Handing "the community" substantial control just means a veto for the faction that shouts loudest.
[C1.4] Look at New York's African Burial Ground. Community protest rightly halted desecration. But the recovered knowledge of those individuals' lives and deaths — what made the memorial truthful — came from letting independent bioarchaeologists study hundreds of remains before reinterment. Consultation won that victory; a veto over study would have erased the archive.
[C1.5] The answer to state sanitization is not a community veto over "intolerable words." It is rigorous, publicly accountable history naming all perpetrators and all victims. Descendants must be central consultants, not holders of substantial control over the truth.
