[P1.1] The moment a car is sold, its installed hardware becomes the owner’s property. Software should not turn that property into a rented cage. If a vehicle leaves the factory with heated seats, remote start, a premium sound system, or driver-assistance sensors, those physical capabilities are already paid for in the purchase price. Charging again, every month, merely to unlock them is not a service; it is a toll booth on the owner’s own car.
[P1.2] The defensible line is this: subscriptions are legitimate for genuinely ongoing services—live traffic, streaming, cellular data, server-side navigation, emergency response centers. Those cost money every month. But an embedded heater, motor, sensor, or amplifier does not become more expensive because it is switched on. The manufacturer already built it, installed it, and sold it.
[P1.3] Retaining a remote kill switch over that hardware is a double charge and an assertion of control that ownership should forbid. If the hardware is present and its cost was baked into the sticker price, a recurring unlock fee is not access—it is ransom.
[P1.4] The harms are practical, not theoretical. Owners lose value at resale because buyers must inherit recurring fees. Safety features can be paywalled, so protection depends on a credit card. A manufacturer can remotely disable a feature after sale, leaving the owner with hardware they possess but cannot use.
[P1.5] And because software locks are buried in end-user agreements, consumers cannot negotiate.
[P1.6] CON will say subscriptions fund innovation and ongoing software. Fine—charge for ongoing services. But do not pretend a seat heater is a service. The principle is simple: if it is bolted into my car and I bought the car, I should own its function permanently.
[P1.7] A car is not a vending machine that keeps charging after the sale. Decide for ownership, not indefinite rental.

[C1.1] The proposition rests on a category error: it treats a car like a toaster. A heated seat is a component; adaptive cruise, hands-free driving, remote diagnostics, and even some “hardware” functions are software-defined capabilities running on computers, sensors, maps, and cloud services that must be maintained after the sale. The owner buys the physical car, not an unbounded promise that every installed part will be activated forever at no additional cost.
[C1.2] That promise has a price. Modern vehicle software requires continuous cybersecurity patching, bug fixes, map and traffic data, feature validation, warranty support, and recall compliance. Those are not costs that end when the car leaves the lot. If that principle were adopted, manufacturers would have to fund lifetime software support from a one-time purchase, raise sticker prices across the board, or strip features from lower trims.
[C1.3] The motion calls this ownership; in practice it is an unfunded mandate and a cross-subsidy from drivers who never use the feature to those who do.
[C1.4] Nor is “already installed” the same as “already paid for.” Automakers commonly build one hardware platform and enable different capabilities by software so buyers can choose a lower entry price and pay only for what they use. That is a pricing choice, not theft of a toaster. The real abuse the PRO side identifies—disabling a paid-for safety feature or hiding a lock in a clickwrap agreement—calls for disclosure, repair rights, and limits on remote disabling, not a blanket rule that every installed component must work forever without a subscription.
[C1.5] A car is a maintained, regulated, connected machine. Ownership includes the right to repair and to refuse unfair terms. It does not include a right to perpetual software labor paid for by someone else. Reject the motion as written; adopt the narrower consumer-protection rule instead.
