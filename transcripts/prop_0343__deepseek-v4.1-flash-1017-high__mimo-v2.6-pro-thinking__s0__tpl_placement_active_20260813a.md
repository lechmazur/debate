[P1.1] The question is not whether students will use AI to draft coursework; it is whether universities will pretend they don’t. A flat ban cannot be enforced at scale, so it selects for concealment. The honest student who uses AI to brainstorm loses to the student who hides it; the instructor cannot tell which work was assisted, so assessment becomes guesswork. Conditional permission flips that: disclosure makes assistance visible, and accountability keeps the student answerable for the final product.
[P1.2] That is the decisive comparison. Under this policy, the university assesses what it actually cares about: whether the student can verify claims, spot weak reasoning, revise prose, and defend conclusions. A student may use AI to produce an outline, but must check sources, remove fabricated citations, and explain why the argument holds. A coding student may ask AI for a function, but must test it, explain its limits, and fix it.
[P1.3] The drafting tool is not the author; the student is. This is how writing already works with spellcheck, citation software, and calculators in quantitative courses—tools change the labor, not the responsibility.
[P1.4] Disclosure also improves teaching. If instructors see where AI helped, they can design assessments around verification, oral defense, and revision. They can distinguish mere copy-paste from genuine collaboration. A ban, by contrast, teaches students to launder AI output and leaves universities chasing detection software that is probabilistic and easily gamed.
[P1.5] The equity point matters too. Students with money and connections already have tutors, editors, and private advice; AI is a widely available drafting aid. Banning it does not create a level field—it penalizes students who follow the rule while others quietly gain the advantage.
[P1.6] So the policy choice is clear: invisible use with deniable cheating, or disclosed use with enforceable accountability. The proposition chooses the second. It preserves academic integrity by making the student’s reasoning the thing that must be demonstrated—not by banning a tool students will use anyway.

[C1.1] The motion treats drafting as mere transcription—as if the words arrive from elsewhere and the thinking happens separately. In most coursework, that is false. Drafting *is* the reasoning. When a student constructs a sociology essay paragraph by paragraph, the argument is discovered through the writing: the first claim is refined, evidence is tested against the emerging thesis, the structure is revised because the reasoning demands it.
[C1.2] Hand that labor to a model and the student can "verify" the output—read it, check a source, nod along—but verification is shallow recognition, not construction. They can defend a conclusion they never had to reach.
[C1.3] That makes the accountability clause hollow. What does "remain accountable for accuracy and reasoning" mean when the university cannot see whether the reasoning was the student's? It is a signature on a document someone else wrote. The policy shifts the burden of proof onto the instructor to distinguish genuine from perfunctory revision—a judgment that is slow, subjective, and impossible at the scale of large introductory courses where most coursework sits.
[C1.4] Second, disclosure does not fix the incentive problem PRO identifies; it formalizes it. Under a ban, concealment is cheating. Under this policy, the student who discloses bears scrutiny and an accountability burden; the student who lies gets the same AI advantage with none of the friction. The honest student is disadvantaged *by the rule*.
[C1.5] Universities would need enforcement against false disclosure anyway—meaning PRO inherits the detection problem it blames on bans, plus a new one.
[C1.6] Third, the analogy fails. Spellcheck corrects spelling; a calculator executes a procedure the student must still choose and set up. AI drafting generates the very intellectual content being assessed. A tool that solves the exam is not a tool that supports it.
[C1.7] Finally, "most coursework" is the giveaway. If AI drafting is allowed across most assignments, students accumulate degrees without ever practicing sustained argumentation. We certify graduates who can review a machine's reasoning but cannot originate one. Universities cannot outsource the skill they exist to teach and then certify it on trust.
