[P1.1] Consent is a necessary condition for ethical exchange, but it is never sufficient on its own. A transaction can be freely agreed to and still be morally bankrupt because ethics demands more than mere permission. It demands fairness, non-exploitation, and the absence of harm to others. When we treat consent as the sole ethical criterion, we grant a blank check to exploitation, commodification, and systemic injustice that no society can accept.
[P1.2] Consider the desperate worker who agrees to labor in dangerous conditions for starvation wages. She consents, yet the transaction is exploitative because her options are artificially constrained by poverty. Consent here merely masks coercion of circumstance. Similarly, a vote becomes meaningless if sold: both parties consent, but the transaction corrupts the democratic process and harms third parties who never agreed.
[P1.3] Organ sales illustrate the same failure—wealthy buyers obtain organs from impoverished sellers who "consent" out of desperation, commodifying the human body and preying on systemic vulnerability.
[P1.4] Consent also cannot legitimize harmful objects or acts. Two adults may consent to a contract killing, but the agreement does not make the act ethical because it violates the fundamental right to life and creates grave societal harm. Ethics evaluates the substance of the transaction, not merely the form of agreement. Power imbalances—between employer and employee, doctor and patient, adult and child—mean that apparent consent often reflects domination rather than genuine free choice.
[P1.5] Some contend that consent respects autonomy, but autonomy is not the only moral value at stake. Justice, dignity, and harm prevention constrain what may rightfully be consented to. A transaction requires not just a "yes," but a context where that yes reflects genuine freedom, fair terms, and no collateral damage to third parties. Without these additional safeguards, consent is merely the veneer of legitimacy stretched over exploitation.
