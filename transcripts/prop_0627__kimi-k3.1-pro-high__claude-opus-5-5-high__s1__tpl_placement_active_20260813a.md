[P1.1] A government that may knowingly leave its people drinking contaminated water or breathing dangerous air, without any enforceable legal duty to act, can promise “life,” “liberty,” and “health” while making their physical exercise impossible. A fundamental environmental right closes that gap. It recognizes a minimum condition of human dignity, not a judicial guarantee of pristine nature.
[P1.2] The right should be deliberately modest: protection against serious, foreseeable environmental threats to basic health, enforced against governments that cause them, permit them, or persistently refuse to address them. It would not prohibit every emission, eliminate every risk, or tell legislatures which energy policy to choose. It would establish a floor below which governmental neglect cannot push people.
[P1.3] That floor is necessary because ordinary politics systematically underprotects environmental health. Polluters often receive concentrated benefits, while harms are dispersed across neighborhoods, workers, children, and future generations. Those bearing the greatest burdens may have the least political influence, and damage can become irreversible before elections provide a remedy. Statutes alone do not solve this problem: governments can weaken them, create exceptions, decline enforcement, or leave affected communities to litigate fragmented procedures.
[P1.4] An enforceable right supplies a backstop when the ordinary system fails.
[P1.5] Consider a community whose public water system remains dangerous long after officials know the risk. Without a fundamental right, residents may have to wait for new legislation or prove that some narrower statutory duty applies. With one, they can demand a justified governmental response and judicial review of continued inaction. The court need not design pipes or select treatment technology; it can require lawful standards, a credible compliance plan, and an end to unreasonable delay.
[P1.6] The institutional objection therefore confuses setting a constitutional floor with administering environmental policy. Courts already review complex health, prison, and public-safety conditions while leaving governments substantial discretion over means. Here too, judges can rely on established scientific and regulatory benchmarks, require evidence of serious harm and governmental responsibility, and tailor remedies prospectively.

[P5.1] The decisive question is not whether judges can allocate environmental budgets better than legislatures. It is whether a legislature’s lawful decision can immunize government from answering for serious, foreseeable threats to basic health. CON says yes: if standards are repealed, exemptions enacted, or known dangers left unaddressed, residents should accept the tradeoff and wait for elections. That is precisely the power a fundamental right exists to deny.
[P5.2] Calling review “overriding” whenever government complied with its own law proves too much: statutes cannot define the limits of constitutional protection. Nor does review require judges to price pipes against hospital beds. Government must provide evidence, consider feasible interim protection, and justify delay; it still chooses the means and initially sets the budget. Scarcity may alter a timetable, but cannot become an unexplained license for indefinite exposure.
[P5.3] CON offers elections after injury and private lawsuits after contamination. The motion offers something different: a minimum government cannot legislate away and a forum where neglect must be justified before harm becomes irreversible loss. Courts need not guarantee clean air or water; they must make governmental neglect answerable. Democracy governs environmental policy, but it does not own a veto over the basic health of the politically unheard.
