[P1.1] The creation of novel synthetic viral pathogens with clear pandemic potential is a risk humanity cannot afford. History proves that even top-tier labs fail: the 1978 Birmingham smallpox leak killed a photographer and triggered a citywide emergency; more recently, a U.S. lab in 2014 discovered forgotten vials of smallpox, revealing persistent containment gaps. A pathogen engineered for airborne spread and high lethality could, if released, cause a global catastrophe before we even detect it. As DNA synthesis and gene editing become cheaper and more widespread, the cumulative probability of an accidental release over time and across many labs rises significantly, as COVID-19 showed, natural viruses cause global devastation; engineered ones could be far deadlier by design.
[P1.2] Defensive preparedness does not require manufacturing such threats. We can study existing natural viruses, use computational models to predict transmission, develop broad-spectrum antivirals, and test vaccines against less dangerous proxies without creating pandemic-capable agents. The 2014 pause on H5N1 gain-of-function research, followed by intense ethical debate, showed that even leading virologists recognize the risks can outweigh the benefits. Furthermore, this research is inherently dual-use: the same techniques and knowledge that bolster defense can be repurposed for offense, and digital sequences or lab strains can be stolen, leaked, or replicated by malevolent actors.
[P1.3] Without a global ban, we invite a security dilemma where states, fearing others’ capabilities, pursue their own engineered pathogens, accelerating an arms race that increases the very risks we seek to mitigate. The only sensible path is a global prohibition that removes the catastrophic risk, the dual-use temptation, and the perverse incentives driving this dangerous research forward, and aligns with the precautionary principle: when stakes are existential, we must err on the side of caution.
