[P1.2] My standard is simple: fees should track the costs a user imposes.
[P1.3] Start with the physical facts nobody disputes. Length and width are measurable, and a vehicle that overhangs a stall, blocks a bike lane, or forces a delivery van into the travel lane is consuming more of a fixed resource. Two compacts can share what one full-size pickup occupies.
[P1.4] Second, mass: pavement damage rises sharply with axle weight, and heavier vehicles carry more kinetic energy into every collision. Third, geometry: tall front ends and high hoods worsen forward visibility and strike pedestrians in the chest and head rather than the legs. [P1.5] Cities already accept this logic everywhere else. We charge oversize-load permits, weight-based commercial truck fees, and higher tolls for larger axle classes. [P1.6] Nor is this exotic. Paris voters approved sharply higher parking charges for the heaviest SUVs, and weight-linked registration is common practice in various jurisdictions. The administrative machinery already exists: curb weight and dimensions sit on the registration record.
[P1.7] And note the design freedom this gives cities. Higher fees on the largest vehicles can fund crosswalk upgrades — or lower permit costs for small cars, so the median driver pays less.
[P1.8] The alternative isn't fairness. It's a subsidy, paid by pedestrians, cyclists, and everyone driving something modest.
