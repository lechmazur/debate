[P1.1] Religious freedom deserves strong protection for worship, doctrine, and the internal choices that make a faith a faith. A church must be able to choose its priests, a mosque its imams, a synagogue the teachers who transmit its theology, without the state rewriting its creed. That core is conscience itself.
[P1.2] But that is why exemptions must stop where the pulpit ends and the marketplace begins.
[P1.3] When a religious institution opens a bakery, hospital, hotel, or commercial service to the public, hires workers from the public, and profits from the public, it is no longer merely exercising religion. It is exercising market power. And market power exercised discriminatorily does real harm: the employee fired because she married a same-sex partner, even though she cleaned floors or taught algebra; the couple turned away from a public service; the patient told equal citizenship stops at this counter because ownership has changed.
[P1.4] Anti-discrimination rules exist for those settings. Employment, health care, and public accommodations are gateways to livelihood and dignity. A broad commercial exemption does not just protect the institution; it transfers the cost of its beliefs onto third parties who did not consent to live by them — workers who need a job, customers who need a service, often where alternatives are scarce.
[P1.5] A tight, core-function test solves both sides better than a blanket exemption. Ask what the person actually does: Do they lead worship, preach, define doctrine, or form adherents in the faith? If yes, autonomy controls. If they perform a secular commercial function in a business held out to everyone, civil rights control.
[P1.6] That protects ministry while preventing religion from becoming a corporate shield for discrimination.
[P1.7] No other freedom works as an unlimited commercial license. Speech does not exempt a business from fraud law; assembly does not exempt it from fire codes. Entering commerce means accepting the neutral rules of commerce. Religious institutions should be free to be religious, not exempt employers and sellers at will.

[C1.1] The proposition draws a line religion does not draw, then hands the pen to the state. Worship and clergy selection are not the whole of religious life. For many traditions, healing the sick, teaching the young, sheltering the poor, and ordering their common work by religious conviction are the practice of faith, not side businesses beside a pulpit. A rule that protects only what officials call "core," and treats the rest as ordinary commerce, does not balance liberty and equality.
[C1.2] It shrinks religion to liturgy and polices everything else.
[C1.3] That shrinkage is not neutral. Whether a nurse in a Catholic hospital, a math teacher in a religious school, or a caseworker at a faith-based agency is "core" is a theological ranking — the judgment religious freedom exists to keep from the state. The error is deeper than an unstable line. The exemption follows the institution's religious governance, not a court's ranking of job descriptions.
[C1.4] A tight test assumes the wrong question. It spreads state theology across employment, health care, and public service.
[C1.5] The marketplace label does not rescue the test. A church hospital that bills insurers is still a ministry. A religious college that charges tuition is still forming students. Once money changes hands, this rule lets anti-discrimination law rewrite hiring, benefits, and service until the institution contradicts its doctrine or leaves the field.
[C1.6] When those hospitals, schools, and shelters close or secularize, people who sought them lose the provider, not merely a preference.
[C1.7] Fraud laws and fire codes do not compare. They stop deception and danger without forcing a community to affirm a moral claim it rejects. Rules that force a religious institution to staff, serve, or structure itself against its doctrine do compel that affirmation. Workers and clients often choose these institutions because their character is public.
[C1.8] The heavier coercion is the state's: faith may govern the sanctuary, but not the work in which it actually lives.

[P2.1] Your case rests on letting the institution define its own exemption: if it calls healing, teaching, or staffing “ministry,” anti-discrimination law must retreat. That is not a limiting principle. It would allow a church-owned hotel, retail chain, or for-profit clinic to claim immunity by sincere declaration. Sincerity establishes that a belief is religious; it cannot establish that every commercial act done in its name overrides the civil rights of employees and customers.
[P2.2] Nor does a core-function test require state theology. The state does not rank whether healing matters as much as preaching, or decide true doctrine. It accepts the faith’s account of its beliefs and asks a secular, observable question: does this person lead worship, preach, teach the faith, or set doctrine? A billing clerk, janitor, or algebra instructor who does none of those things is not made clergy because the employer is religious.
[P2.3] That avoids theological judgment; your alternative creates it, by requiring courts to bless any job description the institution religiously labels.
[P2.4] The claim that workers and patients choose the institution’s character proves too little. People choose a nearby hospital in an emergency, a job in a tight labor market, or the only school with an opening — not a waiver of equal treatment. And when an institution advertises to the public, takes public customers, and benefits from public licensing and infrastructure, its character is not a private compact. It is power over people who never joined the faith.
[P2.5] Finally, complying with employment law is not compelled affirmation. Employing a woman in a same-sex marriage as a payroll clerk does not make the church celebrate that marriage, just as serving divorced, remarried, or nonbelieving customers does not make it endorse their lives. What does compel is your rule: it compels third parties to finance another faith’s doctrine with lost jobs and denied services. Institutions can still heal, teach, and shelter under civil-rights law, but if they enter the public market, they cannot make equal citizenship the price of their ministry.

[P5.1] The decisive choice is not whether faith can heal or teach. It can. The choice is who pays when that faith operates a public workplace.
[P5.2] The other side now concedes that a church-owned hotel or retail chain is subject to anti-discrimination law because ownership is not ministry. That concession gives away the principle: sincere religious purpose alone does not create immunity, and drawing a public, observable boundary is not forbidden theology. Once that is true, the only question is whether the boundary stops at the hospital door or follows the actual work inside.
[P5.3] It must follow the work. A payroll clerk, billing clerk, or nurse fired for her marriage does not become clergy because her employer calls the building a ministry. Her lost livelihood is not made voluntary by a mission statement, and "go elsewhere" is not equal citizenship where she needs this job or this care.
[P5.4] Protect the priest, the imam, the teacher of doctrine without apology. But when an institution hires from the public and sells to the public, the freedom to preach its creed cannot include the power to make non-adherents forfeit theirs. Religion governs its rites; it does not purchase the market.
