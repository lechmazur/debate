[P1.1] Every health budget is a life-and-death triage decision. A million dollars spent to buy a few extra weeks for one terminally ill patient is a million not spent preventing thousands of premature deaths. Justice demands we save the most life and health we can with scarce funds.
[P1.2] Preventive public health does exactly that. Vaccination, blood pressure control, smoking cessation, prenatal care, clean water, cancer screening — these interventions are cheap, proven, and compound across populations. Controlling hypertension costs pennies a day and averts strokes, heart attacks, kidney failure. Helping a teenager never start smoking prevents decades of cancer and lung disease.
[P1.3] Prevention does not merely postpone death; it preserves years of healthy, productive life. One nurse running a community screening clinic can do more for population longevity than an intensive care wing running at full capacity.
[P1.4] By contrast, marginal expansions of high-cost end-of-life care buy the least health at the highest price. We are not talking about basic comfort, pain relief, or human presence at the bedside, which must be protected. We are talking about the next expensive machine, the fourth-line chemotherapy or intensive intervention that on average adds days or weeks, often spent in hospitals, with toxicity and confusion. That is a tragic return on resources that could have kept families whole.
[P1.5] Prevention is also fairer. Expensive last-month technologies tend to flow to well-insured patients at elite centers, while preventable illness ravages the poor, the rural, and the young. A child spared measles, a mother surviving childbirth, a worker avoiding diabetes — those benefits go to people with entire lives ahead and fewest alternatives. Prioritizing them corrects inequality rather than magnifying it.
[P1.6] When we must choose — and budgets force choice — we should fund health, not just prolong dying. Prevent first, so fewer need rescue at the end.
