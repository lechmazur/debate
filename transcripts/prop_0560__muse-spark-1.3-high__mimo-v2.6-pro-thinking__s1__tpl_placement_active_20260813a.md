[P1.1] Every health budget is a choice about whose life gets saved. A million dollars can fund childhood vaccines, blood pressure clinics, tobacco quit programs, and cancer screening for thousands — or it can pay for a late-line treatment that extends life by weeks for a handful of patients already in intensive care. Prioritizing prevention is not abandoning the dying; it is refusing to sacrifice the living.
[P1.2] The return is lopsided. Preventive measures stop disease before it causes suffering: clean water, vaccines, hypertension control, diabetes prevention, smoking reduction, early screening. They buy years of healthy life at low cost per person, and they compound — a prevented heart attack or stroke means decades of work, parenting, and independence preserved. Marginal end-of-life expansions do the opposite: very high prices for very small average gains, often with painful side effects, hospital time, and false hope in the final months.
[P1.3] Prevention is also fairer. Serious illness strikes hardest at the poor, yet expensive end-of-life treatments flow mainly to those already inside top-tier hospitals with specialists, insurance, and time to navigate the system. A neighborhood clinic that controls blood pressure and catches cervical or colon cancer early helps working people, children, and marginalized groups who never reach those wards.
[P1.4] Compassion demands limits. No one proposes denying comfort, pain relief, or basic dignified care. The question is marginal expansions — another costly device, drug, or ICU protocol that adds little life and little comfort. A system that keeps pouring money there while prevention clinics close is not more humane; it watches people become preventably ill, then pays a fortune to fail them at the end.
[P1.5] Fund health first, not just dying last.
