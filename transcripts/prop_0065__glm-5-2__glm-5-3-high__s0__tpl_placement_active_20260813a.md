[P1.1] The motion draws a clear line: screening embryos for disease prevention is one thing; screening for height, appearance, or predicted intelligence is fundamentally different and should be legally restricted—regardless of how accurate the technology becomes.
[P1.2] Reliability changes nothing because the core objection was never "it doesn't work well enough yet." It's that selecting children based on cosmetic and cognitive traits treats human beings as products to be optimized. When parents discard embryos predicted to be shorter, or less attractive, or of average cognitive ability, they aren't preventing suffering—they're expressing a preference about what kind of person is worth bringing into existence. That is categorically different from preventing a child from inheriting a devastating illness.
[P1.3] And reliability intensifies the problem, it doesn't resolve it. If the technology is unreliable, few use it and its impact stays marginal. If it becomes dependable, it scales—and with scale comes collective harm. Consider height: if screened embryos become common, the average shifts, and what was once normal becomes the new selection floor.
[P1.4] Individual choices, rational in isolation, produce an arms race no one benefits from and everyone feels pressured to join. This is exactly the kind of collective action failure that legal restriction exists to prevent.
[P1.5] Inequality compounds this. Trait screening is expensive. If permitted, affluent families select for taller, sharper, more conventionally attractive children, widening biological gaps between classes in ways no education policy can touch. We already struggle with inherited advantage; engineering it directly into the genome makes equal opportunity a fiction.
[P1.6] The deepest issue is normative. A society that permits trait selection endorses the premise that children's worth is partly a design specification—that being shorter or less clever is a defect to be screened away rather than a human variation to be accepted. Once that premise takes root, no quantity of individual liberty restores the norm that children are unconditionally welcomed. Restriction now prevents a threshold we cannot quietly step back from.

[C1.2] Start with consistency. Parents already select for traits constantly. Sperm banks publish donor height, education, athletic history, and eye color, and customers choose accordingly. Nobody prosecutes a woman who conceives with a tall, Ivy League donor because she hopes her child will be tall and bright.
[C1.3] If the outcome is lawful when chosen through mate selection, it cannot become contraband when chosen through screening. The moral act—preferring certain traits in one's child—is identical; only the technology differs. A law that punishes the method while permitting the preference is arbitrary.
[C1.4] Second, the disease/trait boundary is fiction in practice. Predicted cognition collapses the distinction: many inheritable conditions affect learning and cognition, and polygenic scores for cognitive traits overlap with risk scores for schizophrenia, Alzheimer's, and autism. A statute restricting "non-disease" screening either bans scores doctors already use for legitimate medical purposes or hands regulators an impossible definitional task. Laws built on unworkable lines get litigated, patched, and selectively enforced against those least able to defend themselves.
[C1.5] Third, prohibition fails on PRO's own terms. Arms races and unequal access are real worries—but they are arguments for regulation, equity of access, and governance, not criminalization. A wealthy family facing a domestic ban flies abroad; the practice continues, now unregulated and beyond oversight. Bans don't stop trait selection; they stop public scrutiny of it.
[C1.6] Finally, the normative claim—that welcoming children unconditionally requires forbidding parental preference—proves too much. It would condemn donor selection, prenatal nutrition, and every ambitious parent who reads to a toddler hoping to raise achievement. Preference is not rejection. The state should not enter the IVF clinic to decide which parental hopes are legitimate.
[C1.7] Restriction is the wrong tool for a problem better handled by access rules and transparency.
