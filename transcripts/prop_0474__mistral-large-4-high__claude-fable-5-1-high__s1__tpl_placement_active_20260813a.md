[P1.1] State-tolerated botnets are not background noise; they are weaponized infrastructure. When a government knowingly allows criminal groups to operate command-and-control servers, bulletproof hosting, or money-laundering channels within its borders, it is not merely failing to police—it is providing safe haven to digital artillery that strikes hospitals, pipelines, and power grids.
[P1.2] The logic is straightforward. We sanction states that harbor terrorist training camps. We sanction states that shelter nuclear proliferators. A persistent botnet—one that survives takedown attempts and reconstitutes itself across jurisdictions—is a weapon of mass disruption with global reach.
[P1.3] The only difference from a missile silo is the delivery mechanism. When that infrastructure is state-tolerated, the host government is complicit in every ransomware attack, every DDoS assault, and every disinformation campaign it enables.
[P1.4] Consider the alternative. Without sanctions, states face zero cost for tolerating these networks. A government might calculate that allowing a botnet to operate from its territory yields intelligence benefits, economic kickbacks, or geopolitical leverage against adversaries, while the damage lands entirely on other countries. This is moral hazard in its purest form.
[P1.5] Sanctions comparable to those used against serious cyber-aggressors internalize that cost immediately. They force the host state to choose: dismantle the infrastructure or bear severe economic and diplomatic consequences.
[P1.6] This is not collective punishment. It targets the specific failure to act—the knowing toleration of persistent criminal infrastructure. It creates deterrence where none currently exists. When harboring a botnet carries the same sanctions weight as launching a direct cyberattack, rational states will finally invest in enforcement, prosecution, and rapid takedowns rather than looking the other way.
[P1.7] The choice becomes stark: police your territory or pay the price.
[P1.8] The proposition asks for a simple standard: accountability for providing the ground from which digital weapons are fired. That standard is just, necessary, and long overdue. We cannot condemn cyber-aggression while rewarding its safe havens.

That collapses the single distinction every deterrence system depends on: the gap between tolerating and doing. If a government knows it will be sanctioned at the same tier whether it looks the other way or actively runs the operation, we have just removed its last reason not to run the operation.
[C1.2] Flattening the escalation ladder doesn't deter; it invites states to climb it.
[C1.3] Now look at the missile-silo analogy. A silo sits in one place, owned by one state. A botnet is thousands of infected routers and laptops scattered across every country on earth, with command servers rented from legitimate hosting companies and moved within hours. By raw volume, some of the largest concentrations of malicious infrastructure sit in the United States, the Netherlands, and Germany, because that is where cheap, fast hosting lives.
[C1.4] Under this standard, either we sanction our allies, or we admit the rule will only ever be applied to adversaries we already dislike. The first is absurd; the second is a geopolitical weapon dressed as cybersecurity law.
[C1.5] "Knowingly tolerated" solves nothing. Who proves knowledge? Attribution of a single attack takes months of contested forensics; attribution of a government's state of mind about a shifting network is unfalsifiable. Any state can allege it, none can disprove it, and the accused has no court to appeal to.
[C1.6] Finally, the policy destroys the tool that actually works. Every major botnet takedown in recent memory was a multinational police operation that depended on cooperation, shared evidence, and coordinated seizures. Sanctions sever precisely those channels. An isolated, sanctioned state has nothing left to lose and a standing grievance; it becomes a permanent, resentful safe haven rather than a reluctant partner.
[C1.7] We would be converting fixable negligence into locked-in hostility, and calling it accountability.
