[P1.1] Banning the creation of heritable gene‑edited embryos is not a hostile stance against genetics; it is the only defensible precaution given what we know about the technology’s power and limits. The motion permits non‑heritable therapies, which already treat the patient who consents, and that is precisely why the distinction matters. Germline editing rewrites DNA that will be passed to every descendant, making a single mistake permanent for an entire lineage. Off‑target cuts, mosaicism, and unknown epigenetic interactions can produce diseases that do not appear until later generations, as the 2018 Chinese CRISPR babies scandal illustrated: the edited twins carry edits whose long‑term health effects are completely unknown.
[P1.2] Because the edited person cannot consent, and because future people have no voice, we must apply the precautionary principle to protect them.
[P1.3] Even a modest error can propagate: a single edited allele can spread through a population if carriers reproduce, creating a hidden genetic drift that no individual chose. Unlike a drug that can be withdrawn, a germline edit cannot be recalled. A ban therefore prevents irreversible, population‑wide harm while still allowing somatic therapies that cure disease in consenting patients.

A prohibition preserves the moral baseline that human reproduction is not a commercial product. A ban also prevents a patchwork of permissive jurisdictions that would spur “genetic tourism,” undermining any national standard and exporting risky practices to less regulated regions.
[P1.5] Thus the proposition safeguards intergenerational safety, respects the consent of those who cannot speak, and blocks a new form of social stratification—all without curtailing the legitimate medical advances already permitted. The only responsible policy is a clear prohibition on heritable embryo editing.
