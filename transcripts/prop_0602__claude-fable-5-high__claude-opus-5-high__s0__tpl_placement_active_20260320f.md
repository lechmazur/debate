[P1.1] Two companies control the software gateway to over ninety percent of the world's smartphones. Every app, every in-app purchase, every subscription must pass through their toll booths — typically at a fifteen to thirty percent cut — under rules they write, enforce, and change unilaterally. The motion asks something modest: that governments require these gatekeepers to allow third-party payments and sideloading, subject to baseline security standards. That last clause matters — this is regulated openness, not a free-for-all.
[P1.2] Three arguments carry this debate.
[P1.3] First, the competition case. App store commissions function as a private tax on the digital economy. Developers either absorb it or pass it to consumers — which is why subscriptions bought through an iPhone often cost more than the same service bought on the web. When Apple can charge Spotify thirty percent while its own Apple Music pays nothing, that isn't a security feature; it's self-preferencing by a monopolist.
[P1.4] Payment competition disciplines prices the way competition always does.
[P1.5] Second, the sovereignty case. The device is the user's property. A general-purpose computer whose owner cannot choose what software to run is not a security model — it's a control model. Every desktop operating system, including Apple's own macOS, permits installing software outside a single store, and civilization has not collapsed.
[P1.6] Notarization, code-signing, malware scanning, and permission sandboxes can all persist under the baseline standards the motion explicitly requires.
[P1.7] Third, the security objection is answered inside the motion itself. The choice is not "walled garden versus chaos"; it's "unaccountable gatekeeper versus audited openness." Governments can mandate security review, revocation mechanisms, and liability rules for alternative stores. Meanwhile, scam apps already sail through the official stores' review today — the wall is more marketing than fortress.
[P1.8] The EU's Digital Markets Act already imposes these requirements, and iPhones in Europe still work, still update, still get patched. The question is whether other governments should extend that basic market freedom. They should.

[C1.4] And the security answer doesn't live in the malware statistics; it lives in social engineering.
