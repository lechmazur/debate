[P1.1] Traffic deaths are not a mystery of evil drivers. They are a physics problem first and a legal problem second.
[P1.2] A person hit at 20 mph will very likely live. At 35 or 40 mph they will very likely die. Kinetic energy grows with speed squared, stopping distance nearly doubles, the driver's peripheral vision narrows. No penalty after that impact can reverse the energy.
[P1.3] Lowering the default limit and designing streets to make that lower speed feel natural directly attacks the mechanism that kills.
[P1.4] That is why redesign beats punishment. Narrower lanes, tighter corners, raised crosswalks, chicanes, protected bike lanes, pedestrian islands - these are not decorations, they are behavioral engineering. They make fast driving feel uncomfortable and slow driving feel inevitable. You do not have to catch anyone.
[P1.5] The street does the enforcement 24 hours a day, for every driver, sober or distracted, rich or poor, regardless of whether a police officer happens to be nearby. Penalties cannot do that.
[P1.6] Harsher post-crash penalties depend on a broken theory of deterrence. Drivers who kill rarely intend to kill. They misjudge, they glance at a phone, they turn through a crosswalk they did not see. Nobody in that moment is calculating years in prison.
[P1.7] Severity of punishment matters only if detection is certain and swift, and traffic enforcement is the opposite - rare, delayed, and selective. Increasing a sentence from three years to six does not make a blind corner any more visible.
[P1.8] Cities that understand this have seen results. When Paris dropped to 30 km/h and rebuilt intersections, deaths fell. Hoboken rebuilt with daylighting and curb extensions and went years with zero traffic deaths, not by jailing more drivers but by making it hard to drive fast enough to kill.
[P1.9] If the goal is fewer funerals, change the street, not just the sentence.
