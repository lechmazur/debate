[P1.1] The case for controlled human infection studies turns on a comparison, not an absolute standard. The question is never whether infecting volunteers is costless — it plainly is not — but whether blocking these studies produces better or worse outcomes than permitting them under strict oversight. On that comparison, restriction loses.
[P1.2] Start with the problem the proposition names: inadequate animal models. For many respiratory, enteric, and vector-borne pathogens, animal models reproduce disease poorly or not at all. Mice challenged with a human pathogen may never develop the syndrome we need to treat; ferrets and primates approximate transmission imperfectly; immune responses in rodents routinely mispredict human efficacy. When the model does not model, a negative result tells us little and a positive result can be a mirage — yet regulators still process candidates through this pipeline, and failures surface only in enormous phase III field trials, sometimes after billions of dollars and years of delay, sometimes after a product reaches patients with efficacy we cannot explain or reproduce.
[P1.3] Controlled human infection studies fix precisely this defect. Deliberately challenge a small group of healthy, informed, screened volunteers with a characterized pathogen strain under monitoring, with rescue therapy available, and you obtain a clean read on whether a vaccine or antiviral blocks infection or disease. This is not hypothetical: cholera, typhoid, malaria, influenza, and SARS-CoV-2 challenge work has supported vaccine and therapeutic development, with challenge units reporting decades of experience and a strong safety record in carefully selected young healthy adults.
[P1.4] The sample sizes are small, the setting is monitored around the clock, and the answer arrives in weeks rather than the years a field trial requires.
[P1.5] Ethical objections are real and I do not dismiss them — but they prove too much if treated as disqualifying. Every phase I trial asks healthy volunteers to accept risk for others' benefit. What distinguishes a challenge study is not that risk exists but that risk is concentrated, minimized by design, and offset by enormous downstream benefit: faster identification of futile candidates, smaller and better-targeted field [clipped]

[C1.1] Deliberate infection is not a smarter early-phase trial. It is the intentional production of disease in people who were healthy. [P1.1] frames the choice as speed versus restriction. The proposition asks regulators to permit challenge studies more broadly whenever animal models are inadequate, while conceding that the ethical objections remain serious.
[C1.2] A serious objection to causing disease is a constraint on permission, not a cost to be priced against faster pipelines.
[C1.3] [P1.5] says those objections prove too much, because ordinary phase I research also imposes risk on healthy volunteers. It does not. Those trials expose people to a candidate product whose effects are uncertain. A challenge study introduces a pathogen in order to infect.
[C1.4] Rescue treatment, when one exists, is a fallback, not a promise. Severe illness, lasting injury, and spread to staff or household contacts — people who never consented — remain real. Wider permission multiplies that risk across more pathogens and more sponsors, and it lands on volunteers, often young and paid, who were not sick.
[C1.5] "Inadequate animal model" will not stay a narrow gate. For many respiratory, enteric, and vector-borne pathogens, animals fail to reproduce the human syndrome. If that routine failure licenses deliberate infection, the exception becomes the method. Pressure will not stop at familiar agents with dependable cure.
[C1.6] It will reach emerging and incompletely characterized strains, where monitoring cannot make the design safe. A tolerable record in a few bounded programs does not justify the expansion.
[C1.7] The scientific return is thinner than claimed. A measured dose in screened young adults is not efficacy in older, pregnant, or immunocompromised patients, or under natural exposure. The wrong strain or endpoint can manufacture a confidence that field trials later contradict. Large trials are not avoided.
[C1.8] They are preceded by intentional harm and sometimes by a misleading shortcut.
[C1.9] When models fail, regulators should demand better evidence, not a broader license to infect the healthy.

[P5.1] The debate turns on one thing: CON has now admitted, in [C4.3], that no standard would ever permit a challenge study. That is not a serious objection doing its job — it is a prohibition wearing an objection's clothes. And a prohibition has consequences it must answer for.

Years pass while candidates that could have been killed early in weeks instead reach late-stage testing and fail, and the diseases those trials study keep killing people who never consented to anything. [C4.8] calls deprioritization "burial," but a futility signal from a filter is a decision to spend scarce trials elsewhere — whereas CON's world buries the same candidates later, after more illness and more delay.

What regulators should care about is preventable harm across the whole pipeline: concentrated, contained, rescuable risk in volunteers who consented, versus dispersed risk and slower cures for everyone else. Only one side pays the full ethical cost of its position. That is why the proposition stands.
