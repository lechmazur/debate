[P1.1] A satellite earns money for its owner while it works. When it dies in orbit, it becomes everyone else's problem. That is the whole case in two sentences: the profit is private, the wreckage is public, and a deorbit bond is the simplest way to make the bill land on the party that created it.
[P1.2] Consider the operator's incentives at end of life. Deorbiting costs fuel, and fuel is revenue, because every kilogram saved for disposal is service time given up. So the temptation is always to squeeze out one more quarter of operation and hope the last burn works. If it fails, the operator loses nothing, since the satellite was finished anyway.
[P1.3] Everyone else inherits a dead object moving at several kilometers per second through crowded shells for decades, and a single collision can produce thousands of fragments that threaten every other spacecraft.
[P1.4] After-the-fact fines cannot fix this, for a structural reason. Satellites are most often abandoned when their operators are broke, dissolved, or acquired. A bankrupt company cannot be fined, and a derelict cannot be deterred. Regulation that depends on collecting money later fails in exactly the cases where it matters.
[P1.5] A bond reverses the timing. The money is set aside while the operator is solvent and motivated, and it comes back when the spacecraft is safely retired. Three things follow:
[P1.6] - **Low cost to responsible operators.** They pay only the cost of capital, or a surety premium, while reckless ones pay the full amount.
[P1.7] - **Reliability gets priced.** Sureties will charge less for redundant propulsion, lower orbits, and passive deorbit designs, so the market rewards good engineering without regulators having to dictate it.
[P1.8] - **Cleanup is funded.** If the operator fails, the forfeited bond pays for removal or mitigation instead of leaving nothing.
[P1.9] The tool is not new. We already require financial assurance for mine reclamation and oil-well decommissioning, because experience showed that companies vanish and holes in the ground do not. Orbit is the same problem with higher stakes and no fence to put around it. [clipped]

[C1.1] The proposition sounds like a deposit on a rental car. Orbit is not a rental car. A deorbit bond would lock up capital for decades against a risk that is partly stochastic and often outside the operator’s control, then forfeit it on a pass/fail test regulators cannot calibrate.
[C1.2] Start with cost and entry. A small Earth-observation firm with one satellite may have to post a bond for its entire operating life. Sureties will demand collateral or charge premiums for a decade-long technical risk they cannot easily model. That is capital not spent on propulsion, tracking, or redundancy.
[C1.3] For a constellation, the bond multiplies per spacecraft. The result is not “responsible operators pay only the cost of capital”; it is consolidation toward incumbents and fewer independent operators.
[C1.4] Second, forfeiture is strict liability dressed up as safety regulation. A satellite can fail to retire because of a propulsion anomaly, a tumbling failure, a solar event, or a collision that damages it. The operator may have done everything reasonable and still lose the bond. That does not price negligence; it prices bad luck.
[C1.5] And if the bond exceeds the cost of controlled disposal, an operator near end of life has a perverse option: abandon the spacecraft and forfeit the bond rather than spend more on a risky deorbit attempt.
[C1.6] Third, the bankruptcy argument cuts against the proposition. If operators are most likely to vanish when broke, they cannot maintain a large bond either. A fully funded bond is enormously expensive; a surety bond is only as good as the insurer’s willingness to pay when the operator is failing. The “money set aside while solvent” is either a barrier or an illusion.
[C1.7] Finally, orbital debris is global. A unilateral bond burdens domestic operators while doing nothing about foreign abandonment. The real task is enforceable design and disposal standards, shared tracking, and targeted liability—not a forfeiture scheme that punishes bad luck and rewards financial size.
