[P1.1] Corporate net-zero pledges are increasingly built on a shaky foundation of carbon offsets, many of which do not represent genuine emissions reductions. Forestry projects, the most common offset type, are riddled with flaws: baselines are often inflated to claim credit for trees that would have grown regardless; permanence is an illusion, as wildfires or policy changes can erase stored carbon; and leakage means deforestation simply moves elsewhere. When major emitters like airlines or oil companies offset 70-90% of their footprint through such schemes, they face no real pressure to decarbonize their polluting core activities.
[P1.2] The voluntary offset market has thus become a mechanism for greenwashing rather than meaningful climate action. This undermines both corporate accountability and global climate efforts.
[P1.3] The value chain is where companies must focus because that is where they have direct control and where emissions reductions are most impactful. Direct cuts in operations, energy use, and supply chains—like switching to renewables or sourcing low-carbon materials—force innovation and create lasting change. Offsets, by contrast, are indirect and temporary. They allow firms to claim climate leadership while avoiding real transformation.
[P1.4] Legally limiting offsets to a small share—such as 10-20% of total emissions—would ensure that the bulk of corporate climate efforts go toward direct reductions. This aligns with the Paris Agreement’s call for deep, absolute emissions cuts. It also reflects the polluter-pays principle: companies must clean up their own emissions first. Without such limits, net-zero pledges risk becoming meaningless accounting exercises, as companies buy cheap credits instead of cutting emissions.
[P1.5] The climate crisis demands real, immediate action—not the illusion of progress through unreliable offsets. Real decarbonization starts in the value chain, not in a distant forest that may not survive the next decade.

[C1.1] The proposition diagnoses a quality problem and prescribes a quantity cure. Inflated baselines, burned forests, leakage—every flaw PRO names is a flaw in bad credits, not in the idea of paying for a ton of carbon removed somewhere other than your own smokestack. A legal cap of ten or twenty percent does nothing about that: it leaves the junk credit inside the allowance and bans the high-integrity removal outside it. If a forestry credit is fraudulent, it is fraudulent at five percent of a footprint too.
[C1.2] Regulate integrity—durability requirements, conservative baselines, independent verification, liability for reversals—and the greenwashing case collapses without the collateral damage.
[C1.3] And the collateral damage is severe. The atmosphere does not care whether a ton is cut in Rotterdam or Rwanda; it cares how many tons. A rigid percentage ignores that a software firm and a cement maker have nothing in common. Roughly half or more of cement emissions come from limestone chemistry itself—no renewable contract touches them.
[C1.4] Tell that company most of its path must be in-value-chain by law and you get one of two outcomes: it pays extraordinary sums per ton that would have abated several tons elsewhere, or it quietly abandons the pledge altogether. Companies already drop targets when frameworks tighten unrealistically. Fewer pledges, less climate finance, same chemistry.
[C1.5] Worse, the cap strangles exactly what net-zero requires. Residual emissions must be balanced by removals; durable removal—direct air capture, mineralization, biochar—is expensive today precisely because demand is thin. Corporate buyers are the main thing scaling it. Legally shrinking that market freezes the technology we need for mid-century.
[C1.6] Finally, "in-value-chain" is gameable: sell the dirty subsidiary, outsource the furnace, and your boundary shrinks while global emissions do not. Mandate honest accounting and quality credits, not an arbitrary ratio that punishes the firms doing the hardest work.

[P2.1] CON misdiagnoses the problem. The issue isn’t merely bad credits; it’s that offsets, even high-quality ones, enable companies to delay hard decarbonization. Forestry dominates today’s voluntary market not because it’s the most effective, but because it’s the cheapest and most abundant—precisely the kind of low-integrity credit a cap would push out. A 10-20% limit forces firms to prioritize high-quality removals for their scarce offset allowance, actually accelerating the durable removal technologies CON says we need.
[P2.2] Regulating credit quality alone fails because verification is inherently imperfect and often compromised. Even with conservative baselines and liability for reversals, additionality remains notoriously hard to prove, as seen in repeated exposés of over-crediting by independent investigators. The Paris Agreement’s temperature goals require absolute emissions cuts, not net accounting. A legal cap ensures the bulk of effort goes to real reductions in operations and supply chains where companies have direct control.
[P2.3] Industry-specific challenges don’t invalidate the principle. Cement firms can offset truly residual process emissions under a cap, while still pursuing every feasible in-value-chain reduction such as alternative fuels, efficiency improvements, and emerging carbon capture. The current system lets companies like Shell offset over 70% of emissions with questionable forestry credits while expanding oil production—this is the behavior a cap prevents.
[P2.4] Finally, the ‘gameable boundary’ concern is a red herring. Without a cap, firms game the offset system by buying cheap, unreliable credits. With a cap, they must focus on real decarbonization. Meaningless pledges built on excessive offsets are worse than no pledges at all—they lull consumers and regulators into false complacency.
[P2.5] Corporate accountability requires that companies clean up their own emissions first, and only then use limited, high-quality offsets for the truly unavoidable residue.
