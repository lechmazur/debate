[P1.1] Social media polarizes democratic politics more than cable news because its algorithms create private information environments that reinforce each user’s priors at massive scale. A cable channel still broadcasts one script to everyone watching at that hour; viewers may select Fox or MSNBC, yet they receive the same material as thousands of others and remain open to spillover from family, colleagues, or competing outlets.
[P1.2] Platform design turns selection into isolation. Recommendation systems optimize for dwell time and reaction, so content that angers or flatters travels farther and faster than measured analysis. Users do not passively receive this material; they forward it, comment on it, and see their own network’s version of the same story, tightening the loop. Cable news rarely produces that daily, person-by-person repetition inside social circles.
[P1.3] The political consequences follow directly. Cable segmented the electorate into durable blocs that could still recognize one another’s existence. Social media fragments those blocs into overlapping micro-audiences whose members encounter almost no shared corrective facts. Elections and legislative fights therefore occur between citizens who no longer inhabit the same description of events, raising the cost of any concession.
[P1.4] This structural difference in reach and reinforcement explains why polarization accelerated after social platforms became central to political information rather than when cable news first expanded.
