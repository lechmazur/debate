[P1.1] Visible like counts and repost metrics are not neutral features. They are engine parts, designed to make social platforms run on social comparison. Platforms should let users switch those displays off by default because the defaults shape behavior, the harms are well documented, and the benefits of visibility accrue mostly to the platform, not the user.
[P1.2] Consider what a like count actually does. It transforms every post into a public scorecard. A teenager shares artwork and within minutes sees it ranked against a friend's post that got ten times the engagement. A journalist watches a careful thread get buried under a viral joke.
[P1.3] The metric becomes the message. People stop asking whether their post was worth making and start asking whether it will perform. That is a popularity contest by design, and platforms reinforce it because engagement-driven anxiety keeps people scrolling, refreshing, and posting again.
[P1.4] The default matters enormously. Behavioral research consistently shows that defaults stick—most users never change settings, so whatever ships as the baseline becomes the lived experience. If hiding metrics is an opt-in toggle buried in settings, almost nobody uses it. If visibility is the opt-in choice, the platform signals that popularity tracking is optional, not the purpose.
[P1.5] That single framing decision changes the atmosphere of the entire product.
[P1.6] Hiding public counts does not eliminate engagement. People can still like, share, and respond. Creators can still access their own analytics. What changes is the social pressure—the performative audience watching a number climb in real time, the ranking anxiety, the incentive to post what triggers rather than what matters.
[P1.7] The counterargument will likely say users want visible metrics, that they drive discovery and motivation. But wanting something that harms you is not the same as benefiting from it. People want cigarettes too. The question is whether platforms should default toward the version of the product that serves users' wellbeing or the version that serves the engagement machine.
[P1.8] The proposition asks for the healthier default. That is not radical regulation—it is basic product ethics.
