[P1.1] To build a truly just society, we must draw an absolute line: the state does not possess the authority to execute its captives. This principle cannot be compromised, even—and especially—in cases of terrorism, mass murder, or other heinous crimes. Once we carve out exceptions for "extremely serious" offenses, we undermine the entire moral and practical foundation of human rights.
[P1.2] First, the definition of "extremely serious crimes" is dangerously malleable. In times of national panic, political crisis, or under authoritarian shifts, labels like "terrorist" or "threat to national security" are easily expanded to target dissidents, minorities, and political opponents. By maintaining the machinery of death for exceptional cases, we keep a loaded weapon on the table for any future regime to abuse. Absolute abolition is the only secure barrier against state-sanctioned violence.
[P1.3] Second, executing terrorists is actively counterproductive. Ideological extremists often seek death as a badge of honor. By executing them, the state grants them the very martyrdom they desire, creating powerful propaganda that fuels recruitment, radicalizes followers, and inspires copycat attacks. Life imprisonment without parole, by contrast, quietly neutralizes them.
[P1.4] It denies them a heroic narrative and forces them to face the reality of their actions in obscurity.
[P1.5] Finally, the justice system is human and therefore fallible. High-profile, high-pressure investigations—such as those following terrorist attacks—are precisely where the risk of wrongful conviction is highest due to intense public clamor for quick retribution. Coerced confessions, tainted evidence, and political pressure have led to catastrophic errors in the past. An irreversible punishment must never be used by a fallible system.
[P1.6] By abolishing the death penalty completely, we do not show mercy to the guilty; we assert the absolute limits of state power, prevent the creation of martyrs, and protect the innocent.
