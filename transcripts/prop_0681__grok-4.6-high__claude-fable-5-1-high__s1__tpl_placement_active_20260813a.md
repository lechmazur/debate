[P1.1] Mental health crises are medical events, not crimes. Sending armed officers as the default confuses speed with safety. A faster squad car is not safer if the person who steps out has a gun, thin de-escalation training, and a job that ends in arrest or force.
[P1.2] Civilian crisis teams—clinicians, medics, peer workers—change what happens after they arrive. They talk someone down instead of taking them down. They divert from jail and the ER into housing, medication, and follow-up. Families already hesitate to call, knowing who will show up.
[P1.3] A later specialized van can still mean earlier real help than a crisis that never gets reported. That is a better public-safety outcome even when a van comes later than a patrol car in some neighborhoods.
[P1.4] Response time is the wrong primary metric for this class of calls. For a heart attack, minutes matter because the intervention is standardized and time-critical. For a person in psychiatric distress on a porch, the wrong intervention in four minutes can be worse than the right one in twelve. Escalation, injury, a criminal record, and trauma travel with the badge.
[P1.5] Those harms already fall heaviest on the neighborhoods most likely to get aggressive policing. Fast harm is not equal service.
[P1.6] Delays are a real cost. They do not justify keeping the wrong first responder on nonviolent crisis calls. Police remain available for weapons, violence, and genuine unknowns. Expanding alternatives shrinks the share of 911 that should never have gone to law enforcement.
[P1.7] If capacity is tight, allocate toward the calls where specialization prevents the most damage—not toward equal wait times for an unequal and often damaging service.
[P1.8] The current bargain is fast armed response and slow repair of lives. Speed without the right skills is not safety. Better teams, even imperfectly distributed, beat criminalizing crisis at high speed.

[C1.3] PRO's equity argument runs backwards: the community that already gets the least gets a specialty service that arrives last.
[C1.4] Second, time is not a secondary metric for this category of call. Psychiatric crises are not calmly sitting on porches. [C1.5] A twelve-minute response is not "the right intervention later." For a meaningful share of these calls it is no intervention at all.
[C1.6] Third, dispatchers cannot reliably sort "nonviolent" from "violent" through a phone call from a frightened relative. [C1.7] Fourth, the trade-off is unnecessary. Co-responder units that pair a clinician with an officer, crisis-trained patrol, and clinicians embedded in dispatch all deliver the de-escalation PRO wants without telling a neighborhood to wait. Cities should build those. What they should not do is adopt a policy whose stated terms include accepting slower arrival for someone bleeding, overdosing, or standing on a ledge.
[C1.8] Picture the mother at 2 a.m. whose son has locked himself in the bathroom. Patrol is six minutes away; the van is forty. She does not care about metrics. She cares that someone comes.
