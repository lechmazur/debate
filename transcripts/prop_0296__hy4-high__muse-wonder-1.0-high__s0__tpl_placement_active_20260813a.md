[P1.1] Public names are not footnotes; they are civic honors. A holiday tells citizens what to celebrate, a school tells children whose character to admire, and a street makes a political legacy feel ordinary. When a name primarily honors a colonizer, slaveholder, or dictator, the state is not preserving information; it is asking living citizens to keep joining in that person’s glorification. This is no sainthood test: the trigger is an honor whose main civic meaning is conquest, bondage, or dictatorship.
[P1.2] History belongs in records, curricula, museums, and argument; honor belongs only where public veneration is justified.
[P1.3] That distinction defeats continuity. A calendar or street sign is too thin for nuance; it cannot add, "also dispossession, also slavery, also repression." It says the opposite: this person deserves public esteem. Keeping such a name freezes one flattering judgment and renews state sponsorship.
[P1.4] A child entering a school named for a defender of slavery, or a family marking a conqueror's holiday, is told to treat another group's humiliation as civic wallpaper.
[P1.5] Nor is continuity neutral. Many names were installed to normalize domination: colonial officials, Confederate leaders, imperial generals, dictators. Keeping them lets old propaganda keep its lease on public space. Governments already rename roads for new developments and democratic settlements; the real objection is that this change would disturb an inherited hierarchy of honor.
[P1.6] Renaming can make memory fuller. Replace the dictator's avenue, but keep the old name in an exhibit and teach the regime's crimes; choose a school name reflecting present students while explaining the former namesake; keep a holiday's date but honor survivors rather than conquerors. The record expands; only the civic compliment is withdrawn.
[P1.7] Equal civic standing must outweigh continuity. No citizen should be asked to fund, attend, or live under public honors for people whose central public meaning is subjugation. Historical continuity is a value, not a veto over who the state asks us to admire.

[C1.1] A street sign, a school name, and a holiday are not just compliments. They are coordinates of a shared past — evidence of who built a city, who ruled it, and what earlier generations valued. Removing every name said to primarily honor a colonizer, slaveholder, or dictator does not recover that past more honestly. It edits the landscape until only acceptable ancestors remain visible.
[C1.2] The trigger is unworkably subjective. Is George Washington honored primarily as a slaveholder or as the founder who relinquished power? Is a Columbus holiday primarily about conquest or about an age of navigation later claimed by immigrant communities? Reasonable citizens will answer differently across time, region, and identity.
[C1.3] Turning that disagreement into a renaming mandate hands governments a permanent purification project: each new majority can relabel the past in its own image, and no name is ever settled.
[C1.4] Continuity is not the same as endorsement. Most people who give an address, attend a school, or take a day off are not pledging admiration for a namesake; the name has become place, habit, and memory layered over generations. That layering is valuable because meaning can change around it. The same name that once celebrated empire can now prompt teaching about dispossession, slavery, and resistance — if schools and plaques tell the truth.
[C1.5] Renaming short-circuits that reckoning. It lets a community feel cleansed while the work of honest curricula, memorials to victims, and material repair is postponed.
[C1.6] Finally, compelled renaming divides the very public it claims to unite. It forces residents, alumni, and communities through costly, symbolic fights over words on buildings while classrooms, housing, and public health wait for resources. Worse, it teaches citizens that belonging depends on passing a present-day moral audit of one's heroes. A confident democracy can keep the name, expose the crime, honor the victims alongside it, and trust citizens to hold complexity.
[C1.7] Erasing the sign is easier — and far less honest.

[C2.7] Finally, compatibility is not priority. Governments have finite time and political capital. A renaming fight consumes both, then offers the feeling of reckoning. Keeping the name and forcing the explanation into view is harder, less cleansing, and more honest.
