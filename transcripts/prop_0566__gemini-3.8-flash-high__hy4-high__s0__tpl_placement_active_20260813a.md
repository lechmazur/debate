[P1.1] Admissions to selective universities represent a zero-sum gateway to social mobility, leadership, and public influence. Every seat awarded to an applicant based on who their parents are or how much money their family donates is a seat denied to someone who earned it on their own merits. Sharply limiting legacy and donor preferences is not merely an administrative tweak; it is an urgent defense of higher education’s core purpose.
[P1.2] First, these preferences entrench hereditary privilege. Legacy and donor carve-outs function as affirmative action for the already advantaged, compounding generational wealth. When institutions that enjoy massive public subsidies through tax exemptions and research grants allocate scarce spots based on bloodline or patronage, they cease to function as engines of upward mobility.
[P1.3] Instead, they act as dynastic wealth-preservation trusts. An applicant's life chances should reflect their talent and character, not their ancestry.
[P1.4] Second, the standard defense—that universities must auction access to fund financial aid—is both morally bankrupt and demonstrably false. Elite institutions do not need to sell seats to survive. Renowned universities like MIT, Caltech, and the University of California system compete at the highest global levels and sustain robust philanthropic giving without legacy preferences. Alumni contribute because they value an institution's mission, prestige, and societal impact, not merely in a crude quid pro quo for their children.
[P1.5] Subordinating academic integrity to a donor pipeline corrupts the entire admissions process.
[P1.6] Finally, these preferences poison public trust. At a time when cynicism about institutional fairness is at an all-time high, preserving an overt system of inherited privilege confirms the suspicion that the game is rigged. Universities cannot credibly claim to be engines of opportunity while quietly maintaining a velvet rope for the wealthy and well-connected. To restore institutional legitimacy and educational equity, these dynastic shortcuts must end.
