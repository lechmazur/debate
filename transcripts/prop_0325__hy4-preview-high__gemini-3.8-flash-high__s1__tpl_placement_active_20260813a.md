[P1.1] Ask what a typical fan actually buys. Most people are not completists: they follow one club, or one tournament. Yet the dominant streaming model forces that fan into a broad sports bundle—hundreds of matches, channels, and leagues they will never watch—just to see the team they love. A Seattle Mariners fan living abroad, for instance, does not want the entire league; she wants her team.
[P1.2] MLB has long sold a cheaper single-team out-of-market package alongside its full pass, proving the model works; the bundle should not be the only door.
[P1.3] The economics now fit this choice. Bundling was a cable-era answer to scarce channel space and high carriage costs. Streaming has near-zero marginal distribution cost, viewership is measurable, and billing is direct. That means a broadcaster can profitably sell a Champions League pass or a single-club baseball package at a lower price, because it converts fans who are priced out of the bundle instead of leaving them to pirate or go without.
[P1.4] Forcing everyone into one expensive bundle is not revenue magic; it is price discrimination that fails—charging superfans less than they would pay and casual fans more than they will pay.
[P1.5] The likely defense of bundling is solidaristic: leagues collectively sell rights and spread money to smaller teams, so everyone must buy the whole package. But the proposition does not abolish bundles; it ends the coercion. Broadcasters can still sell the full bundle to fans who want everything, while targeted passes reach the long tail. Central leagues can still collect and redistribute rights money; how fans access games need not be a blunt bundle.
[P1.6] Cheaper access grows the base, lowers churn, and builds the next generation of supporters. When distribution is cheap, the just thing and the profitable thing converge: stop forcing most fans into broad bundles, and let them pay for the sport they actually follow.

[C1.2] The broad bundle is the only mechanism that aggregates diverse audiences to cover those colossal commitments.
[C1.3] Because of this, the "cheap" single-team pass is an economic illusion. When you unbundle, broadcasters must extract those same fixed billions from a radically smaller, self-selected pool of subscribers. The result is not cheap access, but hyper-inflated niche pricing. We already see this in regional sports networks forced into direct-to-consumer models: standalone team passes, such as NESN or the YES App, cost $25 to $30 a month for a single team.
[C1.4] Even MLB's single-team tier saves fans barely 15% off the full league package [P1.2], precisely because a fan's willingness to pay is already concentrated on their own club. Unbundling merely shifts costs onto captive superfans.
[C1.5] Second, unbundling destroys the financial backbone of competitive sports. Collective bundle revenue funds league-wide parity. Pooled rights allow smaller-market clubs, non-marquee matchups, and emerging women’s competitions to survive alongside global giants. Slicing distribution into unbundled passes concentrates revenue into a handful of elite teams and marquee tournaments while starving the broader ecosystem, accelerating competitive imbalance and broadcaster insolvency.
[C1.6] Finally, unbundling triggers a consumer fragmentation trap. Teams do not play in isolation; they compete across domestic leagues, cup ties, and international tournaments. Splitting broadcasting into competition-specific passes forces fans to juggle multiple subscriptions that collectively cost far more than a single bundle, while eliminating the cross-sport discovery that mints future fans. Bundling is what keeps sports solvent, competitive, and genuinely accessible.
