[P1.1] The line is not anti-technology; it is between practice and judgment. AI belongs on the practice side. It should not be the main source of writing feedback in secondary school.
[P1.2] Mastery practice is where AI is genuinely strong. A student learning comma rules, sentence combining, vocabulary, citation formats, or argument templates can get instant, patient, inexhaustible reps. It can vary examples, wait without sighing, and let a teenager fail privately. That is exactly what deliberate practice needs: clear targets, repetition, immediate correction, and low social cost.
[P1.3] Used this way, AI frees teachers to do the work only humans can do.
[P1.4] Writing feedback is different in kind. Writing is not a set of correct answers; it is a chain of judgments about purpose, audience, evidence, tone, and meaning. The same sentence can be a flaw in one essay and the whole point in another. A machine that sees the text but not the student's intent, the assignment's context, or the classroom conversation cannot reliably decide what the writing should become.
[P1.5] If it becomes the main feedback source, students learn to write for the algorithm: safer claims, blander voice, more generic structure, fewer risks. They get corrections without understanding why.
[P1.6] There is also a diagnostic loss. When a teacher responds to writing, she learns what the class misunderstands. She can ask a question that makes a student rethink the argument, or notice that a brilliant idea is buried in confusing prose. AI can flag surface patterns, but it cannot take responsibility for a young writer's development.
[P1.7] And when it misreads irony, dialect, or a deliberately unconventional choice, the student is likely to trust the confident machine over her own judgment.
[P1.8] Supplemental AI—brainstorming, grammar checks, revision prompts—can be useful. But the main source of writing feedback must remain the teacher and structured peer response. Mastery practice builds fluency; writing feedback builds judgment. AI can coach the first.
[P1.9] It should not own the second.

[P2.1] CON's case rests on a false equation: because writing improves through repetition, any frequent responder counts as a main feedback source. But repetition builds judgment only when the response is calibrated to meaning and context. AI can flag endless comma splices; it cannot reliably tell whether a fragmented sentence is a mistake or the narrator's voice. That is not a niche edge case; it is the core of secondary writing.
[P2.2] If the machine is the main source, students get volume—volume aimed at generic correctness rather than purpose and audience.
[P2.3] "C1.5" says "main source is volume, not abdication." That distinction collapses. A teacher who sets criteria but lets AI deliver most comments has abdicated the feedback students actually internalize. Teenagers will not treat a confident, instant, always-on comment as merely "claims to test" unless someone teaches them how—and that teaching is exactly the human feedback the motion protects.
[P2.4] The overload argument cuts both ways. Yes, a teacher with 150 students cannot line-edit every draft. But the answer is not to make the least context-sensitive responder the main one. It is to triage: teacher feedback on argument, evidence, and structure at key moments; peer response for audience; AI for mechanics, drills, and optional revision prompts.
[P2.5] CON's alternative is not "prompt notes vs. silence"; it is "AI as first responder vs. AI as assistant." The motion chooses the latter and preserves teacher judgment where it matters most.
[P2.6] Finally, the record claim in "C1.9" is backwards. Patterns become visible only when a human reads for them. An AI summary reflects the AI's categories, not the class's thinking. A teacher may see that introductions are weak; she will not see that three students buried brilliant arguments because the machine told them to sound formal.
[P2.7] Mastery practice builds fluency; feedback builds judgment. AI can coach the first. If it owns the second, students learn to write for the algorithm.
