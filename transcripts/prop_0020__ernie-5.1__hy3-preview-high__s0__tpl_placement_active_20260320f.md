[P1.1] The euro was sold as the ultimate engine of European unity, but in practice it has become the Union’s greatest structural liability. By locking nineteen economies into a single monetary policy, the euro stripped member states of the most basic tool for absorbing shocks – the ability to devalue their own currency. When the 2008 crisis hit, countries like Greece, Spain and Italy could not adjust exchange rates; instead they were forced into brutal austerity, deepening recessions and sowing resentment that still fuels populist movements today.
[P1.2] The fiscal architecture that was supposed to accompany the euro never materialised. Without a genuine fiscal union, the common currency turned into a “one‑size‑fits‑all” interest‑rate regime that benefited strong economies at the expense of weaker ones. The resulting sovereign‑debt crises required massive bail‑outs, conditional on painful reforms, which eroded public trust in EU institutions and gave ammunition to Eurosceptic parties – from the UK’s Brexit vote to the rise of nationalist governments in Italy and Hungary.
[P1.3] A Europe without the euro would retain the benefits of the single market – free movement of goods, services, capital and people – while preserving national monetary sovereignty. Flexible exchange rates would have allowed peripheral economies to regain competitiveness organically, reducing the need for politically toxic austerity packages. Moreover, the EU could have pursued deeper coordination on fiscal rules, banking union and investment without the constraints imposed by a rigid common currency.
[P1.4] In short, the euro has turned economic divergence into political fragmentation. Removing it would give the Union the flexibility, legitimacy and resilience it needs to stand strong today. The Union’s strength lies not in a shared coin, but in the capacity of its members to act decisively when crises strike – a capacity the euro has systematically undermined.
