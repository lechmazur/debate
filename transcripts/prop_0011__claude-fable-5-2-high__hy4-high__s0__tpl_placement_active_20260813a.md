Vouchers extend to poorer families a power wealthier families already exercise.
[P1.2] That is the case for expanding them.
[P1.3] Take the two concessions built into the motion and see what they actually prove.
[P1.4] **"Mixed evidence" is not negative evidence.** It means some programs show test-score gains, some show little difference, and a few show early dips that tend to fade. But vouchers typically cost noticeably less per pupil than the public system spends. Roughly comparable academic results at lower cost, chosen voluntarily by the family, is a win on efficiency grounds. And test scores are not the only thing parents are buying.
[P1.5] The research fairly consistently finds higher parental satisfaction, and several studies point to gains in graduation and attainment even where scores are flat. Parents choose for safety, for freedom from bullying, for a school that fits a disabled child, for a community that shares their values. A bubble test captures none of that.
[P1.6] **"Public schools may lose resources" confuses the system with the student.** Education funding exists for children, not for buildings. When a child leaves, the district loses that child's revenue but also the cost of educating her, and in most designs it keeps a share of local funding, so per-pupil resources for those who stay often hold steady or rise. The evidence on competition is also encouraging. Where public schools have faced real exit pressure, as studies of Florida have suggested, they have tended to improve, not collapse.
[P1.7] So the question is who should bear the burden of uncertainty. When evidence is mixed, the tiebreaker should be the judgment of the parent who knows the child, not the institutional interest of the system that assigned her. No one is forced to take a voucher. [clipped]

[C1.1] The motion asks states to expand vouchers precisely when gains are mixed and public systems may lose resources. “Mixed evidence” is not proof of failure; it is no warrant to scale. A parent may choose for safety, fit, or values. A state spends public money for all children, so its test cannot be private preference alone. It must ask whether expansion improves learning and equity without undermining universal provision.
[C1.2] The wealthy-family analogy hides that distinction. Private tuition uses private money and claims no public entitlement. A voucher is a public subsidy for exit; exit is not costless. A district losing students still has buildings, bus routes, nurses, special-education staff, and grade-level teachers.
[C1.3] The claim that both revenue and cost leave treats schools like subscriptions, not institutions with fixed obligations. Departures can mean larger classes, fewer electives, deferred repairs, and less capacity for high-need students; private schools may lack the same duty or capacity to serve them.
[C1.4] Thus uncertainty falls on people who did not choose it: children who remain, taxpayers funding dual systems, and families with less information, transport, or ability to supplement tuition. Parental satisfaction is real, but not a statewide accountability standard. Scores are imperfect, but still one public check on whether public dollars produce learning. With that check mixed and cream-skimming or fiscal drain plausible, expansion loses the tie.
[C1.5] Nor does competition settle it. Exit can help a responsive school; it can also strip it of engaged families and money, then blame it for declining results. “No one is forced to take a voucher” misses the force on the other side: no one remaining chooses a depleted budget.
[C1.6] The public-school claim is not institutional self-interest. The state must maintain a universal floor before subsidizing alternatives. With mixed gains and resource loss possible, prove it safe for all children before making the common system pay for a few exits.
