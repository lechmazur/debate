[P1.1] A multiplex has a fixed number of screens and a fixed number of hours. Every premium slot is carved out of that finite grid, and the motion only asks one thing: don't carve so much that the standard-price showing becomes a rumor.
[P1.2] This isn't a ban on IMAX or Dolby. Nobody is arguing that a large-format screen shouldn't exist, or that people who want to pay more shouldn't be able to. The question is what happens when a tentpole opens and a twelve-screen house puts nine screens on it, tags most of them premium, and leaves the ordinary ticket at 10:40 in the morning and 11:15 at night. On paper there's still a choice.
[P1.3] In practice, a family of four is choosing between a hundred-dollar night out and not going.
[P1.4] Notice how the price increase arrives. No exhibitor raises the standard ticket. They simply shrink the menu until the only seats left carry a surcharge. That's a price hike laundered through scheduling, and it's why "consumers can choose" rings hollow: the choice is made for them before they ever reach the box office.
[P1.5] And the incentive runs one direction. Premium surcharges are shared up the chain, so every conversion looks like found money, while the family who quietly stays home appears nowhere in the ledger. That's a structural push toward over-allocation, not a neutral reading of demand.
[P1.6] CON will say: wait two weeks, go Tuesday. But blockbusters are front-loaded cultural events. Waiting means eating spoilers, missing the conversation, and paying a penalty in participation for having less money.
[P1.7] The floor we're defending is modest: keep enough standard showings, at hours normal people can use, that going to the movies stays an ordinary habit rather than an upgrade purchase. Premium is a fine upsell; it cannot be the only door.

[C1.3] PRO never establishes that premium scheduling is done to moviegoers rather than chosen by them. A large-format show stays on screen because people keep buying tickets to it; chains allocate toward the format audiences demonstrably pay for. If the surcharge were a trap, demand would dry up and the schedule would revert within a week. And a surcharge for a genuinely different product — a bigger screen, better sound — is not a "price hike laundered through scheduling."
[C1.4] It is a second tier of the same market, like an aisle seat.
[C1.5] Premium revenue is also what lets a twelve-screen house keep a mid-budget Tuesday matinee on a standard screen at all. Cap the premium allocation and you do not get more standard showings of the blockbuster; you get fewer showings overall, thinner margins, and the slow closure of exactly the neighborhood multiplexes PRO claims to defend. A mandated 1:00 PM standard screening helps nobody if the theater cannot stay open.
[C1.6] Finally, PRO's own example gives the case away. A family choosing between a hundred-dollar night out and staying home is describing weekend prime-time in the first days of a cultural event — scarcity that eases as demand spreads across the following week. Ordinary moviegoers have real exits today: discount Tuesdays, matinees, subscription passes, and the standard auditoriums that still outnumber premium ones in most houses. Scarcity at the peak is not exclusion.
