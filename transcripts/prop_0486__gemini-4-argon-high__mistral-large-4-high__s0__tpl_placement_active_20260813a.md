[P1.1] A city running out of room for the living cannot permanently privatize its remaining land for the dead. Because mortality is continuous while urban land is strictly finite, allocating permanent burial plots in land-scarce cities is a mathematical dead end. Every permanent plot removes public land from circulation forever. Eventually, the cemetery fills, closes to new residents, and forces the city into an impossible choice: cannibalize land desperately needed for housing, transit, and parks, or price ordinary families out of burial entirely.
[P1.2] Public cemeteries are civic infrastructure held in trust for every generation, not just the first to arrive. Transitioning to human composting and alkaline hydrolysis preserves this public mission. Instead of rationing a shrinking pool of permanent plots, cities can use circular processes that require no permanent footprint. Human composting gently converts remains into nutrient-rich soil within weeks; alkaline hydrolysis uses water and alkali to yield sterile bone ash without the heavy emissions of flame cremation.
[P1.3] This shift also replaces subterranean pollution with ecological renewal. Conventional burial routinely packs scarce urban soil with concrete vaults, metal caskets, and formaldehyde that threaten local groundwater. Composting and hydrolysis eliminate that contamination. The resulting soil can even nourish the cemetery’s own trees and gardens, turning static graveyards into restorative green space.
[P1.4] Finally, this reform honors grief without sacrificing the city's future. It disturbs no existing graves, nor does it bar private or out-of-town cemeteries from offering traditional burial for religious communities that require it. It simply governs how the state stewards scarce municipal land. After all, an exhausted cemetery with locked gates serves no faith at all, and rationing the final municipal plots by price only turns burial into a luxury for the wealthy.
[P1.5] Under our proposal, families still keep a tangible connection—whether placing mineral ash in compact columbaria or planting memorial trees with rich soil. We honor the dead best when our memorials sustain, rather than crowd out, the living.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a land-management problem for a cultural verdict. It assumes that because urban land is finite, the only rational response is to dissolve permanent burial into industrial processes. That leap ignores what cemeteries actually are: not merely repositories for the dead, but physical archives of a city's history, sacred ground for faith communities, and irreplaceable spaces for collective grief that bind generations together.
[C1.2] Permanent in-ground burial serves needs that composting and alkaline hydrolysis cannot meet. A grave is a fixed address for memory. Families return to the same coordinates across decades; the deceased remain literally rooted in the soil of the community they built. The grieving process often requires a specific place to visit, to tend, to remember.
[C1.3] Composting reduces a person to nutrient-rich soil to be distributed or scattered; hydrolysis reduces them to sterile bone ash in a container. Neither preserves the spatial permanence that many cultures, religious traditions, and grieving families require. The PRO side dismisses this as nostalgia, but for communities with burial mandates—Jewish, Muslim, many Christian denominations—permanent ground burial is not preference but covenant.
[C1.4] The "mathematical dead end" is a false dilemma. Cities already solve land scarcity without banning burial: vertical cemeteries stack interments, mausoleums concentrate remains above ground, lease-based systems recycle plots after contractual periods, and green burial eliminates vaults and embalming fluids. These preserve permanent options while managing density. The PRO side skips these proven tools to leap to industrial processing.
[C1.5] Most dangerously, the claim that religious communities can simply go elsewhere ignores economics. The PRO proposal creates a two-tier death system: the wealthy buy permanent plots in private or out-of-town cemeteries while the public system offers only composting or hydrolysis. It prices permanence out of the public square. That is not equity; it is privatized permanence and public impermanence.
[C1.6] Manage cemetery land intelligently; do not abolish the permanent grave.
