[P1.1] Lifetime seats on a supreme or constitutional court allow the accident of death or retirement to lock in political power for a generation. A leader elected once can determine abortion law, election rules, or presidential powers thirty years after voters rejected that leader's movement. That is not the rule of law. It is rule by timing.
[P1.2] Fixed terms, long, staggered, and non-renewable, would restore the basic democratic idea that no unelected official exercises peak power forever.
[P1.3] Regular turnover improves legitimacy directly. When each vacancy is predictable, every election carries a fair, similar stake in the court's future instead of some elections deciding three seats and others none. The public can see itself reflected over time as new generations of judges arrive. Strategic retirement — waiting to quit only when an ally holds power — loses its payoff.
[P1.4] The confirmation battle cools because no appointment is a thirty-year prize anymore.
[P1.5] The fear that this destroys independence misunderstands what independence requires. Independence means deciding without fear of punishment or hope of reward. A twelve- or eighteen-year term with no reappointment gives exactly that: no president to please for promotion, no party to beg for another term, no need to audition for a future job while on the bench. Judges remain fully protected while they serve — secure salary, removal only for misconduct.
[P1.6] In fact life tenure now threatens independence more. It encourages leaders to pick the youngest reliable ideologue available, rewards justices for timing retirements for partisan advantage, and keeps declining judges on the bench because retirement is a political surrender. A fixed term depoliticizes the person and protects the office.
[P1.7] Democratic legitimacy gains are therefore large and structural, while any cost to independence is avoidable by design. Long non-renewable terms preserve decisional freedom and add rotation, predictability, and equal democratic input. That trade is overwhelmingly worth making.

[C1.1] A constitutional court is not a slow-motion legislature. Its legitimacy comes precisely from not tracking election returns, because its central duty is to tell current majorities what they cannot do. By promising that every election carries a guaranteed stake in reshaping the court [P1.3], fixed terms redefine constitutional law as an ordinary spoil of victory. That does not build legitimacy; it teaches citizens that constitutional rights are just delayed campaign promises.
[C1.2] Worse, the claim that a twelve- or eighteen-year term removes any "need to audition for a future job" [P1.5] gets career incentives completely backward. Life tenure works because it makes the high court a judge's final job. Fixed terms force justices off the bench in their fifties or sixties, with decades of earning power and political ambition left. In the final years of a fixed term, the most powerful litigants appearing before a justice—the executive branch, party networks, corporate giants, elite law firms—are also that justice's prospective employers, patrons, or clients.
[C1.3] You do not free judges from hope of reward by pushing them back onto the market.
[C1.4] Nor do scheduled vacancies cool political warfare [P1.4]. Guaranteeing appointments on a fixed calendar turns every national election into an explicit campaign over court seats, with candidates promising in advance which precedents their scheduled nominees will erase. And once on the bench, regular turnover produces doctrinal whiplash. When everyone knows a court's majority can be rotated on a timetable, losing factions do not accept rulings as settled law; they simply wait out the clock and relitigate fundamental rights as soon as the next seats turn over.
[C1.5] Life tenure's flaw—strategic retirement [P1.6]—distorts one choice at the very end of a career. Fixed terms distort incentives across the entire institution: electoral bidding at entry, unstable precedent on the bench, and careerist auditioning before exit. That sacrifices genuine judicial independence and destroys the court's distinct legitimacy in the process.

What truly creates auditioning is the present ladder, where ambitious appellate judges shape rulings for years to win elevation to the lifetime prize, and sitting justices time their exits as partisan gifts. Fixed terms cut both incentives at once: no higher prize to chase, no retirement to weaponize.

Today some presidents fill three seats and others none, so each vacancy is apocalyptic and worth any lie or procedural arson to win. When one seat turns over predictably every couple years, no single appointment decides everything and losing sides need not burn legitimacy to survive a loss. Nor does gradual rotation mean whiplash.
[P2.4] Staggered terms change majorities slowly, one vote at a time, while the death lottery can flip the entire court overnight — that is real instability.
[P2.5] Independence risks can be engineered away. Democratic illegitimacy cannot, except by renewal. That is why the balance favors fixed terms.

Fixed terms sacrifice real decisional independence—career finality and insulation from the electoral clock—only to turn the court into an inferior echo of the elected branches.
