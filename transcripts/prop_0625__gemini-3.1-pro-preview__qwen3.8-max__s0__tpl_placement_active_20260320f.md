[P1.1] Solar geoengineering represents the ultimate transboundary technology. Injecting reflective aerosols into the stratosphere to artificially cool the planet is not a localized science project; it is a direct manipulation of the Earth’s shared thermostat. Conducting field experiments without a binding international governance framework is reckless, inequitable, and geopolitically dangerous. A temporary moratorium on these experiments is urgently needed until global rules are established.
[P1.2] First, solar geoengineering carries profound transboundary risks. The Earth’s climate system is deeply interconnected. Altering solar radiation in one hemisphere could shift global precipitation patterns, potentially disrupting vital weather systems like the South Asian or West African monsoons. The billions of people whose food security depends on these systems currently have no voice in whether, how, or where these experiments take place.
[P1.3] Allowing unilateral field tests without global consent violates the fundamental principle of the global commons.
[P1.4] Second, without a moratorium, we invite a geopolitical "Wild West." Currently, any rogue state, private corporation, or well-funded individual could theoretically launch an atmospheric experiment. A binding framework is essential to establish liability, transparency, and multilateral oversight before a single balloon or aircraft alters the stratosphere.
[P1.5] Finally, field experiments inevitably create technological lock-in. Outdoor testing is never purely neutral; it attracts massive capital, builds institutional momentum, and normalizes the technology. Once we cross the threshold from laboratory modeling to outdoor experimentation, the slippery slope toward full-scale deployment becomes remarkably steep. We must build the regulatory guardrails before we set this machinery in motion, not after the fact.
[P1.6] A temporary moratorium is not an anti-science mandate; it is a vital diplomatic pause. It ensures that any manipulation of our shared sky is governed by democratic international consensus, rather than the unilateral hubris of a few well-resourced actors. Governance must precede experimentation.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a field experiment for full-scale deployment. A field experiment is not a planetary thermostat switched on; it is a bounded, reversible, instrumented test designed to discover whether the thermostat can be touched safely. Banning experiments until a binding treaty exists assumes the only legitimate knowledge is ignorance. It hands the future to whoever eventually tests in secret, because public, peer-reviewed, regulated research will have been driven out.
[C1.2] PRO’s transboundary fear presumes that tiny, monitored releases must alter monsoons. But the physical leap from a stratospheric aerosol injection trial or a marine cloud-brightening test to a global rainfall shock is enormous. We can require small quantities, short durations, atmospheric modeling, environmental review, and independent monitoring. If a test is so small that its climatic signal is indistinguishable from natural variability, it cannot plausibly be blamed for droughts or floods.
[C1.3] A moratorium is a sledgehammer aimed at a problem that experimental design can solve.
[C1.4] The demand for a binding international framework before any experiment is also a de facto permanent ban. Global climate agreements take decades, often with major emitters outside. While diplomats negotiate, climate risks intensify and vulnerable countries remain unable to assess whether solar geoengineering might reduce extreme heat, protect crops, or buy adaptation time. The equitable answer is not a knowledge monopoly preserved by paralysis; it is open data, shared protocols, and inclusion of at-risk states in oversight.
[C1.5] Finally, PRO’s rogue-actor argument undermines the moratorium. A noncompliant state or billionaire will not wait for permission. What a moratorium does is weaken the very transparency, measurement, and norms needed to detect and respond to unauthorized activity. We can build governance alongside experiments: national licensing, public registries, liability rules, scientific review, and stepwise escalation.
[C1.6] The practical alternative is graduated testing under public scrutiny. That path yields real control. The proposition yields delay, secrecy, and untested fears.
