That is the case I want this debate to turn on: when a government is both perpetrator and record-keeper, "closing the chapter" is not neutral bookkeeping.

[P1.3] Three reasons compensation and archival access should stay open rather than close.
[P1.4] First, harm here is ongoing, not historical. Sterilization is irreversible. A woman sterilized without consent in 1975 is still infertile in 2024; her descendants who never exist are a permanent, compounding loss. [P1.5] Second, archives are the only mechanism by which survivors can even prove what was done to them. Many victims did not know they'd been sterilized until decades later, buried in medical records under euphemisms like "appendectomy" or "elective procedure." [C1.2] First, finality is a principle of justice, not a bureaucratic convenience. Every legal system uses statutes of limitations and res judicata for a reason: evidence decays, witnesses die, and adjudication decades removed becomes guesswork dressed as redress. Where living survivors were identifiable, states have compensated them—rightly.
[C1.3] But "stronger" compensation now means claims resting on fragmentary records, funneled through lawyers and claim-mining intermediaries, with real risk that money misses actual victims. A remedy that cannot distinguish valid claims from invalid ones dishonors the valid ones.
[C1.4] Second, the descendant extension has no limiting principle. If grandchildren of sterilization victims hold inheritable monetary claims against the state, so do descendants of every historical state wrong—conscription abuses, residential schooling, wartime internment, colonial taxation. That is not a reductio; it is the actual logic of the motion, and it converts the public treasury into an open-ended intergenerational liability while today's living needs—disability services, reproductive healthcare, indigenous health funding—compete for the same dollars. Prospective justice for the living should outrank retrospective transfers to the unharmed.
[C1.5] Third, "archival access" collides with the privacy of victims themselves. These are medical records: diagnoses, institutionalization histories, family details about third parties who never consented to disclosure. Opening them broadly to descendants and researchers can re-expose the very people the motion claims to protect.
[C1.6] Acknowledge, apologize, compensate living survivors, teach the history, seal the file. That is a closed chapter done honorably—and it is the better path than perpetual, degrading litigation over the dead.
