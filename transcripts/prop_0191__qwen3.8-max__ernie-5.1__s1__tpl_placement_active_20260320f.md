[P1.1] The central issue is not whether drug development can be faster, but whether regulators may grant a public license to treat a whole population on evidence drawn from only part of it. Approval does more than reward a sponsor; it tells clinicians and patients that a product has been shown acceptable for the people who will actually use it. If trials omit the old, women, or ethnic minorities in proportion to disease burden, that assurance is false for those groups.
[P1.2] Medicine is not average-only. Age changes kidney function, frailty, and polypharmacy; sex can alter metabolism and adverse-event profiles; ethnicity can track real differences in disease prevalence, genetics, or response shaped by biology and social conditions. A trial dominated by younger, healthier, easier-to-recruit participants may identify an effect in the lab but cannot tell a cardiologist how the drug behaves in an elderly diabetic patient or a psychiatrist how it interacts with medications common in older adults. The missing data become post-market surprises.
[P1.3] Proportionate representation must therefore be required, even when it slows approval, because voluntary recruitment will usually lose to convenience. Sponsors have strong incentives to fill trials quickly at sites with accessible, homogeneous patients. A mandate shifts that incentive and treats representativeness as a condition of scientific validity, not a public-relations bonus. Slower trials also protect public trust, especially among communities that have been abused by research in the past and then left out of its benefits.
[P1.4] The obvious objection is delay. But delay is not a neutral cost if speed is bought by licensing ignorance. The people harmed by unrepresentative approvals are often not counted until years later: withdrawn drugs, unexpected adverse events, doses calibrated to the wrong bodies, and treatments that fail in real-world clinics. It is better to approve later on knowledge adequate for all than sooner on knowledge adequate only for some.

[C1.1] Mandating proportionate representation in every trial turns a scientific tool into a bureaucratic straitjacket, and the price is paid by the very patients the rule claims to protect. The first cost is simple arithmetic: most diseases disproportionately affect certain ages or sexes. A trial for a pediatric cancer must enroll children; forcing adult enrollment to match the overall population would dilute the signal, inflate sample size, and extend timelines by years. For rare diseases, where total eligible patients may number in the low hundreds, a proportionate mandate could make a trial impossible altogether.
[C1.2] Second, delay is not a neutral inconvenience; it is a measurable loss of life. Every month a life‑saving therapy sits in a regulatory queue translates into preventable deaths, especially for fast‑moving conditions like aggressive leukemias or pandemic influenza. History shows that accelerated pathways—such as breakthrough‑therapy designations—have saved hundreds of thousands of lives precisely because they accepted less‑than‑perfect demographic data and compensated with post‑market monitoring.
[C1.3] Third, the scientific community already has better, more efficient methods than blunt proportionality. Adaptive trial designs, enrichment strategies, and pre‑specified subgroup analyses allow sponsors to target the populations where the drug is expected to work, while still generating evidence for broader use later. Real‑world data, electronic health records, and registries can fill gaps after approval far more cheaply than forcing diverse enrollment at the outset.
[C1.4] Finally, proportionate quotas invite perverse incentives: sponsors may enroll token participants who do not reflect the true diversity of a group, merely to check a box, undermining data quality. The result is a veneer of inclusivity that satisfies a regulation but erodes scientific rigour.
[C1.5] The real question is whether we should sacrifice speed, feasibility, and ultimate patient access for a symbolic ideal of demographic mirroring. The answer is no: a flexible, evidence‑driven approach that prioritizes timely access and rigorous subgroup analysis serves patients far better than a rigid quota that slows the pipeline.
