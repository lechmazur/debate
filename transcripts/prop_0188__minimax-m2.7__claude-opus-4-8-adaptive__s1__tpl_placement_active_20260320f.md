[P1.1] The replication crisis in psychology, the retracted studies in medicine, the quietly buried null findings across social science—these are not isolated failures. They are symptoms of a system that rewards positive results and punishes negative ones, and that system is built on publication bias.
[P1.2] Preregistration fixes this. When researchers declare their hypotheses, sample size, and analysis plan before collecting data, they cannot quietly delete conditions that did not work, cannot p-hack their way to significance, and cannot pretend a post-hoc finding was always the plan. The registration creates a public timestamp. The scientific record becomes honest.
[P1.3] Journals committing to publish regardless of outcome removes the other half of the problem. Right now, editors reject null results not because they are methodologically flawed but because they are seen as unmarketable. A study finding that a parenting intervention has no effect on child outcomes will not generate press releases or citations. So it disappears into a drawer.
[P1.4] But that finding is still scientifically valuable—policymakers, funders, and other researchers need to know what does not work. Without a publication commitment, preregistration alone is insufficient; researchers who register and then find negative results still face the same publication gate.
[P1.5] When we fund research with public money, taxpayers are buying knowledge, not just successful interventions. A negative result is not a waste—it is information. Mandatory preregistration with a publication guarantee means the entire dataset of human knowledge grows correctly, not just its most flattering subset.
[P1.6] Consider the alternative: meta-analyses built on biased samples overestimate effect sizes, clinical guidelines rest on skewed evidence, and policymakers make decisions based on a literature that systematically hides its failures. This is not a minor technical concern. It is a structural distortion of evidence that shapes real-world decisions about drugs, therapies, and social programs affecting millions of people.
[P1.7] Public funding creates public ownership of the research record. Mandatory preregistration with journal commitment ensures that ownership delivers full value.
