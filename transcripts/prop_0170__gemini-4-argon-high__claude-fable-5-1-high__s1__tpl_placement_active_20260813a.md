[P1.1] You cannot conserve water that never falls from the sky. In arid regions facing depleted aquifers, shrinking snowpacks, and growing populations, demand management hits a hard mathematical ceiling. Once a city fixes its leaks, replaces lawns, and mandates efficient appliances, the easy gains disappear. Beyond that point, relying mainly on conservation inevitably becomes permanent rationing—a strategy of managed decline that treats human thriving as the problem rather than insufficient infrastructure.
[P1.2] Primary investment must go to large-scale desalination because it is the only water source completely decoupled from the climate. Reservoirs, recycling, and conservation all remain hostage to rainfall; during a multi-year megadrought, cutting demand by twenty percent cannot save a region whose river flows have dropped by half. The ocean is the only reservoir that does not dry up. Israel proves this model works: by investing heavily in coastal reverse-osmosis plants rather than settling for perpetual austerity, it transformed itself from a drought-stricken country facing strict household limits into a water-secure economy able to supply growing cities, sustain high-value agriculture, and even pump surplus water back into depleted natural lakes.
[P1.3] Relying mainly on rationing carries devastating, hidden costs. Capping water supply freezes housing construction, forces productive farmland out of cultivation, blocks modern manufacturing, and hits working families with punitive pricing tiers. By contrast, the classic criticisms of desalination—cost, energy, and brine—are manageable engineering challenges, not hard limits. Reverse-osmosis membranes have grown dramatically more efficient, angled ocean diffusers safely disperse salt back into marine currents, and the world’s driest regions are almost always drenched in sunlight, allowing abundant solar power to turn clean energy into freshwater.
[P1.4] Conservation is a useful supplement, just as insulation helps a power grid. But no growing society solves an energy shortage mainly by sitting in the dark, and no water-scarce region can secure its future mainly by turning off the tap. Desalination builds permanent abundance where rationing only administers scarcity.

[C1.1] Water-scarce regions do not have a water problem so much as a water-ordering problem: which gallon do you buy first? [C1.2] Start with where the water actually goes. In most arid regions, agriculture takes roughly three-quarters of supply, much of it flooding fields of alfalfa and rice. Desalinated water costs several times what farmers pay; nobody will grow fodder with it. So desalination cannot touch the largest slice of demand, while drip irrigation, crop switching, leak repair, and pricing reform can.
[C1.3] Las Vegas added hundreds of thousands of residents while cutting total water use through turf removal and reuse. That is not "sitting in the dark." That is insulation—which, in the energy world, every sane utility funds before the new power plant, not after.
[C1.4] PRO's own showcase proves my ordering. Israel spent decades building drip irrigation, near-universal wastewater recycling, and steep pricing before desalination made sense. Desalination was the capstone on an already-efficient system. Pour desalinated water into a leaky, lawn-heavy, flood-irrigated region and you simply subsidize the waste at the highest price available.
[C1.5] Then there is geography. Desalination is a coastal technology. Rajasthan, the Sahel, Central Asia, the interior American West—most of the world's thirsty people live far from the ocean and uphill from it. Pumping water inland multiplies cost and energy.
[C1.6] Conservation works everywhere.
[C1.7] Finally, lock-in. Desalination means multi-billion-dollar plants financed on long debt. Australia built several during its long drought; when rains returned, plants sat idle while ratepayers kept paying. Worse, abundant supply invites new demand—sprawl, lawns, thirsty crops—so the ceiling PRO fears simply moves outward at a far higher price.
[C1.8] Build the plant only where it is the last resort, not the first instinct. Primary investment belongs in efficiency and reuse.
