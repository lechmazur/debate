Peer-to-peer trading disrupts this by allowing price discovery at the local level.
[P1.3] A solar producer gets a better return on their investment. A nearby consumer—perhaps a renter who cannot install panels—accesses cheaper clean electricity. Both parties win; the only loser is the utility's monopoly margin.
[P1.4] Second, the environmental incentive structure. When homeowners know they can trade surplus energy at fair market rates rather than accepting the utility's discounted buyback, the payback period shortens and adoption accelerates. Countries serious about decarbonization should be removing friction from distributed generation, not protecting incumbent business models.

A system where thousands of rooftops produce, consume, and trade locally reduces long-distance transmission losses and distributes risk. During extreme weather events—heat waves spiking demand, storms knocking out substations—neighborhoods with active local energy trading can maintain supply more effectively than those entirely dependent on distant power plants.
