[P1.1] Statutes are instructions from the public’s representatives to the public. When their meaning is available only through professional translation, obedience becomes expensive, rights become difficult to exercise, and democratic consent thins out. Requiring plain-language drafting therefore protects a basic condition of self-government: people must be able to discover what their government demands, prohibits, and promises.
[P1.2] The benefits are practical, not merely aesthetic. A tenant facing eviction, a small business navigating licensing rules, or a worker claiming unpaid wages often encounters the statute before encountering a lawyer. Dense syntax, circular definitions, and archaic terminology impose a translation tax that falls hardest on those with least money and time. Two people may hold identical legal rights, but only the one who can afford interpretation can confidently use them.
[P1.3] Plain language narrows that gap, improves voluntary compliance, and helps officials apply rules consistently. A right hidden in syntax is not equally available to everyone.
[P1.4] This requirement also improves legislative accountability. Complexity can conceal favors, broaden discretion, and make it difficult for citizens—and sometimes legislators themselves—to identify a bill’s real operation. Drafting that ordinary readers can follow exposes policy choices to scrutiny before enactment. It becomes harder to smuggle substantive decisions into procedural fog or to blame administrators for consequences the legislature never clearly acknowledged.
[P1.5] The objection is that legal precision prevents loopholes and litigation. Precision matters, but technical complexity is not its reliable equivalent. A statute can deploy elaborate cross-references and specialized terminology while remaining uncertain in application; meanwhile, misunderstanding, inconsistent administration, and avoidable professional costs are certainties imposed on the public. Nor does plain language mean careless slogans.
[P1.6] It demands disciplined structure, ordinary vocabulary where possible, and explicit explanation where difficult concepts remain necessary.
[P1.7] The motion knowingly accepts that some marginal precision may be lost. That trade is justified because law needs more than elegance at the margins: it needs legitimacy, usability, and equal access throughout society. CON must defend a system in which making rules marginally neater for specialists can justify keeping their meaning distant from the people bound by them. [clipped]
