[P1.1] Framing homelessness as a policing and public order problem is a costly, counterproductive failure that traps people in a cycle of displacement and criminalization, while centering housing and service access delivers measurable reductions in homelessness and associated public harms.
[P1.2] Punitive responses produce no durable solutions. Encampment sweeps, anti-camping laws, and quality-of-life arrests do nothing to address root causes: skyrocketing housing costs, gaps in mental health and substance use care, and stagnant wages. They displace people further from outreach services, employment, and social support, while creating criminal records that bar access to housing and jobs, deepening the crisis. Low-level citations for sleeping outside often come with fines people cannot pay, generating warrants that make them ineligible for public housing and many private rentals, trapping them in a revolving door of enforcement.
[P1.3] Los Angeles spends over $100 million annually on encampment enforcement, yet its unsheltered homeless population has risen steadily for years, with no corresponding drop in street encampments.
[P1.4] By contrast, housing and service access models work. The Housing First framework, which prioritizes permanent housing without preconditions paired with voluntary supportive services, has a decades-long track record of success. Utah cut chronic homelessness by 90% between 2005 and 2015 via a statewide Housing First initiative, simultaneously reducing costs on emergency services, jails, and hospitals. Houston slashed its unsheltered homeless population by more than 60% since 2011 by prioritizing housing placement and coordinated services over enforcement.
[P1.5] The public order case for policing falls apart under scrutiny. Stable housing is the single strongest predictor of reduced public disturbance: people housed through Housing First programs have 70-80% lower rates of jail incarceration and emergency room use than unsheltered peers, directly reducing the community harms that enforcement proponents claim to address. Punitive approaches criminalize poverty, waste public funds on cyclical, ineffective measures, and fail to deliver the public safety they promise. Centering housing and service access is not just more humane—it is the only proven strategy to actually reduce homelessness and its associated community impacts.
