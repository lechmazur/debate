[P1.1] When a studio pulls a theatrical film from cinemas, the film still exists: on disc, in libraries, on rival platforms. When a streaming service deletes its own original, the work disappears. There is usually no DVD, no rerun, no lawful copy anywhere. Streaming originals are exclusive by design, so removal is not a scheduling decision; it is erasure.
[P1.2] And it is happening for accounting reasons. In recent years, major services have pulled dozens of their own titles, some only months after release, as with Disney's Willow series, and whole slates of animation at HBO Max, to book write-downs and stop paying residuals. Finished work, made by hundreds of people, vanished because it was worth more as a loss than as art.
[P1.3] Three harms follow; a minimum period fixes each.
[P1.4] First, subscribers. Services advertise originals to win sign-ups, then delete them. A series you were halfway through, sold to you as the reason to subscribe, can be gone with no way to buy it at any price. That is a bait-and-switch no other consumer market tolerates.
[P1.5] Second, creators. Writers, actors, and animators lose residuals and lose the work itself: the portfolio that gets them hired next. Their labor is converted into a deduction they never share in.
[P1.6] Third, the public. A write-down reduces tax owed, so taxpayers help fund the disappearance of culture they can no longer watch.
[P1.7] The remedy is modest. Keep each original available for a fixed minimum, say, several years, on the service itself or, if the company prefers, through sale or licensing elsewhere. Hosting a finished file costs almost nothing; the only real "burden" is honoring obligations the company already accepted when it commissioned the work.
[P1.8] So the question for the other side is simple: what legitimate interest requires that a completed work be made impossible to watch?
