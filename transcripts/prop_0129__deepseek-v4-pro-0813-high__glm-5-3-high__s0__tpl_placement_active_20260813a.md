[P1.1] The strongest reason to adopt this model is that it aligns criminal law with the actual structure of the sex trade. The buyer creates the demand; the seller is usually the person with fewer options, often facing poverty, addiction, housing instability, coercion, or trafficking. A law that criminalizes purchase while decriminalizing sale therefore targets the source of exploitation without adding another punishment to the exploited.
[P1.2] Decriminalizing the sale of sex is a concrete harm-reduction improvement over full prohibition. Under prohibition, a person who sells sex cannot safely report a violent buyer, an abusive pimp, or a trafficker without risking arrest, a criminal record, or deportation. Once sale is decriminalized, sellers can seek police protection, medical help, and exit services without self-incrimination. That alone makes it safer than full prohibition.
[P1.3] At the same time, criminalizing purchase avoids the central failure of a fully legal market. Legalization does not merely regulate existing prostitution; it normalizes buying sex as an ordinary consumer transaction and expands demand. Greater demand requires greater supply, much of which is filled through trafficking, economic desperation, and vulnerable migration. When buyers face legal risk, demand contracts, making pimping and trafficking less profitable and less attractive as a business model.
[P1.4] This is not merely a symbolic position. It treats the seller as a rights-bearer and the buyer as the person choosing to fuel a harmful market. It is safer than full prohibition because sellers are not driven underground by fear of their own arrest. It is safer than full legalization because it refuses to turn sexual access into a legally protected consumer good.
[P1.5] No regime eliminates all exploitation, but this compromise reduces demand while preserving exit, reporting, and access to justice for the people most harmed. That is the strongest available balance.

[C1.2] When buying is a crime, the transaction itself is illegal—so the market does not stay above ground just because the seller holds the lighter charge. Buyers who fear arrest want speed, discretion, and anonymity. That means less screening, more pressure to meet in isolated locations, and less time to negotiate condom use or boundaries. Sellers competing for a shrinking pool of nervous clients lose bargaining power precisely where it matters most: over what happens in the room.
[C1.3] Falling demand does not protect sellers; it lowers prices and makes saying "no" more expensive. This is not harm reduction—it is prohibition with the seller's name moved off the charge sheet.
[C1.4] Worse, the promised benefits of decriminalized sale largely evaporate. A seller who reports a violent buyer is reporting a crime that requires admitting she facilitated the exchange, in front of police who have spent the last year arresting her clients and surveilling her workplace. In practice, enforcement of buyer laws has meant stakeouts, hotel and apartment evictions, and migrant sellers deported under immigration pretexts. If police attention follows the transaction, the seller still carries the risk.
[C1.5] Call that decriminalization and you have legislated a slogan.
[C1.6] And the "compromise" claim fails on its own terms. Unlike full decriminalization, sellers get no labor rights, no ability to work together legally, no health-and-safety standards, no contracts, no legal redress for nonpayment. They get a colder market policed harder, without any of the institutions that make other work safer.
[C1.7] The proposition asks us to accept a regime built on a theory—that scaring buyers helps sellers—against the lived logic of the street: whoever needs the money most absorbs the risk. Safer for whom? Not for the seller, and she is the person this motion claims to protect.
