[P1.1] No market can function fairly when the referee also owns a team on the field. Yet today’s mobile economy relies on exactly this distortion. Major app-store operators control the sole practical gateway between billions of consumers and software developers, while simultaneously competing against those very developers in music, gaming, cloud storage, fitness, and payments. This dual role creates a permanent, irreconcilable conflict of interest.
[P1.2] The platform operator dictates the rules of discovery, charges rivals steep commissions that its own apps bypass as internal accounting transfers, and controls access to critical hardware and software interfaces. When an independent developer builds a breakout service, whether in music streaming, parental controls, or location tracking, the gatekeeper can bury it in search rankings, stall its updates in opaque review queues, reserve seamless system integration for its own competing app, or study non-public performance data to clone it.
[P1.3] Only structural separation can cure this because behavioral remedies attempt the impossible: forcing a monopolist to act against its own financial interest inside a technical black box. Regulators cannot police every tweak to a ranking algorithm, every shifting app-review standard, or every security pretext used to deny rivals access to payment chips or background processing. Because the operator writes the code, it enjoys massive information asymmetry, allowing it to disguise self-preferencing as routine product design. In fast-moving digital markets, regulatory delay is fatal.
[P1.4] By the time an agency investigates a subtle restriction or levies a fine years later, the rival developer has already been starved of scale and the market has permanently tipped.
[P1.5] Structural separation solves the problem at its root by eliminating the incentive to discriminate. An independent app store succeeds only by making the marketplace safe, efficient, and fair for all participants. Meanwhile, the separated first-party apps must finally compete on their merits, including price, quality, and innovation, rather than relying on privileged defaults and crippled rivals. To end self-preferencing, we must remove the conflict of interest that makes it inevitable.
