[P1.1] Peer review is the quality gate for virtually all human knowledge, and we staff it with anonymous volunteers: paid nothing, accountable for nothing, rewarded with nothing. Then we act surprised when reviews arrive four months late, run two paragraphs, miss fatal errors, or quietly bury a rival's work. The retraction pile keeps growing; the delays keep lengthening; early-career researchers keep absorbing the cost. A system that runs on unaccountable charity performs like one.
[P1.2] The motion fixes both defects at once, and each half reinforces the other. Payment converts goodwill into obligation. Journals and funders can enforce deadlines, expect thoroughness, and actually recruit busy experts instead of begging them. Note that we already do this for grant review—study-section members receive honoraria, and nobody seriously argues that grant panels are corrupt because of it.
[P1.3] Money didn't poison that well; it professionalized it.
[P1.4] Published reports convert invisible labor into accountable scholarship. A reviewer whose report appears beside the paper—with their name or, at minimum, their reasoning—writes a better report, because it becomes part of their professional record. Readers gain something too: they see the objections the paper survived, which is often the most illuminating part of the scientific story. And this is not hypothetical.
[P1.5] Journals that publish review reports, like eLife and F1000Research, have found the reviews become more civil, more constructive, and more useful. The catastrophes predicted for open review have been tested, and they didn't happen.
[P1.6] The likely objections are cost and candor. Cost: reviewing fees are a rounding error next to article processing charges already in the thousands and publisher profit margins that would make a pharmaceutical company blush. The money exists; it is simply not flowing to the people doing the work. Candor: anonymity today shields the lazy reviewer and the conflicted gatekeeper far more reliably than it protects the brave truth-teller.
[P1.7] Published reports let the community itself police retaliation, because a vindictive review is now visible to everyone.
[P1.8] The burden tonight is to defend a status quo that everyone who touches it complains about. Accountability plus compensation [clipped]

[C1.1] Anonymity and lack of payment are features, not bugs. They create a rare space in academia where power hierarchies are suspended, allowing a junior scholar to eviscerate a senior’s flawed work without fear of retaliation. This is why, despite its flaws, the current system produces more rigorous scholarship than any alternative yet tried. Payment would shatter this.
[C1.2] It turns review into a market: reviewers will cultivate reputations for being ‘easy’ to secure more paid gigs, while journals will subtly favor those who rarely reject to maintain acceptance rates and revenue. A two-tier system would emerge, with well-funded fields able to afford premium reviews while others make do with whatever the market provides. The financial burden would be enormous—at $200 per review, with over two million papers published annually, we’re looking at $400 million yearly, a cost that would bankrupt smaller journals or be passed to authors, deepening global inequality in scholarship.
[C1.3] Publishing reports compounds the harm by making every review a public performance. Junior researchers will self-censor to avoid offending potential collaborators or funders. Established critics will soften their language to dodge Twitter storms or vengeful tenure letters. Open review trials already show this effect: feedback becomes shorter, vaguer, more polite. eLife’s published reports are hailed as ‘constructive,’ but that’s often code for ‘less honest’—reviewers focus on minor issues to avoid direct confrontation.
[C1.4] The reviewer pool would shrink dramatically. Many senior researchers already decline most requests; add public exposure and transactional payment, and only the most reckless or most protected will participate. Early-career scholars, who often provide the most thorough reviews, will opt out en masse. The result: a system less fearless, less diverse, and less reliable.
[C1.5] This cure is far worse than the disease.
