[P1.1] The modern sports fan is being held hostage by an outdated economic model. By forcing viewers into broad, expensive subscription bundles, broadcasters are effectively levying a tax on loyalty. [P1.2] First, forced bundling is fundamentally anti-consumer. A dedicated fan of a single local basketball team should not be forced to subsidize broadcasting rights for professional golf, international rugby, or rival leagues they have zero interest in watching. Team-specific passes align price with actual utility, giving consumers the freedom to pay only for the product they value.
[P1.3] Second, exorbitant bundle pricing actively drives away the next generation of fans while fueling piracy. When the barrier to entry is a massive monthly or annual fee, younger and lower-income demographics are simply priced out of legal viewership. Offering affordable, targeted passes converts these shadow viewers into paying customers.
[P1.4] It drastically lowers the barrier to entry, ensuring that sports remain an accessible mass cultural phenomenon rather than a walled-off luxury good.
[P1.5] Finally, unbundling is the natural evolution of the digital age. Just as the music industry had to stop forcing consumers to buy entire albums to get a single song, sports broadcasters must adapt to an on-demand, personalized market. Team-specific passes allow broadcasters to build direct, data-rich relationships with highly engaged fanbases, unlocking new, sustainable avenues for targeted merchandise, ticketing, and community engagement.
[P1.6] Clinging to bloated bundles protects short-term monopoly profits at the expense of the fans who actually keep the industry alive. Offering targeted, affordable passes is the only fair and sustainable path forward.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds intuitive—let fans buy only what they watch—but it rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of sports economics. The "cheap" team-specific pass is a mirage. Sports broadcasting rights are negotiated and sold in massive bundles because that is how the economics work. If you disaggregate the revenue, the price per team does not stay low; it balloons.
[C1.2] Consider the logic: a broadcaster paying billions for a league's entire inventory currently spreads that cost across millions of subscribers, most of whom watch casually. Strip away the casual subscribers—those who tune in for marquee matchups, playoffs, or channel-surf into unexpected games—and the same rights fee must be recovered from a dramatically smaller base of die-hard fans. The team-specific pass does not become cheaper; it becomes a premium product. The PRO side's promise of affordability collapses the moment you follow the math through.
[C1.3] More importantly, bundles are not a bug—they are a cross-subsidy system that keeps the sports media ecosystem alive. Niche sports, smaller-market teams, women's leagues, and secondary competitions survive because broad bundles direct revenue from casual mass audiences toward content that could never sustain itself on standalone subscriptions. Unbundling sounds like liberation but functions as starvation for everything outside the top tier. The dedicated fan of a single local team benefits from a system where millions of incidental viewers fund the infrastructure, production quality, and coverage that make that team's broadcasts possible in the first place.
[C1.4] The music analogy is rhetorically convenient and economically illiterate. A song costs nearly nothing to reproduce and distribute. A live sports broadcast requires stadiums, cameras, crews, analysts, and rights deals running into the billions. The cost structure bears no resemblance, and the comparison obscures rather than illuminates.
[C1.5] What PRO calls a "tax on loyalty" is actually the pooling mechanism that makes broad coverage affordable. Unbundle it, and fans do not gain freedom—they lose access, pay more for less, and watch the diversity of sports coverage shrink.
