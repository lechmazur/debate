[P1.1] The default on your device should protect you, not monetize your inattention. Browsers and operating systems should be legally required to provide robust ad and tracker blocking, switched on by default, with clear controls to disable it globally or for particular services. That is not a ban on advertising. It is a guarantee that advertising reaches people on their terms.
[P1.2] The first reason is meaningful consent. Tracking is difficult to observe, and its consequences extend beyond the page someone chooses to visit. Reading about debt or a medical condition should not silently invite unrelated companies into that interaction. Requiring users to discover, install, configure, and maintain defensive tools makes privacy depend on technical skill.
[P1.3] An opt-out default reverses that burden: protection is universal; choosing an advertising-supported experience remains possible.
[P1.4] Second, the protection must reach the system level. A browser extension cannot reliably govern advertising and tracking inside other apps or embedded browsers. Operating systems can provide a baseline across those environments, while browsers address page-level behavior. Blocking also reduces exposure to malicious advertising, intrusive overlays, and unwanted resource consumption.
[P1.5] Robustness means maintained protection against common evasions—not an impossible promise that every advertisement will disappear.
[P1.6] Third, law is necessary because the companies controlling these defaults can profit from the activity they should constrain. Voluntary protection is vulnerable to commercial exceptions and strategic weakening. A legal duty should require transparent standards, independent testing, straightforward overrides, and no paid exemptions. It should protect users, not let platform owners selectively handicap competitors.
[P1.7] The strongest objection is lost publisher revenue. Some services may earn less or change their funding models. But a business model does not create an entitlement to occupy someone’s device or monitor their behavior. Publishers can request an exception, offer subscriptions, or explain why users should enable ads.
[P1.8] They must win that choice rather than inherit it through inertia. The decisive principle is simple: access to our attention and data should be granted, not presumed.
