[P1.1] The question is not whether prostitution is ideal—it is which legal framework best limits the harms that surround it. The asymmetrical model—criminalize buying, decriminalize selling—wins on three grounds that full prohibition and full legalization both fail.
[P1.2] First, it targets the demand that drives exploitation. Traffickers and exploitative third parties profit because buyers create the market. Full prohibition punishes the people with the least power—the sellers—while leaving demand intact and pushing it underground. Full legalization legitimizes and expands that demand, and markets in commercial sex attract organized exploitation precisely because the product is human beings.
[P1.3] The Nordic model cuts the root: when buying carries legal risk, the market shrinks, and with it the profits that make trafficking worthwhile. Sweden's experience after adopting this approach in 1999 is the canonical case: street prostitution declined substantially while neighboring countries with legal regimes saw expansion.
[P1.4] Second, it removes the injustice of punishing the exploited. People in prostitution are disproportionately poor, young, abuse survivors, or coerced. Arresting them compounds harm, gives them criminal records that block exit, and teaches them to fear the police rather than seek help. The asymmetrical model flips the legal relationship: the seller can report violence, testify against traffickers, and access services without self-incrimination.
[P1.5] That is a real safety gain that prohibition can never deliver.
[P1.6] Third, it avoids legalization's failure mode. Where buying is legal and normalized—think of the documented expansion of the legal brothel sector in parts of Germany and the Netherlands alongside persistent illegal markets—demand outstrips voluntary supply, and trafficking fills the gap. Legalization does not eliminate the black market; it launders it.
[P1.7] The compromise is principled, not timid: punish the conduct that creates the harm, protect the person caught inside it. Opponents will claim full decriminalization is safer for sellers—but safety gained inside an expanded market built on coerced supply is safety for some at the expense of others. Asymmetry is the only model that shrinks the industry while shielding the people in it.

[C1.1] The asymmetrical model is not a safer compromise; it is demand-side prohibition wearing a victim-protection mask. Its first failure is safety. When purchase is criminal, buyers have strong reasons to stay anonymous, avoid stable venues, insist on rushed encounters, and leave no records. That strips sellers of exactly the tools that reduce violence in a legal market: screening clients, taking identification, sharing bad-date warnings, meeting in known spaces, and reporting problems later.
[C1.2] Criminalizing the buyer does not make the seller safer; it makes the entire transaction furtive.
[C1.3] Second, decriminalizing the sale is mostly formal, not real. A purchase crime still needs evidence, and the seller is the natural evidence. Police surveil sellers to catch buyers, treat condoms or cash as proof of a transaction, and pressure sellers into cooperation. Hotels, landlords, and payment platforms can still be penalized for facilitating buyers’ crime, so sellers lose housing, income channels, and access to justice.
[C1.4] Third, the demand-reduction claim overreads visible declines. A fall in street prostitution often means displacement online or indoors, not a reduction in exploitation. A smaller visible market is not the same as a safer one, and trafficked or coerced people are not helped by being pushed further from services.
[C1.5] The deeper flaw is that the model treats all buyers as interchangeable and all sellers as helpless victims. A law cannot distinguish a violent client from a respectful one if both must hide. A legal or fully decriminalized market better serves the stated goal: workers can set terms, share information, organize, and go to police without self-incrimination. If safety is the test, the asymmetrical compromise fails on its own logic.
