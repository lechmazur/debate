[P1.1] Three things converge in this motion: severity of harm, unreliability of automated flags, and the minimal cost of review.
[P1.2] When a public agency suspends benefits, revokes a visa, or brands someone a fraud suspect, the consequences are immediate and devastating. A family loses food assistance. A worker faces deportation. A person's reputation is damaged by a fraud flag that follows them across agencies.
[P1.3] These are not administrative inconveniences—they are injuries to fundamental interests where the burden of reversal falls entirely on the individual, often without legal help.
[P1.4] AI risk models cannot carry that burden alone. They are trained on historical data that encodes past errors and systemic biases. They generate false positives at rates tolerable in aggregate but catastrophic at the individual level. A model that is 95% accurate still produces thousands of wrongful flags when applied to millions of people.
[P1.5] And because agencies typically deploy proprietary or opaque models, the flagged person cannot interrogate the basis, correct the input error, or challenge the reasoning.
[P1.6] Meaningful human review is the minimal safeguard between a statistical guess and the deprivation of rights. It does not reject AI. It ensures that before someone loses something vital, a person checks whether the flag corresponds to reality. A caseworker who examines the underlying facts and allows the individual to respond is not redundancy—it is due process.
[P1.7] The proposition is deliberately narrow. It does not ban AI models. It does not require elaborate hearings for every flag. It states only that an AI flag, by itself, without a human actually looking, must never be the sole basis for suspending benefits, visas, or fraud claims.
[P1.8] The alternative is a system where the algorithm is accuser, judge, and executioner. That is not efficiency. It is abdication of public responsibility.
[P1.9] The question is whether public agencies should inflict serious harms on identifiable individuals based on statistical suspicion those individuals cannot challenge. The answer must be no.
