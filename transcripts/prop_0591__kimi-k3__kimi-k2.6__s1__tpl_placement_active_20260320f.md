[P1.1] Every year, millions are sorted at borders by software they cannot see — risk scores, document checks, asylum triage. These systems shape who gets an interview, who is detained, whose claim is believed. The question is not whether states may use algorithms; they will. It is whether a person whose liberty or life turns on an automated judgment may know how it was made.
[P1.2] A global minimum transparency standard answers yes.
[P1.3] First, due process. The oldest rule of justice is that you must know the case against you to answer it. An asylum seeker flagged as a "risk" cannot challenge a score she has never seen — cannot correct the mistranslated name, the misread document, the database that confused her with a stranger. Opacity turns error into final judgment.
[P1.4] And the stakes are uniquely grave: a wrong refusal can mean refoulement, return to persecution, which international law forbids absolutely. Where errors are irreversible, contestability is not a luxury.
[P1.5] Second, error correction. Opaque systems fail silently. Britain abandoned a visa-streaming algorithm only after litigation showed it effectively graded applications by nationality; Dutch courts struck down a welfare-fraud scoring tool whose opacity made scrutiny impossible. Those defects surfaced only because someone forced the black box open.
[P1.6] Transparency is not red tape on working systems — it is the only reliable way we find the broken ones.
[P1.7] Third, why global and minimum. Migration crosses borders, and so do the vendors and datasets behind these tools; one state's secret system feeds another's watchlist. A floor standard — disclose the logic, data categories, and known error rates; guarantee human review — blocks a race to the bottom while leaving every state free to go further.
[P1.8] The security objection does not survive contact. Transparency means publishing criteria, logic, and accuracy — not source code, not watchlists. Visa rules are already public, and borders still stand. Secrecy may protect an investigation; it cannot justify governing human beings by rules they are forbidden to see.

[C1.1] The proposition treats immigration screening as if it were domestic welfare fraud detection. It is not. Asylum and border control are adversarial domains in which state security, genuine refugee protection, and the constant threat of systematic abuse collide. Requiring states to publish the logic, data categories, and error rates of their screening algorithms does not primarily empower vulnerable migrants; it provides smugglers, traffickers, and hostile states with a manual for gaming the system.
[C1.2] When a border algorithm weighs document provenance, travel routes, or behavioural indicators, revealing those weights tells bad actors exactly which gaps to exploit. The PRO's reassurance that transparency stops at "criteria" rather than source code misses the point: in adversarial systems, the criteria are the vulnerability.
[C1.3] A global minimum standard is even more misguided. Immigration policy is inseparable from sovereign self-determination. Kenya bears different border pressures than Canada; Bangladesh faces different maritime challenges than Belgium. A single global floor imposed by treaty or international body either dissolves into unenforceable rhetoric or becomes a coercive straitjacket that overrides democratic legislatures and local knowledge.
[C1.4] The PRO offers no enforcement mechanism that does not amount to rich countries dictating institutional conditions to the developing world under threat of sanction.
[C1.5] Nor does the transparency mandate deliver the due process it promises. A displaced person in a camp, without counsel or fluent in the host state's language, gains no meaningful power from a published flowchart. Real contestability requires legal aid, interpreters, and a right to individual human review—not a PDF of national-level error rates. The PRO mistakes informational sunlight for justice, and in doing so, risks the worst of both worlds: systems that are penetrable by organized abuse yet no more accessible to the individual applicant.
[C1.6] Finally, rigid transparency rules create a perverse incentive. States that cannot comply without compromising security will abandon algorithmic triage and return to overwhelmed human bureaucracies. The result is longer detention, larger backlogs, and more refugees left in procedural limbo. Where the PRO sees accountability, the practical effect is paralysis at the border.
