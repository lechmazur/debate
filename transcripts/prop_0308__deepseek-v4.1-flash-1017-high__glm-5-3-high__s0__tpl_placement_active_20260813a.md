[P1.1] Populism is not merely anti-elite politics. Its defining move is anti-pluralist: “we, and only we, are the people.” That is why it is usually a warning sign rather than a healthy corrective. A corrective repairs the machinery of accountability—courts, a free press, competitive parties, elections that can be lost. Populism treats that machinery as illegitimate whenever it constrains the leader’s claim to embody the people.
[P1.2] The mechanism is predictable. Once in office, populists face institutions that can say no. Rather than accept plural opposition, they delegitimize it: judges become “enemies of the people,” independent media become “fake,” election losses become fraud. Then they concentrate power—rewriting constitutions, packing courts, capturing media, weakening local government. Hungary’s Fidesz used its majority to reshape constitutional rules and courts; Turkey’s Erdoğan purged judges and prosecutors and later replaced the parliamentary system with a powerful presidency; Venezuela’s Chávez and his successor rewrote the constitution and eroded checks.
[P1.3] These follow from the populist premise that opposition is illegitimate. And they rarely build durable pro-democracy organizations; they build leader-centric vehicles. A healthy corrective would leave democracy more resilient; populism leaves it more dependent on one person.
[P1.4] The grievance may be real. Inequality, corruption, and distant elites can decay democracy. But a real diagnosis does not make the remedy healthy. A fever can signal infection; it is not the cure.
[P1.5] The comparison is not populism versus perfect elites; it is populism versus ordinary democratic correction—organizing, litigation, elections, watchdog journalism—that repairs institutions without claiming a monopoly on the people. The relevant test is comparative: after populists govern, are institutions more capable of holding power accountable, or more personalized and less constrained? Usually the latter.
[P1.6] Not every populist movement becomes authoritarian. Some fade, some force concessions. But the proposition says “usually,” and the usual trajectory is clear: populism arrives as a protest against elite failure and leaves behind weaker checks on the next elite. That is a warning sign, not a corrective.
