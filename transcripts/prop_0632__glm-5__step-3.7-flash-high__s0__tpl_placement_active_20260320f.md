[P1.2] The argument for mandated coverage rests on three foundations: medical legitimacy, fiscal responsibility, and basic equity.
[P1.3] Obesity satisfies every criterion for a disease warranting treatment—identifiable pathophysiology, progressive course, and severe health consequences. [P1.4] Value-based and outcomes-based contracts eliminate the cost objection. [P1.5] The current system deepens inequality. Obesity disproportionately burdens lower-income Americans, yet anti-obesity medications can exceed $1,000 monthly without coverage. Value-based contracts ensure insurers share in those savings rather than absorbing only upfront expense.
[P1.7] This mandate requires no blank checks—only that insurers cover FDA-authorized treatments when clinically appropriate, using payment models that reward outcomes. Patients diagnosed with a recognized disease deserve nothing less.

[C1.1] This mandate rests on a false equivalency between FDA approval for obesity drugs and therapies like insulin. The FDA’s standard for anti-obesity medications is remarkably low: approval only requires a drug to produce at least 5% greater weight loss than placebo over 12 months, with no requirement to demonstrate reduction in hard clinical endpoints like heart attack, stroke, or diabetes-related mortality. This gap has real consequences: fenfluramine, a once widely approved anti-obesity drug, was pulled from the market decades later after being linked to life-threatening heart valve damage, proving FDA authorization is no guarantee of long-term safety or meaningful clinical benefit for this drug class.
[C1.2] The proposed outcomes-based contracts to control spending are unworkable in practice. Weight loss is an extremely noisy metric, shaped far more by a patient’s socioeconomic status, access to healthy food, safe spaces for exercise, and underlying health conditions than by the drug itself. Tying payment to weight loss will let manufacturers off the hook for poor long-term outcomes, while saddling insurers with massive upfront costs for drugs that deliver negligible clinical benefit for most patients. Existing outcomes contracts for GLP-1 agonists have already failed to curb double-digit annual price hikes from manufacturers, who have refused to tie payment to hard clinical endpoints rather than easy-to-game weight loss targets.
[C1.3] Worst of all, this mandate will divert resources from higher-impact, evidence-based preventive care, worsening overall public health. Insurers forced to cover these high-cost drugs will raise premiums across the board, pricing millions of low-income, working-class Americans out of coverage entirely, and cutting funding for proven interventions like nutrition counseling, subsidized recreation programs, and prenatal care that address the root drivers of obesity far more effectively than a pill. The supposed equity gain for a small subset of patients will come at the cost of access for far more vulnerable people.
