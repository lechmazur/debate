[P1.1] The problem with advanced tracking is not that students learn at different speeds. It is that schools take a difference in speed today and use it to decide who gets to learn what tomorrow. A seventh grader who reasons well about patterns but stumbles over fractions may miss the cutoff for algebra. A year later, her lack of algebra looks like evidence that she belongs where the school put her.
[P1.2] Replace most of those separate sequences with mixed classes built around demanding mathematics. In a lesson on linear relationships, everyone might compare two phone plans and determine when one becomes cheaper. Students ready for abstraction can derive a rule for finding the crossover point and explain when none exists; students working with tables can use them to reach and defend a conclusion. They have something to discuss together without having to finish the same worksheet at the same minute.
[P1.3] The essential counterpart is intensive tutoring during the school day: frequent small-group sessions with a consistent tutor who knows what is coming in class. Give the student who needs fractions repeated practice with immediate feedback, without pulling her out of algebra to do it. Unlike a track assignment, the support can end as soon as the difficulty does.
[P1.4] Advanced learners deserve more than extra problems or a job helping classmates. Let them skip material they have mastered, and retain acceleration for those who need the next course. Neither requires sorting whole cohorts into separate pipelines. Tutoring takes staff and scheduled time, but it buys something sorting alone cannot: instruction aimed at overcoming the difficulty that prompted placement.
[P1.5] The better system is the one that can act on what a student needs this month without deciding what she may study for years.
