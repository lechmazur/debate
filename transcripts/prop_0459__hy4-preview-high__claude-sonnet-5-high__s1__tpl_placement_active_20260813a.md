[P1.1] Clean-tech supply chains are not ordinary markets; they are strategic infrastructure for the next economy, and the motion turns on whether countries should pay for resilience. They should.
[P1.2] Concentration is the core risk. A handful of locations dominate solar wafers, battery-grade materials, processing of rare earths and magnets, and key electrolyzer inputs. When a single supplier controls a chokepoint, trade becomes a lever. Recent export controls on critical minerals and battery inputs show how quickly a commercial dependency can be converted into coercion.
[P1.3] A country that cannot source solar modules, magnets, or battery cells without permission has outsourced part of its energy and industrial policy. A missing shipment of magnets can idle an EV plant; a delayed solar consignment can stall a whole farm.
[P1.4] Industrial policy fixes a market failure. Private firms underinvest in redundancy because the payoff is a tail risk—disruptions are rare until they are catastrophic. Subsidies bridge the early cost gap; procurement creates guaranteed demand that lets factories reach scale. This is why costs fall with deployment.
[P1.5] Solar and batteries did not become cheap through passive free trade alone; sustained support and demand pulled them down the learning curve. Paying more now buys a domestic base that later competes.
[P1.6] Critics will say this means higher prices and trade disputes. Correct, and that is the price of insurance. The premium is transitional; learning-by-doing and scale cut it. The tension is containable: localizing a chokepoint is not autarky, and allies can coordinate subsidies and procurement instead of spiraling into conflict.
[P1.7] The alternative—trusting comparative advantage and global markets—leaves the energy transition exposed to embargoes, crises, and sudden price spikes that can be far costlier than modest, explicit subsidies.
[P1.8] The choice is not cheap versus expensive. It is fragile dependence versus controlled autonomy. Localizing critical clean-tech supply chains is expensive today; being held hostage tomorrow is worse.

[C1.1] Concentration risk is real. But the proposition asks us to accept a specific, expensive fix—full domestic localization funded by subsidies and procurement—when cheaper, faster tools exist. That is the case CON will win on.
[C1.2] First, localization is the wrong response to a chokepoint problem. If the danger is that one country can weaponize a supply line, the answer is diversifying suppliers across multiple friendly nations, building strategic reserves, and signing long-term contracts with allies—not rebuilding an entire wafer-to-magnet chain at home. Diversification gets you redundancy at a fraction of the cost; localization gets you redundancy plus a permanent price premium, because you are now paying to duplicate infrastructure that already exists efficiently elsewhere.
[C1.3] Second, industrial policy is a blunt, capture-prone instrument. Governments are bad at picking which link of the chain to subsidize, for how long, and at what price. History is littered with expensive missteps—flagship solar and battery ventures that collapsed after receiving state money, and tariff regimes that raised costs for downstream manufacturers without ever producing a competitive domestic industry. Subsidies tend to become permanent political entitlements, not temporary bridges, because no domestic producer wants to be exposed to global price competition once protection is in place.
[C1.4] Third, this trades away the very goal clean tech policy is supposed to serve: rapid decarbonization. Higher prices for solar modules, batteries, and electrolyzers slow deployment. Every dollar spent on a costlier domestic panel is a dollar not spent on more panels overall. If the emergency is climate change, artificially raising the price of abatement to chase supply-chain purity is self-defeating.
[C1.5] Fourth, trade tensions are not a manageable side effect—they are corrosive. Retaliatory tariffs, chain fragmentation, and slower technology diffusion raise costs globally and invite tit-for-tat escalation that makes every country's transition harder, not just the localizer's.
[C1.6] The real choice is not fragile dependence versus controlled autonomy. It is expensive, slow, protectionist autonomy versus cheaper, faster, diversified resilience. CON wins that comparison.
