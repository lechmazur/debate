[P1.1] Modern teenagers live significant portions of their social, emotional, and intellectual lives inside devices. When a fifteen-year-old can bypass an app's content filters by downloading a different browser, or when a mature-rated game remains accessible because the app store's parental controls are trivially defeated, something has gone wrong. The proposition before us calls for operating-system-level limits—controls embedded in the device's core software—that third-party applications cannot easily work around.
[P1.2] Parents bear primary responsibility for their children's wellbeing. That responsibility means nothing if the tools available to exercise it are hollow. Today, the most sophisticated content restrictions exist at the app layer, where they can be sidestepped with a VPN, an alternative store, or sometimes simply by clearing a cache. This creates an illusion of protection while delivering none.
[P1.3] Operating-system-level controls solve this problem structurally. Because they operate beneath the applications themselves, a teenager cannot simply download a different app to escape them. The restriction follows the device, not the software. This mirrors how we protect children in the physical world: a lock on the liquor cabinet works because it is structural, not advisory.
[P1.4] The autonomy objection misses the mark. We are not discussing government mandates or adult surveillance. We are discussing whether parents should have effective tools to set boundaries for their children. A parent who decides their thirteen-year-old should not access graphic violence or adult dating apps faces an absurd choice today: trust an honor system with a teenager's impulses, or accept that any determined child can circumvent the available controls.
[P1.5] OS-level limits make parental judgment meaningful rather than cosmetic.
[P1.6] Critics will argue determined teenagers can still find workarounds. This is true of every protective measure humans have ever invented. The question is whether we give parents tools that raise meaningful barriers—tools that work for the typical teenager navigating an ordinary day—or whether we abandon them to an all-or-nothing model where the only effective restriction is no device at all. The proposition offers a better path.
