[P1.1] The key difference between streaming and cable should not merely be the price or the picture quality; it should be the customer’s ability to control the subscription. Cable’s familiar strengths—wide selection and simple collection—should not come bundled with long commitments, hidden charges, and months of difficulty if one channel is unwanted. Streaming bundles should therefore let customers pause, split, or reconfigure service on a monthly basis.
[P1.2] That flexibility would reflect how households actually live. A family may need sports for part of the year, a student may temporarily stop watching while traveling, and two people sharing an account may want different services. Today, changing one part of a bundle often requires cancelling everything, beginning again, or accepting a large package simply because one unwanted component is embedded in it. Monthly adjustment would let the customer preserve the valuable services while removing the irrelevant ones.
[P1.3] This does not require providers to build a custom contract for every household. A simple account interface could show each service and add-on, its monthly price, and the next available change date. Customers could pause, add, or drop components with automatic proration. Providers would still offer curated packages and negotiated rates, but those packages would become choices rather than traps.
[P1.4] The business objection misunderstands this model. Flexible plans can reduce cancellations caused by frustration, make seasonal demand predictable, and allow providers to sell optional content without repackaging the entire subscription. Churn is not always loss; avoidable churn from rigid bundles is. Providers can compete on service quality, recommendation, and exclusive programming rather than on contractual friction.
[P1.5] The decisive standard is simple: if removing one unwanted service is harder than replacing the whole bundle, the bundle is reproducing cable’s lock-in in new clothing. Monthly reconfiguration makes the customer—not the provider—the architect of the subscription, while transparent pricing and modular billing preserve provider revenue. That is the better model for both consumers and a competitive streaming market.

[C1.1] The motion treats every click of friction as cable-style lock-in. That is a category error. Cable’s cruelty came from contracts, technicians, and equipment. A streaming bundle can be cancelled today; the bundle is a discount, not a chain.
[C1.2] If a household wants the sports service alone, it can buy it—what it cannot always get is the discounted aggregate price while shedding the parts it dislikes. The proposition does not remove lock-in; it removes cross-subsidization.
[C1.3] That distinction matters because content is financed by certainty. Sports rights and original programming are underwritten on multi-year revenue expectations. Monthly reconfiguration converts a stable subscriber into a volatile, revolving customer. To cover that risk, providers will raise component prices, cut mid-budget and niche programming, or lean heavier on advertising—reintroducing the friction and hidden costs PRO claims to eliminate.
[C1.4] A family that pauses sports for three summer months may return to a pricier, ad-supported version of the same service. The promise of flexibility is paid for with higher prices and thinner libraries.
[C1.5] PRO imagines a clean dashboard. The lived experience is messier. Proration, promotional expirations, and shared-account negotiation multiply decisions. When one household member pauses sports and another re-adds it, the interface does not resolve the dispute; it schedules it monthly.
[C1.6] A predictable bundle bill is simpler than a spreadsheet of toggles. Bundling reduces search costs and bargaining friction; forcing modularity shifts that burden onto customers.
[C1.7] The decisive standard should be honest pricing and content quality, not administrative churn. A stable, honestly priced bundle already gives consumers what they actually want: predictable value and good programming. The proposition would fragment the audience, raise the cost of ambition, and replace one transparent monthly charge with a recurring management task. That is not liberation; it is a worse deal dressed up as freedom.
