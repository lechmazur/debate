[P1.1] Infertility is not merely disappointment with a lifestyle preference. It is the impaired functioning of a bodily system, often caused by conditions such as blocked fallopian tubes, endometriosis, hormonal disorders, or severely reduced sperm production. When medicine can diagnose and treat that dysfunction, including through IVF when simpler measures cannot work, it is healthcare.
[P1.2] Calling it basic healthcare does not mean promising unlimited cycles, guaranteeing a child, or funding every request regardless of prognosis. Basic healthcare systems already set clinical eligibility rules, begin with less invasive treatments, limit repeated procedures, and prioritize according to likely benefit and safety. The proposition requires meaningful access to medically indicated treatment—not a blank cheque.
[P1.3] The decisive issue is equitable access. Without public coverage, infertility treatment is effectively reserved for people who can produce thousands in disposable income at precisely the stage of life when many are least financially secure. Two patients can have the same diagnosis, prognosis, and suffering, yet only the wealthy patient receives treatment. That is not neutral rationing; it is rationing by wealth.
[P1.4] Healthcare should respond to medical need, not bank balance.
[P1.5] Nor is infertility uniquely unworthy because treatment helps patients pursue parenthood. Medicine routinely treats conditions whose importance lies in enabling central human activities: mobility, sexual function, hearing, pain-free living, and mental well-being. Fertility is likewise a basic dimension of bodily function and life planning. Patients need not prove that survival is at stake before their condition counts.
[P1.6] Public provision can also make treatment safer and more efficient. Regulated coverage can require appropriate screening, evidence-based protocols, and practices that reduce risky multiple pregnancies. By contrast, patients paying ruinous sums per attempt may feel pressure to maximize the chance of immediate success, even when that creates avoidable medical costs later.
[P1.7] Scarcity justifies sensible limits, not categorical exclusion. A defined package—diagnosis, appropriate first-line care, and a limited number of IVF attempts for patients with a reasonable clinical prospect—can balance cost with need. Countries should not guarantee outcomes; they should guarantee that a genuine medical condition receives a fair medical response.

[P2.5] Scarcity also does not establish categorical exclusion. [P2.6] The choice is therefore not IVF versus infinite resources. It is transparent clinical rationing versus rationing by personal wealth. Under CON’s model, a wealthy patient with a modest prognosis may proceed repeatedly, while a poorer patient with a strong prognosis receives nothing. [P2.7] If scarcity demands rationing, medical criteria are more defensible than the size of a patient’s bank account.
