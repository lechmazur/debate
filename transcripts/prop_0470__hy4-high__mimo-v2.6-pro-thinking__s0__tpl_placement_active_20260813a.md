[P1.1] Hyper-realistic political deepfakes are forged evidence, not contentious opinion. A fake video of a candidate confessing, conceding, or urging supporters not to vote uses that person's face and voice to certify a false event. Democracy can survive spin, abuse, and vicious cartoons; it cannot preserve electoral consent when “who said or did what” can be cheaply counterfeited at scale. The analogy is a forged ballot notice, forged signature, or official impostor—not an unpopular pamphlet.
[P1.2] The damage outruns ordinary rebuttal. A convincing clip released near voting can flood chats and feeds before correction; denial then looks self-serving, while the image sticks. Worse, deepfakes create a liar's dividend: authentic recordings of corruption become deniable as fake. So the injury is not merely that some citizens are fooled.
[P1.3] It is that public evidence is destabilized at the moment of choice, and real misconduct gains an alibi.
[P1.4] The free-speech objection starts too late: it treats counterfeit perception as another message. Anonymity protects nameless criticism of power, not the use of a politician's face and voice as a mask. Satire usually depends on recognizable artifice—caricature, captions, exaggeration. If the joke works only because viewers think the event happened, it has become impersonation.
[P1.5] Some sharp anonymous parody would be chilled; that is the conceded cost. Obvious parody, labeled fiction, commentary, and pseudonymous organizing still give dissent room.
[P1.6] A targeted ban on knowingly making or recklessly spreading hyper-realistic false depictions of political actors is election-integrity law for a new forged instrument, not viewpoint suppression. Democracies already regulate fraud, forgery, impersonation, and false voting instructions without becoming censorial; an audio-visual counterfeit is not sacred expression. The comparison is bounded loss of politically deceptive masks versus loss of a shared evidentiary world. Self-government can tolerate angry, unfair, and anonymous arguments; it cannot tolerate anyone manufacturing the apparent acts of rulers at the decisive moment.
