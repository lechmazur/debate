[P1.1] Housing shortages are fundamentally a supply problem. Any policy we evaluate must answer one question: does it reliably unlock more units while sustaining public revenue? Land-value taxation does this; transaction taxes and rent caps actively work against it.
[P1.2] The mechanism is straightforward. A land-value tax levies charges on the underlying parcel, not the structures built on it. This changes the calculus for every landowner holding a vacant lot or a low-density building in a high-demand area. Currently, such owners face no penalty for sitting idle—they can watch land appreciate while contributing nothing to local services.
[P1.3] Under LTV, idle holding becomes expensive, and the rational response is to develop. Parking lots become apartment buildings. Single-family parcels near transit become mid-rise housing. The tax doesn't punish building; it punishes *not* building.
[P1.4] Compare this to transaction taxes, which are levied when properties change hands. These taxes discourage exactly the sales and transfers that facilitate redevelopment. A developer who wants to buy several adjacent lots and combine them into a larger project faces a tax penalty for each transaction. The result is friction that locks land into its current, often suboptimal, use.
[P1.5] Rent caps produce a different distortion. By capping returns on rental housing, they directly reduce the incentive to build new rental units. Economists across the political spectrum recognize this tradeoff: you can make existing units cheaper for current tenants, but you shrink the future supply pipeline. Cities with strict rent control historically see declining construction permits relative to comparable cities without it.
[P1.6] On public finance, LTV has a decisive advantage: land cannot flee. When you raise income taxes, people restructure income. When you tax transactions heavily, volume collapses and revenue becomes volatile, tied to boom-bust cycles. Land value is assessed on immovable parcels with transparent market data, creating a stable, predictable revenue base that local governments can rely on for long-term infrastructure planning.
[P1.7] The proposition is clear: LTV aligns incentives with supply growth and provides durable public revenue in ways that transaction taxes and rent caps structurally cannot match.

[C1.1] The proposition sells land-value taxation as a clean supply unlock, but that promise rests on a theory that real housing markets routinely refuse to follow. The binding constraint on housing supply in most high-demand cities is not idle landholding—it is zoning, permitting delays, environmental review, and construction costs. A land-value tax makes sitting on a parking lot more expensive, true.
[C1.2] But if zoning still prohibits apartments on that parcel, if permitting takes years, if labor and materials are scarce, the owner cannot develop regardless of tax pressure. LVT raises the cost of holding land without removing the barriers that actually prevent building. That pushes owners toward the next-best option: sell to someone who faces the same barriers, or convert to whichever low-friction use local rules already permit.
[C1.3] On public finance, the claim that land "cannot flee" is technically true and practically misleading. Land does not move, but development activity does. A city that imposes high land-value taxes relative to its neighbors will see investment shift toward jurisdictions where carrying costs are lower. The tax base is immobile, but the tax base's value depends on the economic activity surrounding it—activity that is quite mobile.
[C1.4] Assessment creates its own problem. Land-value taxation requires separating unimproved land value from structure value, a notoriously contested exercise. Every reassessment becomes a political fight, and systematic misvaluation erodes both revenue reliability and public trust.
[C1.5] The proposition also frames this as a clean comparison against two flawed alternatives, but transaction taxes can be structured with redevelopment exemptions and first-time buyer carve-outs, while rent caps protect existing tenants from displacement that LVT can actually accelerate by raising carrying costs for small landlords. The question is not whether LVT is theoretically elegant but whether it delivers more housing and better finance in practice—and on that score, the proposition's confidence outruns its evidence.
