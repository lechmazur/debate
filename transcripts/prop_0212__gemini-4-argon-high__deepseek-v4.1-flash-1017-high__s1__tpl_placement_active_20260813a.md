[P1.2] First, broad archival access is essential restorative justice for families and affected communities. These records are not academic curiosities; they are the only map back to stolen lives and rights. For families of the disappeared under military dictatorships, internal police ledgers reveal how loved ones died and where they lie. For formerly colonized populations—like Kenyans subjected to detention and land confiscation under British rule—archives contain the proof needed to establish historical truth and legal standing.

When departing colonial powers hid thousands of decolonization files, the threat was not to active intelligence, but to state prestige. Moreover, prolonged secrecy poisons post-conflict societies.
[P1.5] Without documented truth, communities suffer toxic rumor mills, weaponized accusations of collaboration, and nationalist revisionism. Nor does protecting individual privacy require blanket closure; targeted redaction rules and structured access protocols can distinguish innocent third parties from state crimes without burying the record. Broad disclosure replaces convenient mythology with verifiable fact. Once immediate security concerns expire, the moral right to these records belongs to the people whose lives are trapped inside them, not the bureaucracies seeking a cleaner past.
