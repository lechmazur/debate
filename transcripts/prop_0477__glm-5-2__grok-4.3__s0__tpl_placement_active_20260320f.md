[P1.1] The arteries of modern national security run along the ocean floor. Over ninety-five percent of intercontinental data—military communications, financial clearing systems, diplomatic traffic, cloud services—travels through a fragile network of undersea cables. A handful of anchors, trawls, or deliberate cuts can isolate a nation, fracture allied coordination, and freeze commerce. Yet these cables receive a fraction of the funding and institutional attention devoted to visible platforms: warships, fighter jets, armored brigades.
[P1.2] The proposition's framing is the crux. It asks whether we should shift resources from *visible* procurements to *invisible* infrastructure. The political economy of defense spending rewards visibility—photo ops, parades, fleet counts, ribbon-cuttings. Cable protection offers no such spectacle.
[P1.3] That is precisely why it is underfunded and precisely why correcting the imbalance is urgent.
[P1.4] The threat is asymmetric and growing. Damaging a cable requires modest equipment: a ship's anchor, a fishing trawl, or a submersible with cutting tools. Repairing one demands specialized cable-laying vessels that are globally scarce and can take weeks to mobilize, especially when multiple cables fail simultaneously. Recent suspicious cable damage in the Baltic and near critical chokepoints suggests the threat is not theoretical.
[P1.5] A nation can possess the most advanced surface fleet in the world, but if its command-and-control traffic cannot reach allied capitals because the cables are severed, that fleet operates blind.
[P1.6] Redundancy is not a luxury. Multiple geographically diverse cable landings, hardened routing, and rapid-repair capability convert catastrophic single points of failure into manageable risk. These investments cost less than a single frigate or a fighter squadron. The price of one major surface combatant could instead fund redundant cable paths, seabed monitoring arrays, and a dedicated repair fleet for an entire region—protecting the connective tissue on which all modern military and economic activity depends.
[P1.7] The choice is not defense versus infrastructure. It is defense that photographs well versus defense that actually secures the systems a modern state needs to function. The cable network is the latter, neglected precisely because it fails the photo-op test.

Shifting money from hulls and squadrons to cable redundancy therefore removes the first line of defense rather than reinforcing it.
[C1.2] The low cost of cable attacks works against the proposition. An adversary willing to risk open conflict will also be prepared to sever multiple redundant paths simultaneously, especially during hostilities when repair ships are themselves targets. Under those conditions, the decisive margin remains the ability to contest the sea and air space, not the number of parallel cables already laid.
[C1.3] Opportunity costs are concrete. Funds spent on another frigate or maritime patrol squadron buy both general warfighting capacity and incidental cable security through presence and rapid response. The same sums directed exclusively at cable protection buy narrow resilience that collapses if the surrounding military balance tilts. Nations that chronically underfund visible forces have repeatedly found their infrastructure exposed regardless of prior engineering investments.
[C1.4] Prioritizing visible procurements does not ignore cables; it recognizes that functioning cables are a consequence of superior conventional strength, not a substitute for it.
