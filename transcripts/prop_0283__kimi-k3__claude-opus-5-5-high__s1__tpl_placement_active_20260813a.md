[P1.1] Judge this strategy by its ledger. Since Mexico embraced "hugs, not bullets," it has suffered the deadliest years in its modern history: roughly thirty thousand homicides a year, over a hundred thousand people disappeared, and the last six-year term the most murderous on record. That is not the price of confrontation; it is the cost of avoidance.
[P1.2] Nor have the cartels been pacified; they have been promoted. They tax avocado growers in Michoacán, run extortion rackets in the capital's markets, man highway roadblocks, field armored trucks, drones and roadside bombs, and recruit children. Across parts of Sinaloa, Chiapas, Guerrero and Zacatecas, the question is not whether the state fights the cartels but whether the state is present at all. A government that cedes taxation, territory and force is not keeping the peace; it is losing a war by forfeit.
[P1.3] The strategy's own emblem gives it away: Culiacán, 2019, when federal forces captured a cartel boss and then released him as gunmen overran the city. Every cartel learned that sufficient violence bends the Mexican state. Impunity compounds the lesson: the vast majority of homicides are never solved, and each unsolved murder purchases the next one.
[P1.4] My opponent will say Calderón's war already proved force fails. But the kingpin strategy failed in execution — decapitating cartels while leaving their plazas, finances and captured local police intact — not in principle. Here is the decisive point: the avoidance years delivered comparable or worse bloodshed with none of the degradation of cartel capacity. Mexico gets the war's body count without the war's results.
[P1.5] What is justified is not martial law but a harder posture: intelligence-led operations that retake territory, dismantle extortion networks, shield prosecutors and journalists, and make capture rather than accommodation the cartels' lived expectation. A state that will not defend its monopoly on violence has not chosen peace. It has chosen who rules instead.
