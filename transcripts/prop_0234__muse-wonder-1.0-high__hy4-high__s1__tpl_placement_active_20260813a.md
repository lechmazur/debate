[P1.1] Secret pay is not flexibility. It is leverage, and large employers hold almost all of it.
[P1.2] When a worker applies, she does not know if an offer sits at the bottom of a hidden range or near its top, what colleagues in the same role earn, or how often people in that role get promoted. The employer knows all three. That asymmetry turns negotiation into guessing, and guessing favors the side that wrote the rules. Requiring large employers to publish pay bands and promotion rates by role gives workers the map the company already uses internally.
[P1.3] That matters most where discretion becomes discrimination. Under secrecy, two people doing the same work can drift far apart because one negotiated harder, had a better outside offer, knew a manager, or looked more like those already promoted. No victim can prove a pattern that is hidden. Published bands make outliers visible.
[P1.4] Published promotion rates expose dead-end roles and pipelines that narrow at one level. Managers can still differentiate, but for reasons they can defend.
[P1.5] Transparency also makes labor markets work better. Workers who see a band can target fitting roles, stop wasting months on mismatched openings, and invest in steps that lead to promotion. Employers gain self-selected applicants, less resentment when pay leaks through gossip anyway, and retention built on a credible path rather than suspicion. Pay is rarely secret for long; it is unevenly secret, shared in whispers among the well-connected.
[P1.6] The flexibility objection confuses useful discretion with arbitrary discretion. A band is not a single salary. Firms can still pay more for scarce skills, performance, or experience inside the range, revise bands, and document exceptions. What they lose is the ability to lowball the uninformed and reward favorites without a record.
[P1.7] That loss is not worth protecting, especially for large employers that already maintain these bands for budgeting and HR. Disclosure makes data already collected usable by those it governs.

[C1.1] The demand borrows a tempting image: workers receive “the map the company already uses internally” [P1.2]. Large firms rarely have one clean map. They have provisional ranges, leveling disputes, geographic differentials, scarce-skill premiums, retention exceptions, and jobs that change midyear. Forcing those estimates into public pay bands and role-by-role promotion rates does not merely reveal information; it turns managerial judgment into a compliance artifact.
[C1.2] Flexibility is not the privilege to favor friends. It is the capacity to pay the unusual case accurately. Two “project managers” may be doing different work: one coordinates a routine rollout; another rescues a failing implementation with regulatory exposure. A public band invites both to compare numbers while stripping managers of room to explain context without publishing a small novel about every exception.
[C1.3] The safe response is defensive: midpoint offers, narrower exceptions, slower approvals, fewer bespoke roles.
[C1.4] Promotion-rate disclosure is worse. A rate by role sounds like a fairness metric, but it is often noise. In a team of nine, one promotion is a dramatic percentage; a reorganization or hiring freeze can distort the next quarter’s number. Employers can game it: split roles, relabel levels, invent titles.
[C1.5] Workers get precise-looking data less useful than an honest conversation about a specific path.
[C1.6] The PRO side treats hidden deviations as discrimination risk. That risk supports targeted audits, enforceable anti-bias rules, internal appeals, and contextual review—not this public role-by-role mandate. This mandate treats every deviation as suspicious, including ones that help overlooked workers: higher offers for nontraditional backgrounds, accelerated moves for people without elite sponsorship, retention adjustments after a competitor bid.
[C1.7] The motion treats lost flexibility as cheap. That loss falls on adaptation—the way large employers match heterogeneous work to heterogeneous people. Require broad public categories and firms manage to the categories. The least powerful employee will not gain a ladder; she will get a narrower box.

[P2.6] If exceptions consistently favor the already sponsored, workers finally see it.
[P2.7] That is why their alternatives fail on their own terms. Private audits, appeals, and honest conversations ask the worker to trust a process controlled by the side holding the data. [C2.3] Nor does public visibility equal accountability. PRO dismisses audits and appeals because the firm holds the data [P2.7]. But this mandate also lets the firm define the role, level, year, eligible pool, and exception coding. A public percentage cannot inspect offers, performance records, business necessity, or who was considered.

[C2.5] "An imperfect signal beats enforced blindness" [P2.4] stacks the deck: the alternative was never blindness. Written criteria, individualized range information, appeals, and targeted bias review all give checks without freezing classifications. The choice is contextual accountability versus numbers that make context expensive. PRO says exceptions are not banned [P2.5], but a manager who must justify every counteroffer, scarce-skill premium, or accelerated promotion on a defensible record will seek approval or avoid the exception.
[C2.6] A rule need not ban discretion to make it disappear.
[C2.7] That is why the claim that public bands and rates merely discipline judgment [P2.8] is too neat. Broad bands bless lowballing with an official label; narrow bands suppress legitimate differences; promotion rates become targets, not descriptions. The least powerful worker gets a dashboard, not a remedy. The conceded loss of flexibility is not a small price for fairness; it is how a contextual problem becomes rigid categories, with precise-looking numbers deciding where judgment was needed.

[P4.2] The reviewer CON prefers works only after suspicion exists. Publication creates suspicion, targeting, and a remedy path.
[P4.3] A2: Because most counteroffers still sit inside the band. Moving a scarce hire from midpoint to the top quartile is exactly what a range permits; it does not require a public defense of that person. Large employers already demand approval and a business rationale for retention pay. Disclosure records where offers land in aggregate; it does not veto them.
[P4.4] If managers defaulted to midpoint while competitors paid for scarce skills, they would lose the hires flexibility is supposedly for. Competition preserves useful exceptions; secrecy mainly preserves unjustified ones.
[P4.5] Those answers expose the real divide. CON asks workers to trust two invisible safeguards: managers will exercise hidden discretion benevolently, and hidden reviewers will correct them when they do not. Opposing disclosure does not protect contextual judgment from rigidity; it protects it from being tested.
[P4.6] The conceded cost is narrow: less room to make an unexplained outlier offer or leave a dead-end role unexamined. For large employers that already track these bands and rates, that is a fair trade — flexibility inside visible guardrails over flexibility that only the powerful can verify.
