[P1.1] The central question is not whether these animals are human, but whether they possess interests that the law should protect with enforceable rights. Great apes, cetaceans, and elephants share capacities that make captivity and exploitation not mere regulation problems but rights violations: self-awareness, rich social lives, long-term memory, communication traditions, and evident grief or distress when isolated or confined.
[P1.2] Legal personhood has never been reserved for Homo sapiens. The law grants personhood to entities that need protection or can hold interests through representatives. What matters here is not the ability to sign contracts but the ability to suffer, to choose, and to be harmed. A chimpanzee kept alone in a roadside exhibit, a dolphin forced to perform in a concrete tank, or an elephant chained for tourism is not merely poorly managed property; each is a socially complex individual whose basic interests are overridden for profit.
[P1.3] Recognizing non-human personhood gives courts a coherent way to say: this creature may not be treated as a thing.
[P1.4] This does not mean giving them voting rights or criminal responsibility. It means a narrow bundle of basic negative rights: no arbitrary confinement, no forced labor or performance, no breeding for display, no sale as chattel. Guardians or advocates could enforce those rights, as they do for human beings who cannot speak for themselves.
[P1.5] The practical result is not chaos. Existing facilities would face phased obligations: end new captures and breeding, prohibit transfer for entertainment, and provide sanctuary where release is impossible. That is what basic rights require when the alternatives are lifelong exploitation.
[P1.6] If the law continues to call them property, welfare rules will remain permissions to harm. Personhood draws the line: some beings matter too much to be owned.
