[P1.1] When a morning show host plugs a skincare line or a radio presenter reads a paid endorsement, strict disclosure standards apply. The sponsorship must be unmistakable, announced clearly within the broadcast, and backed by real enforcement. Yet when a digital creator with millions of followers promotes that exact same product to an even larger audience, the disclosure often shrinks to a tiny hashtag buried below a "read more" fold. That regulatory gap makes no sense.
[P1.2] Influencers with large audiences should face advertising-disclosure rules as strict as those governing traditional media personalities.
[P1.3] First, regulation should track commercial function and reach, not legacy technology. A major influencer is not a hobbyist chatting with friends; they are a commercial broadcaster operating a high-revenue advertising vehicle. Brands shift massive budgets from television and print to major creators precisely because they command mass attention. Treating them more leniently simply because they distribute content through a social feed rather than a broadcast tower creates an arbitrary loophole that rewards covert marketing.
[P1.4] Second, the risk of consumer deception is actually higher in influencer media than in traditional broadcasting. Television and radio have built-in structural cues: viewers recognize studio formats, commercial breaks, and formal presenter scripts. [P1.5] When a finance creator casually mentions a trading app during a vlog, or a fitness streamer praises a supplement mid-workout, the audience perceives a friend's candid advice rather than a bought placement. Only strict, broadcast-grade rules—requiring clear, unavoidable verbal and visual disclosures during the endorsement itself—can protect consumers from disguised advertising.
[P1.6] Finally, applying this standard to influencers with large audiences ensures proportionality. Major creators sign formal contracts, employ management, and earn substantial sponsorship revenue. Requiring professional digital broadcasters to state plainly when they are being paid to sell a product does not burden creativity; it enforces basic marketplace honesty.

[C1.1] The proposition rests on a false equivalence: it treats a TikTok creator as a television network simply because both reach millions. But broadcast regulations were built for a world of scarce spectrum, scheduled programming, and rigid separation between editorial content and paid spots. Social media is neither scheduled nor linear; it is a continuous, interactive stream where the boundary between personal expression and commerce has always been fluid. Forcing "broadcast-grade" verbal disclosures onto a fifteen-second story or a casual livestream comment is not consumer protection—it is regulatory malpractice that would either destroy the medium or drive advertising underground into even less transparent forms.
[C1.2] More fundamentally, the premise that influencers currently escape disclosure rules is simply wrong. The same federal standards that govern endorsements on morning television already apply to influencers; endorsement guidelines do not carve out exceptions for Instagram or YouTube. The real problem is not a lack of legal strictness but the practical impossibility of policing millions of individual creators across platforms with wildly different formats. You cannot fix an enforcement challenge by pretending a vlogger is a broadcast licensee subject to oversight designed for networks with legal departments and scheduled commercial breaks that simply do not exist in a feed.
[C1.3] Finally, the claim that influencer audiences are uniquely deceived misunderstands the psychology of social media. Viewers of traditional television historically consumed advertising passively; social media users, by contrast, are native to the platform and reflexively skeptical of sponsored content. They scroll past disclosures not because they are hidden, but because they recognize the commercial context instantly. Imposing rigid, one-size-fits-all broadcast rules would not protect these savvy consumers—it would crush individual creators who lack corporate compliance teams, while doing nothing to stop the sophisticated bad actors who already ignore the rules that exist.
[C1.4] The result would be a chilled internet where only well-funded creators can afford to speak, while deceptive practices simply migrate to less regulated corners.
