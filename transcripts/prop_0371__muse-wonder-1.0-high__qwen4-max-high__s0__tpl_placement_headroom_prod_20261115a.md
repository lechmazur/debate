[P1.1] A ticket that does not let you bring clothes for the trip is not a cheaper ticket; it is an incomplete one. Requiring every airline to include a reasonable carry-on — one personal item and one standard bag that fits the overhead bin — restores the meaning of the price travelers see first and makes airlines compete on the cost of flying, not on who can hide that cost longest.
[P1.2] That hiding is the business model. Search results reward the lowest headline fare, so each carrier has an incentive to strip another necessity and sell it back later, after the traveler has invested time, compared options, and is halfway through checkout. At that point switching is costly, comparison is distorted, and consent is nominal. This is not unbundling; it is a race to fragment the product until the advertised fare bears little relation to what a trip requires.
[P1.3] The fragmentation also imposes costs airlines do not bear. When carry-ons are paywalled, passengers cram belongings into personal items, fight for exemptions, check bags they would rather keep, or arrive at the gate unsure what will be allowed. Boarding slows, bins become a contest, staff become fee enforcers, and delays and confrontations rise. A baseline makes boarding rules predictable for everyone and lets charges remain where they belong: checked luggage, overweight bags, extra bags, and optional extras.
[P1.4] A floor is not a price freeze. Airlines could still compete on routes, schedules, service, and total price, and charge more for more. What they could not do is win customers by pretending an essential is free to provide only when no one brings it. Travelers who flew light still gain, because honest headline prices let them find the cheapest complete trip instead of the cheapest illusion.

A seat, basic safety, and a reasonable way to bring clothes are that unit for air travel.
