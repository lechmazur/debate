[P1.1] Supermarkets operate on thin margins, but they also generate mountains of perfectly edible food that never reaches a plate. Treating that waste as a routine line item externalizes real costs onto society and the environment. When edible groceries are landfilled, they decompose into methane, a greenhouse gas dozens of times more potent than carbon dioxide.
[P1.2] Meanwhile, food banks struggle with inconsistent supply and families stretch budgets to afford basics. A requirement to donate or sharply discount unsold edible food before disposal corrects this mismatch. It forces retailers to internalize the true cost of waste and redirects surplus toward human consumption instead of rot.
[P1.3] The logistics are already proven. Major chains routinely run evening markdowns, partner with regional food banks, and use digital platforms to move near-expiry stock. The barrier is not feasibility; it is inconsistency. Without a baseline requirement, responsible stores absorb the labor and coordination costs while competitors simply trash surplus to avoid hassle.
[P1.4] A mandate levels the playing field, making donation and discounting the standard operating procedure rather than a voluntary perk. Liability concerns are routinely overstated; good-faith donation frameworks already shield retailers from frivolous claims, and discounting carries no liability at all. The marginal labor of sorting and routing edible items is easily offset by reduced hauling fees, potential tax incentives, and the customer traffic that deep discounts generate.
[P1.5] The central question is whether we accept food waste as an inevitable business expense or treat edible surplus as a resource that must be exhausted before disposal. The proposition does not demand perfection or zero waste; it requires a simple hierarchy: feed people first, then discard what remains. That hierarchy delivers immediate environmental relief, strengthens community safety nets, and aligns corporate practice with basic resource efficiency. Normalizing disposal when viable alternatives exist is a policy choice, and it is the wrong one.
