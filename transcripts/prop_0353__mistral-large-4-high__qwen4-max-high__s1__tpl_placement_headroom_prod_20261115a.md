[P1.1] In wealthy democracies, citizens do not lose faith because a handful of ineligible ballots slip through; they lose faith when they watch institutions openly serve wealth over voters. Legal corruption and regulatory capture operate at the level of policy itself, shaping who pays taxes, which drugs reach the market at what price, and whether environmental rules survive intact, while outright voter fraud remains statistically marginal, episodic, and undetectable in aggregate. The damage to trust is therefore not comparable in kind, let alone in scale.
[P1.2] Consider the mechanism. When a pharmaceutical regulator approves prices that bankrupt patients, or when a tech or financial watchdog is staffed by veterans of the industry it oversees, the public witnesses a system that is visibly rigged before the election even happens. This breeds a generalized cynicism: the belief that votes do not matter because outcomes are purchased in advance. Voter fraud, by contrast, even where it occurs, appears as isolated cheating by individuals, not as the design of the system.
[P1.3] A citizen who suspects one fraudulent ballot does not conclude that democracy is broken; a citizen who sees insulin cost ten times more than in peer nations because of lobbying concludes that the game was fixed from the start.
[P1.4] The polling reflects this asymmetry. Across wealthy democracies, large majorities consistently report that their governments serve special interests rather than public will, while far smaller shares believe their own elections are stolen through fraud. Trust collapses not when people fear that someone else's vote counts twice, but when they see their own vote counting for less than a donor's check. Legal corruption hollows out democracy from within, making every election feel ceremonial, whereas voter fraud would at most taint a single contest.
[P1.5] The former corrodes the entire edifice of representative government; the latter scratches its surface.
