[P1.1] Farmland is the bedrock of our food security, not a speculative asset. When non-farming institutional investors accumulate large shares of agricultural land, they prioritize short-term financial returns over long-term ecological stability. This drives a shift toward monocultures that undermine resilience to climate shocks, pests, and market volatility. The evidence is visible from the U.S.
[P1.2] Midwest to Eastern Europe: as institutional ownership rises, diverse cropping systems decline and rural communities hollow out. Local economies suffer as profits flow to distant shareholders instead of circulating through main street businesses. Schools and hospitals lose critical tax revenue when land is owned by tax-exempt entities like pension funds. Young farmers, already facing steep barriers to entry, find land prices pushed further out of reach by financial speculation.
[P1.3] When these investors exit during downturns, they trigger rapid consolidation, leaving communities with fewer, larger operations that offer fewer local jobs and reduce food source diversity. Capping institutional ownership reverses these trends. It keeps land in the hands of active farmers who maintain soil health through cover cropping and reduced tillage, preserve biodiversity through crop rotation, and adapt practices to local conditions. These are the stewards who implement the sustainable practices that ensure long-term productivity.
[P1.4] Caps also sustain rural social fabric by ensuring land use decisions serve community needs rather than portfolio performance. Most critically, they prevent the concentration of control over our food system in the hands of entities with no long-term stake in its viability. The alternative—a free-for-all where farmland is just another asset class—risks turning our food supply into a house of cards. Without local roots, a single shock can trigger cascading failures, leaving regions without food or livelihood.
[P1.5] This is not about rejecting investment; it is about ensuring that investment serves food security and rural communities, not the other way around.

[C1.1] Mandatory ownership caps would throttle the capital infusion rural economies desperately need while trapping aging farmers in illiquid assets they cannot sell. The proposition assumes that financial investors treat land like a poker chip, but the reality is that pension funds and agricultural trusts typically hold farmland for decades—longer than many family operators who might flip land during a bad season. These institutions bring the deep pockets necessary for precision irrigation, soil carbon sequestration, and climate-adaptive infrastructure that cash-strapped individual farmers simply cannot finance.
[C1.2] The cap would backfire on entry barriers too. When institutional buyers exit, land values do not magically drop to help young farmers; instead, liquidity dries up, leaving parcels locked in estates or sold only to wealthy local elites with cash on hand. Retiring farmers lose their pension—the equity in their dirt—because the pool of qualified buyers shrinks arbitrarily.
[C1.3] Meanwhile, the tax revenue argument collapses upon inspection: pension funds pay property taxes unless specifically exempted by separate statutes, and a cap would reduce overall land values, shrinking the tax base for rural schools regardless of whose name is on the deed.
[C1.4] Food-system resilience requires diversification of risk, not concentration in the hands of local operators exposed to the same regional drought or crop disease. Institutional portfolios spread holdings across continents and climates, absorbing localized shocks without abandoning the land. When Iowa floods or Ukraine faces conflict, global investment flows can stabilize operations rather than trigger the fire sales the proposition fears. Arbitrary caps replace this risk distribution with geographic concentration, making the food system more fragile, not less.
[C1.5] Finally, defining "non-farming institutional investor" invites regulatory capture. A pension fund leasing to a tenant farmer becomes a farming entity overnight; a hedge fund buys the local grain elevator and claims agricultural status. The compliance costs and loophole-chasing would waste administrative resources better spent on actual resilience policies—like subsidizing crop insurance or conservation easements that work regardless of whose name is on the title.
