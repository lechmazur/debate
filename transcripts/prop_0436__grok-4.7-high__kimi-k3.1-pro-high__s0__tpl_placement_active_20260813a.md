[P1.1] Whether ordering dinner or booking a table, the first results a user sees should be nearby, affordable, and worth choosing. Too often they show who paid. [P1.3] This is not ordinary advertising beside a fair ranking. Default order, visual weight, and “recommended” modules are the product. A chain or funded brand can buy that weight; a family restaurant on thin margins usually cannot. The sort then rewards ad spend over price, distance, and ratings.

[C1.1] The motion sounds harmless because it builds its conclusion into its adjectives: who could defend “struggle”? But the question is not whether apps may hide good options indefinitely. It is whether sponsored prominence crosses a special moral line whenever a cheaper, nearby independent restaurant takes effort to find. That rule is unjustified.
[C1.2] First, prominence is not a public utility owed to one ranking. Delivery and reservation searches serve different goals: ratings, cuisine, reliability, delivery time, availability, dietary needs, and price. A cheap restaurant three blocks away may be closed, slow, poorly rated, or lack the requested cuisine. PRO’s demand that defaults sort by distance and price simply replaces commercial bias with their own preferred bias.
[C1.3] Sponsored modules can coexist with organic results, filters, and maps; extra scrolling is an interface cost, not proof that users were denied meaningful choice.
[C1.4] Second, sponsorship is not synonymous with chain power. Advertising is often how a new independent restaurant without hundreds of reviews gets its first orders and escapes the cold-start trap. Restricting that channel protects restaurants already advantaged by ratings and name recognition. And if platforms lose advertising revenue by making premium placement less valuable, they can recover it through commissions, subscriptions, or delivery charges.
[C1.5] Those costs also fall on small kitchens and price-sensitive users. PRO assumes the tollbooth disappears; the motion may merely move the toll.
[C1.6] Third, the threshold is unadministrable. How much scrolling counts as “struggle”? Is a restaurant cheaper before or after fees, portion size, discounts, or a reservation’s fixed menu? Does a two-location family business stop being independent?
[C1.7] A rule built on such unstable judgments invites paternalistic redesign rather than clear consumer benefit.
[C1.8] The precise safeguards against actual abuse are honest ad labels, no disguising sponsorship as organic rank, accessible price and distance filters, and user control. But PRO needs the broader claim that sponsored privilege becomes wrong once bargain discovery becomes inconvenient. That claim sacrifices advertising-funded discovery, ranking flexibility, and new entrants’ visibility to an undefined standard of scrolling. Reject it.
