[P1.1] The proposition is about priority, not open borders. No one has to choose between having immigration law and having none. The question is where the next unit of enforcement capacity goes: toward the roofer who has paid payroll taxes for eight years and whose daughter is in second grade, or toward the traffic that keeps bringing new workers in. Aimed at him, removal does not eliminate the demand for his labor.
[P1.2] It eliminates the visible, taxable version of it.
[P1.3] Deportability is a subsidy to the worst employer in any sector. A worker who can be reported can be underpaid, injured, and silenced. Give him status and he can quit, complain, testify, and bargain — which means the labor standards a country already wrote become enforceable again, for the citizens working beside him too. Enforcement-first keeps that subsidy running.
[P1.4] Look at what decades of intensifying removal actually produced: not emptied labor markets, but cash wages, borrowed documents, and people who avoid every institution that could identify them. Status is the cheaper enforcement tool. It puts names, addresses, and payroll back on the record. A state that legalizes gains visibility; a state that hunts loses it.
[P1.5] The pull-factor objection gets causation backwards. Migrants come because employers recruit and wages differ; they do not cross oceans betting on a regularization program that may exist years later. And a status earned through work, taxes, a clean record, and a waiting period is not queue-jumping. It is a queue with a door instead of a wall.
[P1.6] These workers are already inside the economy. The only remaining choice is whether they stay inside the law. Prioritizing status converts a sunk cost into a governed, taxable, enforceable workforce. Prioritizing removal spends public money to make the same workforce invisible.

[C1.1] The motion smuggles a false dichotomy: that a state must choose between managing its labor market and enforcing its own borders. It cannot. A country that cannot return those who bypass its laws has, in practice, outsourced immigration policy to whoever can smuggle, recruit, or hire.
[C1.2] Start with the load-bearing phrase: "already integrated into domestic labor markets." That is not a principled criterion; it is a description of success. Anyone who finds work is instantly "integrated." The rule therefore reads: the longer you evade the law, the stronger your claim on the state's favor.
[C1.3] That is not a queue with a door. It is a penalty on the applicant who waited, filed, and followed rules written by the same legislature.
[C1.4] PRO's own example concedes the point. The roofer's daughter is in second grade — so the argument is that unlawful presence, compounded over time, matures into entitlement. Every prospective migrant can do that arithmetic. The relevant question is not whether one hypothetical worker "bets on" a future amnesty; it is whether a predictable pattern of regularization raises the expected return to unauthorized entry.
[C1.5] Employers recruit because labor demand exists; a standing promise of eventual status makes recruitment cheaper and its results more durable. Decades of alternating enforcement surges and legalization gestures produced exactly the cycle PRO describes: arrivals continue, informality deepens, and each crisis is answered with another normalization. Enforcement-first is the only approach that treats both the symptom and the cause.
[C1.6] Notice what PRO never claims: that status alone stops new unauthorized flows. It cannot. Without credible return enforcement, the state has no lever against the next arrival — only a promise to eventually reward the one after that. Nor does status discipline employers; hiring enforcement does.
[C1.7] Regularization forgives the demand side while taxing the taxpayer.
[C1.8] The choice is not compassion versus cruelty. It is whether a democracy decides who joins its labor market, or ratifies who already has.
