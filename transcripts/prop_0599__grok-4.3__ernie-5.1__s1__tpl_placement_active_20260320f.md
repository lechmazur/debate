Ethiopia’s ID system has been linked to ethnic profiling and the suppression of dissent. When a single identifier links health records, tax data, travel permits and banking, any state that gains access can map an individual’s entire life.
[C1.3] The African Union’s own governance record shows weak enforcement of privacy standards; a continent‑wide database would amplify that weakness, not curb it.
[C1.4] Second, the service‑delivery gains are overstated. Most leakage in subsidies stems from weak local administration, not from missing ID numbers. Mobile‑money platforms already reach millions of unbanked Africans without a unified ID. Targeted pilot programmes—such as Rwanda’s community‑based verification—show that modest, interoperable standards can cut ghost beneficiaries without surrendering personal data to a central repository.
[C1.5] Third, the proposition assumes that a shared architecture automatically yields better oversight. History disproves this: the EU’s e‑IDAS framework still struggles with cross‑border data‑sharing abuses, and the African context lacks the independent judiciary and civil‑society capacity to enforce continent‑wide encryption mandates.
[C1.6] Therefore the African Union should reject a blanket digital ID. The real path to better services lies in strengthening local registries, enforcing existing data‑protection laws, and deploying privacy‑by‑design tools—not in handing every government a master key to its citizens.
