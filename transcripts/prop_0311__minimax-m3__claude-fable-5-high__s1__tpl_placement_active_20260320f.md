[P1.1] The opposition's framing rests on a quiet confusion: it treats the order in which couples do things as evidence of how seriously they take each other. That confusion is the heart of this debate, and it is wrong.
[P1.2] First, the descriptive point. Cohabitation before marriage is already the modal path to marriage across most Western societies, and rising in many others. When a behavior becomes the dominant route by which couples arrive at marriage, calling it a sign of weak commitment does not describe reality—it simply rebrands the majority as deficient. The proposition asks for accurate labeling, not endorsement of any lifestyle.
[P1.3] If "ordinary" is empirically true, refusing the word is a rhetorical, not a moral, position.
[P1.4] Second, the functional argument. Marriage is a high-cost, hard-to-reverse legal and personal contract. Sharing rent, sleep schedules, household labor, debt, and family dynamics before signing that contract is not a sign of low investment. It is what a thoughtful person would do.
[P1.5] A couple who discovers incompatible habits before marriage has spared two people a much worse outcome than breaking up. Treating that discovery as evidence of weak commitment inverts what commitment actually means: commitment is the willingness to test whether a union will work, not the refusal to look at the evidence.
[P1.6] Third, the harm of the alternative framing. "Weak commitment" is a moral verdict imposed on couples—often women, often those with fewer resources—who cohabit for ordinary reasons: pooling rent, sharing caregiving, navigating long-distance moves. That label adds no information; it only adds stigma. And stigma changes behavior.
[P1.7] Couples who feel they must hide cohabitation make worse decisions than couples who can discuss it openly with family, employers, and clergy.
[P1.8] The opposition's case requires the premise that the traditional sequence is itself the substance of commitment. Strip that premise away, and the rest collapses.
