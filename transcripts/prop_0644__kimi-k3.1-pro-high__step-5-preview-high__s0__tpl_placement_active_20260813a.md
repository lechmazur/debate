[P1.1] Emergency power is democracy’s most dangerous loan: granted quickly, secured by public fear, and repaid at the borrower’s convenience. Automatic sunset hearings and renewal votes change that bargain without preventing swift action. The executive can still declare an emergency and act immediately; after a fixed interval, broad powers expire unless officials publicly justify them and the legislature affirmatively renews them.
[P1.2] That design corrects a dangerous asymmetry. Declaring an emergency takes one decision; repealing emergency authority can require a new majority, scarce legislative time, and a fight with the very executive using the power. Inertia therefore protects government, not citizens. Automatic sunsets reverse the burden: continued extraordinary power, rather than restored liberty, must win approval.
[P1.3] Ordinary administration remains untouched; the requirement applies to broad emergency powers, for which self-restraint is an inadequate safeguard.
[P1.4] The hearing also creates the record a meaningful vote requires. What threat remains? Which powers were actually used, against whom, with what benefits and harms? Can targeted rules replace blanket authority?
[P1.5] A health crisis might justify temporary limits on gatherings without justifying unrelated surveillance or indefinite procurement exemptions. Renewal need not be binary theater: legislators can extend necessary powers, narrow their scope, or let unjustified ones lapse.
[P1.6] Speed is no answer. Initial action is untouched, and known deadlines allow legislatures to prepare expedited hearings and votes. A genuine, continuing emergency should make renewal easier to justify, not harder. If evidence is sensitive, officials can present the relevant case through appropriate safeguards; secrecy cannot become a blanket exemption from consent.
[P1.7] Elections alone are insufficient: broad emergency powers can transform daily life between elections, while fear discourages dissent. Democracies should not demand proof before a firefighter breaks down a burning door; they should demand proof before officials keep entering homes months later. Immediate action, periodic justification, affirmative consent: that is how democracy responds to emergencies without making emergency government its permanent operating system.

[C1.1] The motion treats a calendar as if it were a constitution. Sunsets and renewal votes sound like discipline, but they answer the wrong question. The danger in an emergency is not that broad power lacks an expiration date; it is that the legislature granted authority too broad to be safely used at all. A fixed renewal does not narrow that authority, improve its targeting, or prevent its misuse.
[C1.2] It merely guarantees that the same sweeping power will return, ceremonially legitimized, unless legislators happen to be braver at the deadline than they were at the grant. The most damaging emergency powers—suspension of normal procurement, movement restrictions, communications and data measures—often begin as temporary measures and become permanent through successive renewals rather than one bad law.
[C1.3] Nor does automatic renewal solve information problems; it can worsen them. Legislatures are asked to vote during peak crisis, with incomplete facts and maximum public pressure. The result is often reflexive extension: no government wants to appear to strip away protection when threat estimates are uncertain. Renewal can therefore manufacture consent rather than test it, while giving the executive an incentive to keep the threat description alarmingly vague.
[C1.4] The timing can be actively harmful. Crises do not move in neat intervals. Stop-start authority creates legal gaps, conflicting orders, and pressure to re-declare emergencies with broader powers than the expiring statute allowed. During a public-health emergency, an expiring power can force a choice between chaos and blanket renewal at precisely the moment when a narrow, evidence-based adjustment is needed.
[C1.5] Democracies already possess sharper tools: define powers narrowly at enactment, require legislative committees and courts to police scope, mandate reporting and audits, and make misuse politically and legally costly. Those controls discipline what government can do. Automatic sunsets too often discipline only when it must ask again. If a power is so broad that its renewal is dicey, the mistake was granting it.

[P2.3] [C1.2] then offers a strange indictment: emergency powers become permanent through “successive renewals.” Without automatic renewal, they can become permanent through no decision at all. CON prefers silent continuation to a public hearing and affirmative vote. Renewal can narrow, condition, or reject powers; inertia cannot. A legislature may rubber-stamp renewal, but the possibility of failure is no argument for making accountability optional.
[P2.4] The information objection also cuts the wrong way. [C1.3] describes crisis votes with incomplete facts and maximum pressure. That describes the original grant even more strongly. Later hearings have evidence the initial declaration lacked: which powers were used, against whom, and at what cost.
[P2.5] If executives keep threats vague, legislators can demand specifics and refuse unrelated authority. A rubber stamp is a possible failure; unreviewed executive power is CON’s built-in default.
[P2.6] Timing is overstated. Deadlines are known in advance, so votes can occur before expiry through expedited procedures. Nothing in the motion requires legal gaps, conflicting orders, or broader re-declarations; it requires a hearing and vote. A genuine emergency supplies reasons for renewal, not a reason to make consent optional.
[P2.7] Finally, [C1.5]’s “sharper tools” leave the decisive asymmetry intact. Courts usually respond after powers are used; committees can report and be ignored; initial drafting cannot anticipate every mutation of a crisis. Without a sunset, ending power requires legislators to defeat the official already wielding it. With one, continued power must earn consent.
[P2.8] CON asks democracies to trust better drafting and later courage. PRO creates a standing moment when government must explain itself. Emergency powers should be loans with due dates, not possessions surrendered only when someone finds the strength to take them back.

[P4.1] A1: I concede only that a sunset is not a drafting pen; I do not concede that breadth can be “fixed once and for all.” A power proportionate to the first week of a crisis may become excessive as facts and threats change. The hearing makes continuing breadth contestable using evidence of actual use, and the vote can narrow or condition it. CON’s drafting solution cannot anticipate every mutation, while courts and committees leave termination dependent on someone overcoming executive resistance.
[P4.2] A2: The sunset itself defeats clock-running: continuation requires affirmative approval, so executive delay produces expiry, not indefinite power. Legislatures know the deadline and can hold the hearing and vote before it, using expedited procedures. If they refuse blanket renewal, ordinary law resumes; they can renew only necessary provisions. “Chaos or blanket renewal” is therefore a political choice CON treats as a legal necessity. Automaticity removes the executive’s ability to preserve power merely by stalling.
[P4.3] That also answers the rubber-stamp objection. If fear guarantees ratification, CON’s legislative drafting, committees, and political costs are equally hostages to fear. Yet its alternative leaves the same executive power legally in force without the hearing, record, public responsibility, or opportunity to reject. A court facing silent statutory continuation has no obviously firmer footing than one facing renewed authority; claiming otherwise does not show why less legislative scrutiny is safer.
[P4.4] The decisive difference is who must act. Under CON, liberty returns only if legislators or judges successfully take power back. Under PRO, extraordinary power ends unless its continuation earns consent. A bad renewal remains possible; silent permanence is no longer the default.
[P4.5] Review is not a substitute for narrow drafting—it is the safeguard for everything initial drafting could not foresee.
[P4.6] CON predicts that legislators might fail and responds by requiring no decision at all. PRO preserves immediate emergency action while making continued exceptional authority publicly explain itself. A safeguard that can be rubber-stamped still beats an accountability regime whose default is silence.
