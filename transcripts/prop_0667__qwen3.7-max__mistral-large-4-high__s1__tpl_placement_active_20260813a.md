[P1.1] Mandatory nationwide E-Verify is the most effective, least discriminatory tool available to close the unauthorized labor loophole. Our case rests on three pillars: systemic efficacy, digital objectivity, and market adaptability.
[P1.2] First, E-Verify meaningfully reduces unauthorized employment by neutralizing document fraud. Currently, employers only visually inspect I-9 forms, making it trivial for unauthorized workers to use counterfeit Social Security cards or green cards. E-Verify cross-references new hire data directly with federal databases in real time. When the system flags a mismatch, employment cannot legally proceed.
[P1.3] This eliminates the plausible deniability that allows the shadow economy to thrive, acting as a powerful deterrent for both job seekers and employers.
[P1.4] Second, this system prevents large increases in discrimination; in fact, it actively reduces it. The opposition will likely argue that employers might profile applicants based on accent or ethnicity to avoid E-Verify mismatches. But E-Verify replaces subjective, human document inspection with an objective, digital check. A hiring manager no longer has to guess if an applicant’s paperwork is valid.
[P1.5] The system treats a naturalized citizen and a native-born citizen exactly the same: it simply checks the database. By removing human judgment from document verification, E-Verify neutralizes the implicit bias that currently drives hiring discrimination.
[P1.6] Third, mandatory E-Verify will not cause large-scale labor shortages. The U.S. economy is highly dynamic and resilient. While specific sectors relying heavily on unauthorized labor may experience transitional friction, the broader labor market rapidly adapts. Employers respond to tighter labor pools by raising wages to attract legal workers, investing in automation, or adjusting operational models.
[P1.7] Unauthorized workers comprise a relatively small fraction of the total workforce, and their roles are largely substitutable. The economy will absorb this shift without structural collapse, seamlessly replacing shadow labor with legally authorized, tax-paying employment.

[C1.1] The proposition imagines E-Verify as a frictionless filter that cleanly separates authorized workers from unauthorized ones. In reality, it is an error-prone database check that misfires on legal workers, drives unauthorized labor deeper underground, and destabilizes industries built on that workforce.
[C1.2] First, the discrimination claim collapses under database errors. E-Verify produces false positives for legal workers—naturalized citizens, married women who changed surnames, anyone with a clerical mismatch between documents and federal records. When employers face liability for hiring flagged workers, they rationally avoid interviewing candidates who "look risky." The system does not eliminate bias; it automates it, shifting discrimination from document inspection to pre-screening profiling based on accent, name, or ethnicity.
[C1.3] Second, mandatory E-Verify fails to meaningfully reduce unauthorized employment because it displaces rather than eliminates it. When formal employment becomes inaccessible, unauthorized workers shift to off-the-books cash work, day labor, or identity fraud. Compliance burdens fall hardest on small businesses, which may simply stop hiring formally altogether, expanding the underground economy rather than shrinking it.
[C1.4] Third, the labor shortage claim ignores basic economics. Agriculture, construction, and hospitality depend on unauthorized labor because domestic workers consistently refuse these specific jobs at prevailing wages. Mandatory E-Verify forces immediate wage spikes, automation costs, or crop losses. The claim that markets adapt seamlessly ignores sticky wages and razor-thin margins in labor-intensive sectors.
[C1.5] The proposition asks us to believe a government database can solve labor market complexity without breaking legal workers, pushing work underground, and disrupting food and housing supply chains. That is not evidence-based policy; it is a techno-optimist gamble with American workers as collateral damage.
