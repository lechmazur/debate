[P1.1] Smart education policy is decided at the margin, not on historical averages. High-achieving students bound for lucrative professions are already attending four-year universities. Expanding traditional enrollment subsidies today pulls in marginal students who are less academically prepared, alienated by classroom abstraction, or financially stretched. Pushing that group onto four-year campuses runs straight into diminishing returns: high dropout rates, years of foregone wages, and graduate underemployment.
[P1.2] Expanding paid youth apprenticeships and employer-linked pathways raises long-run earnings more because it fixes the exact failures of marginal college expansion.
[P1.3] First, paid pathways solve the completion and opportunity-cost trap. Even with generous tuition subsidies, traditional college requires young adults to sacrifice four years of full-time income while covering living costs. When financial strain or academic mismatch pushes a student out after three semesters, partial credits carry virtually zero market value, leaving only lost prime years. Paid apprenticeships compensate young people from day one, sustaining completion while generating immediate income, early savings, and years of workplace experience before peers even graduate.
[P1.4] Second, employer linkage guarantees genuine labor-market demand. Enrollment subsidies pay universities simply to fill seats, fueling institutional cost inflation and credential creep without ensuring good jobs await graduates. Too many marginal bachelor's holders end up in routine service or clerical roles that never required a degree. Employer-linked pathways flip those incentives: firms only invest wages and mentorship where well-paid talent shortages exist, from industrial mechatronics and grid infrastructure to clinical diagnostics and cybersecurity.
[P1.5] Employer skin in the game ensures skills match modern technology and connect directly to internal promotion ladders.
[P1.6] Finally, modern pathways do not trap workers below a ceiling; they launch adaptable careers. Starting at nineteen in a paid technical or business pathway builds transferable systems knowledge, portable credentials, and employer-sponsored upskilling, including later degrees, into supervisory and specialist roles. Trading high-risk, uncompensated seat time for paid, demand-driven career launches delivers higher completion and stronger long-run earnings.
