[P1.1] A four-day week asks children to learn nearly as much in less time, for savings that barely dent a district budget and a staffing patch that leaves the real problem untouched. Learning loss is the larger effect.
[P1.2] Time is not interchangeable. Stretching Monday through Thursday does not restore the day that vanished. Eight-year-olds cannot absorb an extra hour of math and call it even. Reading, arithmetic, and writing improve through regular practice, and they decay when that rhythm breaks.
[P1.3] That day also held the extra reading group and the teacher who caught a child slipping. Children with the least support at home absorb the loss; money buys tutoring, while others get screens or an empty house. A schedule that widens the gap has already failed.
[P1.4] The savings do not buy the damage back. Salaries dominate school budgets, and salaries continue. What shrinks is buses, cafeterias, and utility bills—a thin margin beside a year of weaker instruction. Parents who can leave often do, taking enrollment funding with them.
[P1.5] Catch-up programs then spend part of the “savings” repairing what the shorter week broke. Saving on fuel, then paying for recovery, merely shifts the bill.
[P1.6] Retention is the weaker defense. Teachers stay for pay, manageable classes, and respect, not because Friday is dark. A four-day schedule may draw applicants to a hard-to-staff rural post, but it lengthens the teaching day, tires students, and leaves the pay gap intact. The off-day becomes a second job or drift, not serious training.
[P1.7] If strong teachers still leave and families drift to five-day districts, the schedule undercuts the stability it promised.
[P1.8] Lost days compound and resist reversal; the savings and staffing relief do not. Districts can raise pay, share services, and target incentives without erasing a day of school. Four-day weeks harm learning more than they help.
