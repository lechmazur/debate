[P1.1] Give a family cash for childcare in a city with no childcare places, and you have not given them childcare. You have given them a more expensive bidding war for the same scarce slots. That is the core flaw in unconditional transfers: they hand people money to buy essential goods in markets that are broken, undersupplied, and perfectly positioned to swallow the subsidy.
[P1.2] Universal basic services solve the supply problem directly. Free or heavily subsidised transit creates the route. Public childcare creates the places and trains the staff. Public healthcare and broadband build the clinic and the network.
[P1.3] The state is not hoping a market will respond eventually, at the right price, in the right postcode. It guarantees the thing people actually need.
[P1.4] They are also better value for the same public money. One bus network, one broadband rollout, one negotiated system for medicines and childcare wages costs far less per person than thousands of households separately buying cars, private data plans, nannies, and insurance, each paying marketing, profit, and administrative overhead. Cash disperses purchasing power. Services pool it, and pooled power buys more health, more mobility, and more time.
[P1.5] That pooling is what makes the floor real and equal. Cash preserves the hierarchy of the market: the better-off top it up and buy the safer childcare, the faster connection, the doctor who sees them sooner, while the poorest make do with the cheapest failing version. A universal service sets a common standard everyone can rely on, and because everyone uses it, everyone has a stake in defending its quality.
[P1.6] Cash sounds like freedom, but freedom to queue for a service that does not exist, at a price that just rose, is not freedom. Services expand what people can actually do: get to work, learn, raise children, and get treated without first winning in the market. For essentials, governments should build the guarantee, not just fund the gamble.

[C1.1] You cannot pay an eviction notice or buy groceries with a free bus pass. Economic insecurity is a crisis of liquidity and wildly varied bottlenecks, yet expanding universal basic services locks public money into rigid, one-size-fits-none silos. A childless tenant gains nothing from free daycare; a night-shift warehouse worker cannot use nine-to-five municipal nurseries; a rural caregiver miles from a bus route gets zero mobility from urban transit. Unconditional cash solves that mismatch instantly.
[C1.2] Because money is fungible, every dollar goes straight to each household’s sharpest need: rent, heating, food, car repair, or paying a trusted relative for evening childcare.
[C1.3] Nor does state expansion conjure scarce supply out of thin air [P1.2]. With the same budget, governments face the same shortages of nurses, caregivers, and builders, except public funds first bleed into slow procurement, construction overruns, and monopoly bureaucracies insulated from competition, erasing theoretical pooling savings [P1.4]. Worse, making services free for everyone surges demand from affluent households who previously paid privately, replacing prices with clinic waiting lists and daycare lotteries. Unconditional cash avoids single-market bidding wars [P1.1]: unlike earmarked vouchers, families spread spending across competitive essentials, debt relief, and flexible kin or home-based care, while steady purchasing power draws nimble providers into underserved areas with minimal administrative leakage.
[C1.4] Finally, universal service expansion is quietly regressive under a fixed budget. Salaried, urban households live nearest to transit corridors, work standard hours, navigate bureaucratic queues best, and still opt out into private cars and care when public systems clog, undercutting the claim that everyone defends public quality [P1.5]. An equal cash transfer cannot be captured by postcode or shift pattern, and every citizen fiercely defends a visible check. Cash delivers transformative proportional power at the bottom, giving people real agency today [P1.6] rather than forcing them to wait years for planners to build the wrong service in the wrong place [P1.3].

[P5.3] Services make the opposite choice with the same money. Pooled, that money does not ask each family to outbid neighbours for a nursery place, a clinic, or a bus route that may not exist.
