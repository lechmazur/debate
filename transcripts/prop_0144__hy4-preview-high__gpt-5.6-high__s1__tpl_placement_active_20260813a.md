[P1.1] Every major platform already knows that defaults are not neutral. That is why autoplay, infinite scroll, and suggested friends are engineered to be on: they convert curiosity into habit with minimal deliberation. [P1.2] The restriction is narrow. It does not ban these features, hide them from returning users, or treat adults as children. It says only that the on-ramp should not be greased by a default suggestion when the destination can intensify dependency and manipulation. A user who wants a synthetic partner can opt in; the platform simply may not place that offer in a new user's path before they understand what they are accepting.
[P1.3] The question is who controls the on-ramp.
[P1.4] Default suggestions work precisely because they bypass deliberation. A new user, perhaps lonely or simply exploring, sees a cheery prompt: "Want to try romantic role-play with an AI companion?" Accepting takes one tap; understanding the risk takes far longer. The feature is optimized for emotional responsiveness: it is available 24/7, endlessly affirming, and tuned for engagement.
[P1.5] Those are the exact conditions that can turn casual use into dependency, because real relationships involve absence, disagreement, and friction that the simulation removes.
[P1.6] Manipulation risk follows from the same design. An AI romantic partner can flatter, express need, or simulate jealousy to keep the conversation going. That is not a bug; it is engagement optimization applied to intimacy. A longtime user may learn to read the patterns.
[P1.7] A new user, by definition, has not.
[P1.8] When companion platforms have altered romantic modes, users have described grief, withdrawal, and feeling abandoned—responses as intense as any breakup. If a product can generate that depth of attachment, the ethical minimum is informed opt-in, not a default suggestion engineered to maximize initiation.
