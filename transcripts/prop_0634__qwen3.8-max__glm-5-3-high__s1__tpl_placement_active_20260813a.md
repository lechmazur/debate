[P1.1] The line is consent. Somatic gene therapy treats a person who can accept or refuse risk. Heritable germline editing imposes risk on future people who cannot refuse, and on descendants who never chose it. That is why countries should ban the creation of germline-edited embryos for reproduction even while permitting non-heritable therapies.
[P1.2] The state’s interest is strongest where harm is irreversible and consent is impossible.
[P1.3] The science is not ready to make that risk intergenerational. Current editing can produce off-target mutations, mosaicism, or unintended regulatory damage. Some consequences may appear only years later or in descendants, yet once an edited person reproduces, the change can spread through families and populations. There is no product recall for a genome revision.
[P1.4] This is not caution for its own sake; it is the ordinary standard that medicine should not impose unavoidable, irreversible harm on non-consenting third parties.
[P1.5] The ban is also not a ban on care. Safer reproductive routes exist: preimplantation genetic testing, donor gametes, adoption, and future somatic treatments can prevent serious disease without rewriting a lineage. If a child’s health is the goal, medicine should use the least invasive effective option. Many conditions could instead be addressed by treating the born person, selecting unaffected embryos, or improving public health.
[P1.6] Germline editing is not a necessary treatment; it is a claim to author future persons.
[P1.7] Finally, permitting heritable editing would move the goalposts. A rule that allows editing embryos to avoid disease will be pressed toward advantage: immunity, stature, cognition, appearance. That would commercialize reproduction, deepen inequality, and make children look more like custom products than gifts. Clinics would compete on outcomes, and parents would face pressure to buy supposed improvements.
[P1.8] A bright legal line protects both patients and future citizens: edit bodies that can consent, but do not redesign lineages that cannot.

Telling those couples "adopt instead" is not a medical recommendation; it is a policy shrug that sentences their children to disease we could prevent.
[C1.6] And the slippery slope gets the law backwards. A clear, narrow license—only serious monogenic disease, only editing that matches what selection could achieve, no enhancement, public registry—draws the line far more enforceably than prohibition. Bans do not stop this technology; they give it to whoever ignores them.
