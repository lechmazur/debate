When an agency suspends a benefit, a visa, or a fraud claim because a model spat out a flag and no authorized human tested that guess, it is not administering law; it is automating suspicion.
[P1.2] The state has taken someone’s food, status, or money on a correlation it cannot explain and will not defend.
[P1.3] The motion draws the only line that matches the harm: government deprivation must rest on individualized evidence and accountable judgment. [P1.4] Fraud-prevention and backlog worries do not rescue that. Algorithms can allocate scarce human attention; they cannot substitute for it. [P1.5] Consider an unreviewed flag in practice. A caregiver’s benefits stop, rent comes due, and eviction starts before any caseworker hears the story. A student’s visa is pulled over a financial anomaly while the model’s training data stays secret. False positives are not abstract; they hit people with the least cushion, and correction is hardest.
[P1.6] The state, not the individual, should bear the cost of algorithmic uncertainty. The motion requires no suspension from a machine’s hunch until a human with real responsibility examines the case. That is not anti-technology. It is the minimum condition for a government that must justify itself to the people it governs.
