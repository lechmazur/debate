[P1.1] The proposition asks us to reject a false choice between managed decline and honest resignation. Permanent metropolitan concentration is not an economic law; it is the cumulative result of policy decisions that treat cities as default beneficiaries and rural regions as afterthoughts. When we say governments "accept" concentration as inevitable, we are really describing governments that stop investing in broadband, regional transport, and remote-work infrastructure, then act surprised when young people leave.
[P1.2] The economic case for intervention is not nostalgia. Rural regions contain the productive assets that metropolitan areas depend on: food systems, energy corridors, water catchments, and increasingly, the physical space for data centers and renewable generation. But these assets cannot generate local prosperity when policy systematically funnels capital, talent, and connectivity toward already-dense hubs. A town with gigabit broadband, a regional university branch, and a rail link to a city is not a museum piece; it is a viable economic unit that current policy has chosen to underdevelop.
[P1.3] Concentration also creates systemic fragility. When supply chains, housing markets, and labor pools are geographically concentrated, shocks propagate faster and recover slower. The pandemic demonstrated this: remote work did not kill cities, but it proved that productivity no longer requires physical proximity to a central business district. That insight is an opportunity, not a threat—if governments build the infrastructure to distribute opportunity rather than hoarding it.
[P1.4] Finally, there is the democratic cost. When governments write off entire regions, they do not merely lose GDP; they lose citizens' faith that the state works for them. Regional abandonment is fertile ground for political alienation, and the price of that alienation is paid in national capitals, not just in emptying high streets.
[P1.5] The choice is not between propping up failure and embracing efficiency. It is between actively shaping where prosperity lives, or pretending that the map we inherited is the only map possible. Governments should do more, because the alternative is not inevitability—it is neglect disguised as realism.
