[P1.1] Tax privileges for religious organizations rest on a public bargain: we forgo revenue because these institutions primarily serve spiritual and charitable ends the community values. That bargain collapses when they operate as overt partisan political actors. They are then not churches doing politics on the side. They are political machines using a religious wrapper to extract a public subsidy.
[P1.2] Overt partisanship is not a sermon on justice or a moral claim about the common good. It is institutional endorsement of candidates, turning offering plates into campaign infrastructure, and using tax-exempt status to fund get-out-the-vote work for one party. A congregation feeding the hungry is one thing; a congregation running a nominee's turnout shop is another. Other political groups cannot claim that deal.
[P1.3] A PAC lives under PAC rules. A charity that becomes a campaign should too.
[P1.4] Keeping the advantage after the function has changed is not neutrality. It is a transfer: citizens of every faith and none underwrite electioneering they may oppose, while the organization enjoys deductible donations and property-tax relief denied to its political competitors. The result is distorted elections and a race to capture pulpits as cheap campaign offices. Tax law already asks other nonprofits what they primarily do.
[P1.5] Religious groups should not be the unique exception once partisan campaigning has become the operating mode.
[P1.6] The proposition does not police prayer or silence conscience. It asks that the tax code follow reality. When an organization is primarily charitable or spiritual, the advantages remain. When it functions as an overt partisan actor, those advantages end.
[P1.7] That preserves the integrity of charity and the fairness of politics. Anything else pretends a campaign committee is a sanctuary because the stationery still says church.

[C1.1] Tax exemption for churches is not a tip we hand out for good charitable service. It is a boundary that keeps the state from using the tax code to manage worship. [C1.2] That danger is not abstract because "overt partisan political actor" and "primarily charitable or spiritual" cannot be neutrally measured. Is a Black church hosting a candidate to speak about voting rights partisan? Is a Catholic parish condemning one party's platform from the altar spiritual? Is voter registration charity or campaigning?
[C1.3] An IRS examiner would have to parse theology, count sermons, weigh motives. No legal standard survives that without becoming viewpoint policing.
[C1.4] And the policing will never fall evenly. Popular, well-lawyered denominations will frame their politics as moral teaching. Small storefront churches, mosques, and dissenting sects will be labeled campaign operations. The civil-rights movement was run out of churches that endorsed candidates, raised money, and turned souls to the polls.
[C1.5] The abolitionist pulpit was fiercely partisan in its day. Under this rule, the state could have bankrupted them for confusing sanctuary with campaign office.
[C1.6] Stripping all tax advantages is also wildly disproportionate. Losing property-tax exemption and deductible contributions does not just clip a campaign budget; it closes the food pantry, the shelter, the after-school program housed in the same building, for the same congregation. Punish the electioneering if you must through narrow election law, but do not burn down the charity to scold the sermon.
[C1.7] Freedom means a church can be wrong, loud, and political without paying rent to the state for its conscience.
