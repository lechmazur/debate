[P1.1] Picture two brothers. Their mother has dementia. One rearranges his life, drains his savings, and pays for her care. The other stops answering the phone.
[P1.2] Under current law in most places, the second brother wins. He keeps his money, keeps his weekends, and his mother's shortfall lands on his brother or on the taxpayer. That is the status quo we are asked to defend: a system in which the only people who bear the cost of aging parents are the ones decent enough to volunteer.
[P1.3] The proposition is not that families should be squeezed while the state walks away. It is that a duty which is already morally real and already unevenly performed should be legally enforceable, so that it falls fairly rather than on whoever cares most.
[P1.4] Three reasons it should be law.
[P1.5] First, consistency. We already accept that a legally enforceable maintenance duty can run between family members. Child support is exactly this: the state does not simply absorb the cost of a child whose parent has money and refuses to pay. The dependency runs the other direction with an aging parent, but the logic is identical — a specific person is dependent, a specific person has the means, and the law decides whether means must answer need.
[P1.6] Second, fairness among siblings and among citizens. Enforceability turns care from a trap for the conscientious into a shared obligation, and it stops wealthy adult children from routing their parents onto public assistance while preserving inheritances they expect to receive.
[P1.7] Third, design does the work my opponent will attack. A defensible duty is means-tested on the child's actual capacity, capped as a proportion of income, proportioned among siblings, and forfeited where a parent abandoned or abused the child. The state remains the backstop for those who genuinely cannot pay — it simply stops being the first resort for those who can.
[P1.8] The question is not whether someone pays. Someone always pays. The question is whether the law lets the willing be exploited and the unwilling walk.

[C1.1] The child-support analogy is the load-bearing wall of this proposal, so start there. Child support rests on a clear causal chain: two adults chose to bring a child into the world, creating a dependency those adults are responsible for. The child had no say. Adult children did not create their parents' old age.
[C1.2] They did not decide when their parents retired, how they saved, or whether they managed their health. A duty you did not create and cannot prevent is not a maintenance obligation; it is a penalty for being related to someone who needs help.
[C1.3] This matters because enforceability turns family into a liability pool. Consider two adults with identical incomes. One has parents who saved adequately. The other has parents who did not.
[C1.4] Under this proposal, the second person owes money the first keeps — not because of anything either did, but because of decisions their parents made decades ago. That is not fairness. It is a tax on the unlucky, collected from income, owed to a parent.
[C1.5] The state is the right party to backstop old-age need precisely because it spreads risk across the whole population. Pensions and means-tested assistance exist because old-age poverty is a systemic risk, not a family defect. Routing it through individual children reintroduces the arbitrariness the welfare state was designed to eliminate.
[C1.6] The promised safeguards — means-testing, caps, abuse exceptions — are not reassurances; they are confessions of the problem. Means-testing the child requires investigating their finances, their spouse's finances, and their siblings'. Abuse exceptions require adjudicating decades-old conduct in family court. The intrusiveness and cost of enforcing this duty is itself a reason to reject it.
[C1.7] The willing should not be exploited. But the answer is better public support, not conscripting whoever the parent happened to raise.

[C2.1] The spousal analogy [P2.1] collapses on contact. Spouses choose each other and construct a joint life; that voluntary undertaking grounds mutual maintenance. You do not choose your parents. And if child support attaches to "the relationship" rather than fault, that proves the wrong thing — the parent-child relationship is one where the parent owed care *to* the child.
[C2.2] Parenting is not a loan. If eighteen years of being fed and housed creates a repayable debt, then parents were discharging an obligation, not making an investment in a future debtor.
[C2.3] The millionaire challenge [P2.3] is a morality test dressed as a legal argument. Yes, a wealthy child who ignores a destitute parent acts badly. But law and morality diverge constantly — we don't criminalize ingratitude, and we don't fine people for being cruel to relatives in ways that fall short of abuse. The proper response to a millionaire who won't help his mother is a tax system that funds dignified old-age care for everyone, not a court order that makes the quality of your childhood a legally adjudicated debt.
[C2.4] On the "invisible burden" [P2.4–P2.5]: PRO says the nearby daughter already pays. True — and the answer is public support that relieves her, not a legal mechanism that drags her siblings into court. Enforceability doesn't make the caring daughter's burden lighter; it adds litigation, financial investigation, and family conflict on top of the care she already provides.
[C2.5] The inheritance point [P2.6] is an estate-tax problem. If bequests should contribute to old-age care, tax them at the source. Don't construct an entire care-duty regime — with all its intrusion and enforcement cost — to solve what estate policy can address cleanly and universally.
