[P1.1] When a family searches for flights today, the initial comparison screen is a work of fiction. Airlines and booking sites advertise rock-bottom base fares to win the top search slot, only to ambush parents three screens later with steep fees for bags and sitting next to their own young children. Industry defenders call this "unbundling," pretending these are optional upgrades. For a solo flyer on an overnight trip, a bag or seat assignment might be optional.
[P1.2] For parents traveling with young kids, bringing luggage and sitting together are not luxury perks—they are baseline necessities. Calling a fare "comparable" when it excludes the cost of keeping a five-year-old from sitting alone between strangers twelve rows away is pure deception.
[P1.3] This drip pricing breaks market competition. By hiding the practical cost until after families have spent twenty minutes entering names and birthdates, booking platforms exploit cognitive fatigue. Once a parent reaches checkout, comparing that inflated total against other flights requires repeating the entire tedious process across multiple tabs. This rigs the market: the airline with the most aggressive bait-and-switch wins the customer over a competitor offering a cheaper actual total.
[P1.4] Honest carriers that include bags or family seating in a straightforward fare are actively punished by algorithms that rank flights strictly by a stripped-down teaser rate.
[P1.5] Showing the real cost before comparison is technologically effortless. Booking sites already ask users to input the number of adults and children's ages before running a search. Adding a simple upfront prompt for baggage and adjacent seating lets the software calculate the true all-in total on the very first results grid, rather than forcing parents to play detective across five checkout screens. Airlines can still sell stripped-down fares to solo travelers who fly light, but when families compare options, platforms must compete on the price parents actually have to pay.

[C1.1] Start with the phrase doing all the work: "the full practical cost." Whose practical cost? A family of four with a baby might carry one checked bag; a family going skiing checks six. One household insists on a window row together; another is happy anywhere in the same cabin.
[C1.2] There is no single number that is "the" practical cost of a family trip, which means the mandate requires someone—a regulator, or a platform fearing liability—to decide what families need and bake it into every price. That is not transparency. That is a bundle, re-imposed through the display screen.
[C1.3] And bundles are exactly what made flying expensive. The reason a parent can sometimes fly four people for the price of two a generation ago is that carriers stripped out what many passengers never used. If the headline comparison must include bags and seat fees, the market signal shifts: carriers get no reward for cutting the base fare, because the ranked number is the padded one. The predictable result is that the stripped-down fare quietly disappears, and the family that actually travels light pays for luggage it never checked.
[C1.4] Then there is the problem of knowing the number at all. Seat prices vary by row, by date, by how full the plane is; many airlines seat young children next to a parent at no charge at check-in rather than at purchase. A booking site forced to display an "all-in" total before the search has even run must guess—and a guessed total presented as the real price is more misleading than a base fare with a clearly labeled fee table beside it.
[C1.5] Picture the carrier that seats families together for free being ranked below a rival because the platform's default assumption charged it for seats.
[C1.6] The honest answer to drip pricing is already visible: standardized fee disclosure, side-by-side fee tables, and user-set filters like "include one checked bag." Those tools let parents define their own trip. The motion replaces that choice with an assumption, and dresses the assumption up as the truth.
