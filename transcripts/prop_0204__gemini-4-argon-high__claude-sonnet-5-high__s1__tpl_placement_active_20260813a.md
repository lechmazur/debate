[P1.1] Critical minerals—lithium, cobalt, rare earths, gallium—are the foundational inputs of twenty-first-century military power and economic sovereignty. Without them, a nation cannot build precision-guided munitions, advanced radar, semiconductors, or resilient energy grids. Because commercially viable deposits are geographically concentrated and new mines take a decade or more to develop, control over critical-mineral assets is control over an irreplaceable strategic choke point.
[P1.2] Firms closely tied to rival states do not acquire these choke points purely for commercial return. Whether through state ownership, subsidized financing, or domestic laws that compel private companies to obey state intelligence and security directives, these firms act as instruments of geopolitical strategy. We already know how rival states use mineral dominance: when diplomatic tensions rise, export restrictions on rare earths, graphite, and chipmaking metals are swiftly deployed to coerce adversaries and cripple competing industries. Allowing rival-linked firms to buy up crucial mines and processing facilities willingly hands a competitor the off-switch to our defense industrial base.
[P1.3] This reality demands a presumptive block, not a traditional review where the government bears the burden of proof. When regulators must prove that one isolated transaction poses an imminent threat, they are structurally blind to cumulative market capture. It is nearly impossible to prove in advance that a commercial acquisition today will result in diverted off-take agreements, hollowed-out local processing, or deliberate supply cutoffs during a future crisis. Yet once ownership and operational control transfer, the strategic loss is practically irreversible.
[P1.4] A presumptive block corrects this fatal asymmetry. It does not create an unthinking absolute ban; instead, it sets the default to rejection and shifts the burden of proof onto the acquirer. Unless a rival-linked firm can conclusively prove that a transaction confers zero governance control, technology access, or off-take leverage, the deal stops. When the physical inputs of national survival are on the auction block, the default must be security, not trust.
