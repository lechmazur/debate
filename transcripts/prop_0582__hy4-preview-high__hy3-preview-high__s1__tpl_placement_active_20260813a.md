[P1.1] The clash is a simple race: the price Medicare pays for GLP-1s versus the cost of obesity-driven care those drugs prevent in the same patients. Coverage wins because, in the Medicare population, preventable costs are enormous, arrive quickly, and fall on the highest-cost patients in American medicine.
[P1.2] Consider who qualifies. Medicare covers adults over 65, the group with the highest concentration of obesity complicated by type 2 diabetes, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, sleep apnea, and advanced osteoarthritis. These are current, expensive realities. A single heart-failure hospitalization, stroke rehab stay, or year of dialysis can cost tens of thousands of dollars—often more than a full year of GLP-1 therapy.
[P1.3] In patients with obesity and established cardiovascular disease, a large outcomes trial of semaglutide found roughly a 20% reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events over three years, on top of 10–15% weight loss and better glucose and blood pressure control. Trials in kidney disease and sleep apnea point the same direction.
[P1.4] Critics will quote list prices around $1,000 a month. That is the wrong price and the wrong population. Over five years, Medicare will not pay list: it negotiates, rebates are large, new agents and competitors are arriving, and real-world use concentrates on patients with the highest BMI and comorbidity burden. Set that negotiated net cost against high-risk beneficiaries who generate far above-average acute and post-acute spending.
[P1.5] In the top-risk group—obesity plus diabetes, CKD, or prior cardiovascular events—avoiding even a modest number of heart attacks, strokes, heart-failure admissions, joint replacements, or dialysis transitions over five years produces savings larger than the drug bill. Every avoided dialysis year alone can exceed a year of treatment.
[P1.6] Coverage also cuts downstream pharmacy and outpatient costs: less insulin, fewer antihypertensives, reduced sleep-apnea care, fewer orthopedic interventions. The net effect over the five-year window is lower total US healthcare spending once drug costs are included, because Medicare's highest-cost patients stop generating as much catastrophic, billable care.
