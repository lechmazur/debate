[P1.1] A noncompete clause is a private restraint on a person’s ability to earn a living, backed by court orders. For most workers it is not a real bargain: it arrives after the job is accepted, in a stack of onboarding forms, with no separate pay and little chance to negotiate. Governments already restrict contractual restraints that harm third parties and markets. Labor is not a special employer carve-out.
[P1.2] The core problem is that noncompetes turn ordinary employment into economic captivity. A nurse, a maintenance technician, a hair stylist, a junior engineer, or a warehouse supervisor cannot leave a bad or lower-paying job if leaving means sitting out of their field, relocating, or risking a lawsuit. Broad employer exceptions make this the norm, because employers classify nearly everyone as “key” or as holding “confidential information.” Even a weak clause chills hiring: the next firm may avoid hiring rather than pay legal fees.
[P1.3] Vague case-by-case “reasonableness” leaves workers bearing the risk.
[P1.4] That harms workers and competitive markets together. Mobility is how workers convert skills into higher wages; when employers face less competition for labor, they can underpay and underinvest in conditions. Mobility also creates new firms. Startups often come from skilled people leaving incumbents; a thick web of noncompetes protects incumbents rather than innovation.
[P1.5] Employers do not need such broad exceptions. Trade secret law already stops misuse of confidential information. Non-disclosure agreements stop disclosure. Non-solicitation agreements can protect customer relationships.
[P1.6] Paid garden leave can handle the rare sensitive transition. Those tools match the remedy to the harm. A noncompete bans competition itself, usually without compensation.
[P1.7] Governments should therefore presume noncompetes for nearly all workers are void or unenforceable, allowing only narrow executive or sale-of-business exceptions that are paid, time-limited, and tied to identifiable secrets. That puts the burden on employers, not workers, and keeps labor markets open. It is both pro-worker and pro-market.

[C1.3] The safer path is not blanket prohibition but enforceable boundaries. That preserves the useful part of noncompetes: they make knowledge-sharing possible. [C1.4] The workers who lose are precisely those PRO says it protects.
[C1.5] PRO’s alternatives are weaker than they sound. NDAs and trade-secret law are reactive, costly, and often useless once an employee has embedded tacit knowledge in a competitor’s team. [C1.6] Broad exceptions allow the law to ask the right question: is this restraint reasonably necessary? A near-total ban answers a different question: do we distrust employers so much that we forbid a common contracting tool even when negotiated, compensated, and limited?
