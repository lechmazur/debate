[P1.1] The retirement shortfall in most advanced economies is not a subsidy problem; it is a participation problem. So the real question tonight is which lever reaches the households that actually lack financial security—and on that question the comparison is not close.
[P1.2] The least-secure households are disproportionately young, lower-paid, and employed where enrollment means paperwork nobody finishes. Raising the deduction for voluntary contributions does little for them, for two reasons. First, a deduction's value scales with the marginal tax rate: a high earner gets thirty-plus cents on the dollar, while a low-income worker with minimal tax liability gets nearly nothing.
[P1.3] Second, much of the response to bigger deductions is asset shifting—moving existing savings into tax-advantaged accounts rather than creating new saving. Expanding deductions mostly pays people who were already saving to keep doing it: an expensive way to buy almost no additional security.
[P1.4] Automatic enrollment attacks the real bottleneck directly. The same inertia that keeps people from enrolling keeps them enrolled once the default flips. Plans that adopt auto-enrollment typically see participation rise from around two-thirds of workers to around nine in ten, with low opt-out rates, and the gains are largest among exactly the young and lower-paid workers that voluntary systems miss. The opt-out preserves autonomy fully; nobody is forced to save.
[P1.5] The policy simply stops making non-saving the path of least resistance.
[P1.6] Then the long run compounds the advantage. A worker defaulted in at twenty-three gains contributions, employer matches, and decades of compounding; a worker who means to open an IRA every April and postpones until thirty-five never recovers those lost years, however generous the deduction eventually becomes. Time in the market is the one input no tax break can retrofit.
[P1.7] The motion is comparative—deductions need not be worthless, only weaker. Deductions reward decisions already made; defaults change decisions. Long-run household security depends on turning non-savers into savers early, and only one of these policies does that.

[C1.2] Security is measured in dollars accumulated, debt avoided, and liquidity available when the car dies. On every one of those, the default lever is far weaker than its headline numbers suggest.
[C1.3] Start with what auto-enrollment actually does to contributions. Defaults are sticky in both directions. Workers who would have chosen eight or ten percent anchor down to the employer's default, typically three to six percent. Participation goes up; average saving among existing savers goes down.
[C1.4] The net effect on total retirement wealth is a fraction of the participation jump PRO celebrates.
[C1.5] Then look at where the "new" savings come from. Low-income households are liquidity-constrained. Money shifted into an account that penalizes withdrawal before retirement does not vanish from their budget; it reappears as credit-card balances, payday loans, and missed emergency cushions. Automatically diverting cash from a household paying twenty-plus percent interest into an account earning six is a wealth transfer to lenders, not security.
[C1.6] And when that worker changes jobs, the small default balance is frequently cashed out with penalties, erasing the effect entirely. Leakage is the quiet hole in the auto-enrollment story.
[C1.7] PRO's long-run compounding argument cuts against them. Compounding rewards the size of contributions, not the mere act of enrolling. Raising deductions raises the after-tax return to every marginal dollar, which moves the intensive margin—how much people save—where the bulk of lifetime wealth is actually built. Yes, the benefit scales with tax rates; that is also precisely where voluntary saving is elastic and where retirement shortfalls, as opposed to Social Security replacement, actually occur.
[C1.8] For the lowest earners, Social Security replaces most of pre-retirement income anyway; the households genuinely at risk of a cliff in old age are middle earners who under-save, and price incentives reach them.
[C1.9] Defaults change who is counted. Deductions change how much is there.
