[P1.1] Anonymous shell companies are not a neutral financial instrument. They are infrastructure for harm—purpose-built to place criminal wealth, corrupt proceeds, and evaded taxes beyond the reach of any government, creditor, or victim. The proposition asks regulators to close that infrastructure permanently and universally. The case for doing so is overwhelming.
[P1.2] Start with scale. Rough estimates from organizations tracking illicit finance consistently put global money laundering in the trillions annually. A significant share moves through layered shell structures specifically because no single jurisdiction can identify the human being who controls them. Dictators park looted assets in anonymous vehicles.
[P1.3] Sanctions evaders use them to keep operating after the world has decided they should not. Drug traffickers use them to convert cash into real estate. In every case, the anonymous shell is not incidental—it is the mechanism.
[P1.4] The mechanism works because it exploits jurisdictional fragmentation. A Delaware LLC holds a British Virgin Islands company that owns a Cayman trust. Each layer is technically legal. No single regulator sees the full chain.
[P1.5] The only fix is universal: if every jurisdiction requires disclosure of ultimate beneficial ownership, the chain cannot be assembled. A partial ban creates a race to the bottom, where secrecy simply migrates to the least-compliant offshore center. Universal application is not an optional feature of this policy—it is the policy.
[P1.6] Opponents will argue that beneficial ownership registries threaten legitimate privacy. This objection misidentifies the problem. Transparency does not mean broadcasting personal details to the public indiscriminately; it means that regulators, law enforcement, and financial institutions can identify who actually controls an entity. Business confidentiality and personal safety can be protected through tiered access.
[P1.7] What cannot be protected is the right to conduct financial activity through structures deliberately designed to evade accountability.
[P1.8] The harm is concrete, the mechanism is understood, and the solution is proportionate. The only question is whether regulators will act uniformly—and they should.
