[P1.1] Courts should presumptively seal juveniles’ digital records once they reach adulthood because the law’s central premise for juvenile justice is that children are not finished adults. They are more impulsive, more susceptible to peer pressure, and more capable of reform. If that premise is true, then the state should not allow the digital residue of adolescent conduct—filings, videos, social-media exhibits, docket entries—to follow a person into adulthood as a permanent badge of criminality. That premise should govern not only sentencing, but the lifespan of the record itself.
[P1.2] The problem is not merely that records exist; it is that digital records are searchable, copyable, and detachable from context. A seventeen-year-old’s reckless act can live in a court database, a shared police file, or an online archive long after the sentence ends. When employers, landlords, schools, or lenders encounter that record, they often do not see a rehabilitated adult; they see one adolescent moment. That converts a temporary intervention into a lifelong barrier.
[P1.3] Sealing at adulthood restores the limit that juvenile justice is supposed to have.
[P1.4] A presumption is the right tool because it is neither automatic erasure nor permanent exposure. The default shifts toward confidentiality, while leaving room to unseal where the need is extraordinary: ongoing litigation, serious violence, or a specific public-safety justification. Courts already manage sealed and confidential materials; they can make these decisions with process, notice, and records preserved where necessary. Sealing can also preserve limited law-enforcement access without making the record public property.
[P1.5] The alternative leaves the burden on young adults to petition, hire lawyers, and prove they deserve to move on.
[P1.6] The decisive question is whether a justice system that treats youth as less culpable should also let youth become permanent. If the purpose of juvenile adjudication is correction rather than lifelong branding, courts should seal these digital records by default when the person reaches adulthood.

[C1.1] The juvenile justice premise is right: children are not finished adults. But that premise cuts against this motion, not for it. If adolescents are unfinished, then the moment they turn eighteen tells us nothing about whether they have finished changing. A presumption keyed to a birthday seals the record of a seventeen-and-a-half-year-old six months after the offense, before probation ends, before anyone knows whether correction worked.
[C1.2] It rewards the calendar, not reform.
[C1.3] The real alternative to presumptive sealing is not permanent branding. It is earned sealing: a period of clean conduct, an individualized look at the offense, and a judge who confirms the young adult has become who the system hoped. Most jurisdictions already offer this. PRO wants to skip the confirmation step and treat the graffiti case and the armed carjacking identically by default, then shift the burden onto victims and prosecutors to re-litigate why openness matters.
[C1.4] That is not a modest procedural tweak; it is a decision that seriousness of harm stops counting the instant the offender ages.
[C1.5] Consider who else needs these records. A domestic-violence victim seeking a protective order against a twenty-year-old with a juvenile assault history. A sentencing judge deciding whether an adult defendant's offense is a first stumble or the fourth in a pattern. Researchers and journalists trying to expose whether a county's juvenile court treats Black children more harshly than white ones.
[C1.6] Sealed data means invisible disparities. Judicial abuse of children has historically come to light because records could be examined; a default of secrecy protects institutions as much as individuals.
[C1.7] Finally, sealing "digital records" promises what courts cannot deliver. Screenshots, news stories, and reposted videos live outside the docket. Sealing the court's authoritative, contextualized file leaves those decontextualized fragments as the only surviving version. PRO's own worry in [P1.2], the record detached from context, is made worse when the one document that supplies the context vanishes.
[C1.8] Keep sealing available. Make it earned. Do not make it automatic.

Sealing the authoritative record removes the source rather than chasing every downstream misuse.
[P4.4] CON’s use-bar fallback assumes perfect enforcement; PRO’s default reduces the number of actors who can misuse the record in the first place.
[P4.5] This answers the central clash.
