[P1.1] A statue in a town square is not a history lesson; it is a civic thank-you note. We put people on pedestals to say: this person embodies who we are and who we aspire to be. That is why the motion is so modest and so obviously right. It does not call for sledgehammers or erasure.
[P1.2] It says only this: honor is for the honorable, and history belongs in institutions built to explain it.
[P1.3] Three arguments carry this debate.
[P1.4] First, honor is a public statement made in the present tense. A courthouse statue of a slave trader tells every citizen who walks past, today, that the state still counts him among its exemplars. When a Black family passes a Confederate general on the way to vote, the monument is not neutral marble—it is the government speaking. Democracies premised on equal citizenship cannot keep announcing, in bronze, that the architects of subjugation deserve laurels.
[P1.5] Second, this is preservation, not erasure. Museums and contextualized sites are where societies actually teach history—with dates, documents, and consequences. A pedestal teaches nothing; it only venerates. Notice that many of these monuments were themselves propaganda: Confederate statues went up in waves during Jim Crow and massive resistance, decades after the war, precisely to intimidate.
[P1.6] Relocating them with that context is more historically honest than leaving them mute in a plaza.
[P1.7] Third, we already accept this principle everywhere else. Germany does not display Hitler statues in city squares, yet no serious person claims Germans have forgotten Nazism—they remember it better than almost anyone, through museums and memorials. Post-Soviet states moved Lenin and Stalin to sculpture parks. Nobody calls that vandalism; we call it a society growing up.
[P1.8] So the burden falls on the other side: explain why endorsement in the public square is necessary for memory, when every institution actually devoted to memory says otherwise. Move the statues. Keep the history. Reserve honor for those who earned it.
