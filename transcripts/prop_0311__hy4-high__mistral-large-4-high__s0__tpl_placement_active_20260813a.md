[P1.1] Treat cohabitation as a sign of weak commitment only if commitment is best measured by making a lifelong promise before testing everyday reality. That is a poor measure. Marriage is not just a feeling; it is a shared household, a legal and financial tie, and a daily pattern of care. A couple who lives together first is often doing the sensible thing: asking whether the relationship works not on dates, but when bills are due, dishes pile up, work is stressful, and small irritations cannot be hidden by a romantic evening.
[P1.2] This is why cohabitation is reasonable, not evasive. Before signing a lease, people inspect the apartment; before taking a demanding job, people ask about hours and duties. Before marrying, it is hardly immature to learn whether two people can divide chores, discuss money without contempt, respect privacy, and handle conflict. Discovering incompatibility before a wedding is not a failure of commitment; it is commitment to honesty.
[P1.3] Staying together after that knowledge gives a promise more substance: “I choose you” now means “I know the ordinary life we are choosing.”
[P1.4] The social label matters. Calling cohabitation a symptom of weak commitment turns a diverse practice into moral suspicion. Couples move in because of rent, distance from family, a gradual transition, or because they deliberately test what vows should not be used to hide. Stigma also misreads causation: people already uncertain or pressured may cohabit uneasily, but that does not prove the act itself weakens commitment.
[P1.5] Unclear intentions can exist in any arrangement, including engagement and marriage.
[P1.6] The stronger norm is to judge relationships by responsibility, transparency, and follow-through, not by whether matching sheets were bought after a wedding. Cohabitation can be casual; so can dating, engagement, or marriage. But as a social category, it is an ordinary and reasonable step: it helps people enter marriage with open eyes—or leave before pretending to promises they cannot honestly keep.

[C1.1] The strongest image for treating cohabitation as a test is the apartment inspection: look before you sign. But the analogy changes the object. Inspecting an apartment leaves no lease; cohabiting creates one. It intertwines rent, chores, pets, furniture, and daily habits before the couple has publicly agreed what they are building.
[C1.2] That is not a neutral trial; it is a partial marriage with an easier exit. And easier exit is the problem: once two lives are logistically fused, breaking up is expensive, so people stay not because they have re-chosen each other, but because leaving feels harder than resolving doubts. The “test” can become a lock-in.
[C1.3] That is why cohabitation can signal weak commitment rather than its opposite. Weak commitment is not a feeling; it is enjoying a shared life while keeping permanence vague. A couple can divide chores for years and never settle the question that matters: are we willing to make this hard to undo? Moving in often postpones that question.
[C1.4] One partner may read the lease as progress toward marriage; the other may read it as a comfortable substitute. The arrangement does not force that mismatch into the open.
[C1.5] Nor does “ordinary” settle “reasonable.” The relevant question is whether cohabitation makes commitment clearer or murkier. Used well, it should end in an explicit decision: marriage, a timeline, or a clean parting. As a social default treated as beyond question, it can normalize the ambiguity that later produces resentment, rushed marriages, or breakups after years of shared property.
[C1.6] So judge responsibility, not sheets—but responsibility includes making the big choice explicit before merging households. A promise made after cohabitation is not automatically stronger; sometimes it is extracted by pressure or fear of starting over. The stronger norm treats cohabitation as a serious step that demands more honesty, not less: name intentions and set terms before sharing a home. Otherwise we treat unresolved commitment as if it were already resolved.
