[P1.1] The precautionary principle, as commonly applied, doesn't simply say "be careful." It says: when an innovation might cause harm, and the evidence isn't yet conclusive, restrict or block it until safety is proven. That sounds prudent until you notice the asymmetry baked in. The risks of introducing something new are weighed heavily.
[P1.2] The risks of *not* introducing it are barely weighed at all. Every year of delay in a beneficial technology is treated as neutral waiting, when in fact it is a cost paid in lives, welfare, and foregone progress.
[P1.3] This asymmetry is the core problem. Agricultural biotechnology in Europe is a clear case. Golden rice, engineered to produce vitamin A, faced years of obstruction under precautionary logic. The harm it was meant to address—childhood blindness and death from vitamin A deficiency—was certain and ongoing.
[P1.4] The harm it might cause was speculative. The principle as applied treated the speculative risk as decision-weighty and the certain ongoing harm as background noise. That is not precaution in any honest sense. It is paralysis dressed up as prudence.
[P1.5] The same pattern appears in nuclear energy, where precaution-driven regulatory thickening helped stall a low-carbon technology for decades while fossil fuels kept killing people through air pollution and climate disruption. The "potential harms" of nuclear were dramatized; the certain harms of the alternative were normalized and ignored.
[P1.6] The deeper issue is epistemic. Innovation operates under irreducible uncertainty. You cannot prove a complex technology safe in advance without deploying it, because real-world effects emerge at scale. If "satisfy all doubts before proceeding" becomes the regulatory standard, the burden is effectively infinite.
[P1.7] The principle, as commonly applied, converts uncertainty into a veto.
[P1.8] The harm prevented matters. But so does the benefit blocked. A principle that systematically counts only one side of that ledger will, by construction, block more beneficial innovation than it prevents genuine harm.
