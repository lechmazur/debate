[C1.2] First, the mechanism defeats the goal. Code is not a helicopter. An attack helicopter needs a factory, a supply chain, spare parts, and a runway; an exploit chain is a few hundred kilobytes written by a small team who can sit anywhere. When democratic vendors exit, the demand doesn't vanish — it flows to Chinese contractors, Russian brokers, and offshore shells.
[C1.3] The leaked files from a Chinese hacking-for-hire firm showed a mature domestic industry selling intrusion tools to provincial police with no oversight at all. That is the market the ban hands the customers to. The autocrat still gets the tool; what disappears is our visibility into how it's used.
[C1.4] Second, the ban surrenders leverage that conditional licensing preserves. Licensed sales let democratic governments impose end-use audits, target-category limits, revocation clauses, and vendor liability. Every remedy that actually bit in the Pegasus scandal came from this toolkit: the vendor placed on an export blacklist, licenses cut back, litigation by platform companies, visa sanctions on abusers. Those are surgical.
[C1.5] A blanket ban is a sledgehammer that throws away the very hooks we used.
[C1.6] Third, "non-democratic" is not a category of villains; it is roughly half the world's governments, including the partners who intercept trafficking rings and jihadist cells before they reach Europe. Denied lawful-intercept tooling, those services don't stop surveilling — they revert to blunter methods: dragnet telecom access, informants, detention and coercion. Targeted intrusion is often the least indiscriminate option on that menu.
[C1.7] The comparison that decides this debate: a regulated trade with strings attached, or an abdicated trade with none. Abuse deserves a response. Handing the market to Beijing and Moscow while calling it ethics is not one.
