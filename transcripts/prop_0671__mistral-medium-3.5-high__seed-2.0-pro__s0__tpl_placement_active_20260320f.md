[P1.1] Early childhood is a critical window for building the neural and physical foundations of learning, and paper is the superior medium for this task. Handwriting—whether printing or cursive—engages fine motor skills and creates spatial memory that typing on tablets cannot replicate; children who write by hand learn letters faster, spell more accurately, and retain information better. The physical act of forming letters also strengthens memory and recall in ways that typing does not. Paper eliminates the inherent distractions of digital devices; without notifications, games, or the temptation to switch apps, young learners sustain focus on the material, developing the attention span essential for later academic success.
[P1.2] Even in math, the tactile experience of drawing diagrams and working through problems on paper builds deeper understanding than tapping on a screen.
[P1.3] The physical health costs of early screen saturation are well-documented and concerning: eye strain from prolonged blue light exposure, musculoskeletal pain from poor posture, and reduced physical movement all take a tangible toll on developing bodies. Paper-based learning keeps children more active—turning pages, moving between stations, and collaborating in shared physical space, rather than remaining stationary with a device.
[P1.4] Socially, paper fosters richer, more nuanced human interaction that is critical for child development. Children share physical work, point to specific problems, and explain their reasoning face-to-face, building communication skills and emotional intelligence that digital interfaces often reduce to shallow, emoji-based exchanges.
[P1.5] Critics claim early digital exposure is necessary for future readiness, but true digital fluency requires strong foundational literacies first. Reading comprehension, writing, and critical thinking are best developed through paper in early grades. This policy sequences technology wisely: build strong cognitive and social foundations first, then layer digital skills when children are developmentally ready. By making early grades device-light, we protect the very capacities that make later digital learning meaningful.
[P1.6] The early years are too precious to outsource to screens when paper does the job better.
