[P1.1] This rule does not force landlords to accept bad tenants. It only forbids rejecting someone who already meets every other qualification, just because of how they pay their rent. That is the critical guardrail at the heart of this debate, and it is almost always misrepresented. A single mother who passes the credit check, has a clean rental history, no prior evictions, and holds a voucher that guarantees full, on-time rent payments every month - there is no legitimate business reason to turn her away.
[P1.2] Landlords retain every single other right to screen tenants. They can still set minimum credit scores, require rental references, enforce occupancy limits, ban pets, demand income thresholds, reject anyone with a red flag. All this rule removes is one arbitrary, discriminatory filter that targets people who did everything right except being poor enough to qualify for public assistance. We already accept this principle for every other payment method.
[P1.3] You cannot reject a qualified tenant for paying with a bank transfer instead of cash. You cannot reject them for using an employer payroll card. Lawful public assistance is just another guaranteed, legal payment stream. Right now, in areas without this protection, roughly half of all voucher holders cannot secure a home even when they can fully cover the rent.
[P1.4] That is not market logic. That is bare discrimination. It traps working families in shelters even when their rent is already pre-approved. It wastes public funds allocated to house people.
[P1.5] It punishes people for using the safety net we voted to build for them. There is no neutral justification for this exclusion. It is just bias. That is why this prohibition is both fair and necessary.

[C1.1] Rejecting a tenant because rent comes through a voucher is nothing like rejecting a bank transfer. A voucher is not simply a payment method; it is a portal into a government regulatory regime. Landlords who accept vouchers must submit to housing authority inspections, layers of administrative approval, compliance timelines, and payment delays that tenants paying out of pocket do not create. Calling this discrimination confuses a business decision about bureaucratic risk with prejudice against poor people.
[C1.2] Landlords routinely rent to cash-poor tenants; what they avoid is the red tape.
[C1.3] The motion effectively conscripts private property owners into a public subsidy program. It says that because a tenant qualifies on paper, the landlord must accept a relationship where a government agency controls portions of the lease, sets rent ceilings, and can slow evictions for lease violations that would otherwise lead to quick removal. That is compelled contracting, and it erodes the basic right to choose with whom one does business under terms both parties freely accept.
[C1.4] The "otherwise qualified" framing collapses on contact with reality because the voucher itself changes the transaction. Housing authorities often fail to complete inspections on schedule, leaving units vacant and landlords unpaid for months. Rent caps can fall below market value, and special procedural hurdles can make removing a destructive tenant far more costly and time-consuming. These are material risks that alter the economics of the lease.
[C1.5] The tenant may look identical on a credit report, but the deal is not.
[C1.6] Finally, this mandate backfires. When lawmakers strip away one screening tool, landlords do not simply absorb the loss. They tighten every other standard, raise deposits, or convert properties to ownership models beyond the rule's reach. The result is a smaller rental pool and higher barriers for everyone.
[C1.7] True housing abundance comes from expanding supply and streamlining vouchers, not from pretending that government subsidies carry no strings and forcing landlords to ignore them.
