[P1.1] Sustained Western military aid is the only policy that changes Russia's strategic calculus in the right direction. Deterrence is not a mood; it is a calculation of cost, capability, and credibility. When Ukraine can intercept Russian missiles and strike Russian logistics with Western-supplied weapons, the price of continuing the war rises—and so does the price of any wider gamble against NATO. An immediate negotiated settlement does the opposite: it locks in territorial gains, rewards the invasion, and teaches Moscow that sheer persistence eventually fractures Western resolve.
[P1.2] Consider the mechanism in practice. In the opening weeks of the full-scale invasion, Russia expected a rapid collapse of Kyiv. What blunted the assault was not diplomacy but the rapid influx of Western anti-tank and air-defense systems that made occupation militarily ruinous. Since then, every escalation Moscow has attempted has been checked by sustained aid that denied battlefield breakthroughs.
[P1.3] This is deterrence operating in real time: Russia has not struck a NATO member, has not employed tactical nuclear weapons, and has avoided direct attacks on Western supply lines precisely because it cannot afford a wider war while pinned down in Ukraine. Sustained aid keeps it pinned down.
[P1.4] A settlement now would signal the opposite. It would tell Moscow that time is on its side, that Western unity is temporary, and that territorial conquest can be consolidated through patience. The historical pattern is stark: concessions to expansionist powers rarely sate appetite; they sharpen it. Whether one recalls the 1938 Munich agreement or the post-2014 Minsk arrangements—which Russia exploited to rearm for the 2022 invasion—the lesson is identical.
[P1.5] A frozen conflict today is not peace; it is halftime for the next invasion, whether in Moldova, the Baltics, or beyond.
[P1.6] The choice is stark. Sustained aid raises the cost of further aggression until wider war becomes irrational for Moscow. Immediate settlement lowers that cost and invites the very escalation we seek to prevent. Deterrence requires staying the course, not trading territory for a pause.

[C1.1] Deterrence is not the same as prolonging a war. The proposition confuses raising battlefield costs with reducing the probability of a wider war. Wider aggression is likeliest to come from escalation inside an ongoing hot war: a stray missile near NATO territory, a supply-route strike pulling in Western personnel, a nuclear signal misread, or a stalemate-breaking gamble. Sustained aid strengthens Ukraine, but it also multiplies these contact points and keeps every incident inside a crisis with a nuclear power.
[C1.2] PRO says Russia "cannot afford a wider war while pinned down in Ukraine." That is dependence on exhaustion, not deterrence. A pinned-down Russia can still probe red lines, strike infrastructure near NATO borders, or miscalculate under pressure. And NATO's avoidance of direct attack owes as much to Article 5 and nuclear deterrence as to Ukrainian firepower; aid cannot claim that restraint.
[C1.3] An immediate negotiated settlement need not be a gift of territory or Ukrainian disarmament. Pushed seriously, it means an observable ceasefire, a demarcated line without recognition of conquest, sanctions retained with snapback, monitored limits, and explicit Western security commitments for the Ukrainian state that remains. That creates a cleaner tripwire than battlefield fog: if Russia resumes major attacks, violation is visible, attribution easier, and the response pre-committed.
[C1.4] Munich and Minsk are the wrong analogies. Munich was a concession with no monitored barrier; Minsk was vague and weakly enforced. Their failure argues for robust settlement design, not permanent war. A frozen line with outside commitments, as in Korea, can stop a local conflict from becoming a regional one.
[C1.5] Sustained aid also makes Western credibility hostage to the next budget fight, election, or ammunition shortage. A settlement now converts fragile leverage into durable diplomatic architecture while Ukraine has bargaining power. The better deterrent is a war that stops being live, not one kept a miscalculation from spreading.
