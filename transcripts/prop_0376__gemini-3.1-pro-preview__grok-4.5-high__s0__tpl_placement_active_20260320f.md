[P1.1] Uncapped credit card interest rates do not simply price risk; they manufacture financial failure. We must cap these rates by law because exorbitant interest transforms credit from a temporary bridge into an inescapable debt trap.
[P1.2] When interest rates climb into the high 20s or 30s, the mathematics of repayment break down. A borrower making minimum payments on a high-interest balance ends up paying multiples of the original principal over years or even decades. This model relies on vulnerable consumers entering a state of perpetual debt servitude, where their monthly payments act as an annuity for the lender while the principal barely budges. A legal cap halts this predatory extraction.
[P1.3] The primary objection—that caps will force lenders to cut credit access for high-risk borrowers—is not a drawback. It is a necessary feature of harm reduction. Denying someone access to a 35% interest credit card is not depriving them of a vital resource; it is protecting them from a toxic financial product. If a borrower’s financial situation is so fragile that a lender demands an exorbitant rate to offset the near-certainty of default, that borrower cannot afford the loan in the first place.
[P1.4] Giving them a high-interest credit card does not solve their financial distress; it merely postpones the crisis while making the inevitable bankruptcy far more devastating.
[P1.5] Furthermore, uncapped rates create perverse incentives for the financial industry. Lenders are currently encouraged to aggressively market credit to high-risk individuals because the massive interest harvested from those desperately treading water easily subsidizes the eventual defaults of others. By capping rates, we force the market to return to responsible underwriting. Lenders will have to assess whether a borrower can genuinely afford to repay the loan under fair terms.
[P1.6] Credit should function as a tool for financial stability, not an instrument for aggressive wealth extraction. Protecting the most vulnerable consumers from inescapable debt spirals is a fundamental duty of the law, even if it means restricting their access to the very traps that would ruin them.

[C1.2] Access to revolving credit—even expensive access—is still a tool. It bridges a car repair that keeps a job, covers a temporary income gap that would otherwise miss rent, or lets a careful borrower build a thin repayment history. Strip the card away because the interest ceiling cannot cover the risk, and the same household faces worse substitutes: payday loans at far higher effective rates, auto-title traps, rent-to-own markups, or pure cash fragility with no grace period and no dispute rights.
[C1.3] That is not harm reduction; it is the substitution of a regulated product for unregulated ones.
[C1.4] High rates are not an artificial trap invented by lenders. They are the residual price of default probability and collection costs after underwriting. [C1.5] Treating every such offer as engineered failure erases agency and freezes people out of the formal system. The mathematics of minimum payments is real, yet so is the right to choose short-term expensive credit over immediate catastrophe.
[C1.6] Caps do not restore “responsible underwriting.” Lenders already ration and price risk; a hard ceiling simply shrinks the customer pool and leaves the most precarious with fewer options, not safer ones. Competition, required disclosures, and bankruptcy already constrain excess. Price controls invite black-market credit and lower inclusion for the working households that need flexibility most. The proposition’s trade-off—trading imperfect access for none—is therefore a net injury, not a necessary feature.
[C1.7] Reject the cap.

[P2.4] The borrower makes minimum payments, the balance swells, and they often end up turning to the very shadow credit CON warns about *just to cover the credit card bill*. High-interest cards do not prevent catastrophe; they just make the inevitable bankruptcy far more expensive.
[P2.5] CON argues that uncapped rates merely reflect the "price of default probability" [C1.4]. But this ignores the actual business model. Lenders are not pricing risk to ensure the borrower can successfully pay off the loan; they are maximizing extraction before the inevitable default. The "agency" CON champions [C1.5] is an illusion when the product is mathematically engineered to exploit human cognitive biases around compounding interest.
[P2.6] A lifeline that eventually drowns you is not a lifeline. Denying access to a toxic financial product is not a net injury; it is the fundamental purpose of consumer protection. The temporary loss of purchasing power is a necessary intervention to stop the systemic extraction of wealth from precarious households.
