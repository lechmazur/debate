[P1.1] The motion asks who owns an adopted person’s history. An adopted adult is not a ward, not a minor, and not a party to the contract made before they could speak. They are a citizen, and citizenship carries a presumptive right to know one’s own genealogy, biological health risks, and the identity of those who contributed half of one’s genetic material. That presumption should not yield to adult embarrassment, institutional habit, or the moral panic of a previous era.
[P1.2] CON will invoke the birth mother’s expectation of privacy. But a promise made to adults about a third party’s identity is not a property right over another person’s self-knowledge. The law does not let a biological father erase a non-adopted son’s birth record to avoid reputational harm. It does not let a court seal a paternity finding merely because the mother now regrets the disclosure.
[P1.3] Adoptees alone are subjected to a permanent, state-enforced amnesia about their origins, and that singling out is not child protection—it is discrimination by file cabinet.
[P1.4] The medical argument is independently decisive. An adoptee who cannot answer whether a biological parent died of cardiac disease or carried a hereditary mutation is denied actionable risk information. That is not merely an emotional injury; it is a clinical liability that follows the person for life.
[P1.5] Sealing was born in an era of stigma, designed to conceal illegitimacy rather than to protect children. The law should outgrow that panic. We can require a brief contact-preference notice, but we should not let the fear of a phone call justify a lifetime of enforced ignorance. Adults are the primary stakeholders in their own lives.

[C1.3] Start with who the birth parent actually is. A meaningful share of relinquishments come from the worst circumstances a woman can face: pregnancy from rape or incest, a violent partner, a family or community that would punish her. She chose adoption over abandonment or a dangerous back-alley alternative because the state promised her the record would stay closed. The motion tells that woman, decades later, that the promise was conditional on a future legislature's mood.
[C1.4] That is not only unjust to her; it is dangerous to the next infant. Countries that permit anonymous birth, and states that run safe-haven laws, do so for one reason: when confidentiality is guaranteed, frightened women deliver in hospitals and hand the baby to a nurse rather than a dumpster. Make every relinquishment traceable by right, and some of those women will not walk through the hospital door. The adoptee's curiosity is real, but it is not worth a newborn's life.
[C1.5] Now the medical argument, which PRO calls "independently decisive." It decides nothing about identity. Cardiac history, hereditary mutations, cancer risk: all of that can be disclosed as non-identifying information, and routinely is. If a name is required to learn whether grandpa had a heart condition, the system is badly run, not rightly opened.
[C1.6] PRO's analogy to paternity findings fails because those were never promised secret. Here the secrecy induced the choice. And "contact preference notice" is a polite fiction; a preference with no legal force protects no one.
[C1.7] The honest alternative is consent-based access. The motion refuses it, and that refusal is why it should fall.
