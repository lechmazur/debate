[P1.1] The current tax system is rigged to favor the ultra-wealthy: a public school teacher pays income tax on every paycheck, while a billionaire whose net worth grows $100 million in a year from stock appreciation pays nothing, because they did not sell the asset or spend the gains. Relying almost entirely on income and consumption taxes lets the wealthiest households accumulate untaxed economic power for generations, worsening inequality and starving public services of stable funding. A net worth tax fixes this core flaw by taxing the actual wealth households hold, not just the portion they choose to realize or spend.
[P1.2] It closes the loophole that lets billionaires pay lower effective tax rates than working people, ensuring everyone contributes to the public goods that make their wealth possible: infrastructure, education, and stable markets. Critics will claim valuing assets is too burdensome, but we already conduct regular asset valuation for property taxes, estate taxes, and financial mark-to-market rules. A well-designed tax with clear valuation for liquid assets and periodic third-party appraisal for hard-to-value holdings like real estate or private businesses would have minimal administrative costs relative to the hundreds of billions in annual revenue it would raise.
[P1.3] That revenue is stable even during recessions, unlike volatile income tax receipts, and can fund universal pre-K, affordable housing, and climate resilience projects that create middle-class jobs and grow the economy from the bottom up. This is not a punitive tax: it is a fair, sustainable reform that ensures the wealthiest pay their share for the society that enabled their success.

[C1.1] An annual net worth tax rests on a misleading picture: wealth as idle cash. Large fortunes are balance sheets of businesses, land, shares, private claims, and debts—productive structures, not necessarily money waiting to pay a bill. Taxing them yearly is a recurring charge on unrealized, often illiquid value before income arrives or consumption occurs. The teacher-and-billionaire contrast [P1.1] shows a timing problem in capital gains, not a reason to tax balance sheets.
[C1.2] If gains are deferred, tax them fully at sale, gift, or inheritance; tax the proceeds when borrowing finances consumption; and make consumption taxes progressive. These tools follow actual command over resources. A wealth tax instead appraises the whole balance sheet each year.
[C1.3] The valuation analogies [P1.2] fail: estate valuation is episodic; real estate is visible and immobile; mark-to-market is limited to quoted securities, not a household's whole balance sheet. Large fortunes are shifting portfolios and legal structures. Valuations invite discounts, litigation, and avoidance; wealthy households can hire planners, load assets with debt, move residence, or fragment ownership. Revenue assumes a stable base that the tax itself erodes.
[C1.4] Liquidity makes the harm concrete. A founder whose firm is valued for expected future profits may have little cash; an annual bill forces her to sell control, borrow against the business, or cut investment or payroll. In downturns, asset values and cash flow fall together, so stable recession revenue [P1.3] is an illusion: the state bills obsolete valuations or chases collapsing ones when firms are weakest.
[C1.5] Income and consumption taxes should remain the backbone because they tax cash income, realized gain, and spending—where ability to pay is measurable. Close exemptions at realization and transfer; do not replace those flows with a paper-value levy bringing appraisals, forced sales, and avoidance battles. It is neither fairer nor sustainable. Tax wealth when it is used, transferred, or turned into income, not mere paper value.
