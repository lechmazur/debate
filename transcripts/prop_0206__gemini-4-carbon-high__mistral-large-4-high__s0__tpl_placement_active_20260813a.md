[P1.1] Public pensions were built to insure against outliving the ability to work, not to finance ever-lengthening retirements on the backs of a shrinking workforce. When life expectancy rises and retirement ages stay frozen, every passing decade silently expands total pension payouts while handing a steeper bill to a smaller pool of younger workers. Indexing retirement ages to longevity restores the core social contract: maintaining a stable, predictable share of adult life in retirement rather than demanding endless sacrifice from active workers.
[P1.2] Relying mainly on higher payroll taxes is both economically self-defeating and generationally unjust. Payroll taxes fall exclusively on wages, sparing passive capital and accumulated wealth, and are often capped on top earners. Hiking them directly cuts the take-home pay of young and middle-income workers already squeezed by housing, childcare, and the cost of living. Macroeconomically, making labor more expensive discourages hiring, suppresses wage growth, and erodes the very employment base that pay-as-you-go pensions need to survive.
[P1.3] Adjusting the retirement age delivers the opposite result: a fiscal double dividend. When healthier, longer-living cohorts stay in the workforce marginally longer, they simultaneously expand national output, add years of contributions, and keep benefit durations sustainable.
[P1.4] Uneven longevity gains do not rescue the case for payroll taxes. Manual laborers certainly face physical wear earlier than office workers. Yet freezing the retirement age and hiking payroll taxes to help them is perverse: it garnishes the wages of a bricklayer or nurse on every single paycheck throughout their career, largely to subsidize long retirements for healthy, affluent professionals who live the longest. A fair system indexes the retirement age to life expectancy while preserving disability insurance and seniority pathways for those who entered arduous work young.
[P1.5] That protects workers whose bodies genuinely cannot continue, without trapping every working family under a permanent, escalating tax on their labor.

[C1.1] The proposal presents a false choice: either make people work longer or tax workers more. But a pension system can be stabilized by asking those with the greatest capacity to pay to pay more, while protecting those whose lives and bodies do not match the national average. Raising the retirement age in line with life expectancy does the opposite. It takes an aggregate statistic and applies it as an individual obligation, so a nurse or roofer in a low-longevity area must work longer because affluent professionals elsewhere live longer.
[C1.2] Gains in life expectancy are heavily concentrated among the already advantaged; indexing the retirement age to the average therefore locks those inequalities into the benefit formula and calls the result fairness.
[C1.3] The labor-market premise is also weak. A higher eligibility age assumes that a 65-year-old can simply keep working. Many cannot: physically demanding jobs wear out bodies, caregiving gaps interrupt careers, and employers routinely screen out older applicants. When late-career work is unavailable, raising the age does not add a contribution year; it creates an unpaid gap financed by depleted savings, disability, unemployment, or family support.
[C1.4] The projected savings shrink while old-age poverty rises.
[C1.5] Nor is the alternative really mainly higher payroll taxes on every paycheck. Pension finances can be repaired through progressive measures: removing the cap so contributions apply to all earnings, bringing capital income and wealth into the contribution base, trimming benefits for high earners, and offering flexible, actuarially fair retirement for those who can and want to work longer. These tools spread adjustment according to means and life circumstances rather than according to birthday.
[C1.6] The right response to rising longevity is not a compulsory later retirement for everyone. It is a pension system that remains solvent without making the people with the shortest life expectancy and the least wealth pay the largest price.

[C2.2] The pension-ledger saving is then partly an accounting shift, not a net gain.
[C2.3] The safeguard answer also misses the fairness point. Disability and seniority pathways help only those who can document arduous careers and reach the thresholds; many low-income workers have broken records, late entry into stable jobs, or injuries that end work first. Average life expectancy is the wrong metric because longevity gains are concentrated among affluent professionals with easier jobs and longer retirements. Indexing the headline age to that average makes a nurse or roofer work longer to fund retirements they are less likely to enjoy.
[C2.4] Nor does uncapping contributions or trimming top benefits destroy the contributory link. Public pensions are already social insurance with floors and progressive features; asking more from high earnings or capital does not turn insurance into welfare. The alternative to mandatory indexing is not merely “higher payroll taxes on every paycheck” but a mix of broader progressive revenue, actuarially fair flexible retirement, and bridge support—spreading adjustment by capacity to pay rather than by birthday.
[C2.5] That is the decisive test. If solvency is the goal, choose levers that do not depend on employers hiring older workers and do not impose the largest loss on those with the shortest life expectancy. Capacity-to-pay financing can be targeted and adjusted; lost years of a manual worker’s retirement cannot.
