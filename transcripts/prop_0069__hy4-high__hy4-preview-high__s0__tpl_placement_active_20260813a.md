[P1.1] A legal right to disconnect is not a right to escape consequences. It is a right to prevent an opaque reputation trail from becoming a portable identity. A landlord, college, employer, or platform may judge someone using relevant evidence from that relationship, disclosed and open to answer. It should not inherit a score built from workplace monitoring, school proctoring, app ratings, browsing traces, complaints, and guessed correlations from contexts the person never offered to that decision.
[P1.2] Following is the harm. A bad week at sixteen, a disputed delivery rating, a disability-related pause, a faulty sensor, or a poverty-shaped pattern can be flattened into a “risk” label. Once transferred, the label becomes self-confirming: fewer interviews, worse housing options, harsher platform limits, then more data saying the person is unstable. Old references were bounded by purpose, time, and human accountability; these systems are sticky, scalable, and invisible.
[P1.3] The injury is not just bad data. It is permanent context collapse.
[P1.4] Efficiency objections confuse useful local tools with a lifelong passport. Schools may track attendance; employers may check qualifications; platforms may police abuse on their own service. But when the same inferred character follows someone into a lease application or admissions file, cheap sorting replaces fair judgment. Trust is weakened, because the person cannot know which stale proxy is blocking the door.
[P1.5] Consent cannot carry that burden. Nobody individually bargains with a labor market, a housing market, or take-it-or-leave-it terms needed to work or study. Collectors profit from linkage; subjects bear the loss and cannot audit the model. So the law must give an enforceable opt-out from cross-context reputation use, deletion or sealing of stale inferences, human review on local evidence, and protection from retaliation.
[P1.6] Fraud prevention, safety rules, and ordinary accountability remain. People may still be judged for what they do in a context; they should not be sentenced everywhere by a machine’s memory.
