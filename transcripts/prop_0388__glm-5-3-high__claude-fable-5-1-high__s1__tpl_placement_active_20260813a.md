[P1.1] Games with loot boxes, gacha pulls, and randomized "surprise mechanics" aren't merely entertainment products anymore—they are commercial gambling architectures wrapped in cartoon packaging. That is why several governments, including Belgium and the Netherlands, have already treated certain loot boxes as regulated gambling. The design targets the same vulnerability in the same way; only the wrapper differs.
[P1.2] Children cannot legally walk into a casino, and we don't excuse casinos that fund "free entry" by letting kids in the back door. Notice what this argument concedes—it concedes the business model depends on extracting money from a population that includes minors with undeveloped impulse control. A funding model that requires exploiting children's cognitive vulnerabilities to subsidize adults is not a defense; it's an indictment.
[P1.3] The proposition is modest, not radical. Strict youth spending limits and default protections—age verification, spending caps, transparent odds, no default purchase shortcuts, parental controls on by default—preserve the entire free-to-play ecosystem for adults. Developers keep their revenue from consenting adults. What they lose is only the slice of revenue built on minors who cannot meaningfully consent.
[P1.4] If a game's economics collapse when children are protected, the game's economics were predatory, full stop.
[P1.5] We already accept this logic everywhere else. We cap minors' contracts, restrict their access to alcohol, tobacco, and gambling, and mandate defaults like seatbelts and privacy settings because we know defaults shape behavior. The market alone cannot fix this: children don't comparison-shop for psychological safety, and parents can't audit every game's reward schedules.
[P1.6] Free access funded by childhood overspending isn't generosity. It's an externality dumped onto families. The only question is whether we protect kids by default or keep letting the industry run the table.
