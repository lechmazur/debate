Egypt's objections are real, but they are not sovereign objections—they are objections to an allocation inherited from 1929 and 1959 agreements between British colonial administrators and a Cairo government that did not represent any upstream community.
[P1.2] Binding Ethiopia to deals drafted before it had a seat at the table is not international law; it is imperial preference dressed up as treaty obligation.
[P1.3] The stakes are not abstract. To ask Ethiopia to halt filling or submit its reservoir schedule to Egyptian approval is to ask one of the world's poorest large nations to remain deliberately underdeveloped so a richer downstream state can preserve an allocation that was never fair.
[P1.4] The alternative is not chaos. The 2015 Declaration of Principles, AU-led negotiations, and Ethiopia's own filling announcements have repeatedly offered technical coordination on dam operation without conceding Egyptian veto power. Even under realistic worst-case filling scenarios, Egyptian water security can be defended through reservoir management, groundwater use, and the efficiency reforms Cairo itself has long identified as necessary. The "existential threat" framing collapses on contact: Egypt's per capita water availability has been falling for decades, and GERD is only one pressure among many, none of which justify giving one nation a permanent voice in another's domestic energy policy.
[P1.5] The central question is whether downstream objection from a single country overrides upstream sovereignty, equitable utilization, and the right of 120 million Ethiopians to electrify their own country on their own land.
