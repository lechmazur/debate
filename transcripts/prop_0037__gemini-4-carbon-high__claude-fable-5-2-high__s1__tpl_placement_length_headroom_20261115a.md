[P1.1] Congestion pricing diagnoses urban gridlock accurately, then stops halfway through the cure. Charging a toll to enter downtown trims traffic volume just enough for affluent drivers to move faster, while leaving the fundamental mistake intact: dedicating the most valuable, densely populated land on earth to moving and storing two-ton private machines. Major cities do not need slightly fewer cars in their central districts; they need to ban private cars there entirely.
[P1.2] First, only an outright ban crosses the physical threshold required to transform urban space and transit. A street with modestly fewer cars is still a roadway. Cities relying on pricing alone must preserve wide asphalt lanes, complex signal phases, and curb storage for paying motorists. Removing private cars altogether—reserving streets exclusively for public transit, emergency responders, scheduled commercial deliveries, and specialized disability transport—changes downtown geometry overnight.
[P1.3] Pricing advocates tout toll revenue for future transit projects, yet leave today's buses delayed behind paying sedans. A ban delivers rapid, reliable surface transit immediately without digging billion-dollar tunnels. Freed lanes become shaded walkways, protected bike networks, and bustling storefront plazas, boosting foot traffic and commerce just as car-free central streets from Oslo's core to Paris's riverbanks have shown.
[P1.4] Second, banning private cars allocates scarce public land by civic need rather than personal wealth. Congestion pricing converts central streets into luxury lanes: corporate executives and wealthy commuters treat tolls as pocket change, buying the right to inflict exhaust, tire particulates, noise, and lethal crash risk on everyone else. Working commuters get priced out of driving anyway, while still breathing the fumes and enduring intersection delays caused by affluent motorists. In dense urban cores where regional transit lines already converge, nobody needs a private living room on wheels—and removing them lets the entire city move cleanly, safely, and fairly.

[C1.1] A ban is a congestion charge set at infinity — and infinity is the one price guaranteed to be wrong. Pricing asks every trip whether it is worth what it costs the city. A ban refuses to ask, then spends years writing exemptions for everyone it wrongly excluded.
[C1.2] The exemptions have already started. [P1.2] carves out emergency vehicles, scheduled deliveries, and "specialized disability transport." So this is not the end of rationing; it is rationing by permit office instead of by price. Permit offices miss people: the nurse finishing at 3 a.m. when buses run hourly, the daughter driving a frail father who has no disability paperwork, the plumber hauling tools to a burst pipe, the resident who lives downtown.
[C1.3] Under pricing, each can still make the trip that matters. Under a ban, each needs a bureaucrat's permission.
[C1.4] Nor does pricing lock streets into asphalt. Nothing about a charge forces a city to "preserve wide asphalt lanes." London and Stockholm priced their cores, traffic fell substantially, and freed space went to bus lanes, cycle tracks, and wider pavements. The opposing examples prove the point: Paris's riverbanks are particular roads closed to cars, and Oslo's centre mostly removed parking and through-traffic while keeping access.
[C1.5] That is targeted redesign, street by street — what a priced city does, not what "entirely" means.
[C1.6] The fairness claim runs backward. A wealthy driver paying a steep charge funds the bus the cleaner rides; under a ban he pays nothing, and the city buys enforcement instead of service. If the rich drive too much, raise the price. That dial turns.
[C1.7] A ban has no dial.
[C1.8] Cities will misjudge something. Choose the tool that can be corrected: price the scarce space, close the streets that deserve closing, and keep the flexibility a blanket ban throws away.
