[P1.1] The question is not whether platforms must be perfect or censors. It is whether a platform may knowingly keep a synthetic-impersonation scam alive after repeated verified reports show that a specific account or artifact is being used to defraud.
[P1.2] AI changes the scale of impersonation. A cloned voice or a deepfake video can now reproduce a boss, relative, banker, or official cheaply enough for scammers to burn accounts and create replacements. A grandmother hears a call that sounds exactly like her grandson; a finance clerk sees a video instruction that appears to come from the company’s CFO. The harm is not embarrassment; it is wire transfers, credentials, and emptied accounts.
[P1.3] Victims often cannot sue anonymous actors or recover money abroad. The one durable defendant is the intermediary that saw the scam coming and kept serving it.
[P1.4] Verified reports are the key. This is not liability for one angry user, bad algorithm, or debatable takedown request. The proposition covers repeated, verified reports—clear evidence that the same synthetic identity or account is being used for fraud. At that point notice, foreseeability, and cheap prevention converge.
[P1.5] A platform can suspend, label, limit distribution, or add friction; what it cannot reasonably do is nothing while waiting for the next victim.
[P1.6] Liability would therefore not punish hosting. It would price knowing indifference. If platforms have no exposure, rational cost-benefit is to process as many fraud reports as necessary to avoid public backlash and no more, externalizing losses to the least sophisticated users. A tailored duty forces a reasonable response, while preserving defenses for good-faith judgments, rapid action, or contested authenticity.
[P1.7] The choice is narrow: either platform inaction has consequences, or victims absorb the cost of a fraud infrastructure the platform could disable. Civil liability is the only mechanism that reliably makes repeated verified reports mean something.

[C1.1] Synthetic impersonation is cruel, but this proposal turns an unreliable signal — a 'repeated verified report' — into a negligence lawsuit, and that conversion is where it collapses.
[C1.2] Verification sounds like certainty. In practice it means a platform employee or classifier guessing whether media is synthetic, whether consent existed, and whether a transaction is fraud rather than parody, dubbing, an authorized ad, or a disputed payment. Repeated reports do not cure that uncertainty; they can be a coordinated pile-on, a rival's campaign, or an attempt to erase a real but embarrassing clip by labeling it AI. Once damages punish guessing wrong, platforms will not become better investigators.
[C1.3] They will auto-remove on the third complaint.
[C1.4] That hands fraudsters and competitors a cheap veto over legitimate speech and commerce, while real scammers migrate to fresh accounts, encrypted channels, and direct calls no report can reach. The fraudster chooses the victim, crafts the deception, and receives the money. Civil liability replaces that causation with 'you could have done something,' a hindsight standard that invites suit after every viral scam because another warning sign can always be found afterward.
[C1.5] Scale compounds the problem. Major services field vast report volumes across languages and jurisdictions, while scammers mutate names, voices, and payment details after each takedown. Private damages will not yield better detection; they will yield defensive paperwork, slower appeals, and legal reserves easiest for giant incumbents to absorb. Small forums and startups will over-censor or exit, concentrating power in the least accountable platforms.
[C1.6] If stopping transfers is the goal, target points that can block or reverse them: banks and payment processors adding confirmation friction, regulators imposing audited response deadlines and penalties for ignored notices, and criminal enforcement against organizers. Those tools compel action without making every disputed authenticity call a negligence trial. Victims deserve prevention, not a doctrine that rewards fastest deletion, punishes judgment, and still leaves the anonymous scammer holding the money.

Civil liability instead keeps the warning inside litigation after the money is gone, while rewarding platforms for deletion first and accuracy later.

[P4.1] A1: “Verified” cannot mean whatever the platform would prefer to believe. It means objectively checkable convergence: the same account or artifact, the same synthetic identity, repeated reports from the impersonated party, brand, or affected user, corroborating payment or metadata signals, and confirmation where available from banks, payment services, telecoms, rights holders, or law enforcement. A platform cannot avoid the duty by refusing to look; deliberate non-review after such reports is itself evidence of notice and reckless indifference. External verification already exists whenever an impersonated bank, employer, voice owner, or victim confirms the scheme.
[P4.2] A2: The safe harbor is a documented, proportionate response to the verified pattern, not automatic third-complaint removal. Blocking the fraud destination, disabling links and payment rails, cutting distribution, requiring recipient confirmation, and suspending the artifact are all defensible. A label plus reach limits plus payment friction can be reasonable when those steps actually interrupt the scam; removal is sufficient but not necessary. Liability still turns on conduct, not result.
[P4.3] That turns CON’s warning into an admission. Its model is not victim protection but legal-risk avoidance, treating any lawsuit exposure as a reason to over-remove. Ordinary law handles this routinely: a shop told repeatedly that one cashier is passing forged checks can investigate and stop the transactions without closing the business. A platform can likewise verify, cut the scam’s payment and distribution path, and preserve good-faith judgments.
[P4.4] The choice remains whether repeated verified reports mean something. CON offers no mechanism to make them mean something, only deference to platform inertia. Our duty prices knowing indifference without demanding perfection; only the platform sees the repeated pattern, and only liability makes that sight matter.
