[P1.1] Ransomware is a business model, not a mystery. Attackers encrypt systems and wait because payments arrive reliably enough to justify the risk. Governments must break that cycle by prohibiting ransom payments while funding victim support so organizations can recover without bankrolling criminals.
[P1.2] The logic is straightforward. Criminals respond to expected returns. When hospitals, cities, and firms wire funds, they validate the tactic and expand its reach. A ban removes the reliable payday.
[P1.3] Groups still probe for weak points, but the conversion of breach into profit collapses. History shows parallel patterns elsewhere: when markets for extortion shrink, activity contracts. Ransomware is no different; it is organized, adaptive crime that thrives only while the product sells.
[P1.4] A pure ban without support would leave victims stranded, creating political backlash and underground payments that undermine enforcement. That is why support must travel with the prohibition. Governments can fund rapid recovery teams, subsidized offline backups, incident response retainers, and temporary operating liquidity so a locked clinic does not choose between insolvency and feeding a syndicate. Support also finances baseline hardening—patching, segmentation, multi-factor authentication—that reduces the attack surface for everyone, not just those who can pay.
[P1.5] Critics will claim bans are unenforceable or that attackers will simply switch to pure data theft. But pure theft without the lever of operational freeze is already less lucrative for many operators, and enforcement grows more effective as international coordination expands and crypto-tracing improves. The alternative is permanent escalation: each successful payment advertises higher profitability and larger targets. Prohibition plus support flips the incentive.
[P1.6] It tells attackers their product is worthless and tells victims they will not be abandoned. That dual signal is what starves the market globally rather than merely shifting the pain.
