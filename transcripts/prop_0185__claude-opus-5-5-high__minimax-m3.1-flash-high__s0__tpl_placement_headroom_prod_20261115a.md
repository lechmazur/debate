[P1.1] Every democracy faces the same temptation: spend now, and send the bill to people who cannot vote yet. That is not a character flaw in particular politicians. It is a structural bias. The benefits of a deficit are concentrated, visible and immediate.
[P1.2] The costs are diffuse, delayed and borne by future taxpayers. Leaving fiscal discipline to legislative discretion asks the institution that profits from that bias to police it. It almost never does.
[P1.3] Look at the record. Deficits are supposed to open in recessions and close in recoveries. Instead, many advanced economies borrow through booms too. The United States ran large deficits in the late 2010s with unemployment near historic lows, then entered the pandemic with far less room.
[P1.4] Greece showed where unchecked discretion ends: a debt crisis that forced harsher austerity than any constitutional rule ever would. Discretion does not prevent cuts; it postpones them until markets impose them brutally, in the middle of a recession.
[P1.5] Now the crucial design feature. This motion does not demand a crude annual balance that forces cuts during downturns. It exempts automatic stabilizers. When recession hits, unemployment insurance rises and tax receipts fall, and the rule lets that happen.
[P1.6] What it forbids is structural deficit: borrowing that persists after the economy recovers. That preserves Keynesian stabilization while ending the ratchet of permanent debt.
[P1.7] This is not theoretical. Switzerland adopted a constitutional debt brake by popular vote, built on exactly this cyclical logic, and its debt ratio fell while it kept room to respond to crises. Germany's debt brake gave it the fiscal space to act forcefully when the pandemic struck.
[P1.8] Why constitutional? Because ordinary statutes get waived the moment they bind. A rule a majority can repeal in an afternoon is a suggestion. Constitutional status raises the cost of cheating, anchors expectations, lowers borrowing costs, and protects future citizens who have no seat at today's budget table.

[C1.1] A constitutional rule would constitutionalize a forecast of when fiscal judgment is permissible. Automatic stabilizers protect unemployment insurance and falling tax receipts, but not every legitimate stabilization response. A recession may require temporary infrastructure spending or financial rescue outside that exception. If emergency action violates the constitution, legislators must amend it or default; if it does not, the exception may swallow the rule.
[C1.2] The promise also depends on a contestable distinction between cyclical and structural deficits. Identifying structural balance requires estimates of potential output, employment, and policy effects. Authorities can revise those estimates, redefine spending, or phase in policies while calling borrowing temporary. Constitutionally fixing the accounting does not remove political incentives; it transfers disputes to classifiers and courts.
[C1.3] The rule can become a sophisticated loophole rather than a hard limit.
[C1.4] Nor is constitutional status superior to law. Legislatures can require transparent multi-year plans, independent review, and escape clauses, then revise them when circumstances change. Entrenchment raises the cost of correcting a bad rule. Governments may preserve fiscal orthodoxy at the expense of employment, public investment, or inflation control; consolidation at the wrong phase can reduce output and worsen debt dynamics.
[C1.5] PRO’s Swiss and German examples do not establish this exact proposal. Their frameworks include adjustment paths, escape clauses, and discretionary institutions, and crisis flexibility mattered. Greece exposes the danger of delay, but that supports timely, transparent correction, not necessarily constitutional rigidity. Legislative discretion can act before markets impose brutal austerity, particularly when correction rules are automatic rather than discretionary.
[C1.6] Finally, constitutionalization creates a legitimacy mismatch. Elected representatives may reasonably accept borrowing for a flood, pandemic, or recession. A rigid rule can make them ignore legitimate needs while courts or fiscal boards gain authority they were never selected to exercise. The alternative is not unrestricted borrowing but accountable, revisable fiscal judgment.
