[P1.1] Picture two engineers, both sixty-five. One is sharper than ever and mentoring half the department. The other has been coasting for years. A mandatory retirement age treats them identically: both out the door on the same birthday.
[P1.2] That is the whole problem. It replaces judgment about a person with arithmetic about a date.
[P1.3] My case rests on three points.
[P1.4] First, age is a crude proxy when better tools already exist. Employers manage underperformance at every other age through appraisals, competency standards, and fitness-for-duty assessments. If a forty-year-old surgeon's hands shake, we test and act. Nobody proposes firing everyone at forty to be safe.
[P1.5] A retirement age is lazy management. It avoids a difficult conversation with the weak performer by sacrificing the strong one, and it tells capable workers their decline is presumed, not proven.
[P1.6] Second, the economics now run hard against forced exit. Populations in most wealthy countries are aging, pension and healthcare systems strain as the ratio of workers to retirees falls, and many sectors, from nursing to skilled trades to teaching, face persistent shortages. Pushing willing, experienced people out of the workforce by rule is self-harm. The usual reply is that retirees "make room for the young."
[P1.7] That is the lump-of-labour fallacy: there is no fixed stock of jobs. Experienced workers spend, pay taxes, and train successors. Economies that let older people keep working have not seen youth employment collapse.
[P1.8] Third, this is already proven practice, not a utopian experiment. The United States abolished mandatory retirement for most occupations decades ago. The United Kingdom scrapped its default retirement age over a decade ago. Workplaces adapted.
[P1.9] The sky did not fall.
[P1.10] Note the precise shape of the motion: "most occupations," not all. Where a genuine, evidence-based safety link exists, such as certain aviation roles, targeted rules can remain as narrow exceptions. What CON must defend is something far bigger: a routine, blanket cutoff for ordinary jobs. CON must explain why a birthday beats an actual assessment of whether someone can still do the work.

[C1.2] The real choice is not a date versus judgment. It is a date versus drift, surveillance, and litigation.
[C1.3] That matters most where decline is dangerous and invisible to the person declining.
