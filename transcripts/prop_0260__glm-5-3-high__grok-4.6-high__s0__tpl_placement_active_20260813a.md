[P1.1] Tracking is not a private nuisance between consenting adults; it is an industrial system that most people never knowingly joined. The average website today connects to dozens of third parties, and a single profile assembled across sites can encode your health worries, your finances, your politics, and your location history. Nobody clicks "I consent" to that in any meaningful sense. They click "agree" to see the recipe.
[P1.2] When consent is theater, the market cannot fix the problem, because there is no real choice to discipline.
[P1.3] That is why the control point must be the operating system and browser. Ad hoc blockers exist, but they require technical knowledge, installation, and maintenance that most users—children, the elderly, anyone on a work machine—do not have. The platform is the one choke point where protection can actually reach everyone, the same way operating systems already ship firewalls, malware protections, and permission prompts for microphone and location by default. We do not say "let the market provide antivirus for those who shop around."
[P1.4] We treat systemic digital harms as baseline platform responsibilities.
[P1.5] Note what the proposition does not do. It does not ban advertising. It does not block content. It sets a robust, opt-out system-level protection—users who want tracking can decline the protection.
[P1.6] This mirrors long-standing precedents: do-not-call registries, email spam law, and the EU's recent requirement that operating systems let users pick default apps and services. Mandatory platform-level defaults are a standard regulatory tool when individual negotiation is impossible.
[P1.7] The deeper principle: your attention and behavioral data are generated by you, and default control should rest with you, not with whoever got code onto your device. The current default—unrestricted third-party observation unless you discover and deploy countermeasures—reverses the proper relationship between owner and instrument. A law requiring robust, opt-out blocking simply restores it. The burden of justification belongs on the trackers, not the tracked.

[C1.8] The motion treats ads as malware and a default as justice. It is a subsidy cut dressed as safety.

[P2.5] Third, the platform-concentration point cuts my way. Google and Apple already operate the trackers or the walled gardens; they are the least regulated parties today. [P2.6] Finally, "people who want blocking already have it" is the market answer to a market failure. Consent theater means the demand never registers. A default flips who must ask. That is the whole motion, and it stands.
