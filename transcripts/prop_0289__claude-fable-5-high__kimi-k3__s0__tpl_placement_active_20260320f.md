[P1.1] Robotaxis don't operate in a free market of their own making. They monetize public streets, occupy public curb space, rely on city-funded infrastructure, and require government permits to exist at all. When a company asks for the privilege of running driverless vehicles on roads every taxpayer built, the public is entitled to ask for something back. Service obligations are the standard, time-tested price of that privilege — we impose them on utilities, on telecoms, on traditional taxi medallions, and on transit agencies precisely because essential mobility networks left purely to margin-chasing will predictably abandon the poor.
[P1.2] That prediction isn't hypothetical. We already watched ride-hail companies concentrate supply downtown while outer neighborhoods waited twice as long, and before that we watched taxi drivers refuse fares to certain zip codes. Robotaxis threaten to automate that redlining — an algorithm quietly deciding, block by block, who deserves a reliable ride. The people cut out are exactly those who need the service most: households without cars, night-shift workers in transit deserts, elderly residents far from a rail stop.
[P1.3] A wealthy district losing a robotaxi loses convenience. A low-income neighborhood losing one loses access to jobs, groceries, and clinics.
[P1.4] Notice what the motion actually asks: "reasonably similar reliability," not identical wait times or equal fleet density. It's a flexible equity floor, exactly like universal service standards in telephony and postal delivery, which industries absorbed while remaining profitable. And robotaxis are uniquely suited to bear it — there's no human driver refusing a neighborhood, and idle vehicles repositioning to underserved areas cost pennies per mile.
[P1.5] The core question is this: should a technology celebrated as the future of urban mobility be permitted to hard-code the inequities of the past? If robotaxis become the transportation backbone of American cities — as their backers promise — then building them as luxury goods for high-margin districts entrenches a two-tier city. Requiring fair service now, while the industry is still forming, is cheap. Retrofitting equity later is nearly impossible.
