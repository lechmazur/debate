[P1.1] Every food-delivery app profits from a simple trick: bury the true cost until checkout, then hope inertia beats price-shopping. A ten-dollar burrito becomes sixteen dollars after delivery fee, service fee, and "small order" fee — and the same burrito, ordered directly from the taqueria or picked up yourself, might cost eleven. The platform knows this. The customer, mid-checkout, usually doesn't.
[P1.2] That gap is the whole case for PRO.
[P1.3] This is not a hypothetical market failure — it's structural. Comparison shopping across delivery apps requires opening three separate apps, re-entering an address, and re-browsing a menu, all before you know whether the comparison was even worth the effort. Meanwhile the restaurant's own site or a walk-in pickup, which often carries dramatically lower fees since it cuts out the platform's take, is invisible unless you already knew to look for it. Rational consumers don't have time to run that audit on every ten-dollar order, so the platform's fee structure survives not because it's competitive, but because switching costs are engineered to be just annoying enough.
[P1.4] The proposal is narrow and proportionate, which is exactly why it wins. It doesn't ban delivery apps, cap fees, or force any redesign of the business model. It only triggers when the app's own price is *materially* higher — meaning trivial two-percent differences don't require a pop-up, so there's no flood of noise degrading the experience. Compare it to unit-pricing on grocery shelves, or the "cheaper flight available" nudges some travel sites already show: cheap, targeted disclosure that lets markets actually function on price, without dictating outcomes.
[P1.5] The comparison that matters for this debate isn't "regulation versus no regulation." It's "an industry that profits from opacity" versus "a rule that costs the platform almost nothing to comply with but saves consumers real money on real orders." Every argument CON raises about business viability has to explain why a company's revenue should depend on customers not knowing a cheaper option exists three clicks away. That's the case PRO intends to hold.
