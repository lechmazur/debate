[P1.1] Public space is not a museum of 2019. The curb and the sidewalk are scarce land held in common, and the right test is which use serves more people, not which use some residents remember fondly. Outdoor dining took space reserved for stored cars and rigid clearance and made it a place the neighborhood actually occupies. Cities should keep that option even when a constituency wants the old sidewalk rules restored.
[P1.2] The case is practical. A street table keeps cooks and servers employed, brings people onto the block, and gives neighbors a place to linger. Open-air seating met a demand the old frontage did not. A policy that scores success only by resemblance to the pre-pandemic street erases those workers, diners, and neighbors.
[P1.3] The person who needs a clear path matters. So does everyone else the street is for.
[P1.4] Preference is not a veto. Some residents want the old rules back. Those rules were a choice, not a right. They regularly gave one parked car a better claim on the curb than the people who could use that same space all evening, and they imposed one template on blocks that are nothing alike.
[P1.5] A residential side street and a restaurant corridor do not deserve identical treatment.
[P1.6] Flexibility is not a blank check to seize the sidewalk. It means permits, mandatory clear widths, accessible routes, hour limits, and the power to remove a setup that blocks passage or throws noise into bedrooms. Those tools answer the real harms. They do not justify a standing ban merely because change is unpopular with some.
[P1.7] Cities already shift curbs to bus lanes, loading zones, and bike lanes when the public use is stronger. Outdoor dining belongs in that same judgment. Keep what works, correct what fails, and do not turn nostalgia into a property right over the sidewalk.

[C1.1] A sidewalk you can only use if you buy dinner is not more public. It is less public. Flexible dining takes ground held for every pedestrian and hands long stretches to a single business for paying customers only. That is not a neighborhood occupying the street.
[C1.2] It is enclosure: a private dining room built on public land, where the right to linger depends on the bill.
[C1.3] The pre-pandemic rules were not nostalgia. They were safety and accessibility earned the hard way. Clear widths and curb zones exist so a wheelchair user is not forced into traffic, a blind pedestrian can travel with a cane, an older resident is not squeezed past tables and planters. Every exception narrows a path someone else depends on.
[C1.4] Lose the uniform rule and you lose the guarantee.
[C1.5] The profits stay private while the costs spill outward. The restaurant keeps revenue from extra tables; neighbors get noise past midnight, trash and rats, and blocked entrances next door. A laundromat, bookstore or tailor gets no extra sidewalk, only fewer passersby able to reach the door. This is a public subsidy for one industry, paid in sleep, passage and sanitation.
[C1.6] Permits and hour limits sound responsible until a city tries to enforce them on a Friday night across hundreds of structures. Inspectors are scarce, complaints pile up, and sheds sold as temporary harden into decked, wired fixtures. Removal is rare, so violation becomes the business model. A system that needs constant policing to stay humane is not flexible.
[C1.7] It is ungovernable.
[C1.8] Residents who want the old rules are not clinging to 2019. They are defending the basic urban right to walk home, sleep, and use the street without a restaurant's permission. Vibrancy that depends on taking that away is not shared prosperity. Put the tables back inside the property line and return the sidewalk to everyone.
