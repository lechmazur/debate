[P1.1] The decisive question is who controls the work, not what the contract calls it. A courier often cannot set the route, negotiate the fare, reject a delivery without risking deactivation, or build a client base. The platform sets pay, standards, deadlines, ratings, and often the means of work. That combination of unilateral control and dependence is the substance of employment, whatever the app calls the relationship.
[P1.2] Default status should therefore reflect substance, not labels. An employment presumption asks whether the operator exercises an employer’s practical powers. Genuine freelancers can remain outside where they serve several clients on their own terms, control methods and pricing, invest in equipment, and bear genuine commercial risk. Those are concrete indicators, not a personality test.
[P1.3] Agencies that genuinely operate as client-facing businesses should be able to claim the exemption, but they should bear the burden of proving the autonomy they assert.
[P1.4] Independence without bargaining power is not entrepreneurship. If a worker cannot earn less without being deactivated, absorb platform losses, or retain earnings from an established customer relationship, employment protections correct an imbalance created by the platform. Minimum wages, paid leave, social insurance, and safety rules stop firms from shifting business risks onto individuals unable to insure or negotiate against them. Employee status need not mean a fixed desk or schedule: workers can retain flexibility while gaining a floor and a voice.
[P1.5] A default also prevents misclassification. Platforms benefit when workers waive collective rights, paid leave, and social contributions. If employment is presumed unless freedom is clearly demonstrated, firms cannot collect the benefits of employment while assigning its costs to workers. That is not anti-innovation; it lets new business models compete on service rather than labor-law arbitrage.
[P1.6] Europe and Asia need not adopt identical systems, but should share a principle: protect dependent workers by default and reserve exemptions for genuine freelancers. Independence should be earned through autonomy, not presumed from a contract label.

[C1.1] The motion mistakes a real problem—dependent misclassification—for a universal remedy. Gig work is not one labor market. [C1.2] A default that sweeps “most” into employment does not sort dependence from autonomy. It flips the risk onto everyone.
[C1.3] Employee status is not a free label. It brings payroll obligations, paid leave, social contributions, working-time rules, dismissal protections, and collective bargaining. Platforms facing those costs will not magically preserve open entry. They will cap hours, schedule shifts, restrict multi-apping, raise fees, or withdraw from thin markets.
[C1.4] The first casualties are the people using gig work as a bridge—the parent who works school hours, the student funding a course, the rural driver supplementing income. PRO says flexibility can survive employment status, but employment law imports obligations that make marginal flexible work less viable. You cannot mandate a floor without changing who gets hired.
[C1.5] PRO’s control test proves too much. Dispatch, ratings, quality standards, and price setting are often marketplace infrastructure. A booking platform sets standards without making hosts employees. Multi-apping also breaks the dependence story: if a driver works three apps, who provides leave, who schedules, who bears commercial risk?
[C1.6] A blanket default cannot answer, and a “narrow exemption” becomes a litigation trap for genuine freelancers and small platforms.
[C1.7] The better route is targeted protection: portable benefits, minimum per-task pay, transparency, insurance, anti-deactivation safeguards, and sectoral rules. These address bargaining power without forcing every gig into a binary status. Europe and Asia have very different labor markets, welfare systems, and informal economies; a sweeping default is regulatory overreach. Protect the dependent workers—yes.
[C1.8] But do not pretend that every gig is a job.
