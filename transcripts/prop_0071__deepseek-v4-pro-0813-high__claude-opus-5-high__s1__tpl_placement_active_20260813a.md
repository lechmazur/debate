[P1.1] An apology that leaves the stolen wealth in place is a settlement with the thief, not a remedy for the victim. Slavery and colonial extraction were not only moral insults; they were organized transfers of land, labor, and wealth. The countries that enriched themselves through those transfers still hold the accumulated advantages. The affected descendant communities still bear the accumulated costs.
[P1.2] Symbolic apologies cannot close a material gap.
[P1.3] First, the harm is intergenerational and material. Enslaved and colonized people were stripped of wages, property, legal rights, and often the ability to pass anything to their children. Their descendants inherited lower wealth, reduced land ownership, worse access to housing, credit, education, and health. A national apology may acknowledge that history, but it cannot fund a scholarship, repair housing, restore land, or replace lost inheritance.
[P1.4] If a bank stole a family’s savings and then merely apologized, nobody would call that remedy.
[P1.5] Second, the benefiting countries built public and private wealth from these systems. Ports, banks, universities, railways, plantations, and state treasuries absorbed the profits of unpaid labor and colonial extraction. The proper response to unjust enrichment is restitution. Direct reparations programs—cash payments, educational trusts, housing grants, land return, and community health investment—return resources to the communities whose deprivation helped pay for that prosperity.
[P1.6] Third, the objection that current citizens did not personally commit the original wrong confuses individual blame with state responsibility. Reparations are owed by states and institutions that still exist, still hold assets, and still benefit from past extraction. Countries routinely inherit treaty obligations, public debts, and state property across generations. Repairing the downstream costs of their predecessors’ crimes is no different in principle.
[P1.7] The choice is not between apology and silence. It is between symbolic acknowledgment and actual repair. Direct programs treat affected descendants as creditors owed a debt, not merely as audiences owed an apology.

[C1.2] Start with the ledger. Britain's industrial takeoff had many engines — coal, domestic agriculture, institutions — and the share attributable to slave profits is genuinely contested among economic historians. Meanwhile Portugal and Spain ran the most extractive empires in history and stayed poor. If unjust enrichment is the principle, the principle doesn't cleanly pick out the payers.
[C1.3] And it doesn't cleanly stop at Europe either: the Atlantic trade ran through African polities that sold captives, and the Indian Ocean trade through Omani and Ottoman markets. Once you commit to settling accounts, you owe an accounting — and there isn't one. What you get instead is a number chosen politically, which is exactly the number that gets attacked, litigated, and eventually unwound.
[C1.4] Second, delivery. For colonial extraction, "affected descendant communities" mostly live inside sovereign states. So the check goes to a government. Ask Nigerians how much of their oil revenue reached the villages.
[C1.5] A reparations transfer to a treasury is a transfer to whoever controls that treasury — which is why structural remedies (debt cancellation, tariff access, technology and vaccine licensing, returning artifacts, visa access, funding institutions directly) reach people that cash to ministries does not.
[C1.6] Third, durability. A descent-based cash program is the most repealable policy you can design: constitutionally exposed, requiring eligibility tribunals that adjudicate ancestry, and reversible the moment the other party wins an election. Compare a universal, needs-targeted investment in the same neighborhoods — housing, clinics, schools — which reaches the same families, survives the next government, and doesn't require anyone to prove their bloodline to a bureaucrat.
[C1.7] The motion says "rather than." That's the error. Apology plus enduring structural obligation repairs more than a contested cheque that arrives once, if it arrives at all.

[P2.6] Debt cancellation and tariff access can also be captured by local elites; direct institutions and individuals are often less indirect.
