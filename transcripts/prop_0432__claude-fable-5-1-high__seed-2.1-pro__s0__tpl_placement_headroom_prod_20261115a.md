[P1.1] A blanket pet ban is a prohibition standing in for a price. The thing a landlord actually fears is not a dog; it is a chewed doorframe, a stained carpet, a neighbor's complaint. Every one of those is a cost, and costs can be insured, deposited against, and recovered. A ban refuses to price the risk and instead abolishes the tenant.
[P1.2] That is the whole clash here: should the default be a tool that matches the actual harm, or a rule that treats a goldfish and a Great Dane as the same emergency?
[P1.3] Consider who bans actually hit. Most households in many cities own a pet. A ban does not make those animals vanish; it makes them hidden. Hidden pets are the worst of all worlds for a building: no deposit, no insurance, no disclosure, no vaccination paperwork, and a tenant who will never report the water-damaged subfloor because reporting it exposes the dog.
[P1.4] Then there is the workaround everyone knows about: emotional support animal letters purchased online, which landlords often cannot refuse. Blanket bans have already produced a shadow pet population with zero accountability. The proposition simply drags that population into daylight and attaches money to it.
[P1.5] Standardized deposits and insurance also fix the landlord's real complaint about case-by-case screening: unpredictability. A uniform scheme means the same coverage, the same claims process, the same limits, every lease. Landlords keep every other remedy they have now. Nuisance clauses still apply.
[P1.6] Barking at three in the morning is still a lease violation. Unsafe animals can still be refused on specific grounds. The default changes; the enforcement toolkit does not shrink.
[P1.7] And the default matters because defaults decide outcomes. Pet-owning tenants tend to stay longer, turnover less, and pay pet rent willingly. Shelters fill when leases say no. The question is whether we price a manageable risk or exile the people who carry it.

[C1.1] The proposition collapses on contact with three hard realities no standardized deposit or insurance policy fixes. First, the vast majority of pet-related harm in apartment buildings is not the one-time chewed doorframe or stained carpet proponents fixate on—it is chronic, diffuse, and rarely fully insurable. Standard pet insurance covers sudden, accidental damage, not months of urine soaking into subfloors, pervasive dander embedded in shared HVAC systems that triggers severe asthma attacks for disabled neighbors, or persistent late-night barking that drives other tenants to break leases and move out.
[C1.2] Those costs cascade: lost rental income from turnover, mandatory professional allergen remediation that can outstrip even high deposit limits, and liability for neighbors whose reasonable enjoyment of their homes is destroyed for months while glacial eviction processes play out, with no fast-track remedy for those trapped next to a disruptive animal.
[C1.3] Second, the “default to permission” model flips the burden of proof exactly where it should not rest. Landlords will no longer be able to set predictable, uniform rules upfront; instead, they will be forced to mount costly, evidence-heavy cases to remove a dangerous or disruptive animal after harm has already occurred, rather than screening risk before a tenant moves in, inviting legal disputes over what counts as a valid reason to refuse an animal.
[C1.4] Third, most local and state rental laws already cap security deposits at one or one-and-a-half months’ rent, far below the true cost of major pet damage, and standardized insurance policies routinely exclude exactly the gradual, building-wide harms that make pets a uniquely costly risk in dense multi-unit housing. The supposed tradeoff of “money up front for access” is a hollow promise when that money cannot come close to covering the cascading harms pets can impose on an entire building, not just surface damage to a single unit.
