[P1.1] Picture a street where five houses have rooftop panels and fifteen do not. Today the five export surplus at a fraction of retail value, while the fifteen buy grid power at full price. Peer-to-peer rooftop solar trading lets the five sell that surplus directly to the fifteen over the same wires. Countries should allow it because it turns local surplus into cheaper clean power and faster solar payback, without giant projects.
[P1.2] The barrier to rooftop solar is economics, not technology. Panels are cheap, but payback depends on the export price. Under net metering or feed-in tariffs, utilities often compensate exports below retail value, stretching payback and discouraging adoption. P2P trading raises the effective price prosumers receive by letting them sell to neighbors who would otherwise pay full retail.
[P1.3] That compounds: more arrays, more local generation, and a larger clean energy pool.
[P1.4] This is not a handout to panel owners. It is the only way renters and low-income households can access solar savings at all; they cannot install panels on a roof they do not own or cannot finance. P2P lets them buy locally generated electricity below utility rates and share in decarbonization. If the objection is fairness, the answer is inclusive design—consumer protection, transparent pricing, targeted support—not prohibition.
[P1.5] The strongest objection will be grid chaos. But P2P transactions still use the grid and can be settled through smart meters and management systems utilities deploy. Local trading can reduce strain by rewarding generation where load exists, shrinking peak flows and deferring costly upgrades. Allowing P2P is not deregulation; it is updating rules so decentralized resources transact under technical limits and fair network charges.
[P1.6] The real test is whether legalization creates more value than the utility-centered, one-way model. It does: sellers earn more, buyers pay less, emissions fall, and local innovation competes with monopoly supply. Banning peer-to-peer rooftop solar trading is choosing artificial scarcity over abundant local power.

[C1.1] Picture that same street. The five panel owners do not send electrons house-to-house; electricity flows by physics across shared poles, transformers, and control systems paid for by everyone. Peer-to-peer trading is not a new wire. It is a private price negotiated over a public network, and its discount largely comes from using that network without paying its full bundled cost.
[C1.2] Most grid costs are fixed. Maintaining lines, balancing supply second by second, keeping backup for cloudy evenings, and restoring outages cost almost the same whether a household buys slightly less utility energy at noon. When neighbors trade below retail while still relying on the grid for delivery and nighttime power, the margin between them is not created value. It is avoided grid contribution.
[C1.3] Multiply that across wealthy solar streets, and the same fixed costs are recovered from everyone else: apartment dwellers, shaded homes, and low-income customers least able to host panels or chase dynamic prices.
[C1.4] Local matching does not solve local physics. Rooftops produce together at midday and stop together at sunset. Neighbors are often away when surplus peaks, then all demand power in the evening. Paper trades can look local while the real system still exports excess, manages voltage rises and reverse flows, then ramps other supply later.
[C1.5] Thousands of bilateral deals add forecasting, settlement, cybersecurity, and liability problems: when a promised kilowatt-hour is not delivered, the grid, not the peer, must instantly cover it.
[C1.6] None of this requires suppressing rooftop solar. Countries can pay fair time-varying export rates, expand community solar for renters, and levy transparent network charges that keep shared infrastructure solvent. Those tools reward generation without letting better-off households and trading platforms carve private discounts out of a common system.
[C1.7] Allow peer-to-peer trading and you subsidize arbitrage, shift costs downward, and complicate the grid needed for electrification. Reject it, and solar can still grow—on terms where its beneficiaries pay for the network that makes it valuable.
