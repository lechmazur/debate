[P1.1] When a large supermarket throws away edible bread, produce, or prepared food simply because the selling window has narrowed, it is not eliminating the cost. It is shifting that cost onto everyone else: municipalities handle the waste, communities bear unnecessary environmental harm, and families face food insecurity while usable goods are destroyed. A business should not have an unrestricted right to externalize those harms merely because disposal is operationally convenient.
[P1.2] The requirement offers supermarkets two practical routes. First, sharply discount food while it is still saleable, allowing consumers to benefit and stores to recover some revenue. Second, donate what remains edible through food banks, community kitchens, or food-rescue partners. This is not an order to distribute spoiled food.
[P1.3] Ordinary food-safety standards still apply, and sensible rules can include clear handling requirements and liability protection for good-faith donations.
[P1.4] Most importantly, regulation changes incentives upstream. If disposal remains the cheapest default, over-ordering and poor stock management are treated as harmless. If edible surplus must instead be marked down, sorted, and transferred, supermarkets gain a reason to forecast demand better, adjust orders, improve rotation, and discount earlier. The best outcome is not merely moving waste elsewhere; it is preventing surplus in the first place.
[P1.5] Large supermarkets are the proper target because they have scale, inventory systems, refrigeration, staff, and predictable volumes. They already track products closely enough to price, promote, and remove them. Adding markdown schedules and donation pickups is a manageable extension of that infrastructure, not an impossible burden on a neighborhood shop. Compliance costs are also offset by recovered markdown revenue, lower disposal costs, and more efficient purchasing.
[P1.6] The opposing principle—that firms may destroy edible necessities whenever donation is inconvenient—gets the burden backward. Ownership permits profit; it does not justify avoidable waste whose costs are imposed on the public. Where safe food can still feed people, sharp discounting or donation is a proportionate, flexible duty. Waste should be the genuine last resort, not a routine line item.
