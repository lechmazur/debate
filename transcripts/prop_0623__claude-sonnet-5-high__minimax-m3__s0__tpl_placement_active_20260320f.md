[P1.1] Sectoral bargaining means setting wage floors and core standards at the level of an entire industry, not firm by firm. That single design choice fixes the deepest structural flaw in how low-wage labor markets currently work, and it deserves to anchor this whole debate.
[P1.2] Start with the mechanism. Enterprise-level bargaining lets any individual employer undercut the workers who unionize by threatening to move production, franchise out, or simply fire and rehire on worse terms. A fast-food worker who organizes one store gains nothing if the chain next door stays non-union and steals customers on price. Sectoral bargaining removes that race-to-the-bottom lever: if the wage floor applies to every firm in the industry, no employer can win market share by degrading labor standards.
[P1.3] Competition gets pushed back onto quality, productivity, and service, where it belongs.
[P1.4] This is not a hypothetical. Countries with strong sector-level agreements, across much of continental Europe, show lower wage inequality and lower rates of working poverty than countries relying mainly on firm-by-firm bargaining or pure statutory minimums. The pattern holds because sectoral bargaining captures workers that enterprise unions structurally miss, small retail shops, cleaning contractors, gig-adjacent logistics firms, where individual organizing is nearly impossible but industry-wide coverage is administratively simple.
[P1.5] It also solves a coordination problem employers themselves face. Responsible firms that want to pay decently are currently punished for it, undercut by competitors who cut corners on wages and safety. A sectoral floor lets good employers compete without being suicidal, which is why some employer associations actually favor these frameworks once they exist, rather than each firm bargaining alone in a prisoner's dilemma.
[P1.6] Finally, sectoral bargaining is more democratically legitimate than either a single national minimum wage, which ignores real differences between industries, or scattered firm bargaining, which only empowers whoever happens to already have leverage. It lets pay reflect actual industry conditions while still guaranteeing a floor no worker falls beneath.
[P1.7] That is the case CON has to answer: not whether bargaining is perfect, but whether any alternative removes the undercutting dynamic as well.
