[P1.1] Congestion is not a count of cars; it is vehicle-miles pushed through streets already near capacity. Ride-hailing added those miles in exactly the places with the least room to absorb them.
[P1.2] Start with the arithmetic the industry never advertises. A private car trip goes from A to B and parks. A ride-hail trip goes from a staging spot to A, then A to B, then B to the next fare or circles waiting for one. The passenger travels the same distance; the street absorbs roughly twice the driving.
[P1.3] Studies of driver logs in dense cities have repeatedly found a large share of platform miles carrying no passenger at all. That is pure added load, manufactured by the business model, not by passenger demand.
[P1.4] Then ask where those trips came from. Rider surveys consistently show a substantial fraction would otherwise have been a subway ride, a bus, a walk, a bike trip, or no trip at all. Every one of those conversions takes a person out of a mode that consumes almost no road space and puts them into one that consumes a lot. San Francisco's own transportation planners, examining the sharp rise in downtown delay during the 2010s, attributed a major portion of it to ride-hailing.
[P1.5] Traffic engineering explains why the effect is so violent: near saturation, a few percent more vehicles can mean a much larger jump in delay. And a car stopping in the curb lane to disgorge a passenger throttles the bus behind it and the whole lane with it.
[P1.6] Pooling was the promised answer, and it never arrived at scale — uptake stayed small, and pooled routes add detours and extra stops anyway.
[P1.7] The proposition is "worse overall," not "sole cause." CON must show the net effect was positive, not merely that other things also clogged the streets.
