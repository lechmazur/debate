[P1.1] Oversized SUVs and pickups are not just bigger cars. In a city they take more curb, more lane, more sightline, and they hit harder. When that extra size imposes extra costs, the city should charge extra for it.
[P1.2] Curb space is finite. A long-bed pickup occupies a stall that could have served two compact cars, a loading zone, or a bike corral. Wider bodies squeeze cyclists and force buses into opposing traffic. Heavier axles wear pavement faster.
[P1.3] Those street-use costs are paid today by everyone else in congestion, delayed deliveries, and maintenance bills.
[P1.4] The safety gap is just as concrete. A high hood line and a tall A-pillar hide a child at a crosswalk. Greater mass transfers more energy in a crash. The same intersection that is merely dangerous for a sedan becomes lethal when the vehicle is two feet taller and a thousand pounds heavier.
[P1.5] Cities already accept this logic when they restrict trucks on residential streets or charge more for commercial plates. Extending it to personal vehicles whose dimensions create the same harm is consistency, not class warfare.
[P1.6] The fees—higher parking, registration, or curb charges—are not a ban and not a sermon. They are a price. Drivers who need the capability can still buy it; they simply stop shifting the leftover cost onto pedestrians, renters without garages, and taxpayers who never chose the vehicle. Flat fees already treat a city hatchback and a three-row tank as identical users of the street.
[P1.7] That is the distortion.
[P1.8] If a city can meter a parking spot by the hour, it can meter it by the footprint. If registration can vary by weight or emissions, it can vary by the crash and space burden the vehicle actually imposes. The alternative is to keep socializing the cost of private bulk while streets grow more dangerous and more crowded. Charge the difference.

[C1.1] Everything in the case for these fees rests on a false premise: that a parking meter can fix a vehicle-design problem. It can't. A surcharge doesn't lower a single hood line or shrink a single A-pillar. The pedestrian at the crosswalk is exactly as endangered by the F-150 whose owner grudgingly paid an extra fee.
[C1.2] If tall hoods and blind zones kill, the answer is federal design standards—the level of government that actually regulates vehicles. A city fee is safety theater that collects money while the danger drives on.
[C1.3] Second, the fee punishes people for choices they've already made and often couldn't avoid. The plumber with a work truck, the family of six, the suburban commuter who bought the used Tahoe because it was what they could afford—none of them can respond to a city fee by trading in tomorrow.
[C1.4] Meanwhile the wealthy owner of a luxury SUV shrugs and pays. That's the signature of a regressive tax dressed up as safety policy: it changes behavior least among those who can pay most.
[C1.5] Third, the street-use math is weaker than it sounds. Pavement damage scales steeply with axle weight—roughly the fourth power—which means the real wear comes from delivery trucks, garbage trucks, and buses, not a Suburban that's a thousand pounds heavier than a sedan. The claim that heavy SUVs are grinding down our roads charges private drivers for damage overwhelmingly done by other vehicles.
[C1.6] Finally, consider what cities will actually do with this power. "Oversized" has no principled boundary—curb weight? Length? Hood height?
[C1.7] Every cutoff is arbitrary, every arbitrary cutoff invites gaming and litigation, and every new fee category becomes a revenue stream cities defend long after any safety rationale fades. If curb space is genuinely scarce, price the curb for everyone by demand. Singling out one vehicle class isn't pricing—it's a tax on an unpopular purchase, and it fixes nothing it names.
