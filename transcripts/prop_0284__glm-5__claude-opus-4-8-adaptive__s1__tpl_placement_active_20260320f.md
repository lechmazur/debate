[P1.1] Algorithmic price discrimination exploits information asymmetry to extract maximum revenue from each customer, often harming those least able to pay. When an airline charges one passenger $300 and another $600 for the same flight, or when an e-commerce site shows higher prices to users on expensive devices, the mechanism is hidden by design. Customers cannot comparison shop against themselves. This is not market efficiency—it is covert extraction.
[P1.2] The core problem is that consumers cannot defend against what they cannot see. A shopper checking a price on their phone has no way of knowing that the same retailer would have offered a lower price had they checked from a different zip code, at a different time, or with a different browsing history. The algorithm knows everything about the buyer; the buyer knows nothing about the algorithm. This asymmetry corrupts the bargain at its foundation.
[P1.3] Hidden differential pricing also invites discriminatory outcomes. When algorithms use proxies like device type, location, or browsing behavior, they often end up charging higher prices to already-disadvantaged groups—rural customers with fewer alternatives, older users less adept at price-hacking, or communities whose data profiles mark them as less price-sensitive. The discrimination is buried inside proprietary code, immune from scrutiny.
[P1.4] Disclosure provides a minimum safeguard. If companies must reveal that prices vary by customer, consumers can at least attempt to game the system back—clearing cookies, switching devices, using incognito mode. But disclosure alone is imperfect. Many customers will still lack the time, sophistication, or market power to respond effectively.
[P1.5] This is why outright bans deserve serious consideration for essential goods and services, or where the practice becomes predatory.
[P1.6] Either sunlight or prohibition is necessary. The status quo—secret, algorithmic price extraction—treats customers not as participants in a market but as targets in a rigged game.
