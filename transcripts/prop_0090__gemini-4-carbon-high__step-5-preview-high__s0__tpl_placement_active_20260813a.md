[P1.1] Driving moves thousands of pounds of steel through unpredictable traffic, demanding continuous visual attention on the road. Touchscreens are fundamentally hostile to that task because a flat sheet of glass offers zero tactile guidance. To tap a virtual icon while bouncing over rough pavement, a driver must look away from traffic, hunt visually for the target, steady an unsupported hand in midair, and look again to confirm the press. Physical buttons, rotary dials, and switches solve this through fixed placement and mechanical feedback.
[P1.2] Drivers can reach out by muscle memory, feel a defrost button or click a temperature knob, and never take their eyes off the windshield.
[P1.3] That difference matters most in emergencies. Picture highway traffic slamming to a halt while you grope a glass panel for hazard lights, or a windshield fogging over in a nighttime downpour while defrost sits buried inside a menu. Direct sunlight glare, wet fingers, or winter gloves routinely defeat capacitive screens, turning an instant reflex into seconds of blind driving. Worse, routing core controls mainly through a screen creates a brittle point of failure.
[P1.4] Infotainment computers lag, freeze, and reboot. Dedicated hardware switches respond immediately even during a software crash, and they let anyone in a rental or borrowed car find vital controls instantly without decoding an unfamiliar layout.
[P1.5] Substitutes like automatic sensors and voice control cannot close this safety gap. Automatic climate systems cannot anticipate every sudden fogging or rapid weather shift, and speaking commands over road noise, music, or passengers is slower and less reliable than flipping a switch. Keeping physical controls for core functions sacrifices nothing modern drivers value: touchscreens can still handle maps, cameras, and deeper settings where rich visuals actually help. Automakers strip out buttons largely to cut manufacturing costs while marketing austerity as sleek minimalism.
[P1.6] Saving a few dollars per dashboard is a reckless trade when the price is pulling drivers' eyes off the road.

[C1.1] PRO's case rests on a category mistake: the problem is driver distraction and failure recovery, not the presence of glass. A physical switch is not automatically safe, and a touchscreen is not automatically dangerous. What matters is where attention goes, how quickly a task is finished, and whether a backup exists when the primary path fails.
[C1.2] Climate, defrost, and hazards are intermittent adjustment tasks, not continuous controls like steering. They are exactly where adaptive design can help. A screen can enlarge defrost when fog is detected, show climate and seat heating together, reveal recirculation as humidity rises, and present labels in the driver's language. A large, high-contrast prompt can be brighter and larger than a small, low-mounted switch, while haptic or audible confirmation can replace the click.
[C1.3] A physical panel is frozen at manufacture: controls proliferate, useful functions sit buried, labels wear, and backlights fail. In a rental, a hazard button under a flip-up cover is no more intuitive than a persistent, standardized top-level control.
[C1.4] Even the emergency case does not justify the blanket mandate. Hazard lights need a redundant, immediate override, but that can be a stalk, roof switch, steering-wheel control, or always-on screen layer tied to automatic defogging logic. Requiring a specific substrate for every function rules out many safe layouts. Hardware also cannot be patched: a sticky switch or failed backlight requires service, while a software layout can be improved across a fleet and cost savings can fund crash avoidance and driver monitoring.
[C1.5] The proposition would spend dashboard space and money on a fixed solution while treating every driver, vehicle, and climate system as identical.
[C1.6] The correct test is performance-based: measure completion time under glare, gloves, noise, and lag; require consistent hazard access and fail-operable defogging. That standard can approve good touch systems and reject bad ones. PRO mistakes one acceptable answer for the only acceptable answer.
