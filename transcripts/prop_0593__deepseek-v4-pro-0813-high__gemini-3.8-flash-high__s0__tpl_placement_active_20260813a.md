[P1.1] In global supply chains, the worst abuses survive because they are hard to see. A European electronics brand can reasonably claim it did not know cobalt in its batteries was dug by children; a clothing retailer can say a subcontractor hid forced overtime. That ignorance is the business model. Voluntary due-diligence reporting preserves it: firms choose their own definitions, audit only what they select, and publish only what supports their reputation.
[P1.2] Standardized, audited reports replace that selective silence with comparable, verified information.
[P1.3] The state has a proper role here, just as it does with financial accounting or workplace safety. Human rights due diligence creates information that markets alone will not generate, because abuse is an externality: the victim bears the cost, the consumer cannot see it, and the firm keeps the savings. Large multinationals already rely on state-enforced contracts, trade rules, and limited liability. Making market access conditional on demonstrating that their supply chains are free of forced labor and dangerous exploitation is not overreach; it is the same logic as requiring audited accounts.
[P1.4] Standardization matters because it allows investors, buyers, unions, and affected communities to compare like with like and track improvement. Auditing matters because without independent verification, a report is advertising. The predictable objection is that compliance is costly and burdensome. But the alternative is costlier: disasters and forced labor scandals destroy value, and unregulated laggards undercut responsible firms.
[P1.5] A single mandatory standard lowers compliance costs over time by replacing dozens of conflicting private schemes and focusing firms on the risks most likely to become crises.
[P1.6] The core principle is simple: no firm should be allowed to profit from a supply chain whose abuses it refuses to look for. Governments should require it to look, verify, and publish.

[C1.2] First, human rights cannot be audited like financial ledgers. PRO draws a false equivalence to accounting [P1.3]. Ledgers track objective, double-entry transactions; supply-chain audits operate in distant jurisdictions plagued by shadow subcontracting, coached workers, and forged books. Repeatedly, factories certified by social auditors have collapsed or burned shortly after receiving clean inspections.
[C1.3] Mandating standardized audits does not fix these structural blind spots—it codifies them. It grants corporations a legal liability shield: a standardized report to brandish in court and press releases, washing their hands of systemic abuses that continue undetected underground.
[C1.4] Second, mandatory public reporting triggers a destructive "cut-and-run" dynamic. Faced with standardized disclosure metrics and public shaming, multinationals will not patiently invest in resolving deep-seated governance deficits in fragile states. The rational corporate strategy is defensive de-risking: summarily terminating contracts with high-risk suppliers and fleeing developing regions. When Western brands pull out, vulnerable workers—such as artisanal miners or smallholder farmers—are not liberated.
[C1.5] They are pushed straight into the informal economy and local black markets, where labor protections are nonexistent, wages plummet, and abuses are far worse.
[C1.6] Third, standardization fundamentally flattens reality. Forcing radically distinct crises—from debt bondage in agriculture to child labor in mining—into uniform reporting templates reduces nuanced due diligence to an administrative tick-box exercise. Multinationals will divert billions of dollars toward international auditing cartels and compliance lawyers, consuming resources that should fund direct remediation, living-wage initiatives, and local labor enforcement.
[C1.7] We must not mistake compliance paperwork for genuine human rights progress. Because mandatory reporting incentivizes corporate abandonment over engagement and substitutes paper compliance for actual reform, the proposition must be rejected.
