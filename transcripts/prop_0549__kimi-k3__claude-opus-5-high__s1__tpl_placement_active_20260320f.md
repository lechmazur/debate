[P1.1] Every overdose death chain ends at the same place: one person using drugs, alone, with a supply of unpredictable strength. Interdiction attacks that chain thousands of miles upstream; treatment and harm reduction intervene exactly where people die. That asymmetry decides this debate.
[P1.2] First, fentanyl is structurally impossible to interdict. It needs no crops or farmers; it is synthesized from industrial chemicals, and since a lethal dose is a few milligrams, a city's annual supply fits in a car trunk. Decades of escalating seizures and penalties ran alongside rising deaths, and recent record seizures never even raised street prices. Worse, enforcement accelerates potency: pressure on heroin produced fentanyl; pressure on fentanyl now produces nitazenes and xylazine mixes.
[P1.3] Harsher penalties push the market toward stronger, easier-to-hide drugs — the opposite of saving lives.
[P1.4] Trafficking penalties fare no better: they sweep up low-level couriers and addicted street dealers — often the very people at risk of overdose — while the chemists and organizers above them are instantly replaceable.
[P1.5] Second, treatment and harm reduction work exactly where deaths occur. Methadone and buprenorphine cut mortality among people with opioid use disorder by roughly half, yet only a minority of eligible Americans receive them; the expansion headroom is enormous. Emergency departments and jails — where overdose risk spikes after release — can start buprenorphine the same day. Naloxone reverses fatal overdoses directly, whether or not the user ever seeks treatment.
[P1.6] And the first sustained national decline in overdose deaths arrived alongside naloxone saturation and treatment growth, not any enforcement breakthrough.
[P1.7] Third, weigh the five-year window. Treatment capacity and naloxone saturation scale within years; no plausible interdiction surge can constrain a synthetic supply that regenerates in a lab. One side of this motion attacks demand at the point of death; the other chases a supply its own pressure makes deadlier. Over five years, only one of those curves bends.
