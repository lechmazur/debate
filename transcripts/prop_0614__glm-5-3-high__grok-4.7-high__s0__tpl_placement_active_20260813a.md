[P1.1] Three claims win this debate if I defend them: the harm is documented and severe, the buyers cannot be trusted with the tools, and a ban costs democracies almost nothing.
[P1.2] Start with the record. Commercial spyware sold to non-democratic regimes has not occasionally been misused—it has been used, systematically, against the exact people those regimes cannot lawfully silence. Pegasus, sold by Israel's NSO Group, turned up on the phones of journalists investigating corruption, opposition politicians, and human rights defenders from Mexico to Saudi Arabia. Associates of Jamal Khashoggi were targeted with the technology before and after his murder.
[P1.3] FinFisher's spyware reached activists tracked by security services in Ethiopia and the Gulf. These are not edge cases or rogue operators; they are the predictable output of handing surveillance infrastructure to governments that jail dissidents and lack any independent court to check interception. When the tool is a scalpel for autocrats, "misuse" is just use.
[P1.4] Second, the trust argument fails on its own terms. A non-democratic regime by definition lacks the institutional brakes—independent judiciary, free press, meaningful oversight—that make surveillance powers tolerable anywhere. Selling to such a state is not a neutral commercial transaction; it is exporting the capability to persecute without recourse. Vendors cannot contract their way around that, because the buyer writes and rewrites the rules.
[P1.5] Third, the cost side is nearly empty. Democracies that need offensive cyber capability can develop it in-house or buy under strict end-use controls among allies. Nothing about banning sales to autocracies deprives us of defense. Meanwhile the current market produces concrete blowback: exploits circulate, vendors sell to anyone with cash, and allied journalists and diplomats get spied on with Western-built tools.
[P1.6] The proposition asks only that governments stop being arms merchants for digital repression. That is modest, enforceable through existing export-control machinery, and long overdue.
