[P1.1] The euro was a political project wearing an economic costume, and the costume doesn't fit. Europe is not an optimal currency area: labor mobility remains limited, fiscal transfers between member states are minimal, and business cycles stay stubbornly asynchronous. When Germany booms and Greece contracts, a single interest rate cannot serve both. The monetary union removed the one adjustment mechanism—exchange rates—that could have cushioned regional shocks, replacing flexibility with rigidity.
[P1.2] Consider the 2010s crisis. Countries trapped in the eurozone faced a brutal choice: abandon the currency or endure crushing austerity. Greece's economy contracted by roughly a quarter, unemployment soared past 25%, and youth joblessness reached catastrophic levels—all because devaluation was off the table. The United Kingdom, outside the euro, recovered faster precisely because sterling could fall and the Bank of England could cut rates to suit domestic conditions.
[P1.3] The euro transformed a severe recession into a lost decade for the periphery, while creditor nations accumulated surpluses that the system itself made impossible to recycle.
[P1.4] Beyond economics, the currency corroded European solidarity. It created a creditor-debtor dynamic that pitted Berlin against Athens, fueling resentment and populist backlash across the continent. Monetary policy—one of the most consequential levers of government—was removed from democratic accountability, handed to technocrats in Frankfurt who answer to no single electorate. This technocratic insulation bred the very Euroskepticism that now threatens the Union's cohesion.
[P1.5] Without the euro, member states would retain the tools to manage their own economies: independent interest rates, competitive exchange rates, and fiscal space unconstrained by arbitrary deficit rules. The EU would be a looser but more stable association of sovereign democracies, not a rigid monetary straitjacket that amplifies every crisis and deepens every divide. Strength comes from resilience and democratic consent, not from a currency that forces incompatible economies into the same mold.

[C1.1] The proposition rests on a false choice between national monetary sovereignty and EU strength, ignoring the concrete benefits the euro has delivered that far outweigh its adjustment costs. First, the euro eliminated the chronic currency volatility that held back European integration for decades. Before 1999, cross-border trade and investment were burdened by exchange rate hedging costs and recurrent speculative attacks, like the 1992 ERM crisis that forced multiple currencies to devalue and deepened economic rifts between member states. The euro removed this friction, boosting intra-EU trade and integrating capital markets in ways no loose collection of national currencies could replicate.
[C1.2] Second, PRO’s framing of the 2010s crisis misassigns blame. Greece’s economic collapse stemmed from decades of domestic fiscal fraud, unsustainable public spending, and structural labor market rigidities that predated euro adoption. Far from being a straitjacket, the euro provided a collective backstop no individual member state could have accessed alone: the European Stability Mechanism, banking union, and later COVID-era recovery funds like Next Generation EU allowed the bloc to mutualize risk and support struggling regions without fragmenting. The UK, outside the euro, did not recover meaningfully faster: it faced its own sluggish growth, austerity, and regional inequality, with no mechanism to share risk with European partners.
[C1.3] Third, the euro is a core source of EU geopolitical strength. As the world’s second reserve currency, it gives the bloc leverage in global trade, sanctions policy, and financial governance that 27 separate national currencies could never match. The democratic accountability critique ignores that ECB independence protects member states from the short-term political pressure that has repeatedly triggered hyperinflation and currency collapse in Europe’s history. The euro has not eroded solidarity—it has created the mutual interdependence that makes EU cohesion durable, rather than a fragile alliance of sovereign states prone to competitive devaluations and trade spats.
