[P1.1] A brilliant agronomist in Senegal who discovers, with Wololof-speaking farmers, a drought practice that works in the Sahel has two choices: publish in English for journals that will count, or publish in Wolof for the people who can use it and watch her career stall. That forced choice, repeated millions of times, is how English dominance harms non-anglophone cultures and knowledge systems more than it connects them.
[P1.2] It decides what counts as knowledge. Top journals, conferences, databases, and search engines reward English, so questions must be framed in English categories to be legible. Concepts rooted in other languages — relational ideas of land, health, kinship, or obligation with no clean English equivalent — are flattened, footnoted, or discarded. Local-language research is rarely cited, indexed, or taught, even when it is more accurate locally.
[P1.3] Whole ways of observing nature and society lose funding, students, and prestige, not because they failed, but because they spoke the wrong language.
[P1.4] Dominance also starves languages themselves. When schooling, promotion, publishing, and online visibility all signal that English is serious and the mother tongue is private or backward, parents rationally push children toward English, and creators follow the money and algorithms. The local language retreats from science, law, technology, and literature. A language that cannot name new medicines, software, or court decisions in its own words becomes harder to think, teach, and live a modern life in — a self-fulfilling decline.
[P1.5] And the benefits are taxed and uneven. Non-native speakers spend extra years, editing fees, slower reading, and negotiation disadvantages just to reach a starting line native speakers inherit free. In business, the side drafting the contract in its own idioms controls ambiguity; online, English content is made for and ranked for anglophone audiences. This is not neutral sharing.
[P1.6] It is a hierarchy where anglophones keep the home-field advantage while others pay to enter, translate themselves, and too often leave their own knowledge behind.

[C1.1] A shared lingua franca is not an engine of cultural erasure; it is the universal infrastructure that protects non-anglophone societies from scientific isolation and economic marginalization. English today is no longer an imperial possession. With non-native speakers vastly outnumbering native ones, it operates as an open-access global commons—a neutral coordinating protocol, much like open-source code or the metric system.
[C1.2] Consider the Senegalese agronomist PRO invokes. Far from being silenced, she relies on English to access drought-resistant seed genomics developed in India, satellite climate data from Japan, and open-source hydrology tools from Brazil. Without a singular global standard, scientific collaboration collapses into thousands of fractured linguistic silos. Smaller cultures would be entirely cut off from the frontier of human discovery, forced to reinvent mRNA vaccines or climate models in isolation.
[C1.3] English does not force knowledge to be abandoned; it allows non-anglophone researchers to cross-pollinate directly with one another without needing millions of bilateral translation pipelines.
[C1.4] Furthermore, English is the primary vehicle through which non-anglophone cultures protect and project their own knowledge systems. From securing international patents for indigenous botanical medicines to presenting human rights violations at the UN, a shared tongue gives local communities a megaphone against regional oppression and global exploitation. When local insights are translated into the global standard, they don't vanish—they become part of the indelible global canon.
[C1.5] Nor is modern knowledge zero-sum. Bilingualism is the global norm: farmers and scientists routinely apply discoveries in Wolof or Hindi while publishing and negotiating in English. The alternative to a global lingua franca is not a flourishing paradise of untranslated local dialects, but crippling transaction costs, knowledge monopolies held by a few massive language blocs, and systemic exclusion of the developing world. English dominance has democratized the global frontier, ensuring that geography and mother tongue no longer dictate who can learn, trade, and discover.

Researchers who share no colonial language must still meet on anglophone turf, in anglophone journals, under anglophone categories, to be counted as collaborating at all. [P2.4] Knowledge that fits English framing travels; knowledge that does not — about land as kin rather than property, illness as social rupture rather than isolated pathology — is simplified until it fits, or left uncited.
