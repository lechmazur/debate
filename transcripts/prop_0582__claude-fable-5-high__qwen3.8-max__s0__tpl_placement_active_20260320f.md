[P1.1] Start with the ledger. Medicare beneficiaries are the most expensive patients in America precisely because they carry decades of accumulated obesity-driven disease: heart failure, strokes, kidney failure, joint replacements, diabetes complications. A single heart attack hospitalization runs tens of thousands of dollars; a single dialysis patient costs roughly $90,000 a year. These are the costs GLP-1s attack directly.
[P1.2] Large cardiovascular outcome trials in people with obesity but without diabetes showed roughly a 20% reduction in major cardiovascular events, plus meaningful slowing of kidney disease and reduced heart-failure symptoms. In a population where those events are frequent, near-term, and catastrophically expensive, the savings arrive inside the five-year window—not in some distant actuarial future.
[P1.3] Now the cost side, where opponents rely on yesterday's prices. Net prices for GLP-1s are collapsing: manufacturers already sell direct-to-consumer around $500 a month, semaglutide faces Medicare price negotiation taking effect within this window, oral formulations and competitor entrants are multiplying, and broad Medicare coverage itself gives the government enormous leverage to force prices toward hundreds, not thousands, per month. The relevant comparison is negotiated net price versus avoided acute care—not list price versus nothing.
[P1.4] Two structural points seal it. First, "broad coverage" does not mean every senior takes the drug. Real-world uptake concentrates among the sickest patients—exactly where the offsets are largest per dollar spent—and high discontinuation rates, whatever their clinical downsides, mechanically cap drug spending while patients retain months of accrued risk reduction.
[P1.5] Second, the motion asks about total US healthcare spending, not one federal line item. Medicare coverage drags down prices system-wide, reduces the cost-shifting hospitals impose on private payers when treating uncompensated cardiac and renal crises, and prevents complications whose costs ripple across Medicaid and commercial insurance.
[P1.6] The choice is between paying a falling price for prevention or paying full freight for heart attacks, strokes, amputations, and dialysis. Over five years, in this population, prevention is the cheaper ledger.
