[C1.1] PRO's premise is right: impunity for large-scale environmental destruction is real. The proposed remedy is a prosecutor's fantasy that would make accountability worse.
[C1.2] Start with the definition PRO calls narrow. "Severe, widespread, or long-term" is a disjunctive threshold — any one prong can trigger it. Is a hydroelectric dam long-term? Is seasonal burning widespread?
[C1.3] Is a mine severe? Those questions get answered not by ecology but by whoever holds the prosecutor's office. Criminal law cannot rest on thresholds that indeterminate without becoming a tool of selective enforcement. Consider the template: the one crime added to the Rome Statute since 1998 — aggression — took decades of negotiation and emerged so hedged that it is functionally dormant.
[C1.4] That is the realistic model, not a swift new era.
[C1.5] Second, the binding constraint is not definition but custody. The ICC cannot arrest a sitting leader of a non-member state. The states whose decisions actually move the planetary needle will not join, or will carve out exemptions. The practical result is that the crime lands hardest on weak states and Global South governments whose land-use decisions are the most visible and least diplomatically shielded.
[C1.6] PRO's deterrence claim requires certainty of enforcement; international environmental prosecutions would supply stigma without probability — and stigma is precisely what a state can externalize onto a contractor.
[C1.7] Third, the analogy to cultural destruction fails. The Timbuktu cultural-destruction case worked because the harm was discrete, documented, and attributable to identifiable acts. Ecosystem harm is cumulative, multi-causal, and decades in the making. Proving beyond reasonable doubt that one minister's approval caused one irreversible loss means either almost no prosecutions or arbitrary ones — likely both.
[C1.8] Meanwhile, a treaty campaign would absorb the political capital that actually protects ecosystems: domestic liability rules, permitting regimes, supply-chain standards, trade measures, and funding for enforcement where the harm occurs.
[C1.9] Recognition would not close the accountability gap. It would relabel it as progress.

But every serious international crime uses a gravity threshold. Crimes against humanity require attacks that are “widespread or systematic”; genocide requires destruction of a group “in whole or in part.” Those are workable because they set a high bar and are tested by evidence, not a prosecutor’s whim.
[P2.2] Ecocide can be narrowed further with unlawfulness and knowledge requirements. Aggression is the wrong model: it stalled because it regulates inter-state force, not private and official decisions causing mass environmental harm.
[P2.3] The custody objection proves too much. The ICC cannot always arrest war criminals either, yet war crimes law still drives national prosecutions, extradition, and institutional change. Recognition would impose domestic obligations, reach parent companies and financiers in states that join, and enable cooperation. Selective enforcement is worse now: a Northern executive can hide behind a contractor while the damage occurs in a weak state.
[P2.4] Under ecocide, the person who authorizes or facilitates destruction can be liable as an aider or co-perpetrator. Deterrence does not require certainty; real personal criminal risk changes decisions even when enforcement is uneven.
[P2.5] The cultural-destruction contrast fails. Criminal law already proves causation in complex mass harms through modes of participation and command responsibility. Much ecocide is discrete: authorizing mine tailings to be dumped into a river or draining a protected wetland is identifiable and attributable. For cumulative damage, prosecutors need show material contribution, not sole causation.
[P2.6] Hard proof screens out weak cases; it does not make all enforcement arbitrary.
[P2.7] Finally, recognition is not a substitute for domestic rules—it is the criminal backstop that makes them credible. Fines are already priced into project budgets. Permits and liability regimes lack teeth if no individual can face prosecution. The real impunity is not caused by criminalization; it is caused by the absence of a legal category for the worst environmental destruction.
