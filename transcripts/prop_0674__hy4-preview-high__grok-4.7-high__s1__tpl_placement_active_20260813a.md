[P1.1] Deterrence is a forecast about tomorrow, not relief about today. The only credible way to keep Russian aggression from spreading is to convince Moscow that further conquest will fail. Sustained aid does that; an immediate negotiated settlement concluded while Russian forces occupy Ukrainian ground mostly teaches the opposite. It rewards the gamble, freezes the front under the aggressor’s presence, and leaves a battle-hardened army on NATO’s flank with time to rebuild.
[P1.2] The mechanism is deterrence by denial and cost. Every shipment of artillery, air defenses, long-range fires, and training raises the price of the next offensive. A Russian commander facing prepared defenses, working air cover, and strikes on supply nodes cannot assume the next advance will be cheap. That calculation—burned into Russian military and political judgment—deters the wider war: pressure on Moldova, the Baltics, or NATO’s eastern flank stops looking rational.
[P1.3] Aid also binds Western credibility; allies read Article 5 by whether resolve lasts after headlines fade.
[P1.4] History warns against mistaking ceasefire for peace. Post-2014 talks froze the line but not Russian ambition; Moscow rebuilt, rearmed, and returned with a larger invasion in 2022. Paperwork deterred nothing because the incentive—grab territory, absorb pain, keep it—stayed intact. A settlement now, before Russian offensive power is attrited and Ukraine has leverage, repeats that mistake.
[P1.5] It is a pause Moscow can quit at will, not a barrier it fears crossing.
[P1.6] Sustained aid is the precondition for any durable settlement. Talks deter only when they rest on verified capability, harsh facts on the ground, and credible punishment for cheating. Talks without leverage are not diplomacy; they are rehearsal for the next invasion. Remove weapons while Russia still believes it can win later, and you buy today’s quiet with tomorrow’s larger war.
[P1.7] Keep aid flowing, and Russia’s best prediction is that the next neighbor it attacks will be defended the same way. That is deterrence.

[C1.1] Open-ended military aid is the riskier path to wider war, not the safer one. It keeps a grinding conflict alive against a nuclear state and teaches Moscow that pressure, escalation, and patience can still pay. An immediate negotiated settlement is more likely to deter further Russian aggression: it stops the escalatory cycle, conserves Western power for actual treaty defense, and replaces an attrition gamble with a freeze Russia must live with.
[C1.2] The denial theory treats Russian learning as a simple cost curve. It misses the menu a long war creates. While weapons flow and the front stays hot, Moscow has reason to probe NATO airspace, threaten infrastructure, interdict supply lines, and brandish nuclear risk to fracture the coalition. Those are routes to wider aggression.
[C1.3] A ceasefire closes them. Settlement is not a prize for conquest; it is the removal of daily incentives to widen the fight before one of those probes becomes a crisis.
[C1.4] Aid also weakens the deterrent that matters most. Munitions and political capital sunk into an indefinite Ukrainian war are unavailable for the Baltics and the Black Sea. If support later thins because no end is in sight, Russia concludes that Western resolve depreciates. Settlement while stocks and cohesion remain lets alliances rearm, harden Article 5, and make the next grab costlier than living with a frozen line.
[C1.5] Nor does 2014 prove talks fail and attrition succeeds. A weak, unenforced pause plus a later window of Western distraction produced 2022. That is an argument for a harder equilibrium and clearer red lines, not for fighting without an exit the West may not sustain. Demanding Russia first be ground down repeats the gamble at higher stakes.
[C1.6] What Russia expects next decides wider deterrence. A long stalemate pushes a revisionist state toward riskier breaks. A hard freeze, backed by rebuilt forces and unmistakable limits, is more likely to keep the next crisis from becoming the next invasion.
