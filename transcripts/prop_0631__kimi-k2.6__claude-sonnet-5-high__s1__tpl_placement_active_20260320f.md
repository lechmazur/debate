[P1.1] Copyright law is a bargained-for monopoly, not a participation trophy. The Constitution authorizes Congress to promote the progress of science and useful arts by securing exclusive rights to "Authors." Courts have consistently read that mandate to require human origination. Feist established that originality demands not merely effort or expense, but a modicum of creative choice made by a person.
[P1.2] An AI system trained on the corpus of human culture and emitting probabilistic outputs makes no creative choices; it interpolates and regurgitates. The user who types "a cyberpunk cat in neon rain" exercises judgment in conceiving an idea, but copyright does not protect ideas—it protects expression. A prompt, however detailed, leaves the machine in control of the pixel-by-pixel result. Conception is not control over the fixed final expression.
[P1.3] Without the proposed standard, we erase the boundary between idea and expression that the entire regime depends upon, granting monopolies for works no human mind authored and inviting firms to fence off the cultural commons through automated enclosure.
[P1.4] The consequences are immediate and severe. If raw AI output gains protection, a single operator could generate and claim exclusive rights over thousands of novels, images, or recordings in an afternoon, flooding the market and displacing the very human creators the system exists to reward. Copyright would devolve into a rent-extraction tool for machine exhaust rather than an engine of culture. The proposed rule does not ban AI; it simply asks the claimant to document that a human shaped the final work through substantial creative decisions—editing, selecting, arranging, or iteratively refining output.
[P1.5] That preserves protection for the graphic designer who painstakingly guides a diffusion model across hundreds of revisions, while denying it to the commuter who accepts the first draft. The law should protect human expression, not automate monopoly.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds modest, but look closely: it does not ask whether a human authored the work. It asks whether a human can prove it, with paperwork, at the moment of claiming rights. That evidentiary hurdle is where this policy goes wrong, and where CON wins.
[C1.2] First, it's redundant where it might do good, and harmful where it doesn't. Existing doctrine already denies protection to outputs lacking human authorship. Feist and the Copyright Office's own guidance on AI-assisted works already sort protectable human contribution from unprotectable machine noise, case by case. The proposition adds nothing to that except a formal documentation mandate — and mandates like that don't screen out bad actors, they screen out the poor ones.
[C1.3] A well-funded studio can hire counsel to generate a paper trail: version logs, edit histories, sign-off memos, "iterative refinement" documented by design. A solo illustrator who guided a diffusion model through two hundred intuitive tweaks over a weekend, the way artists have always worked, has no such record. The rule doesn't stop enclosure of the commons by big players — it hands them the enclosure tool while disarming the individual creators copyright is supposed to protect.
[C1.4] Second, "document substantial creative control" has no stable meaning. Is thirty prompt revisions enough? Is manual color-correction after generation enough? Courts already struggle with originality; now they must also referee proof-of-process disputes, turning every AI-assisted work into a discovery battle over screenshots and timestamps.
[C1.5] That cost falls hardest on independent artists who can't afford litigation, while corporations absorb it as overhead.
[C1.6] Third, this treats AI tools as categorically suspect in a way we've never demanded of other creative technology. Photographers don't document their camera settings to prove authorship; use of Photoshop's content-aware fill doesn't require a creative-control affidavit. Singling out AI for a documentation precondition doesn't protect human authorship — it just makes proving human authorship a genuinely different, harder, and more discriminatory bar for exactly the people this debate claims to protect.
