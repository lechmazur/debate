[P1.1] Historic-district rules are not neutral heritage policy; they are often zoning by another name. When a city bars apartments or height increases across a whole protected district, it suppresses housing supply exactly where infrastructure, jobs, and transit already exist. The result is not preservation alone: it is higher rents, longer commutes, displacement of lower-income residents, and sprawl at the fringe.
[P1.2] The proposition does not require erasing architectural history. It asks cities to relax protections when they block needed density. That can mean permitting mid-rise infill behind a retained facade, converting a protected but underused structure, or removing district-wide height caps while still protecting individual landmarks. Preservation becomes more principled when it saves specific buildings rather than preserving a low-density neighborhood in amber.
[P1.3] The equity stakes are severe. Historic districts frequently sit in high-demand central neighborhoods, and their restrictions often become a tool for incumbent homeowners to block apartments. The cost of that exclusion falls hardest on renters, service workers, families, and younger people who need access to jobs and schools. A building's age should not give a neighborhood veto over who gets to live nearby.
[P1.4] Density in existing neighborhoods is also climate and infrastructure policy. Every apartment blocked near a transit line tends to push growth outward, paving over open land and locking in car dependence. Relaxing a historic cap is often less environmentally damaging than building the equivalent housing at the edge of the region.
[P1.5] Some architecturally significant buildings will be lost. That trade-off is real but acceptable when the alternative is preserving structures at the expense of thousands of needed homes. Architecture matters, but housing, displacement, and access matter more. A city should be a place people can live in, not a museum that only the well-housed can afford.

[C1.3] That is demolition without a housing strategy.
[C1.4] Worse, the trade rarely helps the renters invoked to justify it. The neighborhood loses both its architecture and its affordability, and longtime residents are displaced in the name of future density that they will never occupy.
[C1.5] What is lost cannot be recreated. Those places anchor tourism, small business, and civic pride, and they make density livable by giving crowded life beauty and continuity.
[C1.6] We do not face a choice between museums and homes. Build up transit corridors, convert offices, legalize apartments on the vast land outside districts. Protect what makes the city worth living in while solving the shortage where it actually exists.
