[P1.1] A voice is not a deliverable like a costume or a camera rig. It is a recognizable piece of a person, and once it is modeled, it can say anything, forever, in any language, in any project — including ones the performer would have refused. That is the thing being licensed. The question is whether a signature buried on page 34 of a take-it-or-leave-it contract can honestly be called permission to do that.
[P1.2] It cannot, for three reasons.
[P1.3] First, boilerplate fails the basic test of informed consent. Consider an actor who signed in 2015 language covering "all media now known or hereafter devised," then discovers in 2027 that a synthetic version of her voice is performing dialogue she never spoke, in a language she doesn't speak, for a sequel she turned down. She didn't agree to that. She couldn't have — the capability didn't exist when she signed.
[P1.4] Consent to an unimaginable future use is not consent; it's a lottery ticket the studio holds.
[P1.5] Second, the bargaining conditions make boilerplate meaningless. Most performers are not stars with lawyers. They are working actors, dubbing artists, and voice performers presented with a standard form at the moment they most need the job. Contract law already recognizes that when terms are non-negotiable, unread, and cover something intimate and irreversible, we demand specific assent — that is exactly why we require separate, explicit sign-off for things like medical procedures and biometric data.
[P1.6] Third — and this is where CON's efficiency argument collapses — opt-in does not ban AI dubbing. It channels it. A clear, specific consent clause with defined scope and compensation gives studios something boilerplate never will: clean title. No mid-release scandal, no performer publicly disowning a film, no litigation over whether 2015 language reaches 2027 synthesis.
[P1.7] Ambiguity is the expensive option.
[P1.8] The choice is not innovation versus artists. It is whether the people whose voices make this technology possible get asked first.

Synthetic voice is simply a better tool for the same licensed job: delivering the actor’s own approved performance across languages.
[C1.2] To suddenly demand a second, separate opt-in is to pretend dubbing is a new exploitation when it is the old one done more faithfully.
[C1.3] That distinction destroys PRO’s lottery-ticket panic. The actor who signed in 2015 did not imagine neural synthesis, but she did imagine — because her contract said so — that her performance would be altered, revoiced, and sold in markets she would never visit in languages she does not speak. Human dubbers already speak lines she never spoke. The purpose was foreseeable even if the software was not, just as streaming was foreseeable under contracts written for videocassettes.
[C1.4] An opt-in veto would strangle exactly what it claims to protect. Localization involves dozens of performers, minor roles, deceased actors, background voices. Give each a separate holdout right and the math collapses: independent films, documentaries, and non-English cinema will not clear rights market-by-market. The result is fewer dubbed works, smaller global audiences, and smaller residuals for working actors.
[C1.5] Stars with lawyers will extract ransoms; day players will simply be written around or replaced by fully synthetic voices that owe them nothing.
[C1.6] Freedom of contract plus collective standards is the fairer safeguard. Broad language negotiated by unions can set pay for AI dubbing, limit deceptive alteration, and allow performers to price the right upfront when they most want leverage — before shooting. Individual opt-in sounds empowering, but in a take-it-or-leave-it market it just means no job unless you surrender the veto for free.
[C1.7] Keep dubbing governed like dubbing, not like organ donation.
