[P1.1] The skill lives in the worker, not the badge. When a hospital funds a nurse's certification course, the competence that results walks on two legs, and it serves patients wherever she works. Yet today, if she moves across town to a hospital with safer staffing, she forfeits whatever balance remains in her training account, and often faces a repayment clause for courses already taken. Nothing about that money was ever a gift to her; it was deferred compensation she earned by showing up.
[P1.2] Letting her keep it within the same sector simply honours the deal.
[P1.3] Three reasons this matters beyond fairness.
[P1.4] First, the economics of sector-specific training are broken without portability. Any single firm hesitates to fund skills a rival can hire away, so the sector collectively underinvests. The fix is well known: pooled, worker-attached accounts. Construction unions have run multi-employer training funds for decades; the Netherlands finances training through sector funds; France attaches a personal training account to the individual.
[P1.5] These systems exist because firm-locked training produces too little training.
[P1.6] Second, non-portable accounts are a quiet monopsony tool. A worker who would lose thousands by leaving accepts a lower raise, a worse schedule, or a bullying manager. Repayment traps have drawn regulatory scrutiny precisely because they function as debt holding people in place. Mobility is how wages rise in a sector; portability restores it.
[P1.7] Third, the same-sector limit answers the obvious objection. Employers worry about financing a competitor's workforce, but within a sector, the churn washes out: the hospital that loses a nurse next year hires one trained on someone else's account. Funding becomes a shared infrastructure, not a loss.
[P1.8] If an employer only invests in training it can hold hostage, that is not investment. It is a leash. Cut the leash, and both the worker and the sector grow.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds generous until you ask who pays the piper. Employer-funded training is not a gift—it is a calculated investment, made on the bet that the worker stays long enough to recoup it through productivity, reduced errors, and institutional knowledge. The moment you make that investment portable across the sector, you destroy the bet. And when the bet is destroyed, the training stops.
[C1.2] Consider the free-rider problem. If Hospital A spends fifty thousand dollars certifying a surgical technician, and she walks next door to Hospital B keeping that funded training intact, Hospital A absorbs the loss while Hospital B reaps the benefit without paying a cent. Rational employers respond not by competing to train more generously, but by training less—or not at all. The result is not a thriving sector with shared infrastructure; it is a sector that collectively underinvests because no one wants to be the sucker funding a competitor's workforce.
[C1.3] The "same sector" qualifier paper[P1.7] over this crack. Sectors are not monoliths. A nurse trained in one hospital's electronic records system, one chain's patient-handling protocols, one firm's safety culture, is not fungible across rivals. Making accounts portable does not honour a deal—it ignores that the deal was never unconditional.
[C1.4] Employers fund specific, proprietary competencies tied to their operations, not generic human capital.
[C1.5] Even where training is genuinely transferable, portability creates moral hazard. Workers face no consequence for job-hopping after receiving expensive training, so they treat employer investment as a free ride. The training account becomes a subsidy the sector cannot sustain, and the workers who most need training—those in smaller firms with thinner margins—lose access first.
[C1.6] The proposition promises mobility but delivers stagnation: less training, weaker institutions, and a race to the bottom where employers ration investment rather than compete on quality.

[P2.1] The free-rider argument is the strongest card in the opposing hand, so look closely at what it concedes. "No one wants to be the sucker funding a competitor's workforce" [C1.2] is not a prediction about portability; it is a description of the status quo. Firm-locked training already produces exactly this underinvestment, because every rational employer already fears the rival poaching its nurse. That is why construction trades and Dutch sector funds moved to pooled, worker-attached accounts in the first place.
[P2.2] Pooling is the cure for free-riding, not its cause: Hospital B hires a technician trained on A's account the same year A hires one trained on B's. Across a sector, the ledger balances, and only the training volume changes.
[P2.3] Notice also that the $50,000 figure is spent at the moment of training, under either system. What portability changes is not whether the employer pays, but whether the worker is held hostage afterwards.
[P2.4] The "proprietary competencies" point [C1.3, C1.4] runs into a contradiction. If the training is genuinely firm-specific, one chain's records system or handling protocols, then it has no value next door, so there is no free ride and nothing for Hospital A to lose. If it is transferable, like a surgical certification, then it is precisely the generic human capital a sector needs more of. The objection cannot be true in both directions at once.
[P2.5] Moral hazard [C1.5] inverts the actual asymmetry. The account does not pay cash; it buys more training. Nobody job-hops to collect a course catalogue. Meanwhile employers already fire trained workers at will with no "consequence."
[P2.6] Portability only restores symmetry.
[P2.7] And smaller firms are the clearest winners: they cannot fund certifications alone, but under a sector pool their hires arrive already trained.
[P2.8] Strip away the rhetoric and the case against portability is a defence of the leash.

[C2.1] The pooling argument sounds elegant until you recognise it smuggles in a new assumption: that employers will voluntarily contribute to a shared fund they no longer control. [P2.1-2.2] describes construction unions and Dutch sector funds as proof the model works, but those are compulsory levy systems, not voluntary generosity. Strip the compulsion and you are back to the free-rider problem wearing a nicer shirt. Hospital A still reasons: why fund the pool when Hospital B will simply hire my trainee next quarter?
[C2.2] Portability does not make the ledger balance automatically; it makes each contribution look riskier, and riskier contributions mean smaller contributions.
[C2.3] The proprietary-versus-generic contradiction [P2.4] is a false dilemma. Most real training is neither purely firm-specific nor purely generic. A nurse learns Hospital A's protocols, yes, but also builds clinical judgment that transfers immediately. Making the entire account portable rewards the generic portion while the firm absorbs the loss on the specific portion.
[C2.4] That is not symmetry; it is subsidised poaching.
[C2.5] On moral hazard [P2.5-2.6], the claim that nobody job-hops for a course catalogue misunderstands incentives. Workers do not need cash in hand to respond to portability. The knowledge that five years of funded training travel with them lowers the cost of leaving any single employer. That alone increases churn, and churn raises recruitment and onboarding costs across the sector—costs that fall hardest on smaller firms [C1.5], not on them as winners.
[C2.6] Calling the status quo a "leash" [P2.8] is rhetoric, not analysis. The leash is what made the training happen in the first place. Cut it and you do not liberate workers; you starve them of investment. The real question is not whether mobility matters—it does—but whether destroying the funding mechanism that creates skilled workers serves anyone except short-term job-hoppers.
