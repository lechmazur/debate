[P1.1] Gun violence is not an evenly distributed, unpredictable phenomenon; it is concentrated within narrow networks trapped in cycles of immediate, retaliatory conflict. To stop shootings, public safety policy must intervene directly at the point of transmission. Scaling civilian community violence intervention (CVI) programs is systematically more cost-effective than hiring additional police officers because CVI deploys a precise, upstream mechanism at a fraction of the fiscal burden.
[P1.2] First, consider the operational mechanism. Police officers are primarily structured for generalized patrol and post-incident enforcement. A uniformed officer cannot easily mediate an interpersonal feud in a living room, nor do they possess the non-punitive credibility required to convince a wounded individual not to retaliate. Civilian intervention models—such as street outreach workers and hospital-based violence intervention specialists—utilize credible messengers embedded within the community.
[P1.3] These specialists intervene at the exact moments of highest volatility, neutralizing disputes among the small micro-population driving gun violence before a trigger is pulled.
[P1.4] Second, the economic comparison is unequivocal. Adding sworn police officers incurs massive, compounding lifetime costs: competitive salaries, overtime, healthcare, lifelong pension obligations, and extensive training. Furthermore, an officer's time is inevitably diluted across traffic enforcement, administrative reports, and non-violent calls. In contrast, CVI programs deploy lean, targeted teams focused exclusively on high-risk mediation and trauma support.
[P1.5] Dollar for dollar, funding civilian intervention prevents far more shootings, emergency room visits, and incarceration costs than absorbing the permanent, multi-million-dollar overhead of expanding a police department.
[P1.6] Hiring additional police is an expensive, blunt approach to a hyper-specific crisis. Scaling civilian community violence intervention provides a surgical, proactive solution that saves more lives for far less public money.

[C1.1] The proposition’s core error is comparing a fully burdened police expansion against a fantasy version of civilian violence intervention. It treats CVI as if scaling simply reproduces small, successful pilots at the same low cost. Scaling changes the program.
[C1.2] Once a city moves from a small team of credible, experienced outreach workers to citywide coverage, it must hire less-proven staff, supervise them in armed disputes, absorb turnover from trauma and threats, fund liability insurance, data systems, hospital security, and 24-hour availability. These are exactly the permanent administrative costs the proposition criticizes in police budgets—but now without arrest powers, training standards, or clear legal authority. The marginal CVI worker is not the exceptional mediator who made a pilot look good.
[C1.3] Second, the mechanism CVI depends on is police capacity. Mediation can deter a particular retaliation only when the credible alternative is swift enforcement. If a city has too few officers to investigate shootings, make arrests, or remove repeat shooters, violent actors can simply ignore an outreach worker. An officer rarely mediates a living-room feud, but the patrol and enforcement threat gives civilian mediators their leverage.
[C1.4] CVI does not substitute for that deterrent; it borrows from it. Hiring police raises the certainty of consequences across violent actors, not just those who voluntarily enter a conversation.
[C1.5] Finally, the denominator is suspect. The proposition claims dollar-for-dollar CVI prevents more shootings, but that comparison relies on small, selected, hard-to-replicate programs and attributes prevented violence too generously. Police hiring also suppresses robberies, group violence, and retaliatory cycles through arrest and incapacitation; measuring only shootings undercounts the protection police provide. Scaling CVI when enforcement is thin would raise costs while diluting the small-program effects that make the comparison seem favorable in the first place.
