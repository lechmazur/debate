[P1.1] Major review calls are where opacity costs most. An ordinary live mistake is part of sport; a title-shaping video decision with hidden rule, standard, and reasoning cannot claim the same deference. The proposal is limited: after major reviews, release the relevant referee/replay audio and a concise explanation identifying the rule, evidence, review standard, and why the on-field ruling stood or changed. Redactions can remove personal remarks or irrelevant chatter; “fuller” means enough to audit the logic, not a live feed of every breath.
[P1.2] Secrecy is not neutral. It protects the institution when trust is most fragile. When a late goal, foul, or boundary call swings a final, a terse statement shows only self-approval by the authority that made the call. Suspicion follows not because losses are corrupt, but because unverifiable power invites speculation.
[P1.3] Audio and reasons create a record: fans can hear whether the right angle was considered, whether the written standard was applied, and whether confusion comes from the rulebook rather than misconduct.
[P1.4] Transparency also makes officiating better. Explanations force leagues to articulate rules consistently across crews and weeks; inconsistent treatment of similar plays becomes visible and correctable. Teams adapt to known interpretations instead of guessing at oral doctrine. Referees benefit too: instead of trial by frozen frame and hostile clip, they can show a reasoned process.
[P1.5] Concealed correctness looks like favoritism; explained correctness can command assent even from the losing side.
[P1.6] The objections do not outweigh that. Officials deserve protection, but abuse is already fed by ambiguity, selective clips, and vague answers. Delayed, redacted releases protect privacy while exposing the logic. Game flow is unaffected because publication is postmatch. “Undermining authority” confuses blind deference with earned authority: high-stakes authority is stronger when it survives explanation.
[P1.7] A league that wants legitimacy should not make secrecy the price of trust.

[C1.6] Ambiguity feeds speculation. A quotable sentence feeds campaigns, threats, and outrage that outlive the match.
[C1.7] Publication also fails as a consistency machine. Similar plays are not identical plays. [C1.8] Explained correctness does not command assent from the side that lost the final. It hands that side a text to reject.
