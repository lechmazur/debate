[P1.1] Public funding should reward demonstrated student progress, not headcount. Enrollment-only funding pays a university the same whether its students graduate or drop out. That is a subsidy for churn: an institution can recruit aggressively, let struggling students leave with debt and no credential, and still receive full public support. A formula that ignores results treats failure as financially identical to success.
[P1.2] The public purpose of a university is not merely to admit students; it is to educate them into completion and economic mobility. Enrollment measures access, but access without completion can become harm. Tying funds to improvements in graduation rates makes that mission concrete. A university that raises completion among high-risk students from low to much higher performs a public good; a university with high raw graduation rates may simply be selecting students already likely to succeed.
[P1.3] Value-added measurement captures that difference, rewarding the institution rather than the incoming student profile.
[P1.4] Earnings are an imperfect but essential check. A degree that does not improve graduates' economic position is not delivering the full public return. Universities should not be paid the same for expanding programs that leave students no better off as for programs that connect students to work. Benchmarking earnings gains to regional wages and field, and measuring improvement over each university's own baseline, avoids simple rankings and focuses on real added value.
[P1.5] Critics will say outcome funding punishes institutions that serve low-income students. The reverse is true under enrollment-only funding: a university can enroll disadvantaged students, fail them, and keep the money. A properly designed value-added formula pays for the difficult work of getting those students through and into jobs. The choice is not between access and accountability.
[P1.6] It is between rewarding effortful improvement and continuing to pay equally for failure and success. Public money should follow demonstrated results.

[P2.2] Grade inflation has a similar limit: if a college lowers the bar, later licensing exams and earnings reveal the hollow credential. [P2.3] The death-spiral claim also reverses the politics. Under enrollment funding, a struggling institution is paid in full for poor completion, so it can keep failing. [P2.4] A college that adds advising, emergency aid, flexible scheduling and sees graduation improve would gain money. One that merely admits more students and hopes for the best would not. The formula pays for the support systems the objection claims to want.

[P4.1] A1: The term is an access-maintenance condition attached to the improvement payment. A campus earns the graduation or earnings premium only if it continues serving a comparable share—or comparable number—of high-need and first-generation students as at its baseline. If it quietly admits fewer such applicants, that composition floor is breached and the premium is reduced or withheld, even though raw graduation rises. This directly answers the worry in [C3.1]: the fall in first-generation enrollment is itself the funding cut.
[P4.2] The condition tracks enrolled-student counts by categories institutions already report, so it does not depend on a perfect risk model. Quiet exclusion stops being profitable.
[P4.3] A2: During the early implementation window, a struggling campus’s funding can stay flat, but only as a conditional bridge, not as the old enrollment subsidy. It would receive support while meeting near-term milestones: adviser caseloads, first-year course completion, early-alert contacts, and persistence. If the later graduation or earnings gains do not appear by the defined date, the outcome-linked tranche is withdrawn or not renewed. That is the difference from enrollment funding: enrollment-only pays flat forever for flat outcomes.
[P4.4] This pays flat temporarily against an enforceable improvement plan, so poor campuses are not forced to spend first and hope.
[P4.5] That dissolves the opponent’s two central objections. The access floor blocks the gaming-by-exclusion route; the conditional bridge blocks the death-spiral-by-lag route. What remains is the claim that any delayed accountability is unacceptable. But universities already accept multi-year evidence for budgets and accreditation.
[P4.6] Taxpayers should not be the only party forbidden from asking whether public money improved completion and earnings. The motion does not demand instant punishment; it ends indefinite payment for stagnation. That is the stronger public bargain.
