[P1.1] When government takes away housing benefits, denies lawful residence, or increases someone’s criminal exposure, it must remain answerable to the person whose life it is altering. The legal right should be simple: before such an adverse decision becomes final, an identifiable human with genuine authority must consider the evidence, hear the person’s response, and own the decision. Automation may assist; it cannot be the final decision-maker.
[P1.2] First, this makes contestability real. Automated systems often detect mismatches, not circumstances. A benefits database may record unreported income because two employers share a payroll provider; an immigration file may miss a document uploaded under another reference number. A person can explain both—but only if someone is legally required to listen and empowered to depart from the system’s output.
[P1.3] Optional “review” is inadequate when agencies can make it inaccessible, delayed, or merely a rubber stamp.
[P1.4] Second, human responsibility protects legality itself. Government decisions are not predictions like product recommendations; they are exercises of public power requiring reasons, discretion, and accountability. If nobody can explain why evidence was weighed as it was, appeals become attacks on an opaque process rather than examinations of a decision. A named decision-maker creates someone who must justify the outcome and whose reasoning courts can review.
[P1.5] That is not symbolism: it determines whether legal safeguards have anything to grip.
[P1.6] Third, the burden of automation falls most heavily on those least equipped to navigate it. People facing language barriers, disability, poverty, or detention cannot always translate their lives into the categories a system accepts. Equal justice requires more than giving everyone the same upload portal.
[P1.7] The objection will be cost and delay. But the motion does not demand a judge for every routine transaction or ban automated triage. It applies only where consequences are significant, and governments can prioritize those cases through deadlines and staffed review. Efficiency gained by making life-changing state power practically unchallengeable is not administrative progress; it is savings extracted from people’s rights.
[P1.8] Humans are fallible, but a human decision can be questioned, reasoned with, and held responsible. [clipped]

[C1.1] The proposition sounds humane, but it mistakes the symptom for the cure. What people actually need when government threatens their benefits, status, or liberty is accuracy, consistency, and a meaningful chance to contest an error. A legal right to a *human* decision-maker delivers none of that reliably, and it actively undermines the first two.
[C1.2] Start with the empirical record. Human adjudicators deciding materially identical cases—asylum claims, parole hearings, benefits appeals—routinely produce wildly different outcomes depending on which individual reviewed the file, their caseload that day, or unconscious bias. A named human is not a guarantee of fairness; it is a guarantee of variance. Structured, auditable decision rules can be more consistent and more reviewable than the discretion of an overworked caseworker who may spend four minutes on a file before signing off.
[C1.3] PRO treats "human" as a proxy for "careful," but the two routinely come apart under caseload pressure.
[C1.4] Second, the right as drafted is a backlog machine. "Significantly affect benefits, immigration status, or criminal exposure" covers an enormous share of state action. Mandating a human decision-maker for all of it, before finality, does not speed up justice for the vulnerable—it delays it. Picture a mother waiting on emergency housing assistance while her file sits in a queue for a scarce caseworker, when an automated system could have approved it same-day and flagged only the genuinely ambiguous cases for a person.
[C1.5] The people who suffer most from guaranteed delay are exactly the people PRO claims to protect.
[C1.6] Third, PRO's own fix—reasons, contestability, accountability—doesn't require a human at the front end at all. Give people a right to a clear explanation and a genuine, timely appeal to a human reviewer *when they contest* the outcome. That captures everything PRO's examples actually need—the payroll mismatch, the misfiled document—without freezing every routine determination behind scarce human bandwidth.
[C1.7] A rubber-stamping human is not accountability; a reasoned, appealable automated decision is better than an unreviewable human one. The resolution picks the wrong lever.
