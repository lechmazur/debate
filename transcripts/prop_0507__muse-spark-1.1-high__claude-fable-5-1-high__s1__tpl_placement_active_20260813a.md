[P1.1] Countries have learned the hard lesson of battery policy backwards. Paying billions to build gigafactories before there is durable, competitive demand creates white elephants, not industries. The state should create customers, not champions.
[P1.2] Large producer subsidies are structurally flawed. They require governments to pick specific firms, chemistries, and locations years before the market knows which will win. That invites lobbying capture, locks in yesterday's lithium-ion while solid-state or sodium displace it, and pours public money into capex that may never run at utilization. We have seen it: the wave of heavily subsidized European cell projects now delayed, scaled back or bankrupt despite generous aid, because cheap Chinese cells and softer EV uptake erased their business case.
[P1.3] The US version is not better - billions committed to plants still importing key materials and machines, with no guarantee of cost competitiveness.
[P1.4] Demand-side policy solves the incentive problem correctly. A guaranteed market - EV buyer credits tied to performance, feebates, stringent fleet CO2 standards, green public procurement for storage, contracts for difference for clean steel - tells every potential producer: if you can deliver cheaper, cleaner, more recyclable batteries, you will sell. It is technology-neutral, so it rewards innovation rather than incumbency. It leverages private capital instead of replacing it, because a credible demand curve de-risks investment across the supply chain without the state owning the risk of one factory.
[P1.5] It is also faster, cheaper, and legally sustainable. Demand incentives deploy this year; factories take five. They do not trigger WTO retaliation the way localized producer grants do, and they can be dialed down as costs fall.
[P1.6] If the goal is abundant, cheap, ever-better clean tech, you don't start by paying factories to exist. You pay to make clean products irresistible to buy.
