[P1.2] The most disaster-exposed coasts are no longer merely unlucky. Rising seas, stronger surge, saltwater in aquifers, and thinning barrier islands turn reconstruction into a ritual. [P1.4] It is a revolving bailout of geography.
[P1.5] Planned inland climate-refuge cities reverse the sequence. Site them on higher ground, with water, energy, transit, clinics, and jobs designed together instead of improvised in debris. [P1.6] State budgets are finite. A dollar spent elevating a house that will flood again is a dollar not spent on housing that will still be dry in 2060.
[P1.7] This is also equity. Repeated rebuilds trap renters, service workers, and the elderly in zones the affluent can leave. Chaotic displacement already happens; it just happens without a destination. A planned city is the difference between exile and a functioning community.
[P1.8] Social fabric survives a move far better than it survives three consecutive disasters.
[P1.9] Heritage, ports, and property values will be offered as reasons to stay. They matter. They do not repeal hydrology. A port can be adapted.
[P1.10] A drowned neighborhood cannot be dried by affection. Every cycle of coastal reconstruction crowds out the inland capacity we will need when the next surge arrives. Invest where the ground holds. Stop paying the ocean twice.

[C1.2] Start with cost and reliability. Planned cities are among the most expensive, least reliable instruments a state can wield. Governments are good at paving a subdivision; they are bad at conjuring an urban economy. New capital cities and greenfield mega-projects typically run over budget, under-occupy for decades, and depend on jobs that never materialize.
[C1.3] A "climate-refuge city" without an economic base is not a refuge; it is a subsidized camp with a master plan. [C1.4] Second, the coast is not interchangeable real estate. Ports, fisheries, shipyards, tourism, naval and energy installations generate the tax base that would fund any retreat at all. [C1.5] It is not. Most coastal communities face managed, incremental risk that responds well to engineering and smarter rebuilding, not abandonment. Buying out a handful of repeatedly flooded blocks is targeted policy; founding a new city to preempt a surge that may never hit that town is a category error.
[C1.6] Finally, relocation is not tidy. Communities, cultural ties, and coastal Indigenous homelands do not transplant. Chaotic displacement is cruel—but so is planned displacement with a ribbon-cutting. Invest in protection and existing inland cities, not speculative new ones.
