[P1.1] An internship that produces ordinary commercial value is work, even when it is called an opportunity. Governments should heavily restrict unpaid internships outside genuinely educational placements because otherwise the word “internship” becomes a legal loophole: employers can obtain labor without wages, while workers least able to afford free labor subsidize their own entry into the job market.
[P1.2] The dividing line should be substance, not paperwork. A genuinely educational placement is structured around learning: close supervision, defined teaching, meaningful assessment, and no displacement of paid staff. A student observing clinical practice under supervision is different from an intern independently answering customer emails, writing marketing copy, or covering shifts because a department is understaffed. The first may legitimately be unpaid; the second should trigger wage and employment protections.
[P1.3] Heavy restriction means a demanding educational test, employer accountability, and real penalties—not an outright ban on apprenticeships, volunteering, or classroom placements.
[P1.4] This protects fair competition as well as interns. Firms obeying minimum-wage laws should not be undercut by rivals relabeling junior vacancies. Nor should recruitment reward candidates according to family wealth. A graduate supported at home can accept months of unpaid work in an expensive city; an equally capable graduate paying rent cannot.
[P1.5] Calling that a “foot in the door” disguises a door deliberately made narrower for those without savings.
[P1.6] CON will invoke choice and flexibility, but consent cannot make every labor-market abuse acceptable: workers also “choose” unsafe conditions or subminimum pay when alternatives are scarce. Nor does a useful reference compensate someone for productive labor; employers already train paid junior staff because training is part of employing people. If a placement truly teaches, it survives. If it collapses once free labor becomes illegal, it was never an educational exception worth defending.

[C1.2] And PRO's test disqualifies exactly that. Close supervision, defined teaching, meaningful assessment, no displacement of paid staff. A junior editor learns by filing copy that runs. A campaign volunteer learns by knocking doors a paid organizer would otherwise knock.

[C1.4] Now ask who can prove compliance. Documented curricula and assessed modules are paperwork that large firms with HR departments produce cheaply. [C1.5] Those slots vanish, or move into informal arrangements no inspector sees.
[C1.6] That is the equity boomerang. PRO says wealth narrows the door; the remedy narrows it further. [C1.7] Finally, PRO says a placement that collapses without free labor "was never an educational exception worth defending." That assumes the only reason a placement cannot pay is that it isn't teaching. Sometimes the reason is four staff and no revenue. [P2.2] Learning by doing is not disqualified. [C1.3] turns “no displacement” into “no useful task,” but those are different tests. A junior editor can draft publishable copy through supervised instruction and revision; displacement arises when an employer uses that person to meet output it would otherwise staff. Education can incidentally benefit an employer.
[P2.3] A staffing shortage is not a curriculum.
[P2.4] Nor does education require university bureaucracy. A small studio can state what will be taught, who supervises, how feedback occurs, and how long the placement lasts. If it cannot explain that, interns cannot verify they are learning rather than supplying free labor. Penalties can target sham placements, not honest documentation mistakes.
[P2.5] The “four staff and no revenue” argument proves inability to pay, not entitlement to labor. A museum can offer a shorter placement, seek funding, or not run the program. Scarcity may justify fewer places; it cannot justify charging the poorest participant the entire cost.
[P2.6] Counting vanished unpaid slots as lost opportunity assumes every door is worth preserving. CON’s “short risk” means months of living costs for some and impossibility for others. Paid vacancies may favor credentials; unpaid vacancies favor savings, housing, and family connections while still favoring credentials. Ending wage-free recruitment enlarges the pool able to compete for paid work.
[P2.7] The teaching placement survives; the placement sustained by a payroll problem does not. That is not deleting education. It is refusing to make employers’ access to free labor the price of young workers’ access to careers.
