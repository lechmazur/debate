[P1.1] A supposed right to leave a platform is hollow when leaving means abandoning years of content, contacts, and community. The law should make exit real: users must receive their data in documented, machine-readable formats, and competing services must be able—with user authorization—to import that data and transfer the user’s connection map through secure, interoperable interfaces. Social-graph transfer need not hand over friends’ private information; it can preserve follow relationships where both parties consent, or enable privacy-protective reconnection requests.
[P1.2] This matters because social platforms compete through lock-in, not merely product quality. A new service may offer better moderation, fewer ads, or stronger privacy, yet users stay on an inferior incumbent because everyone they know is already there. A local group, artist, or small business cannot realistically rebuild an audience contact by contact. Portability lowers that switching cost.
[P1.3] Incumbents must then retain people by serving them well, while challengers can compete on features rather than recreating an entire society from zero.
[P1.4] Voluntary promises cannot solve this. A platform’s social graph is its moat; dominant firms have every incentive to offer incomplete downloads, incompatible formats, or interfaces they can revoke once a competitor becomes threatening. Only an enforceable baseline makes access predictable enough for users and innovators to rely on. We already understand the principle: changing a provider should not require surrendering the identity and relationships that made the service useful.
[P1.5] Privacy and security support careful regulation, not permanent captivity. The requirement can mandate explicit authorization, encryption, identity verification, rate limits, data minimization, audit logs, and certification for receiving services. It can distinguish user-created information from sensitive inferences and forbid transfer beyond the user’s legitimate scope. Indeed, standardized, supervised channels are safer than today’s pressure toward password sharing and uncontrolled scraping.
[P1.6] The decisive choice is between competition governed by user consent and monopoly governed by switching pain. People, not platforms, should control whether their digital history and relationships become prison walls.
