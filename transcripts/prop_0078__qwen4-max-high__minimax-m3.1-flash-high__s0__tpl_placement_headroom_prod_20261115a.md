Every form, every doctor's visit, every complaint about her landlord routes through her husband or a community broker. [C1.1] The proposition collapses two distinct goals: making newcomers able to participate in public life and making them conform to a majority’s preferred culture. Those are not equivalent. Public participation requires law, equal rights, and enough language to navigate schools, courts, and workplaces. Cultural conformity requires surrendering private practices the state has no legitimate right to define as tests of belonging.
[C1.2] That distinction matters because “core civic norms” are dangerously vague unless narrowly defined. If they mean constitutional rights, tolerance, and protection from discrimination, every resident should be held to them, including native-born majorities. If they extend to gender roles, religious observance, political expression, or family structure, government becomes the arbiter of a “core” culture and every internal minority may become an integration failure. The phrase cannot secure equality while remaining open-ended enough to enforce cultural conformity.
[C1.3] Likewise, a “common public language” may be a practical necessity, but one language does not follow merely from living together. Bilingual societies, regional languages, and multilingual families already show that public life can function without demanding a single private culture. Language support is not optional charity: translation, interpretation, adult education, and professional interpreters are part of equal access itself. Public funding follows governmental obligations, not an immigrant’s expected gratitude.
[C1.4] Expectation alone neither teaches a language nor produces trust; coercive framing can instead make people hide mistakes, avoid institutions, or conclude that belonging is conditional on performance.
[C1.5] The best principle is therefore not “integrate or remain outside.” It is: require equal respect for fellow citizens and others’ rights, enable newcomers to acquire whatever language skills public participation requires, and leave private cultural life free. Integration should be supported, not made a burden of proof. A society that creates dependence rather than opportunity fails; but a society that calls equal participation optional and cultural uniformity mandatory has identified the wrong problem.

[C2.2] Conversely, if instruction is funded only because immigrants are expected to conform, PRO has made integration a test of belonging.
[C2.3] The examples given—equal rights, gender autonomy, and rules for political office—are legitimate civic obligations, but they should bind everyone equally. If immigrants are held to them while native-born citizens are exempt, the state is inventing a special citizenship obligation. If PRO means broader norms about family, religion, or gender roles, the vagueness objection returns, and minority communities become subject to majority-defined cultural correction. “One of us” cannot mean “one of us who accepts our preferred way of life.”
[C2.4] PRO’s Switzerland example proves less than claimed. Having a local public language is compatible with multilingual citizenship; it does not show that every immigrant must reach one national linguistic standard on the same timetable. Nor are interpreters necessarily filters: qualified interpretation can protect independent rights, whereas dependence on relatives or community brokers can undermine them.
[C2.5] Language support and civic equality do not require private assimilation. The state can require respect for equal rights, provide accessible public services, and strongly encourage language learning without making cultural conformity the price of membership. PRO needs an expectation to justify provision, but then turns provision into conditional welcome. Better institutions make integration attainable and civic norms enforceable for all; they do not need to pretend national culture is uniform.

[C5.1] The decisive issue is whether the state may make conformity the measure of belonging. PRO insists expectation is needed to make a woman “speaking for herself,” but that conclusion does not follow. Interpretation is not a rival to autonomy; it is an accessibility measure until fluency is chosen. Removing it because a learner has not complied would make access conditional on cultural performance, precisely the proposition’s danger.
[C5.2] PRO never identifies any right or resource generated by “expected.” Classes, childcare, and paid release can be funded by equal-access duties. Calling attendance a standard changes the atmosphere, not the institutional effect. It can make services feel like tests: welcome in practice, probation in principle.
[C5.3] PRO insists the destination is a woman speaking for herself. Yet autonomy does not require immediate direct speech. A person may exercise rights through an interpreter today and attend classes tomorrow without the state declaring her integration complete or incomplete.
[C5.4] Thus the public language can be taught, civic norms can bind everyone, and equal access can be guaranteed without making private assimilation the price of membership. Expectation adds pressure, not access. If the motion’s answer to dependence is conditional belonging, it is not the more principled one.
