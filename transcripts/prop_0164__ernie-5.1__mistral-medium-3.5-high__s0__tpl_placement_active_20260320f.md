[P1.1] Public research money exists to generate reliable knowledge that society can act on. Yet a growing body of evidence shows that a large share of published findings cannot be reproduced, turning billions of dollars into dead ends. The most efficient way to protect that investment is to earmark a fixed, meaningful share of every grant budget for replication—no matter how many novel proposals must be trimmed.
[P1.2] Consider the psychology replication crisis: large‑scale attempts to repeat landmark experiments found that fewer than half of the original results held up. The cost of those failed replications was not the replication budget itself; it was the downstream waste of follow‑up studies, clinical trials, and policy decisions built on shaky foundations. In biomedicine, similar patterns emerge: high‑profile cancer‑biology papers that could not be reproduced delayed therapeutic progress and consumed grant money that could have funded more promising lines.
[P1.3] A fixed replication quota forces the system to treat verification as a core component of research, not an afterthought. It creates a predictable pipeline of confirmation that lets funders and investigators separate genuine breakthroughs from statistical flukes. The reduction in novel funding is real, but it is a deliberate trade‑off: every dollar diverted from a speculative project is a dollar saved from being spent on a line of inquiry that may be built on false premises. Over a decade, the net return on investment rises because the portfolio of funded work becomes more trustworthy, attracting private co‑investment and reducing duplication.
[P1.4] Funders already allocate money for methodology, training, and data sharing; replication is the logical extension of those responsibilities. By reserving a set share—say ten percent of each program’s budget—they guarantee that replication is not left to the goodwill of individual labs but becomes an institutional priority. The result is a healthier research ecosystem where novel ideas can stand on a solid evidential base, and public money yields the reliable knowledge it is meant to produce.
