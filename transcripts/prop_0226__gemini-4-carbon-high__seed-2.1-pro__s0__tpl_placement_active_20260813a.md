[P1.1] Asylum rests on one foundational principle: countries must not force people back to certain destruction. Yet under refugee law designed decades ago for political persecution, protection depends on *what* threatens your life rather than whether you can survive. If a regime destroys your village, you have a legal claim; if rising seas swallow your island nation, or extreme heat pushes regions past the physiological limit of human survival, asylum law offers nothing. Deporting someone back to a submerging, salt-poisoned atoll or lethal ambient heat betrays non-refoulement just as surely as returning them to a persecutor.
[P1.2] Crucially, recognizing a *limited* legal right for sea-level rise and extreme heat is narrow, objective, and workable. Unlike isolated storms from which communities rebuild, inundated territory and chronic lethal heat permanently erase habitability. Making this right limited establishes strict legal guardrails: protection triggers only when objective physical thresholds render a home uninhabitable, and only when internal relocation within one’s own country is impossible or exhausted, as in low-lying island states or nations where internal cities already buckle under deadly heat.
[P1.3] Far from opening unmanageable floodgates, verifying coastal inundation, freshwater collapse, or sustained heat extremes is actually more objective for adjudicators than divining a persecutor’s motive.
[P1.4] Finally, compare the real-world alternatives. Refusing legal recognition will not stop cross-border movement; physics guarantees people will flee when their land vanishes or outdoor air becomes fatal. Without a legal right, displaced families are pushed toward smugglers, undocumented exploitation, functional statelessness, or discretionary humanitarian visas that evaporate whenever domestic politics sour. Ad-hoc charity is revocable, leaving survival hostage to political whim, often in the very nations whose emissions fueled the crisis.
[P1.5] A bounded legal right does not erase borders; it brings the rule of law to them, replacing irregular chaos and legal limbo with orderly screening and binding protection for people whose homes can no longer sustain human life.

[C1.1] This proposal sounds compassionate on its face, but its supposed "strict guardrails" collapse on contact with reality, and it will harm both the people it claims to help and the already fragile global asylum system. First, there are no objective, administrable thresholds for the "limited" right described. Sea-level rise is gradual: saltwater seeps into aquifers over decades, high-tide flooding worsens incrementally, and communities adapt with sea walls, raised homes, and desalination for years before abandonment. Extreme heat arrives in waves, not as a permanent switch to unlivability; populations adapt with shifted work schedules, cooling centers, and heat-resilient infrastructure as resources allow.
[C1.2] There is no bright line between hardship requiring adaptation support and uninhabitability, which means the supposedly limited right will immediately expand through litigation and sympathetic adjudication, with no natural stopping point. Contrary to the claim that these claims are easier for adjudicators to verify than claims of political persecution, every categorical eligibility ruling opens the door to every resident of an affected region, with no individual screening hook to limit grants.
[C1.3] Second, a formal legal asylum right creates perverse incentives that accelerate displacement. Why would low-income coastal governments divert scarce revenue to sea walls, freshwater systems, or heat-resilient housing if outmigration with a legal right to resettle abroad becomes the default path? Even narrow eligibility would cover hundreds of millions of people across coastal South Asia, West Africa, and the Gulf, a caseload no asylum system was built to absorb. This overload will hand nativist governments the perfect excuse to dismantle existing asylum protections for people fleeing targeted political persecution, torture, and genocide, eroding non-refoulement for everyone rather than extending it.
[C1.4] Finally, this legal right is a false promise. Countries will not bind themselves to open-ended, uncapped obligations; they will respond with harsher border externalization, offshore detention, and legal workarounds that leave displaced people in even more precarious limbo than negotiated humanitarian pathways and targeted adaptation funding that actually reduce displacement without breaking the asylum system.
