[C1.3] “High-risk” is equally unstable. Slots, sports betting, offshore casinos, and crypto casinos are presented as one category, although licensing, loss patterns, consumer protections, and legal status differ. The motion supplies no statutory definition. Creators cannot know which endorsements are forbidden, and officials can select whom to punish after the fact.
[C1.4] Uncertainty is especially chilling for smaller creators, while enforcement can focus on prominent targets. A prohibition this vague is not a principled safeguard.
[C1.5] Even accepting that minors are vulnerable, the policy does not follow. The relevant comparison is not influencer promotion versus no advertising, but a blanket ban versus rules aimed at the actual pathway of harm. [C1.6] A categorical ban also creates an arbitrary competitive anomaly: the same offer may remain available through a regulated betting website or another media channel while being forbidden solely because a creator communicates digitally. That makes influencer promotion less safe, not safer, if sponsors can shift into coded campaigns or operators outside regulated systems. It also burdens creators whose analytics cannot establish that every viewer is an adult.
[C1.7] The motion should target youth exploitation, not suppress every mixed-audience advertisement.
