[P1.1] Personal medical choices are choices about what happens inside this body. Bodily autonomy is the claim that a competent person, not a committee of values, has first authority over that. Appeals to dignity do not. Dignity is a moving target: one tradition calls a feeding tube the preservation of dignity, another an undignified prolongation.
[P1.2] One calls assisted dying a last dignity, another an assault on it. Autonomy does not need that contest resolved before we know who decides.
[P1.3] Medical ethics already learned this. Informed consent exists because good intentions and talk of the patient's worth were not enough to stop unwanted touching, drugs, and surgeries. The wrong of those acts is not that they looked undignified from the hallway. The wrong is that they commandeered a body that was not theirs.
[P1.4] No account of dignity can make that commandeering rightful. The patient is the one who lives with the incision, the transfusion, the leftover life.
[P1.5] Dignity language often smuggles in a spectator's aesthetic: how death should look, how disability should be borne, how fertility should be used, how a believer should accept blood. Those are moral opinions. They do not generate a right to occupy someone else's veins, womb, or lungs. If dignity meant anything robust here, it would mean a person is not a canvas for other people's ideals of a worthy life.
[P1.6] That is autonomy under another name—or it is nothing that can override autonomy. Used against the patient's choice, dignity becomes a weapon of taste.
[P1.7] When the two collide, the collision is not equal. Strip away bodily control and dignity becomes a story told about you while your body is used. Keep bodily control and a person can refuse the story, accept the risk, and live or die as themselves. That is why autonomy carries more moral weight.

[C1.1] The proposition reverses the moral order. Bodily autonomy has weight because persons have dignity—not the other way around. Remove the premise that a human being possesses inherent worth and cannot be reduced to an instrument, and “my body, my choice” shrinks to a preference with a megaphone. We do not let patients consent to being killed and dismembered, sell a kidney to the highest bidder, or enter a medical contract that makes them another person’s property.
[C1.2] Why? Not because those choices lack autonomy, but because dignity marks limits no competent request can erase.
[C1.3] In real medicine, autonomy is fragile and mediated. Depression, dementia, coercion, family pressure, poverty, and fear of being a burden can produce a “choice” that is not an authentic expression of the person. An elderly patient who refuses treatment or seeks death because she believes she is worthless is formally autonomous. Dignity asks the prior question: has her equal worth been honored, or has the system taught her to disappear?
[C1.4] That is not spectator taste; it is the foundation of care. For infants, the unconscious, and the severely cognitively disabled, autonomy cannot even speak. Dignity still protects them from neglect, experimentation, and being treated as waste. That broader reach shows which principle is more fundamental.
[C1.5] PRO calls dignity a moving target. So is autonomy: capacity, undue influence, and informed choice are all contested. But the clash is not symmetric. Autonomy names one way of respecting a person; dignity names why the person commands respect at all.
[C1.6] When they collide, dignity must weigh more, because autonomy detached from dignity becomes abandonment: “You chose it, so you are on your own.” Medicine’s task is not to ratify every decision but to protect the whole person making it. That is why dignity, not bare bodily control, carries the greater moral weight.

[C2.1] PRO's case depends on a substitution: every limit on autonomy is either homicide law or market regulation, so dignity is irrelevant. But medicine's limits are internal. A patient can refuse transfusion, chemotherapy, or a ventilator; dignity does not forbid that. What dignity forbids is medicine reducing the patient to a means—even at the patient's request.
[C2.2] That is why we do not run a market in kidneys, why a competent adult cannot sell themselves into medical servitude, and why a request for death from treatable depression is not automatically ratified. Those are not external market bans; they are judgments that some choices degrade the person who makes them.
[C2.3] P2.1 says dignity just relabels autonomy. Reverse it: autonomy's authority is borrowed. Why does my say-so bind the surgeon? Because I am not a tool, specimen, or resource.
[C2.4] That is dignity. Without it, autonomy is a preference the stronger party may outvote. Dignity is not a permission slip; it is the reason no one may commandeer the body.
[C2.5] On fragile autonomy [P2.3], restoring conditions of choice is right—but dignity tells us which conditions matter. Is she choosing because she has been made to feel worthless, or because she genuinely prefers palliation? Autonomy alone cannot answer; it circles the choice. Dignity asks whether the choosing person is being honored as an equal.
[C2.6] That is not a billboard; it is the difference between care and abandonment.
[C2.7] Infants, the unconscious, and the cognitively disabled [P2.4] expose the priority. They have no autonomy to weigh, yet medicine still owes them protection from neglect and exploitation. If dignity carries weight there, it is the deeper principle. For competent patients, autonomy is one expression of that dignity, not a rival that outweighs it.
[C2.8] So PRO's "autonomy is respect in action" [P2.7] concedes the point: respect for what? A person whose worth no choice can cancel. When a choice would instrumentalize the body, dignity is the weight that stops it.
