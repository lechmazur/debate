[P1.1] Schools cannot teach students whose attention has been sold to someone else. A smartphone is not a tool that occasionally distracts; it is a machine designed by trillion-dollar companies to distract, permanently. If schools leave rules to each teacher, they surrender.
[P1.2] Learning requires sustained attention. Every buzz fractures working memory. Even face-down and on silent, a phone in a pocket degrades performance because part of the mind is monitoring it. One teacher allowing "just this once for research" retrains the habit for every other class that day.
[P1.3] A default ban restores the baseline: you are here to think, not to scroll.
[P1.4] Leaving it to individual teachers sounds like autonomy, but in practice it is abdication. A student cycles through six teachers with six policies. The strict teacher becomes the villain to argue with, the lenient one becomes proof that the strict ones are unreasonable. Instead of teaching algebra, teachers become bouncers negotiating the same boundary thirty times a day.
[P1.5] That burns out staff and guarantees uneven enforcement, where the same offense earns a warning in room 204 and detention in room 205. A clear school-wide rule removes negotiation and protects teachers.
[P1.6] Socially, phones turn breaks into isolation booths. Lunchrooms where everyone scrolls alone, bathrooms where fights are filmed for likes, anxiety spiking with every unanswered text. No lesson plan competes with that.
[P1.7] Equity demands a default too. Teacher discretion means students with expensive phones and parents who will complain get leeway. A default levels the field, and schools that have tried it see exactly what you'd expect: less bullying, more conversation, better focus.
[P1.8] A default ban is not no-contact. Offices have phones, schools provide laptops for work, medical needs get exemptions. But the default matters. Make focus the rule and distraction the exception, not the other way around.

[C1.1] A default school-wide smartphone ban trades superficial consistency for actual student and staff needs, and leaves critical gaps that teacher-led rules fill far better. The core flaw in the PRO position is the assumption that uniform prohibition solves the problems it names, rather than creating new, worse ones.
[C1.2] First, context-specific learning needs vanish under a default ban. A 10th grade English language learner using their phone to translate vocabulary in real time during a discussion, a photography student referencing on-location shots for a project, a neurodivergent student using a calming app to regulate sensory overload during assemblies—all of these uses cause no disruption to peers, but a default ban blocks them unless a student navigates a slow, often stigmatizing exemption process. Teacher discretion lets these accommodations happen on the spot, no red tape, no singling students out.
[C1.3] Second, the claim that a default ban advances equity is backwards. Low-income students are disproportionately likely to rely on phones for after-school job coordination, family care responsibilities, or emergency contact with guardians who work irregular hours. Exemption processes are bureaucratic and favor families with the time, resources, and social capital to navigate them, meaning the students who need phones most are the most likely to be penalized by a rigid rule. Teacher discretion lets educators account for these individual circumstances without forcing students to air personal hardships to administration.
[C1.4] Third, the claim that a default ban reduces teacher burnout ignores that one-size-fits-all rules force staff to enforce policies that make no sense for their classrooms. An art teacher who wants students to use reference photos from a field trip, a science teacher documenting lab results with phone cameras, would have to waste time arguing for administrative approval instead of setting a reasonable, class-specific rule. Uniform bans turn every teacher into a full-time phone cop, rather than letting them set boundaries that fit their lesson plans.
