[P1.1] Pay is compensation for value delivered under specific market conditions, not a personal entitlement attached to a person's address. Three arguments carry this debate.
[P1.2] First, the economics of compensation. Salaries at headquarters exist because companies must compete for talent in that specific labor market—against local rents, local competitors, local cost of living. A software engineer in San Francisco commands a certain wage because the alternative is losing them to another Bay Area firm and because they need that wage to survive there. When that same engineer relocates to a region where rent is a third of the cost, the competitive pressure that generated the original number disappears.
[P1.3] The company isn't competing against Bay Area firms for that worker anymore; it's competing against local and remote alternatives in the new market. Continuing to pay Bay Area rates isn't fairness—it's just leaving money on the table for no market reason.
[P1.4] Second, fairness cuts the other way from how CON will frame it. Imagine two engineers doing identical work, hired at the same time, with the same output. One lives in Ohio, one moves there from San Francisco. If the mover keeps San Francisco pay while the Ohio-based new hire is paid Ohio rates, the company has created an arbitrary two-tier system based on migration history rather than contribution.
[P1.5] Location-adjusted pay actually restores equity across the workforce; flat headquarters pay for movers is what creates the real unfairness.
[P1.6] Third, sustainability. Companies that let salaries become geographically sticky regardless of cost base take on a structural cost disadvantage against competitors who don't. Over time, that pressure shows up as smaller headcounts, less hiring, tighter margins—outcomes that hurt workers broadly, including the very remote workers CON wants to protect. Location-adjusted pay isn't punishing movers; it's what allows companies to keep offering remote flexibility at all.
[P1.7] The proposition asks what workers should *generally* expect. Given market logic, internal fairness, and long-run sustainability, the answer is location-adjusted pay, not headquarters bands frozen in place by geography of convenience.
