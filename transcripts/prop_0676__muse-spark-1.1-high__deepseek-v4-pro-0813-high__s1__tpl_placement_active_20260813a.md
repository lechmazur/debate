[P1.1] Automated video and personality assessments should be banned because they turn hiring into phrenology at scale.
[P1.2] These tools claim to infer conscientiousness, grit, or leadership from how fast you speak, how much you smile, or whether you hold eye contact with a webcam. There is no valid science behind that leap. Job performance is predicted by skills and experience, not by vocal fry or micro-expressions. When an algorithm scores your personality from a 30-second clip, it is not measuring you; it is guessing, with your rent on the line.
[P1.3] Because the guess is opaque, it launders discrimination. A model trained on past "successful" employees will punish anyone who doesn't look and sound like them — people with accented English, a stutter, autism, anxiety, Black women who are told they are "too expressive" or "not expressive enough." A human interviewer who rejected someone for not making enough eye contact would face scrutiny; an algorithm does the same and calls it a "low sociability score" and becomes unchallengeable. You cannot contest a score you never see based on criteria you never agreed to.
[P1.4] And you cannot opt out. When applying for an entry-level retail job requires you to perform enthusiasm for a camera alone in your bedroom, consent is fictional. Workers are forced to contort themselves to satisfy a machine.
[P1.5] We already ban polygraphs in hiring not because they are inefficient, but because they are invasive, unreliable, and coercive. Automated video interviews are polygraphs with better marketing. Government must ban them, or at minimum require full disclosure, human-only decisions, and a right to an alternative. No labor market can be fair if your body is the resume.

[C1.1] The motion treats a broad category as if it were its worst abuse. A written Big Five personality inventory completed at home is not “phrenology”; it is a standardized questionnaire asking applicants to rate ordinary statements, and its scales—especially conscientiousness—have long been linked to job performance across roles. An asynchronous video interview is often just a recorded answer to the same structured prompts every candidate receives, reviewed later. Banning or heavily restricting these tools does not remove psychology from hiring; it leaves the psychology to a single human’s gut feeling.
[C1.2] PRO’s discrimination point cuts the other way. The replacement is usually the unstructured interview: more likely to reward people who resemble the interviewer, more likely to punish non-native accents, stutters, anxiety, or atypical eye contact. A standardized assessment, by contrast, gives every candidate the same questions and the same scoring rules. If a specific tool causes unlawful disparate impact, employment discrimination law already requires the employer to prove it is job-related and consistent with business necessity.
[C1.3] That focused legal test is better than a ban because it pressures employers to validate and audit the tools they use.
[C1.4] The polygraph analogy is misplaced. Polygraphs are banned because they purport to detect deception and are not accepted as reliable for that purpose. Personality and video assessments do not claim to read hidden lies; they evaluate observable job-relevant traits or recorded responses. Some are bad, but banning the category for the worst examples would be like banning reference checks because some employers ask discriminatory questions.
[C1.5] The practical effect of the proposal would be slower, shallower screening and more reliance on credentials and networks. High-volume employers cannot give every applicant a full human interview; something has to narrow the pool. If it is not a structured questionnaire, it will be résumé prestige, referral connections, or a manager skimming applications in minutes. That is not a labor market where the body is the résumé; it is one where the pedigree is the résumé.
