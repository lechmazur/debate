[P1.1] Start with the test that separates genuine safety rules from protectionism. A licensing requirement is defensible only if the harm is serious, the risk is likely, consumers cannot easily judge quality, and no less restrictive measure suffices. In interior design, hair braiding, and low-risk trades, that test fails.
[P1.2] But the same rules work very well as protection: every mandated hour, exam fee, and board-approved curriculum shrinks the pool of new workers and leaves consumers with fewer, pricier options.
[P1.3] Hair braiding is the clearest example. Braiders do not mix chemicals or cut hair. Yet many states require a full cosmetology license: hundreds or thousands of hours in barbering, coloring, and chemical relaxing that a braider will never perform. That is not a safety curriculum.
[P1.4] It is a transfer of tuition money and time from newcomers to established schools and license holders. A short sanitation course plus ordinary consumer remedies would address the real, limited risk.
[P1.5] Interior design licensing shows the same pattern. Choosing finishes, furniture, lighting, and layout for homes is not structural engineering. Where states require a license, the exam often stresses commercial code details that look more like architecture. The practical effect is to block independent, younger, or less traditional entrants from using the title.
[P1.6] Actual public safety already rests on building inspectors, architects, and engineers for structural changes.
[P1.7] In low-risk trades, incumbents often dominate licensing boards and apprenticeship committees. The predictable result is higher required hours, restricted exam access, or seniority rules with no demonstrated reduction in accidents or complaints. A license that prices out poor and immigrant workers while protecting the existing market share of established practitioners is an entry barrier wearing a safety costume.
[P1.8] Safety rhetoric is the public justification; controlling who may enter the market is the observable function.
