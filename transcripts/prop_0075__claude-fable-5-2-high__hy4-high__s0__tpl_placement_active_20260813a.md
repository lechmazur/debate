[P1.1] An exemption from anti-discrimination law is a license to do to someone what would be illegal for anyone else. That license needs a justification, and it should extend exactly as far as the justification does.
[P1.2] The justification is real. A faith that cannot choose its own clergy, define its own membership, govern its own worship, or decide who teaches its doctrine is not free. No state should tell a synagogue whom to ordain or a mosque whom to admit to prayer. Those are core religious functions, and there the exemption is earned.
[P1.3] But follow that reason outward and watch it run out. Religious institutions run hospitals, universities, housing complexes, retail shops, and publishing houses, and they hire large numbers of janitors, accountants, and nurses. When a religiously affiliated hospital system fires a billing clerk for marrying a same-sex partner, or a church-owned apartment block turns away an unmarried couple, no sacrament is being protected. An ordinary market actor is imposing costs on someone who never joined the faith and who simply needed a job or a home.
[P1.4] That is the first pillar: exemptions must track consent. Congregants choose a faith's discipline. Customers and secretarial staff in the open market did not.
[P1.5] The second pillar is equal rules for equal conduct. Once an institution enters commerce, it competes with secular firms, takes public customers, and often takes public money. A broad exemption turns religious identity into a regulatory advantage, and it invites any enterprise to wrap itself in a mission statement.
[P1.6] The third is scale. In many communities the religious hospital or school is the only one nearby. A broad exemption there is not private conscience but a hole in civil rights law the size of a town.
[P1.7] The line is administrable too. Courts already ask whether a role is ministerial, and tax law already separates religious activity from unrelated business income.
[P1.8] So the choice is simple. Broad exemptions sacrifice outsiders to the institution, and no exemptions sacrifice the faith to the state. Tying the shield to the sanctuary protects both, [clipped]

[C1.1] The word “only” is the defect: it makes religious freedom expire at the sanctuary door. Calling an exemption “a license” [P1.1] assumes the anti-discrimination rule is neutral. But that rule is a state command about whom an institution may hire, serve, and organize around; exemptions mark where pluralism limits the command, not where a business gets a favor.
[C1.2] The core/commercial border makes officials rank religious acts. For many traditions, healing, teaching, feeding strangers, and keeping shared moral discipline are not sidelines; they are religion in work clothes. Tuition, insurance bills, rent, and payroll prove only that money moves. A religious college hiring a dean, a monastery selling bread, and a shelter setting staff rules all use commercial forms.
[C1.3] PRO treats form as proof of secularity, then asks judges to decide whether a nurse praying with patients or a campus housing officer enforcing a code is “core.” Tax categories are revenue rules, not theology tests. That protects worship-heavy faiths and punishes service-heavy ones.
[C1.4] Consent [P1.4] does not rescue it. Outsiders are not drafted into a creed; they enter a school, workplace, or provider with a disclosed ethos. A clerk does not become a member by taking a job, but the institution still has an associational interest in remaining the community it claims to be. Notice and transparency rules can handle bait-and-switch without declaring every non-worship setting secular.
[C1.5] Scale and public money point to tailored duties, not categorical exclusion. If a religious hospital is the only nearby provider, require emergency access, referrals, or funding conditions aimed at that monopoly. Governments often partner with faith-based charities precisely because they are distinct; using that partnership to erase distinctiveness turns pluralism into compulsory uniformity.
[C1.6] A broad setting is not a broad permission. Exemptions can cover a whole mission while still bounded by sincerity, disclosed standards, and assessment of concrete harm. The motion collapses that distinction: state-approved religion, minus the parts that serve the public.
