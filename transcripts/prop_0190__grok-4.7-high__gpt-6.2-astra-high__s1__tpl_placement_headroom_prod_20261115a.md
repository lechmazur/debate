[P1.1] Actuarial fairness and social solidarity answer opposite questions about who pays for bad luck. One prices each person, or each narrow class, at expected cost. The other holds that when coverage is essential to health, work, housing, or basic mobility, the fortunate carry part of the burden of the unfortunate. Those answers cannot govern the same pool.
[P1.2] Law should limit the first so the second can exist.
[P1.3] The clash is sharpest where coverage is essential. Genetic disease, prior cancer, age, disability, a flood-prone street a family cannot leave: these raise predicted claims without proving fault. Unrestricted rating turns that prediction into a surcharge or a refusal. The person who needs the policy most is the one the market is built to charge most, or to exclude.
[P1.4] That is the mechanism, not a side effect. Calling it fair only repeats the premise that each pays their own expected loss. That premise can stand for yacht insurance. It cannot stand for coverage that decides whether a child sees a doctor or a household remains housed after a storm.
[P1.5] Solvency does not require unlimited rating. Essential systems already finance cost through mandatory participation, community rating or tight rate bands, and subsidy where the pool cannot carry the tail. Costs are real. They should be shared, not expelled onto the sick, the old, the poor, and the unlucky.
[P1.6] Avoidable behavioral rating can remain. What law must cap is sorting on traits people cannot reasonably change, and the gap between the cheapest and dearest essential policy.
[P1.7] Pricing launders inequality. Credit scores, postal codes, and occupation track class and often race, even when unnamed. Blessing every correlated variable recreates the exclusions solidarity forbids. Legal limits are the choice that essential risk will be shared, not a market that is actuarially neat and socially cruel.
