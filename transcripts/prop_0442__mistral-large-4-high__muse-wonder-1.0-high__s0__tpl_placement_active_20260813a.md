[P1.1] International shipping emits roughly three percent of global greenhouse gases—comparable to a major industrial nation—and those emissions are rising as trade volumes grow. Left to market forces alone, the industry will not decarbonize fast enough. Shipowners face a brutal collective action problem: any operator who voluntarily pays for expensive green fuels gets undercut by competitors burning cheap heavy fuel oil, particularly on long-haul routes where fuel is a large share of operating costs. This is a structural race to the bottom that voluntary pledges and aspirational targets cannot fix.
[P1.2] The IMO is the only body with the authority to set binding global standards that apply equally to all flags. A binding timetable requiring the transition to low- or zero-carbon fuels—green ammonia, methanol, or hydrogen—creates the demand certainty needed to drive massive investment in production facilities and port bunkering infrastructure. Without a hard deadline, fuel producers wait for demand that never materializes, and shipowners wait for supply that never arrives. The timetable breaks this coordination deadlock and forces the industry to innovate.
[P1.3] Yes, freight costs will rise. But the increase is modest when spread across global supply chains. Ocean freight typically represents a small fraction of retail prices; even a significant fuel premium adds only a few dollars to a container crossing an ocean. Meanwhile, the climate damage from unmitigated shipping emissions will cost far more in port flooding, agricultural disruption, and extreme weather that disrupts supply chains themselves.
[P1.4] Furthermore, without a binding IMO timetable, individual regions will impose their own patchwork regulations, triggering carbon leakage as vessels reflag or reroute to avoid compliance. A single global standard prevents this regulatory arbitrage and ensures a level playing field.
[P1.5] We are choosing between a manageable, predictable cost passed through to consumers now and catastrophic, unmanageable costs borne by everyone later. Binding low-carbon fuel requirements on a firm IMO timetable are the only credible path to keeping both global trade and the climate stable.

[C1.1] A binding fuel timetable mandates a transition that cannot yet physically happen, then punishes trade when it does not.
[C1.2] Green ammonia, green methanol and hydrogen are not drop-in replacements waiting on shelves. They are produced in tiny quantities, cost multiples of heavy fuel oil, and need new engines, storage, safety rules and bunkering in hundreds of ports. Ammonia is toxic and can emit powerful nitrous oxide if burned poorly; much methanol today is made from fossil gas. Ordering fleets onto these fuels by a fixed date does not create supply by decree.
[C1.3] It creates scarcity, bidding wars between rich carriers, and ships idled, exempted, or sailing elsewhere to bunker.
[C1.4] That exposes the enforcement fantasy. The IMO sets rules, but it does not police the world fleet; flag and port states do. A system dominated by flags of convenience and opaque ownership will respond to an unaffordable deadline with reflagging, dual fleets, waivers and creative compliance. Large operators will secure scarce green fuel and advertise clean corridors.
[C1.5] Older, dirtier ships will be sold on for routes serving poorer markets. Emissions are displaced, not eliminated, while a level field survives only on paper.
[C1.6] The cost dismissal is equally misleading. Freight is trivial for a container of phones, but decisive for grain, fertilizer, building materials and the imported food and fuel island states depend on. For low-value bulk cargo, fuel is not a few dollars of retail markup; it determines whether an export is competitive and whether essentials arrive affordably. A binding timetable therefore taxes most heavily the developing exporters and import-dependent nations least responsible for historic emissions and least equipped with alternative ports or fuels.
[C1.7] Shipping should cut emissions aggressively, through tightening carbon-intensity and efficiency standards, a predictable carbon price, slow steaming, retrofits and technology-neutral rules that reward every verified ton saved. That drives real reductions now without locking the industry into scarce, immature fuels on an arbitrary calendar.

[P2.1] The opposition mistakes cause and effect. A binding timetable does not pretend to create supply by decree; it creates the demand certainty that makes supply chains exist. Fuel producers will not build multi-billion-dollar green ammonia plants for a market that might vanish, and shipowners will not order dual-fuel vessels without a guaranteed fuel supply. The deadlock is real, and only a binding deadline breaks it.
[P2.2] Waiting for fuels to become cheap and abundant before mandating them is waiting forever.
[P2.3] Enforcement is not a fantasy. The IMO sets the rules, but port state control enforces them through market access. A vessel that cannot prove compliance with IMO fuel standards loses access to the world's major ports—Rotterdam, Singapore, Shanghai, Los Angeles. Flags of convenience cannot reflag their way out of needing those ports, and no shipowner can profitably serve global trade from the margins.
[P2.4] The threat of exclusion from lucrative trade routes is a powerful enforcement mechanism that voluntary schemes and carbon prices lack.
[P2.5] The equity argument inverts itself. Climate change already taxes the poor most brutally: island states face existential flooding, and African and South Asian farmers face disrupted monsoons. Delaying the transition preserves a status quo that externalizes catastrophic costs onto nations least responsible. Revenue from carbon pricing or fuel levies under an IMO framework can be recycled to compensate vulnerable developing economies and fund their port infrastructure—something ad hoc regional rules cannot do.
[P2.6] Finally, the alternative of efficiency standards and carbon prices, while useful, cannot deliver zero-carbon shipping. Slow steaming and retrofits reduce emissions; they do not eliminate them. Only a fuel transition does. Without binding fuel requirements, we get marginal improvements while the fleet locks in fossil infrastructure for decades, making future decarbonization more expensive.
[P2.7] The choice is not between affordable trade and expensive trade; it is between managed transition now and climate chaos that destroys trade itself.

[C2.1] Demand certainty is only certainty if the deadline is believable. Investors will not build ammonia plants on a date they expect to be waived when fuel, engines and bunkering are not ready, and the IMO controls neither green electricity supply, electrolyser capacity, nor ammonia safety approval in hundreds of ports. If the date is held despite shortage, the result is scarcity rents and idled tonnage; if it is softened, the signal collapses. A mandate cannot repeal that dilemma.
[C2.2] Port access does not rescue it. Port states compete for calls and transshipment, and checking a certificate is not verifying the lifecycle carbon of fuel bunkered halfway around the world. A dual-fuel ship can burn green methanol into Rotterdam and heavy oil for the rest of the voyage; a carrier can assign its cleanest vessels to strict ports and its oldest to poorer routes. That is precisely the displaced, two-tier fleet this timetable invites, not a level field.
[C2.3] The equity answer adds what the proposition does not contain. A fuel mandate by itself creates no public revenue to recycle; it creates private scarcity profits for fuel suppliers and large carriers able to secure supply. Compensation for island states is hypothetical, while higher grain, fertilizer and imported-fuel costs under shortage are immediate. Invoking climate harm to the poor does not justify a policy that charges them first and promises help later.
[C2.4] Nor is the alternative mere slow steaming forever. A tightening, technology-neutral intensity standard declining toward zero, backed by a predictable carbon price, gives producers a durable market without pretending one fuel will be ready everywhere on one date. It rewards efficiency, wind assistance, retrofits and operational cuts now, then pulls fuels in as they genuinely scale. PRO has shown shipping must eventually change fuels; it has not shown that a rigid IMO calendar beats that trajectory—only that delay is bad, which nobody needs a mandate to accept.
