[P1.1] Public subsidies for professional-sports stadiums amount to regressive wealth transfers from taxpayers to billionaire owners. The proposition doesn't ban these subsidies—it simply insists that if cities invest public money, they must secure enforceable returns. That baseline is both modest and essential.
[P1.2] Consider the typical dynamic. A team demands hundreds of millions in public financing, promising jobs, economic revitalization, and civic prestige. The city complies. Then the team captures virtually all operating revenue—naming rights, luxury suites, parking, concessions—while the surrounding neighborhood sees promised development evaporate and local businesses struggle against stadium-controlled vendors.
[P1.3] When the lease expires, the team threatens relocation unless a newer, more subsidized arena is built. The cycle repeats.
[P1.4] Without enforceable conditions, cities negotiate from weakness. "Good faith" promises dissolve because team owners face no consequences for breaking them. Revenue sharing and neighborhood-benefit requirements transform this lopsided arrangement by making commitments binding and verifiable.
[P1.5] The proposition's logic mirrors any sensible investment: no enforceable return, no subsidy. Cities wouldn't fund a convention center without revenue agreements or a transit project without service guarantees. Stadiums deserve no exception simply because team owners command emotional loyalty and relocation threats.
[P1.6] The enforceability requirement also addresses the documented gap between promised and actual economic impact. Economic research consistently finds that professional sports stadiums generate far less regional economic activity than alternative public investments. Neighborhood-benefit provisions—community spaces, local hiring mandates, affordable housing commitments—ensure that some tangible value remains with residents who fund the project.
[P1.7] Opponents may argue that conditions drive teams away. But cities that refuse to subsidize without conditions still retain teams—witness the Green Bay model of public ownership with revenue sharing, or cities that have called relocation bluffs successfully. The real question is whether public investment should carry zero enforceable strings. The answer is self-evidently no.

[C1.1] The proposition disguises prohibition as prudence. By mandating enforceable revenue sharing and neighborhood-benefit requirements as prerequisites for any public subsidy, it constructs a poison pill that ensures cities lose the deal entirely. In the competitive federal landscape where franchises shop between municipalities, rigid enforceability transforms public-private partnerships into adversarial litigation traps. Binding revenue-sharing clauses create moral hazard: teams lose incentive to maximize gross revenue when forced to distribute it, while cities become co-defendants in every pricing dispute.
[C1.2] Meanwhile, mandated neighborhood benefits—affordable housing set-asides, hiring quotas, community space requirements—function as unfunded liabilities that render projects financially non-viable. The result is not a better stadium; it is an empty lot and a departed franchise.
[C1.3] Recent history confirms this dynamic. When St. Louis, San Diego, and Oakland held firm against flexible subsidy arrangements, the Rams, Chargers, and Raiders did not accept revenue-sharing shackles; they relocated to markets willing to invest without enforceable strings attached. The Green Bay model cited by the proposition is a red herring: Green Bay survives only as a grandfathered, unique public corporation in a microscopic market, a structure modern league bylaws prohibit and other cities cannot replicate. For typical municipalities, the actual choice is between a subsidized stadium with negotiated—but not legally enforceable—community commitments, or watching the team vanish to a competitor city.
[C1.4] The proposition also mistakes stadium economics. These projects generate diffuse civic goods—regional identity, tourism visibility, talent recruitment—that resist quantification in binding revenue contracts. By demanding rigid enforceability, the motion forces cities to either forgo these intangible returns entirely or spend millions policing ambiguous benefit definitions in court. That is not fiscal responsibility; it is a recipe for civic impoverishment.
