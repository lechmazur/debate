[P1.1] Orbit is not empty real estate waiting for developers. It is a shared, finite commons, and we are filling it faster than the rules that govern it. That is the entire case for a temporary moratorium: when the cost of error is irreversible and the cost of delay is merely inconvenient, you pause until the guardrails exist.
[P1.2] Start with collision risk. Tens of thousands of satellites are proposed or already launching, and close-approach alerts now run in the millions annually. Each conjunction forces maneuvers, and each maneuver is a judgment call made under uncertainty, often with no obligation to coordinate with the other operator. We have already seen what two dead objects can do: the 2009 Iridium-Cosmos collision created thousands of trackable fragments.
[P1.3] Cascade that physics across shells ten times denser, and you approach the Kessler scenario — debris begetting debris until entire orbital bands become unusable for generations. You cannot recall a shattered orbit the way you recall a defective car.
[P1.4] Second, astronomy. Mega-constellation trails already streak through long-exposure images from the world's great survey telescopes, corrupting data that took decades of planning and public money to collect. Radio astronomy suffers interference that no paint job or visor fixes. We are degrading humanity's oldest science to accelerate one industry's deployment schedule — without any binding standard requiring mitigation first.
[P1.5] Third, and decisive: the governance gap. Today a single national regulator can license a constellation that imposes collision risk on every nation and every future user of space. Debris-mitigation guidelines are largely voluntary, deorbiting deadlines are honored unevenly, and there is no enforceable international regime for traffic management or liability for near-misses. Licensing is sprinting; law is crawling.
[P1.6] The opposition will say delay costs connectivity and innovation. But the motion is temporary and conditional: lift it when collision-risk, astronomy, and debris rules are materially stronger. That is not a ban; it is basic sequencing. A few years of slowed deployment buys permanent, usable orbit.
[P1.7] Proceeding blind risks losing the commons entirely. Pause first, govern first, launch second.
