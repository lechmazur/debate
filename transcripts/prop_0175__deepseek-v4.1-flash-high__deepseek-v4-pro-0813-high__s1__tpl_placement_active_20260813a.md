[P1.1] Licensing is supposed to answer a market failure: consumers cannot detect dangerous incompetence before harm occurs. Apply that test to hair braiding, interior design, and low-risk trades, and the safety story collapses. Braiding involves no cutting, no dyes, no chemical relaxers; the real hygiene risks—dirty tools, scalp infections—can be addressed by basic sanitation rules and inspections, not hundreds of hours in a cosmetology curriculum covering perms, color, and haircutting that braiders will never use. When states demand that training anyway, they are not screening for competence in braiding.
[P1.2] They are making the entry toll high enough that existing salons face fewer competitors.
[P1.3] Interior design is similar. A decorator choosing paint, furniture, or lighting does not determine whether a building stands or a fire escape works. Structural changes, electrical work, and occupancy safety are already governed by building codes, permits, engineers, architects, and contractors. Licensing decorators adds a credential barrier where the actual danger is either absent or already regulated elsewhere.
[P1.4] Low-risk trades—painting, simple landscaping, minor repairs—are low-risk precisely because failures are visible, reversible, and insurable. A bad paint job produces a callback, not a catastrophe. Consumers can check reviews, demand a contract, withhold payment, or sue. Insurance and warranty markets price the risk.
[P1.5] A blanket license mostly raises prices and reduces the number of people who can earn a living.
[P1.6] The proposition says primarily. That is the right standard. Where regulation targets a genuine, hidden, serious hazard, it can be justified. But when the required training is broader than the risk, when incumbents dominate the boards that write the rules, and when the clearest effect is fewer competitors and higher fees, safety is a slogan covering protectionism.
[P1.7] Incumbents gain directly from fewer rivals; consumers pay more and new entrants bear the cost. The burden is on licensing to show necessity; for these fields, it hasn't.

Tight braids can cause traction alopecia; extension glues can trigger dermatitis; unclean combs, clips, or hands can spread ringworm and impetigo. None of that is visible before the service.
[C1.2] A client cannot inspect a braider's disinfection the way she can spot bad paint. A license—or narrower credential—turns sanitation rules into an enforceable precondition. Basic rules without routine licensing inspections are usually enforced after harm, not before it.
[C1.3] Interior design is not merely choosing cushions. Licensed interior designers make space-planning and finish choices about fire-rated materials, egress, and accessibility. Codes are not self-executing; they require someone at the design stage to know which finish or layout is compliant, distinct from structural engineering.
[C1.4] The label low-risk also does the argument's work in advance. Painting can disturb lead paint or expose families to solvent fumes; landscaping can alter drainage; minor repairs can hit gas or electric lines. These are hidden or delayed harms, not mere callbacks. Reviews, warranties, and lawsuits are after-the-fact remedies; they do not prevent lead dust from being released.
[C1.5] The jump from "licensing raises costs" to "therefore its primary purpose is incumbent protection" is too fast. Every safety regulation raises costs and limits entry; food-safety permits do the same, but they are understood as consumer protection. Practitioner boards are not proof of capture—that is also true in medicine. The test is whether a hidden risk exists and the requirement targets it.
[C1.6] In braiding, design, and these trades, it does. The safety rationale exists before any competitor is protected. Reform may be warranted, but the claim that these licenses are primarily barriers fails.
