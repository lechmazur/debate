[P1.1] Every industry that can kill people faster than a regulator can react is licensed before it operates. We do not let pharmaceutical companies sell drugs and count the bodies afterward; the European Medicines Agency must say yes first. We do not let manufacturers self-certify their aircraft. Frontier general-purpose AI belongs squarely in that category.
[P1.2] Three reasons. First, irreversibility. Risk-based regulation is fundamentally reactive: obligations attach at deployment, enforcement after harm. But frontier models cannot be recalled.
[P1.3] Once weights are released, capabilities proliferate permanently. A model that meaningfully lowers the barrier to engineering a pathogen or attacking critical infrastructure cannot be un-released the way a defective car is. When the failure mode is irreversible and detection lags deployment, permission-before-release is the only coherent regime.
[P1.4] Second, the burden of proof. The current regime leans heavily on providers' own conformity assessments, and even the EU's newest rules for the most powerful models rest substantially on self-assessment and codes of practice — developers grading their own homework. Licensing reverses that: before a frontier model touches the EU market, its maker must demonstrate to a public authority that dangerous capabilities were tested for and safeguards verified. That is not radical; it is the ordinary settlement wherever worst-case costs dwarf compliance costs.
[P1.5] Third, proportionality and enforceability. The motion targets only frontier general-purpose models — a handful of labs worldwide; everything else in the AI economy stays risk-based. A license is market access: one clear, uniform gate instead of fragmented enforcement across twenty-seven member states, concentrating oversight at the only chokepoint where regulators can realistically keep pace — the few firms building the most powerful systems.
[P1.6] Opponents will wave the innovation flag. But Europe runs world-class pharmaceutical and aviation industries under prior authorization; licensing selects for serious firms, not fewer. The real choice is who bears the risk of uncertainty: the companies profiting, or nearly half a billion Europeans. Risk-based rules put it on the public.
[P1.7] Licensing puts it where it belongs.
