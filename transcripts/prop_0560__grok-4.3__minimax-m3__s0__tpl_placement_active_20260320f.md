[P1.1] Prioritizing preventive measures produces larger gains in healthy life years per dollar than further incremental spending on terminal care. Funding vaccines, screening programs, and risk-factor reduction interrupts disease early, when interventions cost far less and affect far more people. Marginal additions to end-of-life treatment, by contrast, frequently deliver only brief extensions measured in days or weeks after most function has already been lost.
[P1.2] Finite budgets force explicit trade-offs. Resources directed toward another round of intensive chemotherapy or prolonged mechanical ventilation cannot simultaneously expand childhood immunization coverage or community hypertension control. The latter options reduce the future incidence of the very conditions that drive expensive late-stage admissions, lowering both mortality and total system expenditure over time.
[P1.3] A clear illustration is tobacco control versus last-stage lung-cancer management. Sustained cessation programs and taxation cut new cases across entire populations for modest ongoing cost. Once advanced disease appears, even the most aggressive supportive regimens add limited survival at unit prices that exceed annual prevention budgets for thousands. Shifting the balance toward prevention therefore compresses overall demand rather than merely redistributing it.
[P1.4] This pattern holds across common conditions. Investment in sanitation and early diabetes management avoids dialysis and amputations; comparable sums spent on repeated hospitalizations near death rarely restore independence. Systems that embed prevention as the default allocation therefore serve more citizens and sustain solvency, while repeated marginal expansions at the end concentrate spending where leverage is smallest.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to accept that health systems should systematically rank prevention above marginal end-of-life care. This framing fails on three grounds: it manufactures a false binary, it hides what "marginal" actually means in clinical practice, and it reduces medicine to a single utilitarian ledger at the cost of its core purpose.
[C1.2] First, this is a false dichotomy. Health systems are not forced to choose between a measles vaccine and palliative chemotherapy. Mature systems fund both prevention and serious illness care in parallel. The proposition only gains traction by assuming that every dollar spent on a dying patient is one stolen from a healthy child, and that assumption is not empirically grounded.
[C1.3] Prevention has its own political constituencies, budget lines, and bureaucratic inertia. Squeezing end-of-life spending does not automatically expand prevention; it often just produces a different population of suffering.
[C1.4] Second, the word "marginal" does enormous hidden work. The proposition quietly defines end-of-life care as expansions that barely help. Yet many interventions routinely labeled "end-of-life" — curative resection of localized cancers in elderly patients, valve replacements in nonagenarians, targeted therapies for advanced but treatable disease, even prolonged ICU stays that return people to families and work — deliver years, not days, in patients we cannot cleanly call "dying." A rule that deprioritizes end-of-life spending will routinely deny meaningful treatment to patients who go on to live well.
[C1.5] The "marginal" qualifier is a rhetorical device, not a clinical category.
[C1.6] Third, and decisive, the proposition treats the dying as instruments of a population ledger. Medicine's foundational duty is to the patient in front of the clinician, including when that patient cannot be cured. A system that systematically downgrades care for those with the least expected recovery abandons the principle that vulnerability, not projected productivity, defines medical obligation.
[C1.7] The right question is not prevention versus the dying, but whether resources serve patients well. On that test, end-of-life care often earns its place — and the categorical ranking this motion demands is the wrong answer.

[P2.1] Prioritizing prevention does not create a false binary; it recognizes that budgets are finite and that repeated expansions of high-cost terminal interventions crowd out scalable upstream programs. When systems add another cycle of intensive care or targeted oncology agents for patients with limited remaining function, those funds are unavailable for population-level screening or vaccination drives that avert thousands of later cases. The claim that both can expand indefinitely ignores real resource constraints and the pattern in which end-stage spending grows faster than preventive capacity.
[P2.2] The defense of non-marginal end-of-life interventions also misses the motion's focus. The proposition targets incremental additions whose survival gains are measured in weeks or months at extreme unit cost, not curative procedures that restore substantial function. Reallocating from the former frees capacity without denying patients interventions that produce meaningful recovery. Systems already distinguish these categories through guidelines on futility and cost-effectiveness; the motion simply asks that prevention receive priority when those distinctions are made.
[P2.3] Finally, the duty to the patient in front of the clinician is not abandoned by favoring prevention. Every prevented heart attack or stroke means one fewer family facing a terminal hospitalization. A system that systematically directs marginal dollars toward the last weeks of life rather than reducing incidence of advanced disease ultimately leaves more patients in precisely the vulnerable position the opponent seeks to protect. Prevention therefore aligns clinical obligation with the larger set of patients who never reach the end-of-life stage.
