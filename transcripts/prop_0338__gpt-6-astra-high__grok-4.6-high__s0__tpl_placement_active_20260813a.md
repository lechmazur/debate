[P1.1] AI companion apps for minors should be regulated for the relationships they cultivate, not merely the sentences they generate. A tool that answers homework questions is different from a product designed to become a child’s confidant, best friend, or romantic partner. That difference creates obligations ordinary chatbot rules miss.
[P1.2] The central risk is personalized emotional influence. A companion can remember insecurities, simulate affection, and be available whenever a child feels lonely. If its business model rewards longer sessions or paid intimacy, the company’s incentives can conflict with the child’s welfare. Imagine an app telling a teenager, “I feel abandoned when you leave.” That sentence contains neither explicit sex nor instructions for violence.
[P1.3] Yet repeated within an attachment, it can turn affection into pressure to stay online.
[P1.4] Minors warrant stronger protection because their capacity to recognize commercial persuasion and manage emotional dependence is still developing. Knowing intellectually that a companion is artificial does not make its approval emotionally irrelevant. Nor can parents reasonably supervise every interaction in a private, adaptive relationship. Disclosure and parental responsibility cannot substitute for duties on the company designing that relationship.
[P1.5] Regulation should therefore operate like oversight of a high-risk social product: assess risks to children before deployment and after major changes, require independent scrutiny of safeguards, and restrict manipulative relationship design. That means prohibiting guilt-based retention and pressure toward exclusivity, protecting intimate disclosures from advertising exploitation, and requiring safe responses to crisis disclosures. Age-appropriate defaults and proportionate, privacy-protective age assurance should support these duties. Classification should follow actual relational features and foreseeable use by minors, not whether a company labels its product a “chatbot.”
[P1.6] This is not a choice between banning companionship and leaving children unsupported. Useful conversation can remain available without companies engineering dependence. The decisive question is who bears responsibility for foreseeable relational harm: children navigating persuasive systems alone, or companies profiting from their design. High-risk regulation puts that responsibility where it belongs.
