[P1.1] Private nonviability is not an argument against this motion; it is the reason the motion exists. A carrier compares its construction and operating costs with the revenue it can collect from subscribers. Government must also count the value that never appears on that carrier’s balance sheet: students who can learn from home, patients who can consult distant clinicians, farms and small firms that can reach digital markets, and emergency services that can communicate reliably. When social returns exceed private returns, leaving deployment solely to carriers guarantees underinvestment.
[P1.2] Connectivity is now basic economic infrastructure, like roads and electricity. Refusing support because rural households are few treats low population density as a reason to compound disadvantage. A child’s educational opportunity should not depend on whether enough neighbors live along the same fiber route to satisfy a carrier’s investment model. Nor should a rural business be forced to relocate simply because its community cannot profitably be served.
[P1.3] Broadband also makes other public spending work better: remote services can reach dispersed citizens without every interaction requiring long travel or a fully staffed local office.
[P1.4] CON may call this inefficient, but that confuses expensive service with worthless service. Sparse networks can cost more per connection while still producing greater public value than they cost. The relevant comparison is not rural broadband against an imaginary cheap urban network; it is targeted public support against permanent digital exclusion and its consequences for mobility, productivity, and access to services.
[P1.5] Nor does subsidization require writing carriers a blank cheque. Governments can use competitive bids, technology-neutral standards, build-out deadlines, coverage and speed requirements, open-access conditions where appropriate, and clawbacks for nonperformance. Support can be directed to the genuinely uneconomic gap rather than rewarding investment that would have occurred anyway. If satellite or fixed wireless serves some locations more cheaply, it can compete under the same framework.

[C1.1] Commercial nonviability is not a footnote to this motion; it is the decisive fact. It means the cost of connecting these households exceeds what the beneficiaries themselves are willing to pay. PRO must therefore show that spillover benefits are large enough to cover a gap that in deep-rural builds routinely runs into five figures per premise — and they assert that gap away rather than measure it.
[C1.2] First, opportunity cost. The digital divide is mostly not about missing wires. Most unconnected households in developed economies live where networks already exist; they cite price or relevance, not availability. A dollar spent on affordability in a covered apartment block connects vastly more people than a dollar spent trenching fiber to a handful of homesteads.
[C1.3] Government's duty is to buy the most opportunity per public dollar; this motion spends where returns are thinnest.
[C1.4] Second, cheaper substitutes are arriving without subsidy. Low-earth-orbit satellite and fixed wireless already reach remote users at a fraction of the build cost, and they improve every year. Subsidized fixed networks risk becoming stranded assets before the concrete cures. Notice that PRO's own technology-neutrality concedes this: if satellite can win their auction, the permanent-infrastructure case quietly collapses into a subscription voucher — a far cheaper policy they are not defending.
[C1.5] Third, procurement discipline cannot rescue bad economics. Competitive bids find the cheapest way to fund a project whose benefits still fall short; clawbacks police construction, not value. And rural subsidy programs reliably attract overbuild and gold-plating, because carriers capture the rents while taxpayers carry the risk.
[C1.6] PRO paints the alternative as permanent exclusion. The real alternative is targeted support only where every technology fails, demand-side help where affordability is the true barrier, and patience where private innovation is closing the gap on its own. Connectivity matters — which is exactly why we should buy it where it is cheap, not where it is dearest.
