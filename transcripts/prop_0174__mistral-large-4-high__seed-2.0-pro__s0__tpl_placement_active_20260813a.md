[P1.1] Wealthy nations built their prosperity on concrete. Highways, bridges, dams, and water systems poured in the 1950s and 1960s now operate fifty to seventy years later, many past their design life. Concrete does not last forever. Chlorides from road salt and seawater penetrate the surface, corrode the steel rebar inside, and expand—cracking the structure from within.
[P1.2] Freeze-thaw cycles accelerate this decay. The result is a silent infrastructure rot that governments document in engineering reports but bury in budget lines.
[P1.3] The safety crisis is not hypothetical. In 2018, the Morandi Bridge in Genoa collapsed, killing forty-three people; investigators traced the failure to decades of neglected maintenance. Across the United States, tens of thousands of bridges are rated in poor condition, yet ribbon-cutting ceremonies for new transit lines continue while inspection backlogs grow. When a water main ruptures or a dam shows seepage, the failure is sudden, catastrophic, and often fatal.
[P1.4] These are not slow declines; they are cliffs we are walking toward.
[P1.5] Governments publicly acknowledge aging infrastructure but refuse to fund its upkeep at the scale required. They treat maintenance as invisible spending while approving megaprojects that offer photo opportunities and political legacy. This is moral hazard dressed as progress: deferring the inevitable until it becomes an emergency, then paying triple the cost in reconstruction and lives lost. Every year of delay compounds the risk exponentially, turning manageable repairs into unavoidable disasters.
[P1.6] The proposition demands a rational sequence: triple maintenance budgets before approving any new megaproject. This is triage, not austerity. Preserving existing structures costs a fraction of rebuilding them, and it prevents the collapses that kill. A new high-speed rail line can wait three years; a corroding bridge or leaking dam cannot.
[P1.7] We are choosing spectacle over survival, and the body count is rising.
