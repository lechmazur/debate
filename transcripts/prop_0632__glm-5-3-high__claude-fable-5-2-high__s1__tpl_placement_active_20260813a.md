[P1.1] Obesity is a chronic, progressive disease, not a failure of willpower—and we don't let insurers refuse coverage for other chronic diseases. That's the core of this proposition: when the FDA authorizes a medication and a clinician judges it indicated, coverage should be the default, paired with value-based contracts to manage cost responsibly.
[P1.2] Start with the medical reality. Decades of evidence show that lifestyle interventions alone produce modest, rarely sustained weight loss for most patients with obesity. Biological adaptation—metabolic slowdown, hormonal hunger signaling—defends body weight. Newer anti-obesity medications work precisely because they target that biology.
[P1.3] When insurers categorically exclude them, they override treating physicians with blanket formulary decisions, something we would never tolerate for hypertension or diabetes drugs.
[P1.4] Second, this is economically rational, not just compassionate. Obesity drives enormous downstream spending: type 2 diabetes, joint replacement, cardiovascular disease, sleep apnea, liver disease. A medication that meaningfully reduces weight can delay or shrink those costs. Insurers resisting coverage are often free-riding—patients change jobs and plans, so the payer who denies today's prescription escapes tomorrow's savings while another payer inherits the complications.
[P1.5] Mandatory coverage with structured pricing fixes that misalignment.
[P1.6] Third, the proposition anticipates the obvious objection: cost. That's why it requires value-based pricing or outcomes-based contracts—paying tied to actual weight loss, adherence, or metabolic improvement, with refunds or price adjustments when drugs underperform. This is how other expensive therapies, from gene therapies to oncology drugs, are already handled. It converts "too expensive" from an excuse into a solvable contracting problem, and it gives manufacturers skin in the game.
[P1.7] What would the alternative be? Leaving coverage to voluntary insurer decisions has produced the status quo: patchy access, arbitrary exclusions, and patients who qualify clinically but get denied administratively. The proposition sets a floor—clinical indication—while keeping cost discipline through outcomes-linked payment.
[P1.8] Coverage when medicine says it's needed, discipline through how we pay. That's not a subsidy; that's treating a serious disease sensibly.
