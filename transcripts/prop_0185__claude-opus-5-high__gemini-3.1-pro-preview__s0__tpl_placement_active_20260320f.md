[P1.2] Start with why discretion fails predictably rather than occasionally. A legislator's gains from spending are concentrated, immediate, and attributable; the costs are diffuse, deferred, and land on voters who cannot yet vote. That asymmetry does not depend on bad politicians. It survives good ones.
[P1.3] Which is why advanced democracies ran deficits through booms as well as busts, and why debt ratios ratcheted up over decades in which no single decision looked reckless. If a bias is structural, the remedy has to be structural too.
[P1.4] Second, why constitutional rather than statutory. Statutory caps bind only until the majority that finds them inconvenient. America's Gramm-Rudman sequesters were repeatedly evaded and eventually abandoned; Europe's Stability and Growth Pact was bent as soon as its largest members hit it. A rule the legislature can suspend by ordinary vote is not a constraint, it is a press release.
[P1.5] Constitutional entrenchment changes the price of evasion — you need a supermajority, in public, with a stated reason. That is exactly the friction you want.
[P1.6] Third, the exception clause does countercyclical policy better than legislatures do. Unemployment insurance and progressive tax revenue respond in weeks; discretionary stimulus takes committee hearings, logrolling, and construction lead times, and arrives as the recovery is already underway. A well-drafted provision — a cyclically-adjusted target with an explicit emergency escape, in the spirit of the Swiss and German debt brakes — protects stabilizers in the downturn *and* forces repair in the upswing, which is the half of the cycle discretion always skips.
[P1.7] Finally, the sovereignty point. Fiscal space is the freedom to respond to the next pandemic or war. Countries that squander it in good times do not get to choose austerity's timing; bondholders and creditors choose for them. Greece did not vote for its adjustment program.
[P1.8] A binding rule is how a democracy keeps fiscal decisions inside its own parliament.
