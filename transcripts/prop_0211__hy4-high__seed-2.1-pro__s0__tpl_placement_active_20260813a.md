[P1.1] Obesity-related complications deserve serious prevention; that is why health systems should not reimburse preventive obesity drugs on an open-ended, pay-per-prescription basis. Broad coverage of a chronic preventive drug is a long-term promise: years of treatment for a large at-risk population, paid long before anyone can verify that diabetes cases or heart attacks have actually been avoided. The key question is who carries the uncertainty.
[P1.2] Under ordinary pricing, manufacturers carry little of it. Payment follows the prescription, not the result. If patients stop early, regain weight, have no meaningful response, or real-world effectiveness trails trial averages, the payer still pays. It also pays the opportunity cost: primary care, bariatric care, screening, mental health support, and future therapies lose ground.
[P1.3] A system can be formally generous and practically insolvent.
[P1.4] Outcome-based pricing or spending caps put that risk on the party that sets the price and promotes volume. If the drug delivers durable weight loss, better metabolic markers, or fewer complications, the manufacturer earns its price. If volume soars while outcomes lag, rebates can claw money back, and a cap limits exposure. These tools can be aggregate and risk-adjusted; they need not punish individual patients or count every kilogram.
[P1.5] A cap is simpler still: the firm that set the launch price cannot transfer unlimited exposure to payers.
[P1.6] This condition is pro-access. Uncapped reimbursement invites tomorrow's backlash: prior authorization, eligibility cliffs, narrower formularies, and cuts elsewhere. Bounded reimbursement lets a health system say yes broadly today because it can still fund the rest of care tomorrow. It also rewards genuine innovation: a company confident in durable benefit should accept some downside risk.
[P1.7] Broad reimbursement should be a bargain: access in exchange for shared risk. If manufacturers refuse outcome-based terms or spending caps, they are not asking for coverage; they are asking payers to guarantee revenue while socializing uncertainty. Health systems should decline that demand.

[C1.1] This policy does not deliver broad, timely access to preventive obesity care—it kills it, by attaching coverage to unworkable, easily gamed conditions that delay access for years while preventable, irreversible damage from obesity-related disease progresses. Severe obesity rates are rising fastest in adolescents and low-income groups who stand to gain the most from early preventive treatment, and every year of delayed access locks in thousands of preventable cases of chronic disease that could have been avoided. The long-term outcomes these drugs are meant to deliver—fewer heart attacks, lower diabetes rates, reduced disability from arthritis and fatty liver disease—take a decade or more to measure.
[C1.2] No manufacturer can lock in pricing, patient follow-up, or fair risk adjustment across that horizon, when diet, exercise, mental health care, income, and other social factors shape those outcomes far more than any single prescription. The only “outcomes” these contracts will actually track in practice are short-term weight targets, a crude proxy that incentivizes perverse care: punishing patients who experience natural weight rebound, muscle loss, or disordered eating, even when they still gain meaningful metabolic benefit from treatment. Spending caps, meanwhile, do not prevent access cliffs—they institutionalize them.
[C1.3] When an aggregate cap is hit mid-year, health systems will simply freeze new starts, tighten prior authorization, or narrow eligibility—the very access barriers this policy claims to prevent—with no accountability, because the cap will be treated as a legal out instead of a prompt to negotiate lower, transparent upfront prices. Drawn-out haggling over contract fine print also entrenches the largest incumbent manufacturers, who can afford to tie up revenue in multi-year clawback provisions, while smaller competitors and upcoming generics are locked out of broad coverage.
[C1.4] That kills the very market competition that would bring costs down sustainably for everyone, rather than papering over high launch prices with opaque back-end rebates that rarely get passed to patients or the broader health system.

[C2.1] PRO frames this as a choice between a sustainable bounded promise and future rationing, but that is a false tradeoff. When PRO claims a cap can be enforced through post-hoc rebates instead of frozen new patient starts, they ignore how every public and private payer actually operates under hard budget constraints.
[C2.2] Rebates are slow, opaque, and booked to future fiscal years; when a line item hits its contracted cap mid-year, payers do not keep approving new preventive prescriptions while waiting for a manufacturer check months later. They freeze enrollment immediately, as we see with every capped specialty drug benefit today, and patients facing irreversible disease progression get turned away with no appeal. PRO’s fallback to short-term, risk-adjusted metabolic markers rather than long-term clinical outcomes concedes our core objection: those crude proxies never fully account for the social, behavioral, and economic factors that drive patient outcomes, so manufacturers will quietly structure contracts to exclude highest-need, lowest-income patients from measured cohorts to avoid clawbacks, making “broad” coverage a hollow marketing term.
[C2.3] Nor do these rules help new competitors, as PRO claims. Deep-pocketed incumbents can afford to tie up revenue in multi-year clawback escrow and negotiate bespoke carve-outs; smaller firms and upcoming generics cannot front that risk, locking them out of formularies and entrenching the very high launch prices these back-end gimmicks claim to fix. Transparent, upfront price negotiation through standard evidence-based formulary review delivers durable, equitable access without these predictable perverse incentives.
