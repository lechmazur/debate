[P1.1] Adolescence is when identity stops being assigned and starts being claimed. A family vlog does the opposite: it freezes a child in stories they never chose, searchable by classmates, teachers, and strangers, under their real name, in their bedroom, bathroom routine, meltdown, or medical visit. A stronger legal right to request removal at adolescence restores the bargain every other citizen enjoys — you cannot permanently publish someone else's intimate life without their continuing consent.
[P1.2] That right matters because consent was never present. A four-year-old cannot understand algorithms, monetization, screenshots, or that a potty-training joke will live beyond any platform delete button through reuploads. Parents may post with love, but love does not transfer ownership of a child's likeness. We already recognize this asymmetry elsewhere: children cannot sign contracts, consent to tattoos, or waive privacy in school records precisely because early decisions haunt later selves.
[P1.3] Family vlogging should not be the one lucrative exception.
[P1.4] The harm is practical, not hypothetical. Adolescents are bullied with old clips, doxxed through school uniforms and daily routes revealed on camera, and forced to perform a public persona at home, where retreat should be possible. Even without bullying, the knowledge that thousands have watched a bedwetting confession or a first period story reshapes how a teenager speaks, dresses, and trusts. The home becomes a set, and childhood becomes content inventory.
[P1.5] A removal right is proportionate. It does not ban family vlogs, punish parenting, or erase family memories kept privately. It says that once the featured person can articulate objection in adolescence, platforms and parents must honor removal of identifying, routine-based, or embarrassing material about that minor. That balances expression with dignity: parents retain their own stories, minus the nonconsenting child's exposure.
[P1.6] Without that lever, a teenager has only pleading inside the family that profits from refusal.

[P2.5] Existing takedowns typically defer to the account holder — the parent who posted the content. That circularity is the gap this right closes.
