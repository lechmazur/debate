[P1.1] A driver who spends thirty-five hours a week delivering for an app, week after week, earns no paid sick day, no contribution toward health coverage, and nothing toward retirement for that work. A warehouse employee doing similar hours gets all three. The difference is not the work, the risk, or the need — it is a label the platform chose because that label lets it avoid the cost.
[P1.2] Prorated benefits tied to hours worked close that loophole without banning gig work. Work ten hours, accrue ten hours' worth of leave and contributions; work across two or three apps, and each pays into the same portable account for the time it used. No one has to become a full-time employee, set a fixed schedule, or lose the ability to log on and off. The mandate simply says flexibility for workers cannot mean zero security in return for real labor.
[P1.3] That security matters because platforms do not just offer occasional side jobs. For many drivers, couriers, and task workers, app work is the main income, performed under algorithmic control over pay rates, assignments, ratings, and discipline. When those workers get sick, they drive sick or lose rent money. When they age, they arrive at retirement with gaps that public programs must fill.
[P1.4] The platform captures the value of a reliable workforce while taxpayers, emergency rooms, and families absorb the predictable costs of illness, injury, and old age.
[P1.5] Requiring prorated contributions also restores honest competition. Restaurants, retailers, and logistics firms that hire employees must build benefits into their prices; apps that classify identical labor as benefit-free undercut them by regulatory arbitrage, not efficiency. A per-hour contribution levels that field and still lets platforms compete on technology, logistics, and service. If a business model only works when the people powering it go uninsured and retire poor, it is not flexible innovation — it is cost-shifting dressed up as innovation.

[C1.1] PRO's case rests on one analogy: thirty-five hours is thirty-five hours. But the motion is not an analogy; it is a government mandate, and it has to survive two questions PRO skips. Who counts the hours? And what do platforms do when those hours become expensive?
[C1.2] Gig work does not have clean hours. Platforms track active time, accepted trips, waiting for pings, and multi-apping. A driver may be logged into three apps while doing one delivery. The mandate forces a surveillance apparatus to decide which minutes count, and every disputed minute becomes a compliance fight.
[C1.3] That is not flexibility with security; it is piecework dragged into a payroll bureaucracy.
[C1.4] Then comes the multi-platform problem. Prorated contributions sound neat until a worker's week is split across four apps. Each platform sees only a slice. The worker may accrue tiny, fragmented balances that never reach eligibility thresholds, or government must build a centralized clearinghouse to aggregate them.
[C1.5] PRO treats portability as a slogan; it is the hardest part of the scheme.
[C1.6] Worse, platforms respond rationally. They cap hours just below costly thresholds, restrict schedules, deactivate marginal workers, or cut per-task pay. The people most likely to lose access are students, caregivers, retirees, and those using gig work to bridge income gaps—exactly those for whom flexible, low-commitment work matters most. Large incumbents can absorb compliance; smaller competitors cannot, so the mandate entrenches the giants PRO claims to discipline.
[C1.7] It creates a hybrid status: enough employer-like contributions to trigger compliance and control, not enough to give workers the full protections of employment.
[C1.8] If the goal is health coverage, leave, and retirement security, tie those protections to workers through broad portable accounts, not to the hour ledger of whichever app happens to dispatch them. The motion as written replaces flexibility with rationing and calls it fairness. Vote CON.

[P2.1] CON asks who counts the hours, warning of a surveillance apparatus to decide which minutes count [C1.2]. The platform already counts them. It timestamps log-ons, offers, pickups, and drop-offs to set pay, ratings, bonuses, and deactivation. Using the engaged time it already records is not new surveillance; it is finally using the same ledger for the worker that it uses for itself.
[P2.2] Disputes over minutes already exist over pay — prorated benefits do not invent that fight.
[P2.3] The multi-platform objection fails for the same reason. Fragmented balances that “never reach eligibility thresholds” [C1.4] assume thresholds. Prorated means there are none: ten hours earn ten hours’ worth, whether split across two apps or four. Each platform contributes for the time it actually used into one portable account.
[P2.4] That clearinghouse is ordinary infrastructure, not a moonshot. Workers with multiple part-time employers already have taxes withheld and retirement contributions aggregated across jobs; no one calls that unworkable because each employer sees only a slice.
[P2.5] The prediction that platforms will cap hours gets the incentive backwards. Capping is rational when crossing a line triggers a lump-sum cost, like reclassification as full-time. A strictly per-hour charge creates no cliff to dodge — the twentieth hour costs the same increment as the second, so rationing hours buys no savings. If a platform instead trims base pay, that cost is at least visible in the market, rather than hidden in uninsured illness and taxpayer-funded retirement gaps.
[P2.6] Finally, CON’s alternative concedes the need and dodges the payer. Portable accounts “tied to workers, not to the hour ledger” [C1.8] still need funding. Either workers fund them from the same inadequate pay, taxpayers fund them and preserve the subsidy platforms now enjoy, or platforms fund them according to work performed — which brings us back to hours or earnings. Flexibility should describe when people work, not whether the business using their labor contributes anything when they do.

[C2.1] PRO's rebuttal turns on two assumptions: that the platform's existing clock is the right clock, and that benefits divide neatly by the hour. Both fail.
[C2.2] "Engaged time" is not the same as working time. A driver logged on for six hours, waiting between pings, may have only three hours of accepted trips. If only engaged time counts, prorated health and leave shrink to trivia. If waiting time counts, every platform is suddenly paying benefits for hours it could not control and did not dispatch.
[C2.3] The mandate forces government to pick one definition, and platforms will then optimize around it: restrict log-on windows, throttle offers, or route workers through intermediaries. That is not using an existing ledger; it is creating a new legal fight over what an hour means.
[C2.4] Second, health coverage is not a bucket of minutes. It has fixed premiums, risk pools, enrollment rules, and participation thresholds. PRO says prorated means there are no thresholds, but health coverage has actuarial and legal thresholds whether PRO likes it or not. Ten hours split across four apps does not produce ten hours of meaningful coverage; it produces tiny contributions or heavy administrative costs.
[C2.5] To make those fragments real, government must aggregate them into a public fund with its own eligibility rules. At that point "platform-based prorated benefits" have become a general social program—arguable, but not what is being defended.
[C2.6] PRO's tax analogy also misleads. Payroll withholding is a single reporting stream to one tax authority. Benefits require contributions, eligibility, claims, and portability across firms that often treat workers as contractors and do no withholding at all. The clearinghouse is not ordinary plumbing; it is the policy.
[C2.7] And the per-hour charge still carries fixed costs—compliance, data, benefits administration, legal risk. Platforms can cut pay, cap access, or use staffing layers. Small competitors suffer most, entrenching the giants. The mandate may deliver fragments, not security, while shrinking the flexible work PRO claims to preserve.
[C2.8] Earnings-based contributions can pursue security without this hour war. Vote CON.
