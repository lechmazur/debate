[P1.1] High-impact genetic results, such as BRCA mutations or APOE4 status tied to elevated disease risk, carry concrete medical and psychological weight that raw data alone cannot convey. Customers receive variant reports without clinical context, yet these findings routinely prompt decisions about surgery, screening schedules, or family disclosure that require accurate risk calibration.
[P1.2] Without required counseling, misinterpretation is predictable rather than rare. A positive result can trigger immediate anxiety or elective procedures whose benefits depend on precise penetrance figures and personal medical history—details DTC platforms do not verify or explain. Certified counselors translate those figures into individualized probabilities, identify whether confirmatory clinical testing is warranted, and flag irrelevant variants that might otherwise drive unnecessary action.
[P1.3] Mandatory access also addresses the information asymmetry built into the business model. Companies profit from volume testing and simplified reporting; they have no ongoing duty to correct downstream harm once results are delivered. Requiring counseling inserts a trained intermediary who owes allegiance to the recipient rather than to sales metrics, shifting the transaction from product delivery to supported medical information.
[P1.4] The alternative—voluntary post-result resources—fails at the moment of delivery. Customers who receive alarming findings often act before seeking optional help, and those least equipped to interpret statistics are least likely to pursue extra steps. Requiring access upfront prevents that window of unsupported decision-making rather than attempting to repair its consequences later.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds protective. It is actually a gatekeeping mandate dressed in clinical language, and it will harm more people than it helps.
[C1.2] Start with supply reality. Certified genetic counselors are scarce—training programs produce a few hundred graduates per year across the entire country. DTC testing companies process millions of tests annually. Requiring pre-delivery counseling for every high-impact result doesn't create a safety net; it creates a bottleneck that effectively denies people access to their own biological information.
[C1.3] The mandate converts a waiting time from days into weeks or months, and in practice will hit hardest the people PRO claims to protect: those without nearby specialists, those who can't take time off work, and those in rural or underserved communities where counselors are essentially absent.
[C1.4] The second problem is definitional power. "High-impact predictive health results" is not a precise category. Someone must decide what qualifies—and once that decision is regulatory or commercial, it becomes a lever to restrict information entirely. Who certifies the certifiers?
[C1.5] The proposition hands gatekeeping authority to a system without defining its boundaries, which is a recipe for scope creep, not patient protection.
[C1.6] Third, and most fundamentally: adults who purchase these tests have already consented to receive results. They are not unwitting recipients of alarming disclosures—they sought this information about their own bodies. Requiring counseling before delivery does not respect that agency; it overrides it. There is no equivalent requirement before someone reads a cardiology textbook, uses a blood pressure cuff, or looks up disease risks online.
[C1.7] The genetic context does not suspend adult autonomy.
[C1.8] PRO argues that voluntary resources fail at the moment of delivery. The correct response is better voluntary resources—clearer result interfaces, mandatory links to follow-up options, physician referrals, robust written explanations. That addresses the information gap without building a wall between citizens and their own DNA. Mandatory gatekeeping is not the only solution to misinterpretation, and it is certainly the most restrictive one available.

[P2.4] Voluntary resources remain available after the fact, when anxiety or elective procedures have already occurred. An upfront requirement prevents that sequence without permanently withholding data. The alternative simply accepts predictable miscalibration as the price of convenience.
