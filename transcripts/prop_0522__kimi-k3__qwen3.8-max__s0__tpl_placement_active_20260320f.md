[P1.1] A family on the Bangladeshi coast or in Funafuti does not experience climate change as a legal puzzle. They experience it as a house flooding for the third time, a well turning salty. The question is whether wealthy nations must wait until lawyers finish defining these people's status before offering them a way out. The answer should be no.
[P1.2] First, delay is not neutral; it is a decision with victims. Disasters already displace millions every year—more than conflict in many years—and sea-level rise is rendering atoll states like Tuvalu and Kiribati progressively uninhabitable. Negotiations over climate-displacement law have crawled for decades. Conditioning pathways on the law being "fully settled" conditions them on never, because international law is never fully settled; it is always contested, always evolving.
[P1.3] Second, the causation runs the other way: practice settles law, not the reverse. The 1951 Refugee Convention was written amid mass displacement, not before it. Customary law crystallizes from what states actually do. Every humanitarian visa and mobility agreement tests definitions, builds precedent, and supplies the raw material that codification needs.
[P1.4] Acting now does not preempt the legal debate; it is how the legal debate gets resolved.
[P1.5] Third, this is not theory. Australia's Falepili Union with Tuvalu already creates a mobility pathway tied to climate impacts. Argentina grants humanitarian visas to people displaced by disasters in Central America and the Caribbean. Pacific labor schemes move thousands annually.
[P1.6] These programs are modest, reversible, and far cheaper than the alternative: crisis-driven arrivals at fortified borders, managed badly and paid for in lives.
[P1.7] Finally, weigh the risks asymmetrically. Act early, and the worst case is helping people a few years before the law required it. Wait, and the worst case is stranded populations, preventable deaths, and unmanaged flows that no retroactive treaty can fix.
[P1.8] High-income countries built the emissions; frontline communities are paying for them. The ocean does not wait for the law. Neither should we.

[C1.1] The proposition asks high-income countries to build a rights-sensitive migration system on a foundation that has not yet decided who has rights, who bears responsibility, and what durable status follows. That is not compassion; it is improvisation. Expanding climate-linked humanitarian pathways before climate-displacement law is settled would substitute political discretion for legal entitlement, privilege the mobile over the trapped, and weaken the durable framework vulnerable communities need.
[C1.2] Legal settlement is not a technical delay. It determines whether people fleeing sea-level rise are owed protection or merely permitted entry, whether host obligations are shared or dumped on a few generous states, and whether solutions include return, integration, or planned relocation. If wealthy states expand pathways now, they will write those answers unilaterally. The result will be a patchwork: visas for people from politically salient countries, temporary statuses with no path to permanence, and selection by skills, savings, or family ties.
[C1.3] The families least able to reach a consulate or pay for a flight—often the ones most severely affected—will be left behind.
[C1.4] Premature expansion also risks poisoning the politics it needs to survive. Humanitarian pathways are fragile when they depend on goodwill rather than obligation. If governments announce broad climate admissions before shared rules exist, opponents can frame them as open-ended charity, not lawful responsibility. Programs can be reversed with each election.
[C1.5] That instability harms applicants and gives high-income publics a reason to reject the broader legal regime later. Practice can shape law, yes—but premature state practice can also cement the wrong precedent: temporary, discretionary, and narrowly controlled by destination states.
[C1.6] Most importantly, expanding pathways first lets wealthy states perform action while avoiding harder duties: deep emissions cuts, adaptation finance, and support for in-country resilience. A small visa program is cheaper than paying for loss and damage, and it can become a moral alibi. The just order is to settle obligations first, then expand pathways under agreed criteria, financing, and rights.
