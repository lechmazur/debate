[P1.1] Fundamental discovery does not operate on a quarterly earnings cycle. It operates on decades, dead ends, and accidents. Short-cycle competitive grants pretend otherwise, and in doing so they deform science to fit their audit sheet.
[P1.2] When you must reapply every two or three years, you cannot afford to be wrong. You propose what you already know will work, slice projects into smallest publishable units, and avoid the deep, risky question that might take five years to answer. The system selects for grant writers, not trailblazers. That is exactly the opposite of what fundamental science needs.
[P1.3] Long-term block funding aligns incentives with reality. Give a lab or institute seven to ten years of stable money, with freedom to pivot when the data says so, and you get the conditions that actually produced modern science. The MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology in Cambridge — block-funded for decades — yielded the structure of DNA, protein sequencing, monoclonal antibodies, and a cascade of Nobel prizes. Bell Labs was not run on two-year project competitions.
[P1.4] The HHMI Investigator program, which funds people not projects for long stretches, consistently produces more breakthrough, high-risk work than equivalent R01 project grants.
[P1.5] Yes, this means less accountability per dollar. Some money will go to ideas that fail. Some labs will be less productive for a spell. That is the point.
[P1.6] Fundamental science is a heavy-tailed domain: one breakthrough in a hundred attempts repays the other ninety-nine a thousandfold. Short-cycle competition optimizes for efficiency in the median — making every dollar look accountable — while killing variance in the tail where progress lives. We do not need a system that guarantees no waste; we need a system that guarantees we do not miss the transistor, the mRNA modification, or CRISPR because the researcher was busy writing their next renewal.
[P1.7] Choose the portfolio that tolerates waste to capture miracles.

[C1.1] Patience without scrutiny is not a funding model; it is a gamble with other people's money. The proposition asks governments to stop checking, for a decade at a time, whether public money accomplishes anything. Even my opponent's examples refuse to run that way.
[C1.2] Look at them. HHMI Investigators win a ferocious national competition and keep funding only through periodic review — that is competition at the door, not its absence. The MRC lab's golden age ran on ruthless internal selection of people and ideas. Bell Labs was a private monopoly's playground and died with the monopoly.
[C1.3] In every romantic story about patient money, selection pressure did the work; the patience was decoration.
[C1.4] Now consider block grants at scale. Money flows to institutions, and institutions allocate by seniority, internal politics, and last decade's prestige. The famous lab coasts; the unknown thirty-year-old at a small university with a heretical idea has no door left to knock on. Competitive peer review is imperfect, but it is the only mechanism ever devised where an outsider can beat an incumbent on the strength of an idea alone.
[C1.5] Abolish it, and science becomes an inheritance system.
[C1.6] Accountability is not a tax on science; it is the price of the money. Public funding must be re-justified to taxpayers and parliaments every budget cycle, and it survives precisely because funders can show what each dollar did. One visible failure under a no-questions block grant — ten years of salaries, nothing to show — and the whole enterprise takes the cut. Nor are long-term promises credible: governments change, and an untraceable grant line is the easiest one to slash.
[C1.7] Risk-aversion is a real disease with a targeted cure: high-risk funding lines, novelty scoring, longer renewable awards — reforms that keep competition's information while fixing its timidity. Science needs patience and judgment. Block grants buy patience by amputating judgment. That trade loses.
