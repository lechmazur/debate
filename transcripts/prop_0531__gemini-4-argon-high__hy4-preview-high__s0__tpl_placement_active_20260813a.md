[P1.1] Civil wars do not end simply because the shooting stops; they end when the political machinery that turns neighbors into enemies is dismantled. Public memorial projects are either the blueprint for a new shared state or the staging ground for the next war. When post-conflict states preserve fully separate, competing ethnic narratives in public memorials, they freeze wartime frontlines into stone.
[P1.2] Consider what preserving fully separate, competing ethnic narratives actually looks like on the ground. In Bosnia and Herzegovina, three segregated memorial cultures operate in parallel. In one town, a monument casts one ethnic group as sole martyrs and their neighbors as eternal aggressors; a few miles away, a rival monument tells the exact inverse story and erases the first group’s dead entirely. That is not healthy pluralism.
[P1.3] It is institutionalized denialism. When each community curates an exclusive narrative of its own victimhood while erasing crimes committed in its name, memorials become permanent engines of grievance, ready to be ignited by the next ethnic demagogue.
[P1.4] Emphasizing a shared civic account of violence solves this without erasing historical truth. A shared civic narrative does not mean false moral equivalence or pretending every side suffered identically. Rather, it means anchoring public memory in universal standards of citizenship and human rights rather than tribal bloodlines. It establishes a single, integrated historical record where every civilian killed is mourned as a fellow citizen, and where atrocities, including asymmetric ones, are attributed to specific perpetrators, extremist ideologies, and failed institutions rather than the collective guilt of an entire ethnic group.
[P1.5] Look at post-apartheid South Africa’s flagship memorials, such as Constitution Hill. They did not pretend apartheid was symmetrical, nor did they grant separate, competing racial narratives equal state sanction. Instead, they framed deeply unequal suffering as a shared national tragedy that demanded a common constitutional citizenship.
[P1.6] After a civil war, former enemies must share a parliament, a legal system, and a street. A state cannot build a unified future while funding segregated pasts.
