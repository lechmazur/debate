[C1.1] The motion rests on a false, unworkable binary that pits residents against tourism, ignoring both the unadministrable nature of its core threshold and far less destructive alternatives to blunt arrival caps.
[C1.2] The conditional standard the motion sets is a recipe for arbitrary, economically harmful policy. There is no consistent, objective way to measure when “resident crowding, pollution, and housing pressure outweigh tourist revenue gains”—the metric shifts depending on who is counting, and can be easily manipulated to justify caps that serve political goals rather than resident needs. A cap set to reduce perceived crowding might gut the tourism tax revenue that funds affordable housing, historic preservation, and public transit in cities like Charleston or Lisbon, leaving residents worse off even if crowds are slightly thinner.
[C1.3] The motion also treats caps as the only solution to tourism-related harms, ignoring targeted, less economically damaging fixes. Cities can mandate shore power for docked cruise ships to cut emissions, create dedicated tourist shuttle routes that avoid residential neighborhoods, and enforce strict zoning to limit short-term rentals that displace long-term tenants. Amsterdam cut port emissions by nearly 90% with shore power requirements before even considering arrival limits, addressing the pollution harm the motion cites without sacrificing tourism revenue or local jobs.
[C1.4] Finally, the motion overstates the extent of revenue “leakage” to global operators. Even when cruise lines or tour operators are international, the local multiplier is substantial: tourism spending supports local restaurant, retail, and hospitality workers, who are overwhelmingly city residents. Blunt caps don’t just cut profits for distant corporations—they slash incomes for the local working class who rely on tourism-adjacent employment, while doing nothing to address the root causes of housing pressure or overcrowding.

[C2.1] PRO mischaracterizes the motion’s conditional threshold as ordinary cost-benefit governance [P2.1], but this is a false equivalence. Congestion pricing measures fungible, comparable costs—time, fuel, wear—against a clear revenue stream; the motion requires weighing incommensurable goods: a tenant’s eviction against a tourism dollar, air quality against a waiter’s hourly wage. There is no neutral, apolitical way to assign those weights, meaning the “conditional” cap will always be shaped by whichever group can frame their costs as more urgent. That is not governance; it is arbitrary rulemaking dressed up as math.
[C2.2] PRO also dismisses targeted, less destructive fixes as insufficient [P2.3], but this is a false choice. Shore power that cuts port emissions by nearly 90% solves the pollution harm without sacrificing revenue; dedicated tourist shuttles that route visitors away from residential squares solve crowding without cutting arrivals; dynamic tourist taxes fund affordable housing to address displacement without shutting off the revenue that keeps those programs solvent. These tools avoid the collateral damage of blunt arrival limits while still addressing the harms PRO cites.
[C2.3] Finally, PRO’s claim that the marginal 10,000th visitor breaks city systems [P2.7] is a failure of management, not an inevitability of tourism. That marginal visitor can be charged a targeted fee to fund extra transit, waste removal, or housing subsidies, internalizing their costs rather than banning them outright. PRO’s logic would justify capping any activity with marginal costs—restaurants, concerts, even new residents—but we do not do that, because we have tools to manage scale without shutting off benefits entirely. A cap does not preserve a historic city; it trades one set of harms for another, with no consistent, fair way to decide which residents lose and which gain.
