[P1.1] Continuous in-car surveillance is not a pricing tweak; it is a permanent observer in the most private machine most people own. The real issue is not accuracy. Because driving is legally mandatory, conditioning affordable insurance on constant monitoring turns a legal duty into a privacy toll. Without a genuine right to refuse—without punishment—consent is fiction.
[P1.2] Insurers will say tracking lowers premiums. It does not matter if a discount exists when the price of refusal is an inflated baseline. Motorists cannot walk away; they must drive. So the "choice" to be monitored is closer to a tollbooth on a public road: pay with data or pay with money.
[P1.3] Buried opt-in in an app you cannot practically leave is still not opt-in.
[P1.4] The harms are concrete. A program logging every trip captures far more than speed or braking; it maps a life: late-night pharmacy runs, clinic visits, union meetings, a second job, routes through poor neighborhoods. From mobility, insurers can infer health, religion, politics, and poverty. A single trip to a clinic can imply pregnancy or chronic illness.
[P1.5] Even if only cornering is scored, the data exhaust can be repurposed, sold, breached, or subpoenaed. Accepting this for affordable coverage is not consumer choice; it is surrendering private-life privacy to a private regulator.
[P1.6] Nor is this meritocracy. Monitoring penalizes the unavoidable. A night-shift nurse brakes hard in unpredictable traffic; a worker swerves around potholes; a parent makes sudden trips for a sick child. Scores fall from circumstance, not recklessness.
[P1.7] The compliance discount is often small next to the surveillance footprint. That is coercion, not a bargain.
[P1.8] Continuous surveillance pricing should be barred unless participation is default-off, revocable without penalty, limited to driving dynamics, transparent, and audited. Those terms must ban resale, require deletion, and face public audit, not insurer marketing. Insurers may reward safe driving; they may not price legal mobility as a permanent, opaque surrender of liberty.
