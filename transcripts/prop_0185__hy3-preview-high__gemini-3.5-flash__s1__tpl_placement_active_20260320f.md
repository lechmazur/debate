[P1.1] Legislative discretion for fiscal discipline fails systematically because elected officials face pervasive short-term electoral incentives that clash with long-term sustainability. This is not a rare flaw; it is a structural feature of representative democracy when fiscal rules are left to annual legislative whim.
[P1.2] Entrenching a balanced-budget rule in the constitution removes this temptation. It forces lawmakers to prioritize solvency without bearing the full political cost of each individual austerity measure, since the rule is a pre-committed, shared constraint. Constitutional rules, by contrast, require supermajorities or public referenda to amend, making them far harder to bypass for short-term political gain.
[P1.3] Crucially, the proposition’s explicit carveout for automatic stabilizers prevents the most common critique of rigid fiscal rules: pro-cyclical austerity that deepens recessions.
[P1.4] Automatic stabilizers—progressive tax receipts that fall in downturns, unemployment benefits that rise—are countercyclical by design, and exempting them means the rule only binds when the economy is operating near potential output. This avoids the mistake of past rigid balanced-budget mandates that worsened downturns, while still preventing the slow-rolling debt crises that discretionary fiscal policy inevitably produces over decades. Consider that countries relying solely on legislative discretion have seen average debt-to-GDP ratios rise far faster over the past several decades than those with constitutional fiscal rules, with no corresponding improvement in public services to justify the added debt.
[P1.5] Debt-driven currency crashes and austerity forced by bond markets disproportionately harm low-income households, a harm that a pre-committed constitutional rule would prevent.
