[P1.1] Picture your last delivery order. You picked the restaurant partly because the total looked reasonable. Then, at the final screen: service fee, delivery fee, small-order fee — and a $22 meal costs $31. You pay anyway, because you have already spent fifteen minutes building the order and you're hungry.
[P1.2] That is not a market transaction; it is a trap, and it works precisely because the fee arrives after you've committed.
[P1.3] The case rests on one principle: a mandatory charge is part of the price. If the customer cannot decline it, calling it a "service fee" rather than part of the price is a labeling trick, not a real distinction. The motion asks only that the true price travel with the product through the buying journey, so the number a consumer sees when deciding is the number they actually pay.
[P1.4] Three harms follow from the status quo. First, drip pricing exploits sunk-cost psychology: by the time fees appear, buyers have invested time and attention, and people systematically underweight late-revealed costs when judging whether the deal is good. Second, hidden fees destroy comparison shopping. A $50 ticket with a $20 checkout fee cannot be honestly weighed against a competitor's $65 all-in ticket, so firms compete on concealment rather than value — and the honest firm that posts its real price up front is punished for its honesty.
[P1.5] Third, the asymmetry: the business knows exactly what it will charge; the consumer learns only after surrendering time, data, and payment details.
[P1.6] The obvious reply — compliance burden — collapses on inspection. A business sets its own fees; displaying them one screen earlier is a software choice, not a hardship. And when jurisdictions like California have recently required advertised prices to include mandatory charges, commerce did not collapse; the checkout simply stopped lying.
[P1.7] Judge this debate on informed consent: which side lets a buyer see the true price at the moment of decision? That is the whole motion, and it is the bare minimum of an honest market.
