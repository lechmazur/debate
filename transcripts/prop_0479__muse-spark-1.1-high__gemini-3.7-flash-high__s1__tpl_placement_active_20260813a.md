[P1.1] Policy lives or dies on forecasts — will inflation persist, will a dam overrun, will a regulation kill jobs — and the current system for getting those forecasts is broken. Expert panels are small, homogenous, selected for pedigree and political compatibility, with zero cost for being wrong and high cost for dissenting. Prediction markets fix the incentive problem by making honesty profitable.
[P1.2] A subsidized prediction market asks a precise, verifiable question — unemployment above 5.5% one year after a minimum wage hike? cost overrun >20% on high-speed rail? — and lets anyone with relevant information bet. The price is a live, public probability. Unlike a panel that votes and goes home, traders gain only by being right, especially when they disagree with consensus. The marginal trader who knows the factory floor, the supply chain, the local bureaucracy, can move the price without needing a PhD or an invitation.
[P1.3] Aggregation beats credentials. That's why even thin, subsidized markets have repeatedly beaten expert consensus on elections, disease spread, and technology timelines — not because traders are geniuses, but because the institution harvests dispersed private knowledge experts never see.
[P1.4] Subsidy is key. Government already pays experts through grants, contracts, and salaries. Paying market makers instead buys liquidity and rewards accuracy, not prestige. With small position limits, verified identity, and open auditing, manipulation and wealth effects are contained, while subsidies ensure markets exist even for important but niche policy questions where internal forecasts are optimized to please bosses, not to be right.
[P1.5] As a formal input, this does not hand policy to gamblers; it hands decision-makers a calibrated, auditable, real-time reality check that expert judgment cannot provide. When we must base billion-dollar bets on uncertain futures, we should base them on the mechanism that punishes error, not the one that rewards conformity.

[C1.1] A market price aggregates bets on whether a discrete event will occur, but governance requires understanding *why*, *how*, and *under what counterfactuals*. Subsidizing prediction markets as a formal policy input replaces accountable, causal analysis with an easily gamed black box that is structurally ill-suited for governing.
[C1.2] First, prediction markets fail at counterfactual policy reasoning. Policymakers must choose between competing hypothetical futures: what happens to inflation if we raise interest rates versus if we do not? But decision-conditional markets suffer from a fatal resolution paradox. If a market predicts disaster under Policy A, the government sensibly rejects Policy A.
[C1.3] Because Policy A never occurs, that market can never resolve against real-world data. Traders know this, which distorts their incentives: you cannot run a reliable market on futures that the market’s own output prevents from happening.
[C1.4] Second, major policy decisions invite severe distortion and financial manipulation. PRO claims subsidies and position limits will contain wealth effects, but major policy choices govern trillions of dollars. For industrial lobbies, defense contractors, or foreign adversaries, burning millions to skew a formal market price is trivial compared to the windfall of influencing legislation. If governments impose strict position limits to block deep-pocketed manipulators, they simultaneously destroy liquidity and prevent genuinely informed traders from correcting false signals.
[C1.5] You are left with a thin, subsidized sandbox easily hijacked by coordinated campaigns.
[C1.6] Third, expert panels provide explanatory reasoning and democratic accountability that prices fundamentally lack. An expert report exposes its causal mechanisms, methodology, and trade-offs to public cross-examination and democratic oversight. A prediction market produces only a naked probability. It cannot explain *why* an intervention might fail, which assumptions are fragile, or what ethical trade-offs are involved.
[C1.7] Sound governance requires transparent, interrogable evidence—not outsourcing crucial policy decisions to the opaque, speculative noise of subsidized betting pools.

[P2.1] The counterfactual objection [C1.2][C1.3] sounds fatal until you describe how conditional markets actually work. We don't run one market that self-negates. We run two: "If government chooses A, what is inflation next year?" "If it chooses B, what is inflation?"
[P2.2] Only the market for the policy actually chosen resolves and pays out; the other is voided. Traders therefore know exactly what to forecast: the outcome *given* that policy happens. That eliminates the paradox.
[P2.3] More importantly, experts suffer the exact same unobservability. If a panel warns that Policy A will be disaster and the government avoids A, we also never test whether the panel was right. The difference is [C1.3] distorts market incentives, but it destroys expert accountability completely, because experts face zero cost for crying wolf while traders who cry wolf lose money when the chosen policy's market resolves.
[P2.4] The manipulation trap [C1.4][C1.5] is a double-bind that collapses under its own logic. A lobby that would burn millions to move a capped, identity-verified, publicly audited price against informed traders who profit by correcting it can capture an expert panel for free with testimony, funding, and revolving-door appointments. Expert capture is cheap and invisible. Market manipulation is expensive and visible — you have to take sustained losses to hold a price wrong, and the subsidy CON fears is what pays your opponents to take that money.
[P2.5] Finally, [C1.6][C1.7] treats "formal input" as if it means replacement. It doesn't. A naked probability is not governance, it's a check on governance. A price alone doesn't tell you why, but it tells you *when* your why is probably wrong.
[P2.6] If your 80-page causal report says high-speed rail will be on budget and the subsidized market says 75% chance of >20% overrun, you now have to defend assumptions in public. That's more accountability, not less — calibration from markets plus reasons from experts, instead of reasons alone with no scorekeeping.

[C2.1] PRO’s defense of conditional markets [P2.1–P2.2] fails because voiding unchosen bets creates a severe new distortion: strategic gaming of policy selection itself. If a trader faces heavy losses under Policy A, they have every incentive to artificially depress Policy B's market to force the government into Policy A, wiping out their bad position when Policy B voids. You cannot insulate a betting pool from political gaming when the market's output directly decides which bets survive. Moreover, voiding guarantees that the rejected policy’s counterfactual is never verified [P2.3], leaving conditional markets just as unscoreable as the expert panels PRO criticizes.
[C2.2] On manipulation [P2.4], PRO commits a fatal economic error by assuming bad actors care about trading losses. When a defense procurement, energy subsidy, or regulatory rule is worth billions, burning five million dollars to skew a formal market price is trivial lobbying overhead. Informed traders will not risk private capital trying to "correct" a market against a price-insensitive corporate or foreign actor with limitless liquidity. If governments impose tight position limits to stop this, they eliminate the financial incentive for informed participants to correct mispricings; if they do not, deep pockets dictate policy odds.
[C2.3] Subsidies do not solve this—they just subsidize the distortion.
[C2.4] Finally, PRO attempts to downplay the stakes [P2.5], framing markets as a harmless supplementary check. But the resolution explicitly demands using markets *rather than relying mainly* on expert panels and internal forecasts. That substitution is disastrous. A naked price is a diagnostic dead end: a 75% probability of a transit overrun [P2.6] cannot tell officials whether the culprit is labor scarcity, flawed engineering, or vendor fraud.
[C2.5] Expert panels provide the causal frameworks, transparent evidence, and democratic accountability required to actually resolve policy trade-offs. Replacing substantive reasoning with an uninterpretable betting ticker is not an upgrade; it is an abdication of governance.
