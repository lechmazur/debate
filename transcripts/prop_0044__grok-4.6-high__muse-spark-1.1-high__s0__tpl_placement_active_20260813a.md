[P1.1] A smart doorbell, a child’s tablet, a kitchen speaker: the hardware can still power on long after the maker stops security patches. The buyer never saw that cutoff on the box. That is the market failure this rule closes.
[P1.2] For a connected device, support life is not a courtesy. It is part of the product. Without updates the camera still records, the lock still clicks, and the router still routes—only now they do so on a widening hole. Shoppers already compare screens and batteries at the shelf.
[P1.3] They cannot compare the one attribute that decides whether the device stays a tool or becomes a household risk.
[P1.4] Voluntary statements fail because they are optional, buried after checkout, and easy to shorten later. A guaranteed figure on physical packaging makes the commitment visible before money moves. It follows the same logic as an energy label or a use-by date: put the decisive fact where the decision happens.
[P1.5] The rule does not prescribe seven years or two. It only requires a firm to state what it will actually stand behind. Makers who already plan long support can advertise it. Makers who plan a short runway must say so, and buyers can walk.
[P1.6] That is competition on a real cost of ownership, not central planning.
[P1.7] Leave the box blank and you keep surprise obsolescence: homes full of unpatchable gadgets, and waste created not by broken parts but by silent abandonment. Printing a support term is cheap, timed to the only moment that matters, and hard to hide. If a company will not put a number on the carton, it is because the number would change the sale. That is precisely why the number belongs there.

For a smart device "support" is a bundle: security patches from a chip vendor, Android versions from Google, an app maintained by three people, and a cloud server that costs money every month. No manufacturer controls all of it.
[C1.3] Requiring a guaranteed lifespan forces them to guarantee what they don't own, or to disclaim everything below the shortest upstream promise. The rational legal move is to print "6 months" to avoid being sued.
[C1.4] That is exactly what [P1.5] gets wrong. It says the rule doesn't prescribe length, only honesty. In practice a legally enforceable guarantee on packaging becomes a liability reserve. [C1.5] Big firms will do the same to avoid class actions if a critical vulnerability forces early termination. Instead of competition upward, you get a race to the smallest defensible number.
[C1.6] Physical packaging makes it worse [P1.4]. Most boxes are printed half a year in advance in another country, then sit in channels. Most devices are bought without ever seeing the box, online. When a policy needs to change you don't reprint a website - you landfill millions of cartons.
[C1.7] When a company goes bust, that guarantee on the carton is still litter, not a patch.
[C1.8] And it will increase waste, not cut it. Shoppers will read "Support until 2027" as "expires 2027" and toss a working device, exactly like misread use-by dates. If we want informed buyers, require a clear, updateable digital disclosure at checkout, not a misleading, unchangeable, litigable number on trash.
