[P1.1] Copyright’s entire constitutional and doctrinal basis in the U.S. exists to incentivize human creative labor, not to grant exclusive monopolies over outputs assembled by statistical models trained on massive swathes of unlicensed existing creative work. Without a requirement that a human document substantial creative control over the final expression, granting copyright to raw AI outputs creates two cascading harms that defeat copyright’s core public purpose.
[P1.2] First, it encloses vast swathes of the public domain for no meaningful creative gain. A user who types a generic prompt like “cyberpunk cat detective in a rain-soaked alley” into a free generative tool, accepts the first unedited output, and claims full copyright could sue independent artists who arrive at similar visual or narrative tropes, or even target the very creators whose work trained the model, without any proof they contributed expressive choices beyond a short, vague request. That is not incentivizing creativity—it is enabling low-effort rent-seeking on work stitched together from other people’s uncompensated labor, creating a new class of copyright trolls with no actual creative investment in the works they claim.
[P1.3] Second, the substantial documented control standard is clear and administrable, not an unworkable hurdle. Without documentation, courts would be forced into expensive, subjective battles over how much generic prompt engineering counts as authorship, with no consistent baseline to separate passive tool use from active creation. The standard recognizes valid human authorship where creators can show iterative, deliberate shaping: manual editing of individual expressive elements, targeted selection across generations to refine specific tonal and compositional choices, or transformative modification that turns a raw model output into a distinct, personally realized work.
[P1.4] This aligns AI copyright with longstanding rules for collaborative works, while avoiding a flood of invalid claims that would smother rather than support new creative work.

[C1.1] Copyright in America has never required a creator to prove anything before protection attaches. Press a camera shutter and the photograph is yours, instantly, with no paperwork, no "substantial" threshold, no showing that you controlled the lighting the sun provided. That has been the rule since the nineteenth century, when courts rejected the argument that a camera, not a person, made the picture. The proposition asks us to abandon that settled architecture for one category of tool, and the three words doing the damage are "document," "substantial," and "final."
[C1.2] "Document" imports formalities that U.S. law deliberately abandoned decades ago. Who keeps a logbook? Corporations with legal departments, not the freelance illustrator iterating through forty prompts at midnight. A documentation requirement does not stop rent-seeking; it decides who gets to rent-seek, and the answer is whoever can afford compliance.
[C1.3] "Substantial" quietly replaces the actual standard, which is a modicum of creativity. Phone-book compilations fail it; almost everything else passes. Creating a special, higher bar for AI-assisted work means a novelist who used a model to draft one chapter faces a different originality test than her neighbor who used a thesaurus. That is a tool tax, not a principle.
[C1.4] "Final expression" ignores where human creativity usually lives in this process: selection, arrangement, rejection, iteration. The law already protects those choices in compilations and photography.
[C1.5] The troll scenario in [P1.2] misreads how infringement works. Copyright in a specific cyberpunk cat image has never covered the trope of cyberpunk cats. Ideas, tropes, and stock elements are unprotectable regardless of who made the image. Low-creativity works get thin protection against near-verbatim copying and nothing more.
[C1.6] The existing doctrine already contains the problem.
[C1.7] What the proposition actually produces is a vast class of unprotectable works that anyone may copy wholesale, plus a strong incentive for creators to lie about their tools. Neither serves the constitutional purpose.
