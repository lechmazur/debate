[P1.1] A ban does not erase a tool students already carry in their pockets and will be expected to use the day after graduation. Treating generative AI as permissible by default, with mandatory citation and process transparency, protects learning without training students to lie.
[P1.2] The alternative fails on its own terms. Broad bans do not remove the technology; they remove the disclosure. A student who pastes an essay into a chatbot and submits it silently is indistinguishable from a student who used the same tool to test an argument, then rewrote it and said so. Detectors are error-prone, and false flags often fall hardest on non-native writers.
[P1.3] The ban regime punishes the honest and the atypical, while rewarding concealment. That is an integrity policy that manufactures dishonesty.
[P1.4] Permission with transparency flips the incentive. If use is allowed when cited, the academic offense is concealment, not the tool. That is how schools already handle libraries, calculators, and grammar software: assistance is legitimate when its role is visible. Citation forces students to name what the model contributed—ideas, structure, wording—and process notes force them to show drafts, prompts, and revisions.
[P1.5] A teacher can then grade the thinking, not the illusion that thinking never touched a machine.
[P1.6] This does not surrender the assignment. Default permission is not default substitution. Teachers remain free to require in-class writing, oral explanation, staged drafts, or tasks where unaided work is the point. Those restrictions should be chosen because the learning goal requires them, not imposed as blanket suspicion of every homework essay.
[P1.7] A ban cannot distinguish a literature review from a timed proof; a transparency rule can.
[P1.8] The competence worth teaching is judgment under disclosure: when the output is wrong, when reliance is lazy, and how to show the work. Bans teach evasion. Citation teaches a norm that survives graduation.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes the purpose of an assignment: it is not a graded product but the formation of a skill. A student who lets a chatbot propose a thesis, outline the body, and smooth the transitions may submit a fluent essay while never developing the capacity to originate a claim and defend it. The citation records the debt; the student does not get better at the hard part. Default permission converts out-of-class work into delegated production wearing academic dress.
[C1.2] We allow calculators after number sense exists, not while students learn division. Under default permission AI arrives precisely when students are acquiring writing and reasoning fluency, removing the struggle that builds judgment. PRO concedes this by allowing restrictions because the learning goal requires them [P1.6]. If those limits are often needed, permission is the wrong baseline.
[C1.3] The default for assigned work should be unaided practice; exceptions should be explicit, task-specific opt-ins, not a permission teachers must individually revoke.
[C1.4] "Process transparency" also overpromises. PRO says process notes force showing drafts, prompts, and revisions [P1.4]. But these tools do not merely produce answers; they can shape that trail itself—hesitant drafts, revision notes, even reflections about where the model erred. A busy teacher cannot authenticate every iteration.
[C1.5] Transparency becomes theater: paperwork certifying that a student used AI in the approved way while the real thinking remains outsourced. The boast that "the academic offense is concealment, not the tool" [P1.4] changes the label, not the learning.
[C1.6] Disclosure does not remove the incentive to substitute; it legitimates it. If AI-shaped structure and ideas are acceptable once cited, the norm becomes "cite and ship," not think unaided. Permission trains dependence under the banner of honesty.
[C1.7] The defensible rule is the reverse: ban by default for assessed work, and authorize AI only for defined purposes—brainstorming, feedback on finished drafts, debugging after effort. That protects honesty and the competence schools exist to build.
