[P1.1] Geography is not a principle of justice. Yet the present system makes a migrant’s point of arrival determine which country must house, screen, and integrate them. Greece, Italy, or Spain can face a sudden surge because of sea routes and conflicts far beyond their control, while states insulated by location contribute comparatively little. That is not a manageable asylum policy; it is an accident of mapmaking.
[P1.2] The EU should therefore adopt binding asylum burden-sharing quotas, distributing responsibility according to members’ capacity, such as population and economic strength. Quotas should relocate applicants and share processing duties, not merely send money to overcrowded frontline facilities. Every state would remain obliged to protect borders, register arrivals, and reject unfounded claims through fair procedures. What changes is that a few states no longer carry most of the common burden.
[P1.3] First, quotas correct a basic collective-action failure. The EU benefits from shared borders and free movement, but allows individual governments to leave the costs of protecting those borders to others. Predictably, frontline states face overflowing reception centres and overloaded courts, while non-frontline states have an incentive to resist arrivals and hope applicants move elsewhere. Mandatory quotas make every member a stakeholder in functioning reception and decision-making.
[P1.4] Second, quotas improve control rather than weaken it. When one state is overwhelmed, identification slows, documentation becomes inconsistent, and people disappear into irregular onward movement. Spreading applicants across adequate facilities enables faster screening, better security checks, and more orderly integration or return. The alternative is not neat national control; it is chaotic control at the EU’s most pressured edges.
[P1.5] Third, quotas protect solidarity before crisis makes cooperation impossible. Emergency bargains encourage each government to wait, bargain, and externalise costs. A standing allocation rule replaces improvised blame with predictable obligations.
[P1.6] Opponents may invoke national sovereignty, but membership already pools border and movement consequences; sovereignty cannot mean claiming the benefits while exporting the costs. Nor should Frontex funding or financial contributions substitute for quotas: money cannot instantly create judges, housing, or local capacity. A common asylum space requires common responsibility. [C1.1] The 2015 relocation scheme is not a hypothetical for PRO to idealize; it is the experiment already run. The EU adopted binding quotas, took it to the Court of Justice against Hungary, Poland, and Slovakia, won the legal case — and still achieved only a small fraction of the planned relocations. Compliance collapsed not because of bad luck but because quotas clash with something more durable than treaty text: democratic consent over who settles in a country. You cannot litigate a population into accepting resettlement it did not vote for.
[C1.2] That failure is the central fact CON asks you to weigh against PRO's theory.
[C1.3] Quotas also misdiagnose the mechanism of migration. Asylum seekers do not distribute themselves according to administrative allocation; they move toward existing diaspora networks, language, labor markets, and family ties in Germany, Sweden, or France. Relocate someone to a quota slot in rural Slovakia or Estonia and the predictable result is secondary movement back toward preferred destinations, exactly as happened under Dublin transfers. PRO's own claim that dispersal improves "control" has it backwards: forced allocation against migrants' actual destination preferences generates more absconding and irregularity, not less.
[C1.4] The political cost compounds the practical one. Mandatory quotas are the single most reliable fuel for the populist right across the EU — it is the policy that produced Brexit-adjacent rhetoric, hardened Visegrád politics, and handed anti-EU parties their best recruiting pitch: "Brussels will tell you who lives in your town." A policy that corrodes public support for the asylum system itself is not solidarity; it is slow-motion sabotage of the system PRO claims to be saving.
[C1.5] There is a real alternative already emerging in EU practice: flexible solidarity, where states choose between relocation, funding frontline reception capacity, or providing personnel and processing support, as reflected in the newer Pact framework. This respects sovereignty, scales to political reality, and still gets resources to Greece and Italy — without the enforcement failure, the secondary-movement chaos, or the backlash that binding quotas guarantee.

[P2.1] The 2015 scheme does not show that sharing responsibility is impossible; it shows the danger of improvising it after a crisis, then allowing governments to treat binding obligations as optional. An emergency relocation programme with contested implementation is not a standing quota system with agreed allocation rules, adequate reception planning, and consequences for refusal. CON’s conclusion is perverse: because some states obstructed cooperation, frontline states should remain exposed and obstruction should be rewarded with a veto.
[P2.2] The secondary-movement objection also cuts against CON. Under the present system, people are concentrated in arrival states regardless of family, language, or employment connections, while overloaded registration and reception encourage onward movement. A quota need not mean assigning someone blindly to “rural Slovakia.” Allocation can account for family unity, existing ties, language, and capacity, while common registration and reception standards make responsibility clear. No system eliminates absconding, but preserving Dublin-style concentration simply preserves the conditions CON criticises.
[P2.3] “Flexible solidarity” is the weakest part of their alternative. If each government chooses its contribution, the states least willing to accept applicants will predictably choose the contribution least costly to them. Frontline states still receive the bodies, the hearings, the accommodation pressures, and the political blame; others send cheques or personnel and call it solidarity. Funding matters, but it cannot guarantee that reception capacity and decision-makers expand at the speed arrivals do.
[P2.4] A menu without a minimum relocation obligation is burden-sharing only when every state feels like sharing.
[P2.5] Finally, CON asks us to abandon fairness because anti-EU parties might exploit it. Yet abandoning Greece or Italy to repeated crisis also fuels resentment: there, the story is that richer members enjoy common borders while outsourcing their costs. Democratic consent cannot mean consent for some members to impose disproportionate burdens on others.
[P2.6] The choice is not between frictionless quotas and frictionless national control. It is between enforceable, predictable sharing and a system whose flexibility reliably protects everyone except the states under greatest pressure.

[P4.1] A1: Capacity and ties answer different questions. Capacity sets each member’s binding share of responsibility; family, language, and other connections help match applicants within that allocation, with states able to exchange placements while retaining their overall totals. Where no relevant tie exists, the capacity formula governs. Dublin instead makes first arrival the organising principle and leaves frontline states carrying the residual burden.
[P4.2] Considering ties therefore improves placement without abolishing burden-sharing; CON’s “either formula or ties” dilemma is simply false.
[P4.3] A2: Put compliance into the standing rules rather than relying on one retrospective court case. Missed allocation deadlines should trigger an automatic schedule of financial penalties and compensatory contributions, funding reception and processing where places are actually provided, with infringement proceedings and court penalties as the backstop. No rule can physically force a hostile government to cooperate.
[P4.4] But CON’s voluntary menu cannot force cooperation either; it merely makes refusal costless. Quotas can make obstruction expensive, visible, and immediately connected to relief.
[P4.5] That is the decisive comparison. CON treats the new Pact’s flexibility as proof that quotas cannot work, but governments choosing a politically convenient option does not prove that option protects frontline states. It proves only that reluctant governments prefer choosing their own exposure. When arrivals exceed capacity, “flexibility” supplies no answer to who must provide the missing beds, interviews, and decisions.
[P4.6] Nor does 2015 establish that every standing system must fail. It establishes that obligations without prompt, rule-based consequences invite delay. CON’s alternative generalises that weakness: every contribution remains subject to each government’s willingness, precisely when pressure is greatest.
[P4.7] The choice is therefore not guaranteed quotas versus guaranteed flexibility. It is a binding floor with enforcement and compensatory relief versus discretionary contributions with no floor at all. On fairness, preparedness, and actual control of arrivals, a system that can require sharing beats one that can only request it.
