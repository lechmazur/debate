[P1.1] Advanced military AI is not a software trick detached from hardware. It depends on enormous compute for training models, rapid inference on edge platforms, and the sensors, radars, drones, and command systems that process battlefield data at machine speed. The bottleneck is not just code; it is access to frontier semiconductors and the machines that make them.
[P1.2] We should restrict exports of advanced semiconductor manufacturing equipment because it targets the hardest-to-replace layer of an adversary’s supply chain. Tools such as extreme-ultraviolet lithography, precision etching, deposition, and metrology are produced by a small number of firms and require decades of tacit engineering knowledge. An adversary can smuggle a chip design, but it cannot easily replicate an entire ecosystem of suppliers, chemistry, optics, and process control. Restrictions therefore buy time: time in which the military balance can shift, sanctions can bite, and adversaries must spend scarce resources on inferior substitutes rather than deploying capable systems.
[P1.3] The commercial cost is real, but it is the wrong metric to optimize. A firm losing sales abroad internalizes profit and loss, while the security externality is paid by everyone else. If exported tools help an adversary field faster target recognition, autonomous swarms, better missile guidance, or more effective cyber operations, the damage is not a market correction; it is a strategic liability. Commercial losses can be absorbed or redirected; battlefield surprise cannot be undone.
[P1.4] Governments exist precisely to prevent that kind of short-term commercial rationality from undermining collective defense.
[P1.5] Finally, delay is itself a strategic benefit. Military AI programs depend on iterative testing: train, fail, improve, redeploy. Slowing chip availability slows the loop. Even imperfect controls raise the adversary’s cost, force design compromises, and reduce confidence in mass deployment.
[P1.6] When the risk is more capable military AI in hostile hands, buying time at commercial expense is prudent deterrence.

[C1.2] Advanced lithography, etching, and metrology are not frozen artifacts. They improve because global customers deploy them, generate process data, and fund successive generations. When leading toolmakers lose billions in addressable market, they do not simply shrug; they cut speculative R&D and consolidate the supplier base. A shrinking, cash-starved industry produces slower innovation for allied militaries too.
[C1.3] Commercial loss is not separate from security; it is the foundation of it.
[C1.4] Nor does denial guarantee delay. Much battlefield AI—edge inference, swarm coordination, sensor fusion—runs on mature or intermediate nodes, not the absolute frontier the controls target. [C1.5] Meanwhile, an adversary facing a comprehensive embargo does not wait; it redirects state capital toward domestic substitutes and parallel supply chains. Export pressure often accelerates self-sufficiency programs that eventually produce independent, unsanctionable alternatives. Porous transshipment networks and component-level workarounds drain enforcement resources. The result is a bifurcated world in which the adversary’s ecosystem matures behind a wall, immune from future leverage.
[C1.6] Better policy targets end-uses and specific choke points, not entire export sectors. Blanket restrictions hand rivals a market, weaken allied suppliers, and invite a technological decoupling that ultimately erodes the West’s edge. The commercial cost is not a sacrifice for safety; it is a compounding strategic liability. Reject the proposition.
