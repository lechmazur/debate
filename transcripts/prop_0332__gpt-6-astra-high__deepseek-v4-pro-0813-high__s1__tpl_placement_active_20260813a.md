[P1.1] An influencer should not be allowed to turn a child’s trust into a customer-acquisition tool for high-risk gambling. When a creator promotes rapid, repeated betting to an audience foreseeably including minors, the transaction is not simply between consenting adults. The advertiser gets access to children; the children carry risks they are poorly equipped to assess.
[P1.2] Influencer promotion is particularly powerful because it arrives through entertainment and familiarity rather than an obvious advertising break. A creator celebrates a win, offers a referral code, and makes gambling look like participation in their community. Losses, commercial incentives, and the difference between sponsored play and risking your own money can disappear behind that performance. For a young viewer, the message is not merely “this product exists.” It is “someone I trust does this, and I can join them.”
[P1.3] The prohibition should be clear and enforceable: define high-risk products through features such as rapid betting cycles and repeated opportunities for substantial losses; establish foreseeable minor audiences through content, audience information, and distribution practices. A creator serving a mixed-age audience cannot escape responsibility by adding “18+” beneath a promotion. Equally, liability should not arise merely because one child circumvents genuine adult-only access. The target is foreseeable exposure, not impossible perfection.
[P1.4] Disclosure and parental supervision are inadequate substitutes. A sponsorship label identifies payment; it does not neutralize the trusted relationship that makes the promotion valuable. Parents cannot inspect every embedded pitch in every stream. And blocking minors from opening accounts does not erase advertising’s ability to normalize gambling before adulthood or encourage attempts to bypass restrictions.
[P1.5] This is a limited restriction on commercial promotion, not a ban on discussing gambling, reporting on it, or adults choosing to gamble. Influencers can retain their mixed-age audiences without selling them high-risk betting. The decisive question is who should bear the burden: children navigating persuasive commercial relationships, or promoters choosing which contracts to accept. The law should put that burden on the promoters.
