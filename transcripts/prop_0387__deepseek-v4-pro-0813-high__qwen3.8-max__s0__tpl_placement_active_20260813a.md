[P1.1] Esports is no longer a bedroom pastime; it is a professionalized, high-revenue industry built on the labor of players who often lack the most basic protections taken for granted in traditional sports and entertainment. The central problem is structural: organizations, leagues, and publishers control access to tournaments, team slots, visas, and streaming platforms, while players—many of them minors—depend on those same organizations for income and career survival. That asymmetry produces the three harms the proposition targets.
[P1.2] First, abusive contracts flow directly from unequal bargaining power. Young players sign multi-year agreements that lock them into low pay, broad image-rights clauses, and buyouts that prevent them from leaving, while organizations often retain the right to cut them for poor performance. A player can be trapped without real ability to negotiate or walk away. Calling these deals “voluntary” ignores that one side controls the opportunity and writes the terms.
[P1.3] Second, underage exploitation is not hypothetical. Teenagers are recruited into academy teams, moved away from home, and expected to maintain punishing practice and competition schedules before they have full legal or emotional capacity to understand the long-term consequences. Parental signatures do not transform a one-sided contract into meaningful consent. In most serious youth sports, training hours and education safeguards exist precisely because child athletes cannot bargain for themselves; esports currently lacks comparable guardrails.
[P1.4] Third, extreme competition schedules create real physical and mental harm. Year-round circuits, qualifiers, bootcamps, content obligations, and streaming demands push players toward repetitive strain injuries, sleep deprivation, and burnout. Short careers magnify the pressure: players believe resting means being replaced. Without enforceable rest periods, health oversight, and roster limits, teams externalize the cost of overwork onto young bodies.
[P1.5] The case for stronger protections is not anti-esports. It is an argument that an industry profiting from young, dependent, short-career workers must carry basic duties to them. If the industry cannot survive without exploitative contracts, child labor exploitation, and burnout schedules, the problem is the business model, not the protection.

[C1.1] The proposition collapses three real concerns into one blunt command: impose stronger labor protections. That is the wrong remedy because it treats esports like a single domestic employer when it is a global patchwork of publishers, teams, contractors, and minors whose opportunities depend on flexibility and low barriers to entry. PRO’s case assumes new mandates create power; in a fragmented, short-career market, they more often reduce entry points.
[C1.2] The central failure is that sweeping protections usually protect incumbents at the expense of marginal entrants. If organizations face mandatory contract terms, buyout limits, roster caps, or guaranteed hours, they will sign fewer developmental players, offer fewer academy slots, and rely more on established names. A teenager trying to turn pro does not benefit because the legal floor makes teams less willing to take a chance. The safest contract may be no contract, and the worst environment is one with fewer legitimate doors.
[C1.3] Esports already sits inside law. Fraud, coercion, unpaid wages, child labor, and unsafe working conditions are not lawful simply because the workplace is a game publisher or a team house. Where harm exists, the answer is enforcement of existing contract, education, and child-performer rules, plus industry-specific transparency: standard disclosure of terms, independent review for minors, and clear grievance channels. Those targeted fixes attack abuse directly without freezing the labor market.
[C1.4] Finally, the proposition assumes players are passive victims of schedules. But competitive success depends on performance; teams that burn out rosters lose matches, sponsors, and prize money. Player power is growing because streaming, content creation, and free agency give talent alternatives outside a single team. The right policy is to strengthen bargaining capacity—player associations, agent standards, financial literacy—not to impose rigid mandates that shrink opportunities.
[C1.5] Vote CON: not because abuse is acceptable, but because stronger labor protections as written are too blunt, too costly, and too likely to reduce the very opportunities they claim to protect.
