[P1.1] Schools should allow parents to receive information, but should stop letting parent-messaging platforms turn schooling into continuous surveillance. The motion does not require silence. It asks for a boundary: communication should serve an educational purpose, not expose every movement and behavior to adult monitoring.
[P1.2] A real-time dashboard changes the relationship among parents, children, and schools. Instead of contacting a teacher when a concern persists, a parent can react to each notification: a missed class, changed grade, library checkout, a child’s location within school systems. The design rewards anxious interpretation and rapid intervention. Convenience becomes pressure: children know adults are watching, and adults feel every delay is negligence.
[P1.3] This is not a marginal privacy preference. Surveillance can alter classroom participation, friendship, and willingness to make mistakes. Children may avoid activities, hide ordinary confusions, or treat the teacher as auditor rather than educator. Families lose control when a platform creates an institutional record of daily conduct.
[P1.4] Commercial communication products may also subject that data to settings and incentives schools cannot fully inspect.
[P1.5] A proportionate rule would limit dashboards to necessary educational updates—attendance, deadlines, urgent safety notices—and prohibit granular real-time activity tracking by default. Parents should not receive continuous feeds of classroom performance or conduct, and sensitive data should not be retained or redistributed merely because software can collect it. Existing channels remain available for individualized concerns, emergencies, and teacher meetings.
[P1.6] The strongest counterargument is that parents need visibility to protect children. But visibility is not constant monitoring. A timely message about a concerning pattern, a teacher conference, and a clear emergency protocol provide accountability without making routine behavior continuously inspectable. Schools should preserve trust and independence, not outsource parental anxiety to software.
[P1.7] The line is simple: inform parents when information is genuinely needed; do not operate a live dashboard of children’s lives.

[C1.1] The motion rests on a false choice between transparency and trust, but trust is built on information, not absence. Parents of minors have both a legal right and an ethical duty to know what happens to their children. A real-time dashboard is the modern report card or phone call home—only faster, and thus better at preventing harm. When a parent sees a missed class, a sudden grade drop, or a concerning behavior flag, they can intervene early, before small problems become crises.
[C1.2] The PRO case treats every notification as surveillance, but this confuses visibility with control. The platform does not force parents to micromanage; it gives them the option to respond to genuine concerns. If schools strip out granular updates, anxious parents will not relax—they will flood the front office with calls, disrupting classes far more than a silent notification. The tool is neutral; parenting style is what matters, and no software setting should regulate that.
[C1.3] Safety demands real-time data. A fight in the hallway, a medical emergency, an unauthorized departure from campus—these are exactly the moments when minutes matter. Limiting platforms to vague "necessary educational updates" forces administrators to make arbitrary judgments about what parents deserve to know in real time; those judgments will inevitably fail in emergencies. Schools act in loco parentis, so parents remain primary guardians; withholding behavioral data treats them as outsiders, not partners.
[C1.4] Finally, the proposed limits are regressive. If official dashboards are restricted, families with resources will obtain the same information through tutors, connections, or constant check-ins, while less privileged families lose visibility. Uniform platforms keep information flowing equally to everyone. Schools should not limit these tools; they should use them to keep parents informed, safe, and involved.
