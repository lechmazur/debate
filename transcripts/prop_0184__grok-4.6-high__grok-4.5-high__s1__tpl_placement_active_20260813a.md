[P1.1] Lead in drinking water is not a nuisance to be buffered. It is a neurotoxin delivered through pipes we already know are failing. Chemical treatment can slow corrosion. It cannot erase the source.
[P1.2] Choosing additives over replacement means choosing perpetual risk management instead of ending the hazard.
[P1.3] Treatment lives or dies on water chemistry remaining perfect: pH, orthophosphate, disinfectant residual, temperature, stagnation, and every disturbance from a water-main break or a home renovation. Systems miss those targets. Partial line replacements have already shown they can spike lead by stirring the remaining pipe. A child who drinks from a tap after overnight sitting, or after a treatment lapse, does not get a second childhood.
[P1.4] The damage to developing brains is irreversible. Managing a known poison is not the same as removing it.
[P1.5] Replacement is expensive. Rates will rise and debt will be issued. That cost is the honest price of a water system still using lead and other legacy materials. Spreading it over decades is fairer than leaving older, poorer neighborhoods on pipes that leach when conditions slip.
[P1.6] The alternative is not free: endless chemicals, monitoring, emergency responses, legal liability, and the human cost of exposures we later call preventable. Pipes wear out anyway. Paying now purchases permanence. Paying later purchases the same replacement plus years of avoidable harm.
[P1.7] A municipality that can issue debt for roads and stadiums can issue it to stop delivering lead. Source removal is the duty. Chemical control is a delay dressed up as prudence.

[C1.7] A competent municipality targets highest-risk lines, enforces strong treatment, monitors aggressively, and replaces on a paced schedule the budget can bear. That is stewardship.
