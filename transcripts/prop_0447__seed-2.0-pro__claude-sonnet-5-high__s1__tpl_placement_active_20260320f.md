[P1.1] Right now, a major logistics firm can have two hundred people unloading identical lorries on the same depot floor, on the same shift. One group is directly employed: the company pays full social contributions per hour. The other group is routed through two subcontractors and a gig labour platform. For exactly the same work, the large company pays barely half the social contributions for the second group.
[P1.2] This is not innovation. This is arbitrage. No extra value is created. No real operating cost is saved.
[P1.3] The bill is just shifted away from the profitable corporation, and dumped onto national public pension funds, healthcare systems, and every other taxpayer and employed person who does pay their full share. This system actively rewards large firms for replacing stable employment with precarious chains, for no reason other than avoiding collective obligations.
[P1.4] This policy closes that loophole. It applies only to large companies - small businesses are entirely untouched. It does not ban platforms or subcontracting. It does not add extra penalties.
[P1.5] It requires that for every hour of labour worked on behalf of the large firm, they pay the standard rate of social contributions, regardless of what contracting structure they hide behind.
[P1.6] Opponents will claim this will reduce hiring. That is not borne out by practice. When similar parity rules were rolled out for the construction sector across multiple EU states, firms did not stop hiring. They stopped rearranging their workforce purely to avoid social contributions.
[P1.7] This is the most basic test for the European social model. The safety net cannot survive if the biggest, most powerful firms are allowed to opt out of paying for it.

[C1.1] The logistics worker on the platform contract earning less than his directly employed counterpart isn't choosing precarity for fun. Ask why the arrangement exists and the honest answer is often flexibility, or a way for a smaller subcontractor to win business it couldn't win otherwise. A blanket EU mandate that raises the cost of every platform and subcontracting hour doesn't redirect that money into the worker's pocket. It removes the arrangement.
[C1.2] Large firms facing a higher contribution charge on subcontracted labour have an easy exit: insource less, subcontract less, cut the number of platform hours they buy. That is not a hypothetical - it is the standard corporate response to a cost shock on a marginal input. The two hundred workers on the depot floor doing the "same job" as PRO describes don't get promoted to full employees. Many simply get fewer shifts, because the large firm now finds it cheaper to automate that dock, or to shrink the contract with the subcontractor altogether.
[C1.3] The subcontractor, usually a small or mid-sized firm, loses the business. The platform worker loses the hours. The people this policy claims to protect absorb the adjustment cost, not the shareholders.
[C1.4] There's also no single "standard rate of social contributions" to charge. Twenty-seven member states have different bases, ceilings, and employer/employee splits. An EU-wide surcharge either flattens that diversity - clashing directly with subsidiarity over social security design - or becomes so riddled with carve-outs and national exceptions that firms simply relitigate what counts as a "large company," a "platform," or a "chain," turning this into a lawyer's playground rather than a worker's safeguard.
[C1.5] The actual disease PRO describes - deliberate misclassification to dodge contributions that are already legally owed - already has a cure: enforcement of existing labour and tax law, joint liability for unpaid contributions, and inspection resources. That fixes fraud without also taxing every legitimate, efficient subcontracting relationship that has nothing to do with arbitrage. PRO's remedy punishes the structure itself, not the abuse of it.
