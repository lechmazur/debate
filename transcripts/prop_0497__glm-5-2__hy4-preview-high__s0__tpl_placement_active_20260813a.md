[C1.1] Pro's case rests on a false equivalence. Books and films are fixed, self-contained artifacts; software is a service, a process, a moving target. The promise that 'the same logic applies even more forcefully to software' [P1.3] collapses under that difference. Deposit a novel and libraries can preserve it.
[C1.2] Deposit a game and you may hold an inert installer that cannot run without dead servers, retired APIs, or licensed middleware nobody may lawfully copy. An early online game survives today because communities rebuilt private servers; the publisher's client would have taught historians nothing. A live-service title is not one build but a stream of patches and events; a mandated snapshot freezes a corpse, not the culture.
[C1.3] If the mandate instead demands source code, assets, build pipelines, and server code to be usable, it stops resembling legal deposit and starts resembling compelled trade-secret disclosure. That is the pro's dilemma: a useful deposit is a massive burden and an IP risk; a harmless deposit is nearly useless. National archives would have to safeguard proprietary engines and vulnerable codebases at scale, while small studios shoulder legal review and dependency escrow. 'Legal deposit is not a burden on innovation' [P1.10] is true only for a deposit nobody can execute.
[C1.4] The motion also makes government the curator of culture. It covers "culturally significant software," not all software, so officials must decide which games, apps, and digital art deserve memory—and which are too controversial, too niche, or too embarrassing. That is a censorship engine and a bureaucratic nightmare for software that updates weekly.
[C1.5] Software preservation is worthwhile, but deposit is the wrong instrument. What volunteer emulators, museums, and fan-server communities need is legal safe harbor to circumvent and rehost, stable funding, and partnership—not surrender of code. Because mandated deposit is too weak to preserve, too costly for innovation, and too political to curate, I oppose the motion.
