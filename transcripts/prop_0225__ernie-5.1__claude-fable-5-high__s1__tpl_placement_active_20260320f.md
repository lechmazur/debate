[P1.1] Public universities are funded by taxpayers, so they owe a duty to allocate those dollars where they generate the greatest public return. Right now, a sizable share of that money pours into programs whose graduates struggle to find work, saddling students with debt and draining resources that could be used where the nation actually needs talent. Capping enrollment in those low‑employment fields and redirecting the saved funds to areas with documented skills shortages is not an attack on academic freedom; it is a responsible stewardship of public money.
[P1.2] Consider the numbers. In the United States, roughly half of graduates in certain liberal‑arts majors are employed in jobs that do not require a degree, while sectors such as nursing, computer science, and advanced manufacturing face chronic vacancies that cost the economy billions each year. When a university spends a seat on a program that yields a 30‑percent employment‑to‑field rate, it is effectively subsidizing unemployment. By imposing enrollment caps on those programs, the state can free up faculty, classroom space, and scholarship dollars for high‑need fields where each graduate is more likely to fill a vacancy, earn a living wage, and pay taxes that replenish the very fund that paid for their education.
[P1.3] Critics will claim this limits choice, but public institutions already set enrollment limits in high‑demand majors like engineering. The proposal simply extends that logic to the other end of the spectrum, ensuring that public subsidies do not subsidize mismatch. Students who still want low‑employment majors can pursue them at private colleges or community colleges, where the cost burden falls on them rather than on taxpayers.
[P1.4] Redirecting funding toward national skills gaps also strengthens the broader economy. More nurses reduce hospital overtime costs; more engineers accelerate infrastructure projects; more data‑science graduates help firms adopt AI, boosting productivity. The net effect is higher tax revenue, lower reliance on social safety‑net programs, and a healthier return on every public dollar spent. That is the clear, measurable benefit the proposition delivers.

[C1.1] Forcing universities to plan enrollment around today's job postings hands a fast-moving labor market to the slowest-moving planner imaginable: a government bureaucracy working on four-to-six-year lag times. A student capped out of a major this fall graduates into a labor market half a decade away, and our forecasting record over that horizon is dismal. A decade ago the consensus shortage field was computer science; by the early 2020s, tech firms were laying off tens of thousands while "oversupplied" fields like skilled communications and healthcare administration tightened.
[C1.2] Petroleum engineering went from gold rush to glut within a single degree cycle. Caps lock students into yesterday's predictions and produce the classic central-planning pattern: synchronized gluts in whatever the state blessed, shortages everywhere else.
[C1.3] Second, the proposal's core metric is broken. "Low-employment program" usually means low employment in-field at graduation — a snapshot that systematically penalizes flexible degrees. Philosophy, history, and English graduates start slower but converge toward and often surpass median earnings by mid-career, precisely because they aren't welded to one industry. They become managers, lawyers, analysts, founders.
[C1.4] Measuring a degree's value at month six is like judging an investment the week you buy it.
[C1.5] Third, consider who actually gets capped. Wealthy students will simply buy their preferred major at private universities — PRO concedes this escape hatch exists. The cap binds only on working-class students at public institutions, telling them, uniquely, that their education must be vocationally pre-approved by the state. That inverts the entire purpose of public higher education.
[C1.6] Finally, real shortages — nursing above all — are rarely caused by too many history majors. Nursing programs turn away tens of thousands of qualified applicants annually because clinical placements and faculty are scarce. Fund those bottlenecks directly; no cap on anyone else is needed. The proposition bundles a legitimate goal with a coercive, badly targeted mechanism, and the mechanism is what we're voting on.
