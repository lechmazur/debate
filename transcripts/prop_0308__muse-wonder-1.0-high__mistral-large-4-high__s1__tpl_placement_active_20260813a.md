[P1.1] Populism is not ordinary criticism of elites. It is the claim that one leader or movement alone speaks for the real people, and that opponents, judges, journalists and inconvenient minorities are not legitimate rivals but enemies of the nation. That distinction is decisive: criticism can renew democracy; a monopoly claim on the people's will corrodes the pluralism democracy requires.
[P1.2] Its rise is first a warning sign. Populist breakthroughs typically follow years in which mainstream parties stopped representing places and voters, insulated themselves from scandals, outsourced hard choices, and treated elections as rotation among insiders. When citizens conclude that voting changes little and institutions protect only the connected, the promise to smash the system becomes credible. The movement is therefore evidence that representation, accountability, and trust have already thinned.
[P1.3] Once empowered, that warning usually becomes backsliding. The left-wing version in Venezuela and the right-wing version in Hungary differ in ideology but converge in method — elections continue, while the field, referees, and information environment are steadily captured.
