[P1.1] Gun violence in American cities is not a diffuse fog. It is a fire concentrated in a few blocks, a few groups, and a few feuds. Less than one percent of a city's population typically drives half or more of its shootings. That concentration is the entire cost-effectiveness story.
[P1.2] Expanding police staffing is the most expensive way to address a concentrated problem with a diffuse tool. An additional officer costs a city well over six figures annually when you include salary, benefits, overtime, training, equipment and long-term pension. Hiring 100 officers to move citywide crime stats can easily cost $15-20 million per year, take years to fill academies, and most of that time is spent on calls for service, traffic, reports — not on the 30 guys about to shoot each other next week.
[P1.3] Focused deterrence and interruption do the opposite. Boston's Ceasefire model, replicated as Group Violence Intervention, identifies the highest-risk individuals, brings them in, delivers a credible message that continued shooting will bring swift enforcement, paired with genuine help, jobs, housing. Violence interruption models like Cure Violence use credible community outreach workers to mediate conflicts before retaliation. Both attack the retaliatory cycle that drives gun homicides, which patrol does not.
[P1.4] Because they target the tail, their costs are small. A fully staffed outreach team and program management for a mid-size city runs in the low single-digit millions per year. Oakland kept Ceasefire running for about $2-3 million while homicides fell by roughly half from peak years, including sharp drops in gang-involved shootings. That is dollars per shooting prevented an order of magnitude cheaper than staffing up a department.
[P1.5] If the goal is fewer guns fired cost-effectively, you buy the scalpel, not the blanket.
