In some of these cities, a large share of the central land near subways sits under some form of preservation control. That is not a footnote on housing policy; it is a hard ceiling on the most valuable land in the country.
[P1.3] And the buildings being protected are, overwhelmingly, not Penn Station. They are ordinary rowhouses and three-story walk-ups, deemed "contributing" to a district's character because they are old, not because anyone would cross the street to look at them.

Allow a mid-rise there and you house several hundred families instead of a few dozen, each with shorter commutes, lower carbon footprints, and rents that stop climbing faster than wages. [P1.5] The pleasure of an unchanged streetscape is real too, but it belongs mostly to the people already inside, who receive it as rising property values while newcomers are priced out to the exurbs. [P1.7] What we call historic is simply the last generation's willingness to build.
[P1.8] So the test is simple: who bears the cost? Under the status quo, teachers, nurses, and young families pay in rent and displacement so that existing owners keep a view. Relaxing the rules asks the city to give up some bricks. [clipped]

[C1.1] Start with the arithmetic PRO carefully avoids. Historic districts cover a small sliver of urban land — in most American cities, a few percent at most. The land actually strangling housing supply is the vast ocean of single-family-only zoning, surface parking lots, and dead strip malls that surrounds every downtown. A city serious about density can add tens of thousands of units without touching a single landmarked block.
[C1.2] Bulldozing rowhouses to solve a shortage created by suburban zoning is like draining the birdbath to fight a drought.
[C1.3] Second, the asymmetry of mistakes. If a city upzones a parking lot and it goes badly, it can rezone. If it demolishes an 1880s streetscape, that decision is permanent. Housing policy is reversible; demolition is not.
[C1.4] When one side's errors can be corrected and the other's cannot, prudence says put the burden of proof on demolition — and the motion explicitly accepts losing "architecturally significant buildings," meaning the genuinely irreplaceable stock, not just cornices.
[C1.5] Third, the mechanism fails on its own terms. Older buildings are, on average, the naturally affordable housing in expensive cores. Demolish a three-story walk-up with rent-stabilized tenants and the mid-rise that replaces it is new construction priced at the top of the market — after displacing the very teachers and nurses PRO champions. "Relax when housing is needed" is not a standard; it is a permanent loophole, because a developer can always assert need.
[C1.6] Every teardown of an occupied historic building becomes presumptively legal.
[C1.7] Finally, preservation and density are not enemies. Cities already add units inside historic districts through adaptive reuse — converting warehouses, churches, and office buildings — plus transferable development rights and upzoning adjacent blocks. Paris, PRO's own example, is dense and protected simultaneously.
[C1.8] So the test for this round: does relaxing protections deliver housing that couldn't be built elsewhere, at a cost worth paying? It delivers marginal units, displaces current tenants, and destroys assets no future generation can recover. That trade fails.
