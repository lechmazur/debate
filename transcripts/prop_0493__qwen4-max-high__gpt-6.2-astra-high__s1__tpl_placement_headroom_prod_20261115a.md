[P1.1] An aquifer is not a commodity; it is a stock of stored time. Water that took centuries to accumulate under a town can be pumped, bottled, and trucked out of the watershed in a single summer, and the town cannot buy it back at any price. That asymmetry — private profit, permanent public loss — is the whole case for the ban.
[P1.2] Notice who bears the risk. A bottling company's obligation runs to shareholders and ends at the fence line. A municipality's obligation is universal service: every household, every fire hydrant, every drought year. When the water table drops, the company can move its pumps or close the plant.
[P1.3] The residents cannot move their aquifer. Handing a non-substitutable public trust asset to an actor with no duty to the public is a design flaw, not a market efficiency.
[P1.4] The obvious reply is "regulate it instead." But permits are exactly where this fails. Groundwater is invisible; monitoring wells and hydrological modelling are expensive, and the party with the best data is the extractor. [P1.5] Michigan and California towns spent years fighting bottling operations they believed were draining their springs, while permits stayed valid. Regulation negotiates the price of depletion; a ban removes the incentive to seek it.
[P1.6] The motion is narrow. It does not ban farming, industry, or drinking fountains. It bans treating municipal sources and aquifers as inventory for retail sale. Bottled water is overwhelmingly a convenience product sold at enormous markup over safe tap water.
