[P1.1] A customer opens a delivery app, taps a nearby burger place, and sees a $24 delivered total. She does not see that the same meal is $16 if she picks it up herself, or $14 at a comparable spot two blocks closer if she orders direct. The app knows the address, knows its own fees, and profits only when the order flows through its checkout. The cheaper, commission-free option is exactly the one the interface leaves out.
[P1.2] The motion fixes that narrow failure, not the whole business model. It does not cap commissions, ban delivery, or force price matching. It bites only when the app’s own total is materially higher than a nearby independent pickup or direct-order alternative, and it requires showing the nearest one. “Materially” spares trivial convenience premia; “nearest” keeps disclosure cheap.
[P1.3] This harm is structural, not imaginary. The app controls choice architecture, defaults to monetized delivery, and has a conflict of interest: every dollar vanishes if the customer walks in or calls. Fees and markups are the costs people underestimate at checkout, and the off-app path is buried by design. A materially higher total is not a neutral convenience price; it is rent charged because the alternative is invisible.
[P1.4] Surfacing the nearest lower-fee independent option restores competition. Restaurants keep far more on direct or pickup orders; consumers pay less. The rule stops the app from taxing discovery between a local business and its customer.
[P1.5] Compliance is trivial. Apps already compute totals, geolocate merchants, and display menus. A “cheaper nearby pickup/direct option” card is a label, not a network. If an app cannot verify a direct-order price, the trigger is not met for that channel—but it cannot stay silent when the pickup gap it can see is material.
[P1.6] Decide on the moment of choice: when an app’s total is materially higher, hiding the nearest cheaper independent option is steering. Show the option; let the customer choose.

That hybrid test scales with basket size and uses only the totals the app already computes.
[P4.2] A2: Yes, a small app that is pricier solely from lack of scale still owes disclosure, because the trigger is the customer-facing gap, not the app’s costs or market share. A low-fee small app simply will not trigger. The practical burden stays on dominant platforms, which routinely add large commissions and markups; small apps bear the rule only on rare baskets where they, too, are materially pricier.
[P4.3] Those answers defeat CON’s operational case. CON’s real objection is not administrability: it is that the rule stops an app from profiting by keeping a cheaper independent option invisible.
