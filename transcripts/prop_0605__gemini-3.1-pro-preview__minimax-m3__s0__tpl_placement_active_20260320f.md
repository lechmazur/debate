[P1.1] The kafala sponsorship model is fundamentally incompatible with both basic human rights and modern economic development. By tying a migrant worker’s legal residency and right to work to a single employer, the system creates an extreme power imbalance. Replacing it with portable, worker-held visas is an urgent necessity for Gulf states.
[P1.2] First, the kafala system structurally enables exploitation. When workers cannot legally change jobs without their employer's explicit permission, the employer holds absolute leverage. This mechanism traps millions of workers in abusive conditions, facilitating systemic wage theft, passport confiscation, and unsafe working environments. If a worker attempts to flee an abusive sponsor, they are instantly criminalized for "absconding," losing their legal status and risking arrest or deportation.
[P1.3] Portable visas sever this coercive link. By granting workers the right to switch employers independently, the state provides them with the most effective, immediate protection against abuse: the ability to walk away.
[P1.4] Second, moving to worker-held visas dramatically improves economic efficiency. Gulf nations are aggressively pursuing economic diversification to build modern, dynamic economies. A rigid labor system that traps talent in mismatched roles actively undermines these goals. Under kafala, employers hoard cheap labor, and wages are artificially depressed because the free market is paralyzed.
[P1.5] A fluid labor market, facilitated by portable visas, allows workers to move to where their skills are most needed. This boosts overall productivity, forces companies to compete for talent by offering better conditions, and accelerates domestic economic modernization.
[P1.6] Finally, abolishing kafala restores state sovereignty over immigration and labor. The current model effectively privatizes immigration enforcement, outsourcing legal control over migrants to individual citizens and corporations. Moving to a state-issued, portable visa system returns oversight to the government. This allows states to properly regulate their labor markets, monitor employment conditions transparently, and align their practices with international labor standards, thereby removing a major stain on their global reputations.
[P1.7] For the sake of human dignity and economic progress, the kafala system must be replaced.
