[P1.1] The question is not whether government contains waste—it does—but whether DOGE-style efficiency drives improve the state’s ability to deliver public goods. They do not. They are fast, top-down cuts built around blunt targets: reduce headcount, cancel contracts, shrink grants, move quickly, and claim savings before consequences appear. That model does more damage than it saves.
[P1.2] First, it confuses capacity with waste. A government’s ability to process disability claims, inspect facilities, answer veteran calls, collect taxes, or administer disaster aid depends on experienced staff, working contracts, and redundant layers of review. When a drive demands a 10% or 20% cut everywhere, it does not surgically remove fraud. It removes the people who know which systems actually work.
[P1.3] A citizen who cannot get a benefit, permit, or inspection experiences not efficiency but state failure. The payroll saving is immediate and photogenic; the service failure arrives later as backlogs, missed payments, uncollected revenue, and emergency fixes that cost more than the original jobs.
[P1.4] Second, these drives destroy institutional memory faster than any audit can replace it. Civil-service expertise is not a commodity you can switch off and repurchase. When senior program staff, grant managers, and technical specialists are pushed out, agencies lose the ability to write contracts, oversee vendors, interpret rules, and correct errors. The result is not leaner government but weaker government: more dependent on outside consultants, more vulnerable to lawsuits, and less able to implement the very reforms its critics demand.
[P1.5] Third, what looks like duplication is often resilience. Multiple offices handling civil rights, procurement, disaster response, or public-health preparedness may seem redundant in a spreadsheet, but redundancy is how large states prevent single-point failure. Cutting the second or third layer removes backup when demand spikes.
[P1.6] The savings from DOGE-style campaigns are usually front-loaded, partial, and reversible. The damage to service delivery is delayed, diffuse, and compounding. That is why the proposition is true.
