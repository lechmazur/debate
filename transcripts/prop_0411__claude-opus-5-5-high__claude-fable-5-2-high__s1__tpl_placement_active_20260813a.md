[P1.1] A wheelchair user who opens a ride-hail app can often tap "accessible vehicle" and then wait an hour, or wait forever, because no car ever comes. A paratransit rider may have to book a day ahead and still accept a long pickup window. Technically, service exists. Practically, a job interview, a hospital discharge, or a friend's birthday is out of reach.
[P1.2] That gap is the whole debate.
[P1.3] First, access that is dramatically slower is not access. It is a second-class tier. We would not call a building open to everyone if the ramp entrance were unlocked only on Tuesdays. Mobility is the precondition for work, healthcare, and civic life.
[P1.4] When disabled riders face waits several times longer than everyone else, they pay a hidden tax in lost time, lost wages, and dependence on others. "Close to standard" is the only benchmark that treats them as customers rather than charity cases.
[P1.5] Second, markets will not fix this alone, and a requirement is what creates supply. Accessible vans cost more to buy and operate, so no individual driver or company volunteers to provide them. That is a classic coordination failure. Set a wait-time standard, and operators respond with driver incentives, dedicated fleets, taxi partnerships, or small per-trip fees pooled into an accessibility fund.
[P1.6] Voluntary pledges leave the problem unsolved. Enforceable benchmarks force someone to solve it.
[P1.7] Third, the cost is modest relative to the systems involved. "Close to," not "identical," gives operators flexibility. Spread across millions of trips, a tiny surcharge funds the fleet. The beneficiaries are broader than wheelchair users.
[P1.8] Older riders, people recovering from injuries, and parents with strollers all use accessible vehicles. Nearly everyone ages toward needing them.
[P1.9] CON will say this is too expensive or impractical. We already require elevators, curb cuts, and lift-equipped buses, and each was once called impossible. The real question is not whether equal service costs something. It is whether disabled people should keep absorbing the cost of being excluded.
