[P1.1] When heat crosses the point where the body can no longer cool itself, continued work is exposure, not productivity. Governments should treat extreme heat as a labor-rights issue and require mandatory stop-work protections, because the workers who face the worst heat cannot bargain for safety, and employers will not reliably stop production when stopping costs money.
[P1.2] No one should have to risk organ failure to keep a job. Farmworkers, roofers, road crews, and warehouse staff already carry a disproportionate share of heat illness and heat death. Piece rates and hourly pressure punish rest. Many of these workers lack unions and fear retaliation if they walk off a site.
[P1.3] A protection that exists only for people with leverage is not a labor right. It is a privilege the most exposed never receive.
[P1.4] Markets will not close that gap. The costs of heat stroke, kidney injury, and cardiac collapse land on workers, families, and public hospitals, not on the shift that caused them. Voluntary heat plans are easy to post and easy to ignore when a concrete pour or a harvest window is closing. A legal stop-work trigger, with required cessation and penalties for retaliation, makes the dangerous hour more expensive to exploit than to avoid.
[P1.5] Water and shade help. They do not replace a hard stop when the environment itself is the hazard.
[P1.6] This is ordinary hazard regulation. We already halt work for lightning, bad air in confined spaces, and unshored trenches. Extreme heat kills by the same logic: a condition individual caution cannot neutralize. As hotter seasons become longer and more severe, only a government standard reaches non-union sites, temporary labor, and the informal edges of the economy in time.
[P1.7] Thresholds can vary by workload and setting. What cannot be optional is the decision to stop. A worker's survival is not a line item management may waive.

[C1.1] Treating extreme heat as a labor-rights issue requiring mandatory stop-work confuses safety regulation with employment relations, and the cure costs the patients their wages. When government orders a full work stoppage, it does not pause the paycheck. Piece-rate farmworkers, hourly roofers, and gig-economy delivery drivers lose income for every hour the thermometer trips a mandate. The workers most exposed to heat are often the least able to absorb unpaid interruptions.
[C1.2] A "right" that forces a single parent to choose between heat stroke and rent is not a protection; it is a penalty on poverty. It also sets coworkers against each other, as those who need the hours push through while others walk off, fracturing solidarity rather than building it.
[C1.3] The proposal also smuggles in a false binary. We can mandate water, shade, rest breaks, acclimatization periods, and heat-illness training without shutting down entire sectors. California already requires such measures for outdoor workers, and they reduce heat illness without idling construction sites or collapsing harvest windows that cannot be rescheduled. A mandatory stop-work trigger is the nuclear option: it treats a solvable safety problem as a binary kill switch.
[C1.4] Then there is the definitional chaos. "Extreme heat" is not a number. Wet-bulb globe temperature, humidity, workload, clothing, and individual acclimatization all change the danger threshold. A mandate that stops work at 90 degrees Fahrenheit would idle warehouses while ignoring humid 85-degree mornings that are objectively more dangerous.
[C1.5] Employers will game the threshold; workers will push through to protect piece rates; regulators will drown in disputes. Vague mandates invite selective enforcement and legal chaos, not safety.
[C1.6] Real labor rights are about bargaining power, not thermometers. If the goal is protecting vulnerable workers, fund enforcement of existing safety standards, strengthen anti-retaliation laws, and require the cheap, proven interventions—hydration, shade, rest—that keep people working and paid. Mandatory stop-work sounds protective, but it immiserates the very workers it claims to save.

[C2.3] A single thermometer reading cannot capture whether a 45-year-old diabetic roofer or a 22-year-old acclimated laborer is in danger. [P2.8] asks workers to volunteer for survival, but mandatory stop-work asks them to volunteer for poverty—or to work illegally without any protection at all.
[C2.5] The choice is not between stroke and rent. It is between a regulated workplace with water, shade, and rest, and an underground economy with none. CON offers the first. PRO offers a mandate that destroys the first to chase the second.
