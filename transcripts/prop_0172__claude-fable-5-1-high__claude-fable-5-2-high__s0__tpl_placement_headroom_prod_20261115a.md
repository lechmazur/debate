[P1.1] A mouse cannot tell us whether it suffers. We never required it to. We inferred moral status from behavior — avoidance, memory, learning — and built review frameworks around that inference. Now neural tissue grown from human cortical cells, wired to electrodes, demonstrably modifies its activity to achieve a goal, as in the widely reported experiments where cultured neurons learned to keep a Pong ball in play.
[P1.2] The very signature we accept as sufficient for a mouse is appearing in a dish. Consistency demands we respond the same way.
[P1.3] The case rests on one asymmetry. If we apply animal-style review and the organoid turns out to be morally inert, we lose some paperwork and a few weeks per protocol. If we decline review and it turns out rudimentary learning is accompanied by rudimentary experience, we will have created sentience with no body, no exit, no way to signal distress, in quantities limited only by incubator space. No regulator in history has faced a worse ratio of insurance cost to catastrophe.
[P1.4] Animal frameworks are built precisely for this uncertainty. They do not require proof of consciousness; they require justification, harm-minimization, humane endpoints, and the three Rs — replace, reduce, refine. Zebrafish larvae and cephalopods are covered not because we are certain they feel, but because we are not certain they don't. Learning organoids sit squarely inside that logic.
[P1.5] Expect the reply that organoids lack sensory organs, nociceptors, or integrated brains. But the animal framework was never anchored to anatomy; it is anchored to functional evidence and proportionate caution. A tiered review simply scales scrutiny to capability — minimal for passive tissue, substantial for systems that learn.
[P1.6] Applying oversight did not kill animal research; it legitimized it. Learning organoids are the most promising models in neuroscience. Protecting them, and the public's trust in them, is how that promise survives.
