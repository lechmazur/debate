[P1.1] Your friends are not a feature a company invented. They are your relationships, built message by message, yet today leaving a dominant social platform means leaving them behind. That lock-in, not better design, is what protects incumbents. Governments should end it by requiring interoperable, user-portable messaging and social graph access for qualified third parties.
[P1.2] Network effects turn a head start into a moat. An entrant can offer stronger privacy, better accessibility, less addictive feeds, or moderation tailored to a school, profession, or community — and still fail because nobody can afford to lose their group chats, followers, and contacts to try it. Interoperability changes the contest: services must win on quality, safety, and price, not on hostage-taking. Email and phone service already work this way; you can change providers without changing your address or number and expecting everyone you know to move with you.
[P1.3] Portability also gives users real power between elections, lawsuits, and scandals. If you can export your graph with consent, connect through another app, and revoke access, a platform that degrades privacy, floods feeds with manipulation, or bans communities arbitrarily faces immediate exit, not just complaints. That credible threat disciplines incumbents even for people who never switch, just as number portability disciplines carriers.
[P1.4] This is not a demand to throw open private data. The mandate is for qualified services: verified identity, encryption and security standards, purpose limits, user-by-user consent, and loss of access for abuse. With those gates, interoperability expands choice without licensing scraping or spam. Without a mandate, incumbents will keep offering only the limited downloads and closed partnerships that preserve their choke point.
[P1.5] The question is whether your social life should be portable property or a platform's captive asset. Require connection, respect consent, and let users take their relationships where they are treated best.
