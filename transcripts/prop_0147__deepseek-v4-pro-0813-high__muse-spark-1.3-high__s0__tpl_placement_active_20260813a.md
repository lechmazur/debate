[P1.1] A toddler learning language does not merely need words broadcast at her. She needs a responsive adult who follows her gaze, notices confusion, repeats a phrase with warmth, and pauses when she points. Early language grows through serve-and-return: the child gestures or babbles, the caregiver responds, the child responds again. A smart speaker can deliver vocabulary, but it cannot follow her eye, read her expression, or know if she has drifted.
[P1.2] When parents routinely hand early reading and conversation to a device, they replace that adaptive loop with a one-way stream.
[P1.3] Reading together is not content delivery. It is joint attention, physical closeness, and a child learning that her questions matter. If a child asks why the rabbit is sad, a parent can stop, connect it to the child’s world, and wait; an AI companion is more likely to move to the next page. Routinely losing this turns books into noise rather than relationship.
[P1.4] Bedtime is even clearer. A bedtime routine teaches a child that the world is safe enough to surrender to sleep through a particular adult’s voice, touch, pacing, and presence. A machine can play a lullaby; it cannot hold a frightened child or calm a nightmare. Outsourcing that ritual does not add a helper; it subtracts an attachment cue.
[P1.5] The proposition does not ban occasional use. The harm arises when the default becomes machine-led conversation, reading, and bedtime, because what is being automated is not a chore—it is the relational work that builds language, self-regulation, and secure attachment. The wrong metric is how many words or stories the child still hears; the right metric is whether the child is getting contingent, caring human response during these formative routines. On that metric, routine AI substitution loses.
