[P1.1] Historic tourism should not be treated as free revenue. It uses finite public space: benefits are often concentrated among businesses, while congestion, pollution, and housing costs fall across residents. When those costs exceed net gains, unlimited cruise-ship and large-tour-bus arrivals transfer wealth from city residents to visitors. A cap is therefore justified precisely where the proposition says: where the social balance is negative.
[P1.2] The mechanism is immediate. A cruise ship can release thousands of passengers into streets with unchanged capacity. Large tour buses occupy lanes and curb space needed for movement, deliveries, and emergency access. Crowding makes parks, markets, and transit less usable, while exhaust and idling add pollution in dense neighborhoods.
[P1.3] Tourism employment and visitor demand can also tighten housing markets, encouraging short-term rentals and removing longer-term homes from residential use.
[P1.4] The cap need not be a blanket ban. Cities can limit concurrent ship calls, reserve bus slots, route coaches to peripheral hubs, and require emissions standards. Triggers can combine pedestrian density, air-quality readings, traffic delay, resident complaints, and housing indicators. This preserves tourism in quiet periods while preventing peak-day overload when costs outrun benefits.
[P1.5] Adding another bus route cannot instantly create space for passengers; rationing arrivals can.
[P1.6] The strongest objection is that regulation sacrifices revenue and growth. But gross tourist spending is not social benefit, and congestion pricing alone cannot repair an overcrowded square or polluted street; it rations access without adding capacity. Selective limits protect what makes tourism valuable: a city residents can comfortably inhabit is more attractive and sustainable than one hollowed out for extraction.
[P1.7] The policy already contains the concession. If arrivals produce net gains exceeding all three harms, the cap need not apply. Once the balance tips, government should protect residents before chasing another marginal dollar of tourist revenue.

[C1.1] The proposition’s trigger is a false choice. A city facing crowding, pollution, and housing strain needs more public revenue, not less. Cruise passenger fees, port charges, and tourism taxes often fund exactly the transit, air-quality, and housing programs that relieve resident harm. Cutting arrivals under net-negative conditions removes the revenue stream that could reverse those conditions.
[C1.2] Workers, shopkeepers, guides, taxi drivers, and restaurant staff are residents too; a cap protects some residents by making others poorer.
[C1.3] Even on its own logic, the cap is poorly aimed. Crowding is a peak-hour spatial problem, pollution is an engine and idling problem, housing pressure is a short-term rental problem. A limit on how many ships or coaches arrive does not distinguish a polluting old coach from a clean electric one, doesn't force short-term rentals back into long-term housing, and doesn't add a single square meter to streets. It simply reduces visitor numbers while the underlying failures remain.
[C1.4] Worse, the comparison demanded by the motion is unworkable. “Costs outweigh revenue gains” is not a stable, measurable ledger. Tourism revenue arrives across seasons and taxes, while crowding is episodic and often driven by local commuters and events. A city that caps on a bad summer weekend can trigger cancellations, lose future bookings, and strand small businesses that depend on predictable arrivals. This dynamic harm rarely appears in a static cost-benefit test.
[C1.5] The superior response keeps arrivals but manages them: timed coach slots, peripheral parking, emission zones, congestion charges, short-term rental regulation, and investment of tourism revenue in resident services. These tools cut the actual harms without sacrificing the revenue that historic preservation and housing relief require. A cap treats the city as a fragile attraction rather than a working place, and that mistake hurts residents most.
