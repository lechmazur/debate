[P1.1] Loneliness is not a software bug to be patched with an app; it is a physical and spatial deficit. When cities invest in digital loneliness interventions, they attempt to solve alienation with the very medium that accelerates it: screens. To foster genuine human connection, municipalities must invest in low-cost social infrastructure—libraries, recreation centers, parks, and late-hour public squares.
[P1.2] First, physical social infrastructure builds "third places"—spaces outside of home and work where people gather naturally. A library or a public plaza allows for passive contact and the development of "weak ties." These casual, face-to-face interactions with neighbors, librarians, and fellow citizens are the proven bedrock of community trust and mental well-being. An app cannot replicate the spontaneous, low-stakes warmth of sharing a physical space.
[P1.3] Second, app-based interventions individualize and pathologize what is fundamentally a structural problem. Downloading an app to combat loneliness requires active, self-conscious enrollment, which carries a heavy social stigma. Worse, it keeps citizens staring at screens, reinforcing sedentary behavior and digital fatigue. Social infrastructure, by contrast, invites organic, stigma-free participation simply by existing in the neighborhood.
[P1.4] Third, physical infrastructure is inherently equitable and multi-functional. The populations most vulnerable to severe isolation—the elderly, the unhoused, and low-income youth—are the least likely to benefit from app-based solutions due to digital divides, lack of privacy, or tech fatigue. A recreation center or a lit public park offers physical activity, safety, and community integration. An app offers only notifications.
[P1.5] By prioritizing physical commons over digital band-aids, cities address the root cause of isolation. We do not need more algorithms managing our solitude; we need shared spaces that compel us to look up, step outside, and meet each other.

[C1.1] A library card does not automatically create a friend. That weak assumption sits at the center of the PRO case. The issue is not whether libraries, recreation centers, or public squares are good in general; it is whether cities should invest more in them than in app-based loneliness interventions. They should not, because the better test is which tool reaches isolated people at lower cost, with lower personal barriers, and with better feedback about whether it works.
[C1.2] Low-cost social infrastructure is rarely low-cost once it is tasked with reducing isolation. Evening staffing, maintenance, security, accessible design, and ongoing programming are major recurring expenses. Meanwhile, a targeted digital intervention can reach thousands of isolated residents at a fraction of that cost and adapt quickly when participation is low. The PRO compares a well-run plaza to a bad app; the real choice is between two imperfect tools competing for scarce city funds.
[C1.3] On cost per person reached, apps are far stronger.
[C1.4] Apps are also more equitable in reach. The most isolated residents often cannot simply “step outside”: they may be homebound, disabled, elderly without safe transit, night-shift workers, new parents, or people whose loneliness is driven by social anxiety. A physical space requires travel, appearing alone, and initiating contact. An app can meet them at home, match them to a peer group, phone volunteer, or local meetup, and move at their pace.
[C1.5] That is not replacing face-to-face contact; it is often the bridge to it.
[C1.6] The screen-based critique is too crude. Digital tools already organize mutual-aid groups, walking clubs, community meals, language exchanges, and social prescribing. Those are not algorithms “managing our solitude”; they are flexible connective tissue between a city asset and an actual relationship. If the goal is reducing loneliness, not merely building more rooms that lonely people may never enter, cities should put marginal investment into app-based approaches that can target, reach, and support isolated residents directly.
