[P1.1] Pricing carbon inside Europe while leaving Europe’s borders open to untaxed, carbon-heavy imports is not climate leadership; it is unilateral industrial disarmament. As the European Union tightens emissions caps and phases out free allowances, domestic producers pay steep costs for every ton of carbon emitted. Without charging imports the same carbon price domestic factories already pay, heavy industry does not decarbonize; it simply relocates to jurisdictions with looser rules and dirtier grids, shipping the same goods back into Europe while global emissions rise.
[P1.2] Crucially, this adjustment must cover most imported industrial goods, not merely raw commodities. Taxing imported steel or aluminum while exempting imported car parts, machinery, and appliances guarantees downstream leakage. It punishes European manufacturers who pay carbon costs on raw inputs while rewarding foreign rivals who assemble finished products abroad using untaxed dirty steel. Applying standardized carbon benchmarks, while letting cleaner exporters verify lower actual emissions, keeps broad industrial coverage practical and closes the outsourcing loophole across the value chain.
[P1.3] Comprehensive border pricing is also Europe’s strongest lever for global decarbonization. Because credit is given for carbon prices already paid abroad, trading partners face a clear fiscal choice: price industrial carbon domestically and keep the revenue in their own treasuries, or watch Brussels collect it at the port. Conditioning access to Europe’s massive consumer market turns domestic climate standards into global incentives.
[P1.4] Even if major trading partners retaliate, holding firm is worth the cost. Retaliatory tariffs on European agriculture, cars, or luxury goods are painful, but they are bounded and negotiable, while border revenues give the EU fiscal room to cushion affected workers and industries. Surrendering to foreign pressure is far worse. Backing down guts European climate credibility, locks in slow-motion deindustrialization, and teaches Washington and Beijing that tariff threats can veto European law.

[C1.1] The proposition does not ask whether carbon border pricing is a nice idea in principle. It asks the EU to impose it on *most* imported industrial goods and then absorb retaliation from major trading partners without blinking. That final clause is where the case collapses.
[C1.2] Start with what retaliation actually looks like between deeply interdependent economies. Europe's exports—machinery, vehicles, pharmaceuticals, luxury goods, agricultural products—are precisely the sectors exposed to counter-tariffs and procurement bans. PRO waves at "bounded and negotiable" retaliation, but offers no mechanism showing why the damage stays bounded once tit-for-tat begins. Escalation is the default in trade disputes, not a worst case.
[C1.3] And the PRO line that border revenues let Brussels cushion the blow is simply the EU taxing its own consumers and importers to compensate its exporters for a conflict it chose to start. That is a fiscal treadmill, not fiscal room.
[C1.4] Second, the economic costs fall inside Europe first. Broad coverage means European manufacturers of machinery, auto parts, and appliances pay more for imported inputs while facing retaliation in their export markets—squeezed on both sides. Downstream producers using steel, aluminum, and chemicals are the backbone of European industry, and taxing their inputs is not "climate leadership"; it is an internal transfer from European assembly to European smelting. Consumers pay through higher prices for vehicles and equipment, which slows the very green investment—heat pumps, grid hardware, electric vehicles—that requires affordable industrial inputs.
[C1.5] Third, the climate rationale is weaker than PRO claims. Retaliation by major partners means the world's largest emitters do not adopt carbon pricing; they respond with tariffs, and global emissions keep flowing. Border charges reallocate trade flows—they do not abolish foreign emissions. Countries simply divert carbon-intensive goods to other markets.
[C1.6] Meanwhile the EU loses leverage and goodwill for the climate clubs and sectoral agreements that actually align standards across borders.
[C1.7] The PRO position turns a defensible, negotiated climate tool into a unilateral trade weapon deployed against resistance. Under those terms, the costs are certain, the emissions savings speculative, and the escalation real. Vote CON.
