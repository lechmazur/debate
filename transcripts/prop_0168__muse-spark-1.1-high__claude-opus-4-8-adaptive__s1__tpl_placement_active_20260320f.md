[P1.1] Exploration is about what we learn, not who gets a window seat. Measured by science per dollar, crewed flight loses by an order of magnitude and has for fifty years.
[P1.2] A human mission is a life-support mission first and a science mission second. You pay for air, water, food, radiation shielding, redundancy, abort systems, and a return trip. A failure kills people, so you over-engineer everything and stop flying. A robotic mission is instruments and propulsion.
[P1.3] When one Mars rover costs less than a single crewed launch, you can afford to send five, lose one, and still come out ahead. The International Space Station consumed well over a hundred billion dollars to produce mostly microgravity research that could be done cheaper uncrewed, while the entire Voyager, Cassini, New Horizons, and Kepler programs combined cost a fraction of that and rewrote textbooks.
[P1.4] Reach matters too. Humans have never left low Earth orbit except for a few days on the Moon. Every picture of a black hole, every exoplanet census, every sample of interstellar space, every flight through the rings of Saturn came from machines. Telescopes like Hubble and JWST look back to the birth of galaxies; probes survive Venus heat, Jupiter radiation, and decades in deep cold where no astronaut can.
[P1.5] If you want to explore the solar system and beyond, you need bodies that don't need to breathe.
[P1.6] The usual defense — inspiration and spinoffs — actually favors robots. Kids grow up with images from Mars rovers and deep fields of galaxies, not cargo runs to orbit. The engineering spinoffs from autonomous navigation, miniaturized sensors, and optics pay off directly, without the overhead of a human-rated capsule.
[P1.7] We shouldn't end human spaceflight. We should stop pretending it is the best way to do science. Put most public exploration money where the universe actually opens up.
