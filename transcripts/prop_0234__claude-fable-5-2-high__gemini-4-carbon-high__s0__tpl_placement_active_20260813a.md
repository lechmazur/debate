[P1.1] Every employment negotiation at a large company is a game where one side holds all the cards. The employer knows what every person in the role earns, how often people get promoted, and who gets passed over. The worker knows one number: their own. Disclosure of pay bands and promotion rates by role fixes that imbalance, and the "flexibility" it costs is mostly the flexibility to exploit it.
[P1.2] Three arguments carry this case.
[P1.3] First, labor markets work better with prices. We require public companies to disclose financials because investors cannot allocate capital blind. Workers allocate something more precious, their careers, with far less information. A candidate choosing between two offers cannot tell which firm's "senior analyst" track actually leads anywhere.
[P1.4] Publish the band and the promotion rate, and firms that pay fairly and promote reliably win talent. Firms that rely on vague promises lose it. That is competition working, not regulation strangling it.
[P1.5] Second, secrecy is where unjustified pay gaps live. When two people do the same job and one earns substantially less, the cause is rarely performance; it is usually who negotiated harder, who was hired in a tight market, or who the manager happened to like. Those gaps fall disproportionately on women and minorities, and they persist precisely because no one can see them. Jurisdictions that have adopted pay-range posting rules, including several American states, have seen ranges appear in job ads without the collapse in hiring critics predicted.
[P1.6] Promotion rates add the missing half: a fair band means little if certain roles are dead ends.
[P1.7] Third, the flexibility at stake is narrower than it sounds. Bands are ranges, not fixed wages. Managers can still reward excellence within them, and firms can still set bands as wide as they can defend. What they lose is the ability to pay outside any explicable structure without anyone noticing.
[P1.8] Discretion that only survives in the dark is not worth protecting.

[C1.1] Treating human careers like corporate financial filings [P1.3] mistakes bureaucracy for fairness. Requiring large employers to publish pay bands and promotion rates by role does not deliver honest market prices; it forces dynamic firms into rigid HR grids, invites gamed metrics, and destroys the flexibility needed to reward exceptional or unconventional talent.
[C1.2] Consider pay bands. If firms "set bands as wide as they can defend" [P1.7], workers get the sprawling ranges already common under state posting laws, which neither inform applicants nor expose whether women and minorities cluster at the bottom [P1.5]. Yet if bands are narrow and binding, public visibility drives wage compression. When stretching pay to land a scarce specialist or beat a rival offer triggers demands for parity across a role, employers grow stingier at the top.
[C1.3] Transparency meant to lift the floor lowers the ceiling and flattens merit pay.
[C1.4] Mandating promotion rates by role is worse, because role-level rates are misleading and easily gamed. Past promotion rates reflect whether a division happened to be expanding, not whether today's hire will advance. Once firms compete on published promotion figures [P1.4], the easiest response is title inflation: splitting one real career leap into three hollow rungs with token raises, while flat firms that pay generously and grow scope in place look like "dead ends" [P1.6]. Above all, public promotion scorecards punish hiring risk: managers stop betting on career-switchers or non-traditional candidates if longer ramp-up times depress a role's published rate.
[C1.5] That shows why managerial flexibility is worth defending. Discretion is not merely favoritism "in the dark" [P1.8]; it is how leaders craft hybrid roles around unusual strengths, move fast to retain key contributors, and reward impact that outgrows an HR rubric. Regulators can already audit payrolls confidentially for discrimination without forcing dynamic workplaces into civil-service rigidity. Trading adaptive management for gamed ranges and distorted career ladders hurts the very workers disclosure promises to help.
