[P1.1] Severe treatment-resistant depression and PTSD are not marginal medical problems—they are conditions where standard care has already failed. When a patient has cycled through multiple antidepressants, psychotherapy, and in some cases electroconvulsive therapy, and remains suicidal or nonfunctional, the question is no longer whether to take risks. The question is which risks, taken under whose supervision.
[P1.2] The proposition is deliberately narrow: severe, treatment-resistant conditions only; carefully regulated; therapy, not dispensing. This is not legalization of recreational use. It is a controlled clinical pathway—licensed practitioners, mandatory screening, dosing in supervised sessions, integration therapy afterward—that simply does not require the artificial bottleneck of a randomized trial slot to access.
[P1.3] Restricting psychedelics to trials indefinitely has three serious costs. First, it withholds a therapy whose evidence base has matured: multiple late-stage trials of psilocybin for depression and MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD have shown substantial, sometimes rapid, and often durable remission in patients whom everything else failed. When the population is treatment-resistant by definition, the relevant comparison is not "drug versus placebo" in the abstract but "therapy versus continued suffering with no effective alternative."
[P1.4] Second, trial-only access is not neutral—it is regressive and arbitrary. Trial slots are few, geographically concentrated, and exclude the most suicidal and comorbid patients precisely because trials exclude risk. So the people who need the therapy most are structurally barred from the setting that supposedly protects them. That is a regulation serving process, not patients.
[P1.5] Third, careful regulation outside trials is how we generate real-world evidence. Cardiology did not confine stents to trials forever; monitored rollout produced better safety data than indefinite restriction ever could. The same logic applies: registries, practitioner certification, adverse-event reporting—all standard tools of medicine.
[P1.6] The opposing case will lean on uncertainty. But uncertainty about edge cases is an argument for regulation's content, not against regulated access. For patients out of options, "keep waiting" is not the safe choice; it is simply the riskiest one, laundered as caution.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds modest: narrow conditions, careful regulation, supervised therapy. But that framing conceals what makes psychedelics categorically different from every therapeutic the cardiology analogy tries to evoke. A stent does not dissolve the patient's capacity to consent mid-procedure. Psychedelics deliberately suspend judgment, heighten suggestibility, and blur the boundary between patient and therapist.
[C1.2] That is not a side effect to manage—it is the mechanism of action. And it makes "careful regulation" far harder to deliver than the proposition admits.
[C1.3] The evidence is also less settled than the opening suggests. Late-stage psychedelic trials have faced serious methodological criticism: unblinding is near-total, expectancy effects are large, and adverse events—psychosis, sustained dissociation, suicidal ideation—may be underreported precisely because patients in altered states are poor historians of their own harm. The recent FDA decision declining approval of MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD is not a footnote; it signals that regulators with full data saw problems the proposition's confidence glosses over.
[C1.4] The population in question is the worst candidate for that uncertainty. Treatment-resistant patients with severe depression or PTSD often carry comorbid bipolarity, psychotic features, trauma-driven fragility, and active suicidality. These are exactly the profiles where psychedelics can destabilize rather than heal. PRO argues these patients have "no effective alternative"—but no effective alternative does not make an unproven, destabilizing intervention safe.
[C1.5] It makes desperation the prescribing standard, which is precisely what clinical trials exist to resist.
[C1.6] PRO calls trials arbitrary and regressive. But expanding access outside trials does not fix exclusion; it removes the structured monitoring that catches harm. Registries and certification are post-market tools for ordinary drugs with predictable pharmacology. Psychedelics require hours of dyadic therapist-patient work in altered states.
[C1.7] That environment is uniquely resistant to the surveillance "careful regulation" promises.

[P2.1] The claim that psychedelics are "categorically different" because they alter consciousness collapses on contact with medicine we already regulate. Anesthesia suspends consciousness entirely; we still permit it, because consent is secured before the procedure, the setting is controlled, and trained professionals manage the window of vulnerability. The same structure applies here: screening and consent happen sober, sessions are monitored, integration follows. Altered-state suggestibility is an argument for what regulation must contain—two facilitators present, standardized session protocols, prohibitions on touch, audit of practitioner conduct—not an argument that regulation is impossible.
[P2.2] The unblinding critique cuts against trial *design*, not against the therapy. Expectancy effects can inflate results, yes—but so can they in trials of electroconvulsive therapy, which no one proposes banning for treatment-resistant depression. And when patients who failed everything show durable remission at rates that embarrass standard care, writing that off as "expectancy" asks us to believe the sickest patients improved for months on enthusiasm alone.
[P2.3] The FDA decision my opponent leans on was, on its face, about data integrity and trial conduct at a particular sponsor—functional unblinding, site-level irregularities, questions about therapist training—not a finding that MDMA is dangerous or ineffective. Regulators asked for another trial; that is a process verdict, not a safety verdict. Citing it as proof the therapy harms patients inverts what happened.
[P2.4] The vulnerability argument actually proves my case. Screening out psychotic features, bipolar instability, and active mania is exactly what "carefully regulated" means in practice. What CON cannot answer is the counterfactual: the excluded-from-trials suicidal patient is not choosing between this therapy and safety. They are choosing between it and continued illness with a real death rate.
[P2.5] "Desperation as prescribing standard" is a slogan; "let them wait indefinitely" is a policy with a body count.
[P2.6] Registries monitor the unusual all the time—implanted devices, gene therapies. Regulated rollout is surveillance; indefinite exclusion is ignorance.

[C2.1] The anesthesia analogy is PRO's strongest card, and it backfires. Under anesthesia, the patient is unconscious; the team acts on the body. No interpersonal relationship is being exploited in real time. Psychedelic therapy requires the patient conscious, responsive, and emotionally open to a therapist whose words and framing during the altered state directly shape the experience and its later interpretation.
[C2.2] We do not let anesthesiologists conduct psychotherapy on sedated patients—because combining suggestibility with a therapeutic relationship creates risks that physiological monitoring cannot capture. That is exactly the combination here.
[C2.3] PRO calls unblinding a trial-design problem separate from the therapy. But if unblinding prevents distinguishing genuine benefit from expectancy and therapist influence, we do not know whether the therapy works—or whether apparent remission is a temporary, expectancy-driven response that collapses outside intensive trial conditions. ECT has decades of replicated evidence; psychedelics have a handful of heavily criticized trials. The comparison fails.
[C2.4] The screening claim exposes a contradiction. The motion targets severe, treatment-resistant patients. But severe treatment-resistant depression and PTSD are precisely the conditions carrying comorbid psychotic features, bipolar instability, and active suicidality. You cannot simultaneously target the most severe cases and screen out the characteristics that make them severe.
[C2.5] If screening is strict enough to be safe, it excludes most of the population the proposition claims to serve. If loose enough to include them, it is not safe.
[C2.6] PRO's "body count" rhetoric assumes the therapy works. But psychedelics can also destabilize, re-traumatize, and trigger sustained psychosis in exactly this population. A registry that counts completed suicides but cannot capture therapeutic dependence, belief distortion, or delayed decompensation is not surveillance—it is theater. Expanding access to an unproven intervention whose harms are hardest to detect precisely in the patients it claims to serve is not courage.
[C2.7] It is caution abandoned.
