[P1.1] The motion's own premise gives away the case. "Commercially viable fusion may still be decades away" is a claim about a timetable, not about worthiness. Almost nothing transformative in energy or computing was commercially ready on a short clock: fission, satellites, GPS, semiconductors, and mRNA platforms all consumed decades of public money before anyone sold a product. We regretted not the research phase but the underfunding of it.
[P1.2] The test for public investment is expected value against cost, and fusion scores well on both sides of that ratio. The payoff is firm, dispatchable, low-carbon electricity at scale — the complement intermittent sources cannot supply alone as grids electrify and demand climbs. Fusion's fuel is abundant, its waste problem is far shorter-lived than fission's, and its worst failure mode is a shutdown, not a meltdown. If it works, it is not a niche; it is a candidate backbone of industrial civilization.
[P1.3] The cost side is the part critics never state honestly. Public fusion programs worldwide are a rounding error next to national energy spending, and they are exactly the long-horizon, capital-intensive, knowledge-spilling research that private markets underfund. The recent wave of private fusion capital appeared not despite public research but because public laboratories de-risked the plasma science and enabling technologies. Public money does not compete with private money here — it crowds it in.
[P1.4] And the spillovers are a second dividend, not a fallback: high-temperature superconductors, advanced magnets, plasma modeling, precision lasers, materials that survive neutron bombardment, and a trained workforce. Those pay out even on partial success.
[P1.5] "Decades away" cuts toward funding, not away from it. It means the nations that start now own the technology, the supply chains, and the standards when it arrives. The only way to guarantee the decades never end is to stop paying for them.
