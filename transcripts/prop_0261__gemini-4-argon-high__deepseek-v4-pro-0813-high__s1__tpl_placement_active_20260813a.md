[P1.1] When someone clicks "Buy" on an e-book, movie, or video game and pays full retail price, they are not agreeing to a rental—yet digital platforms use buried terms of service to confiscate those libraries the moment the buyer dies. A parent who spent decades building a collection of books, films, or games on paper and disc leaves behind both financial value and a personal legacy. A parent who bought the exact same works digitally leaves nothing, unless their family covertly uses a dead person's login.
[P1.2] Digital inheritance law must end this bait-and-switch by guaranteeing that purchased digital assets pass to next of kin.
[P1.3] The status quo erases household wealth solely to hand platforms an unearned windfall. Consumers pay ownership prices for digital purchases, not discounted subscription rates. Allowing non-transferability boilerplate to override estate law lets corporations pocket full retail prices and still extinguish the asset at death, forcing children to repurchase the very same culture their parents already paid for. Just as the law bars fine-print contracts from waiving basic tenant or consumer rights, estate law should invalidate clickwrap clauses that secretly turn permanent purchases into expiring leases.
[P1.4] Guaranteeing inheritance also protects creators because it transfers an existing entitlement rather than multiplying copies. The usual objection to digital transferability is fear of a frictionless resale market where pristine copies undercut new sales. Inheritance creates no such market. Death is an involuntary, verifiable event, not a commercial resale loop.
[P1.5] Platforms already have the technical tools to manage account entitlements: upon proof of death, they can deactivate the decedent’s access and transfer the library to the heir. One person's access ends; one heir's access begins. Because the number of active copies never increases, copyright remains intact while families keep what they bought. Moving from physical shelves to cloud servers should not abolish the right to pass our libraries to those we love.

[C1.2] If “non-transferable” can be voided whenever an heir wants access, no digital licensing term is stable, and platforms must price every purchase as if they owe postmortem support forever.
[C1.3] That cost lands on ordinary users. Digital libraries require storage, authentication, security updates, abuse prevention, and account recovery. Mandatory inheritance adds death verification, heir-identity disputes, and cross-border account migration. Those costs become higher prices or fewer permanent-purchase options.
[C1.4] The likely result is less ownership, not more—platforms will shift faster toward rentals and subscriptions with no library to pass on.
[C1.5] Privacy cuts the other way. A digital library is intimate: purchase history, notes, reading progress, search and viewing history. Automatic transfer exposes that record to next of kin, including relatives the decedent deliberately excluded. A one-size-fits-all guarantee overrides the decedent’s own judgment about who should see that private life.
[C1.6] Nor does death cure the copyright problem. The right to control distribution is part of the creator’s entitlement, not just the number of active copies. If an heir inherits a parent’s film or game library, the heir consumes works that would otherwise require a new sale. One access point may not be copied, but a downstream sale is still lost.
[C1.7] That weakens the very market needed to fund future libraries.
[C1.8] Users who want to pass access can already share credentials or use family plans where offered. A universal inheritance mandate is unnecessary and creates a property right the decedent never had. That is not protecting families; it is overriding the bargain and privacy of the dead to transfer a license they never owned.
