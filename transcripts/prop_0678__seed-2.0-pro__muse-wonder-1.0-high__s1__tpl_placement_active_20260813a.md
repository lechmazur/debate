[P1.1] Right now, most remote worker monitoring is rolled out without notice. People do not discover their keystrokes are logged, their webcam is running idle behaviour analytics, until a coworker gets penalised for looking away from their screen too long. This is not workplace oversight, it is private home surveillance.
[P1.2] Opt-in consent is not a meaningless checkbox. Workers cannot meaningfully refuse monitoring when it is imposed after they are hired, when declining gets you put straight on the layoff list. Genuine opt-in means this is negotiated up front as a clear term of employment, not a hidden background process switched on without warning.
[P1.3] Strict limits are non-negotiable because AI monitoring does not measure actual work. It measures performance theatre. It will flag a parent pausing for ten seconds to calm a crying toddler, it will entirely miss someone scrolling social media while staring dead at the screen. [P1.4] Without hard, enforceable caps on what can be monitored, when, and for what purpose, companies will always creep the scope.
[P1.5] This rule does not ban monitoring. It says if a company truly believes this tool delivers real productivity value, they can make that case, compensate workers for the loss of privacy, and let people choose. If the tool actually works, workers will agree. If it is just a way to exert control without trust, they will not.
[P1.6] Any argument against this proposition ultimately rests on claiming companies are entitled to spy on people inside their own homes, without agreement, for no reason other than power.

[C1.1] A disclosed policy for monitoring a company laptop during paid hours is not spying on a home. It is verifying work the company pays for, on equipment it owns, often containing client data it must legally protect. Employment already supplies consent: terms are stated, and a worker who rejects them can decline or leave the role. A separate opt-in adds no freedom; it gives each employee a personal veto over how a business runs itself.
[C1.2] That veto is unworkable where monitoring does real work. A bank, hospital billing team, or software firm cannot let someone handling payment data, medical records, or source code opt out and become a blind spot for fraud, leakage, and insider misuse. Audits, client contracts, and breach investigations require complete records, not a patchwork set by individual preference. Security optional for the person most motivated to evade it is not security.
[C1.3] Strict limits make the same error. What is proportionate for a trader, a safety-critical support worker, and a designer cannot fit one rigid cap on data types, minutes, or purposes. Needs change during an investigation or a new client contract. Existing privacy, employment, and anti-discrimination law already punishes intrusive or retaliatory abuse, and employers can be held to notice and proportionality without freezing legitimate uses in advance.
[C1.4] Opt-in would also backfire. Employers facing a fragmented, unreliable system will not pay a privacy premium and proceed as before. They will screen out refusers at hiring, stigmatise them afterwards, or end remote work and restore direct office supervision. A uniform, disclosed policy treats staff equally and preserves the remote arrangement itself.
[C1.5] The choice is not consent versus spying. It is a clear bargain — disclosed monitoring confined to work systems and hours, in exchange for pay and working from home — versus a regime that makes protection optional, compliance incomplete, and remote work harder to offer at all.
