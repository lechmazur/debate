[P1.3] Three reasons.

[C1.2] A reputation is also information created by victims, customers, coworkers, and landlords about what happened. A driver banned for harassment, a seller who repeatedly took money without shipping, a tenant who destroyed prior rentals, or a caregiver dismissed for theft does not become safe because the next employer or landlord is forbidden to learn it elsewhere. Disconnection does not correct a record; it blinds the next person asked to trust a stranger with a home, child, money, or workplace.
[C1.3] That blindness harms honest participants too. A gig worker with years of excellent ratings wants them to follow her to a better-paying platform. A tenant with strong payment history wants new landlords to see it. A freelancer without local connections uses past reviews to get a first chance.
[C1.4] Make cross-context transfer legally suspect and you chill that sharing, advantaging insiders who already have references, wealth, and networks.
[C1.5] Enforcement collapses either way. If disconnection is optional, refusal becomes its own signal: employers will prefer applicants who volunteer a clean history, pooling everyone who disconnects with the worst risks. If deletion is mandatory, bad actors gain a statutory method for ban evasion, while platforms cannot cooperate against fraud rings that operate precisely across jobs, marketplaces, and housing listings.
[C1.6] Credit files and expungement do not rescue this. They are not a right to vanish; they preserve reporting for defined periods, allow corrections, and retain exceptions for serious risks. That is the fitting model: accuracy, notice, relevance limits, anti-discrimination rules, and expiration proportionate to the conduct. Reform the score.
[C1.7] Do not hand everyone a legal delete key and call the resulting ignorance fairness.
