[P1.1] Fiscal policy asks governments to do something deeply unnatural: impose visible costs today for invisible benefits years later. Left to ordinary legislation, that asymmetry creates a permanent deficit bias. Politicians can cut taxes and raise spending while deferring the bill; beneficiaries vote now, while creditors and future taxpayers do not. A constitutional balanced-budget rule changes the default.
[P1.2] It makes balance the legal baseline and forces politicians to fight uphill for exceptions, rather than asking self-restraint to defeat the natural incentives of office.
[P1.3] This is not austerity constitutionalized. A serious rule contains automatic stabilizer exceptions: deficits permitted when revenues collapse and spending rises during recessions, balanced by surpluses during recoveries. That preserves countercyclical policy while ending the habit of treating every boom as a reason to relax. Debt should fall when times are good so it can rise when times are bad.
[P1.4] Without a rule, good times become excuses for structural deficits.
[P1.5] Switzerland’s debt brake is a workable model: it ties spending to cyclically adjusted revenue while allowing deficits in downturns. Such provisions do not eliminate judgment; they constrain the direction of discretion. An emergency exception can be objectively triggered by output loss or unemployment, with sunset and repayment requirements, so extraordinary action remains possible without becoming permanent.
[P1.6] The stakes are severe. Highly indebted states enter crises with no room to protect households; they must choose between punishing austerity, inflationary finance, or disorderly default. Those costs fall hardest on the poor, the unemployed, and the young. Constitutional rules do not guarantee wisdom, but they make fiscal irresponsibility harder than fiscal responsibility.
[P1.7] That is exactly the burden the motion places on PRO.
