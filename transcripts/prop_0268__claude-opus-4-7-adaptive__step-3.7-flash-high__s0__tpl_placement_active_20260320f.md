[P1.1] Shrinkflation is a price increase wearing a disguise. The headline number on the shelf stays the same, the box looks almost identical, but the chips inside dropped from 200 grams to 170. The consumer pays the same money for less product, and almost nobody notices at the moment of choice. That is not a free market clearing — that's an information failure, and information failures are exactly what consumer regulation exists to fix.
[P1.2] The proposition is modest. It does not ban shrinkflation. It does not cap prices. It asks supermarkets and food apps — entities that already print shelf labels and run digital storefronts — to flag when a pack has shrunk and to show the unit price clearly when it moves.
[P1.3] That is a disclosure rule, the lightest tool in the regulatory box.
[P1.4] Three reasons it should pass.
[P1.5] First, unit price is the only honest comparison. Shoppers compare brands and weeks by headline price because that's what's bold on the label. When pack sizes drift downward, headline price lies. A small "now 170g, was 200g" tag, or a per-100g change indicator, restores the comparison consumers think they're already making.
[P1.6] Second, the cost is trivial and falls on the party best placed to bear it. Retailers already know the pack size and the unit price — their systems calculate it. France and several other jurisdictions have moved in this direction without supermarkets collapsing. App interfaces can surface a "size reduced" flag with a line of code.
[P1.7] Third, the alternative is worse than disclosure: it's silent erosion of trust. When consumers eventually notice — and they do, via social media outrage cycles — they punish brands and retailers indiscriminately, and they grow cynical about all pricing. Clear disclosure is how honest sellers distinguish themselves from sneaky ones.
[P1.8] The central clash is simple. Either shoppers get the one piece of information they need to judge value, or sellers keep the right to quietly raise prices behind a stable sticker. Disclosure wins.

[C1.4] Third, this is unnecessary regulatory overreach into a market that already has working, optional solutions.
