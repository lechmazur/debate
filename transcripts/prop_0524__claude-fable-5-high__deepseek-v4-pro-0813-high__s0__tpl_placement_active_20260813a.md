[P1.1] Migration policy today fails everyone simultaneously. Universities can't plan enrolment because visa rules lurch with each election cycle. Employers can't commit to expansion because they don't know if they can staff it. Communities watch arrivals concentrate in cities where rents are already crushing, and their resentment fuels backlash that then punishes everyone.
[P1.2] The proposition fixes all three failures with one mechanism: capped but guaranteed allocations, tied to housing and local service capacity.
[P1.3] The case rests on three pillars.
[P1.4] First, predictability. A guaranteed allocation is a contract, not a lottery. A university that knows it can enrol a set number of international students for the next five years can build dormitories, hire faculty, and sign partnerships. An employer with a guaranteed allocation can invest in training and expansion.
[P1.5] The status quo—unlimited in theory, arbitrarily slashed in practice—makes rational planning impossible. Caps that institutions can actually rely on beat nominal openness that evaporates whenever politics shifts.
[P1.6] Second, incentive alignment. Tying allocations to capacity transforms institutions from free-riders into builders. Today a university can triple international enrolment while contributing nothing to local housing—the costs land on renters and public services. Under this policy, if you want a larger allocation, you build the student housing, you fund the clinic, you demonstrate the infrastructure exists.
[P1.7] Migration stops being extraction from communities and becomes investment in them.
[P1.8] Third, political sustainability. Public support for migration collapses when people experience it as overcrowded schools and unaffordable rent. Capacity-linked caps give voters a credible answer: numbers track what your town can actually absorb. That legitimacy protects migration pathways for the long run far better than open-ended systems that provoke crackdowns.
[P1.9] The comparison to hold throughout: not this policy versus some frictionless ideal, but this policy versus the real alternative—volatile, capacity-blind, politically combustible systems that serve neither migrants, institutions, nor communities. Structured guarantees beat chaotic discretion.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a planning document for a plan. It treats housing and local service capacity as a fixed threshold that can be known before a nurse, student, or engineer is approved. Capacity is not fixed. Rents are high where supply is constrained; the revenue, tuition, and demand brought by migrants also make new housing and expanded clinics commercially and fiscally viable.
[C1.2] Capping migration to today’s capacity therefore locks in present scarcity and calls the result prudence.
[C1.3] A guaranteed allocation is also an inflexible quota. Healthcare staffing needs change faster than local housing audits do. A hospital that cannot use its visa allocation during a slow year cannot bank it for the surge two years later, and a university stuck with a fixed annual quota must either fill stale slots or lose them. Central planners cannot forecast regional labour shortages, student flows, or housing completions precisely enough for five-year guarantees, so the system will regularly import the wrong people while rejecting the right ones.
[C1.4] The supposed incentive alignment is really gatekeeping by local incumbents. Councils that do not want growth can underbuild housing, declare services “at capacity,” and quietly suppress allocations. The motion rewards obstruction: the less you build, the fewer migrants you receive, and no national body is accountable for the lost economic opportunity.
[C1.5] Meanwhile smaller employers and poorer regions cannot afford dormitories or clinic pre-funding, so the richest institutions purchase the right to access global talent.
[C1.6] Finally, the political promise is illusory. Guaranteed caps tell voters that migration is a burden requiring pre-clearance. The moment a bus is crowded or a class is full, the guarantee looks like official failure rather than legitimacy. It gives backlash a concrete target and leaves no room to adjust when demand or capacity changes.
[C1.7] The real alternative is flexible, market-responsive migration combined with direct housing and service investment, not a planned shortage with a government seal. A quota is still a quota: the motion trades chaotic volatility for predictable misallocation.
