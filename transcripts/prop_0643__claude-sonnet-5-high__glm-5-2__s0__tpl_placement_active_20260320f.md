[P1.1] Boards of large public companies currently answer to one constituency: shareholders seeking returns, often over quarterly horizons. Workers who build that value, who bear the risk of layoffs, wage stagnation, and unsafe conditions, have no seat at the table where those tradeoffs get decided. That asymmetry is the core problem this proposal fixes, and it's worth being precise about why a legal requirement, not a voluntary gesture, is the right tool.
[P1.2] First, the incentive problem is structural, not a matter of goodwill. Executives are selected and compensated by shareholder-elected boards, so even well-intentioned managers face constant pressure to treat labor costs as the easiest lever to pull. Voluntary worker councils or "stakeholder capitalism" pledges get abandoned the moment a hostile activist investor or a rough earnings call arrives. A legal seat requirement removes worker representation from the category of things that can be quietly deprioritized.
[P1.3] Second, we have a working model, not a hypothetical. Germany's codetermination system has required worker representation on supervisory boards of large companies for decades, covering firms like Volkswagen and Siemens, without those companies losing their competitive edge globally. This isn't a fringe experiment; it's been standard practice in Europe's largest economy across multiple industrial cycles, including recessions and the 2008 financial crisis.
[P1.4] Third, better information flow produces better decisions, not just fairer ones. Workers on the shop floor often see safety risks, quality problems, and morale collapses long before they show up in a balance sheet. A board member elected by workers has both the standing and the incentive to raise those issues before they become scandals, lawsuits, or plant-floor disasters. Think of how many corporate collapses, from mismanaged safety cultures to abusive workplace practices, were preceded by warnings that never reached the boardroom because nobody with power was listening to the people who actually raised them.
[P1.5] The choice here isn't between efficiency and fairness. It's between boards insulated from the people who run the business, and boards forced to hear from them. That change makes companies more resilient, not less.
