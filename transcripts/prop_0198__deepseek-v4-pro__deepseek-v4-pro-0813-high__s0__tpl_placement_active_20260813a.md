[P1.1] Tariffs are taxes. They raise prices on imported goods and distribute costs and benefits across Americans. The Constitution places the power to lay taxes and regulate foreign commerce in Congress, not the presidency. Emergency powers, however, have become a standing exception: a president can invoke a national emergency and impose sweeping tariffs with no floor debate, no committee process, and no vote.
[P1.2] That is precisely the abuse the proposition corrects.
[P1.3] The problem is not hypothetical. Recent presidents have used national security and emergency authorities to impose broad tariffs on steel and aluminum—much of it from treaty allies—and have threatened or used emergency declarations to tax imports over migration or drug enforcement disputes. Whatever one thinks of any individual tariff, the structure is lawless: one person picks the policy, applies an emergency label, and shifts costs onto manufacturers, farmers, workers, and consumers overnight. When allied steel faces tariffs, American factories pay more for inputs; when trading partners retaliate, farm and factory exports lose markets.
[P1.4] This is legislation by declaration.
[P1.5] Broad tariff policy requires the kind of weighing only Congress performs. Tariffs favor some industries while penalizing others; they alter supply chains, invite retaliation, and raise household costs. The Framers assigned such distributional choices to the branch with enumerated tax and commerce power because durable trade policy must assemble majorities across regions and interests. Emergency tariff power dissolves that discipline.
[P1.6] The usual defense is speed. But broad tariff policy is not a hostage rescue; it is economic policy with long-run consequences. If a true emergency occurs, Congress can legislate quickly or authorize a narrow, time-limited response with a mandatory vote. Flexibility cannot justify an open-ended power to unilaterally tax the American economy.
[P1.7] Restoring sole authority over broad tariff policy to Congress would restore predictability, accountability, and constitutional order.

[C1.1] The proposition’s story is constitutionally tidy but practically dangerous. It calls tariffs “taxes” and then treats every presidential tariff action as lawless legislation. Tariffs can be taxes, but in an emergency they also function as economic leverage, defensive sanctions, and a non-military instrument of national security. The Constitution does not require the United States to respond slowly when foreign economic coercion hits quickly.
[C1.2] That is why Congress has enacted bounded emergency and national-security tariff statutes. Those are not presidents inventing authority out of nothing; they are delegations Congress can amend or repeal. The steel and aluminum measures PRO calls an abuse did not come from a bare emergency label: they required an investigation and a national-security finding under a standing statute. That is a design question, not proof that the authority must vanish.
[C1.3] Second, speed is not a mere convenience. A sudden cutoff of critical inputs, hostile manipulation of shipping, or foreign weaponization of payments can impose real harm within days. The proposition says Congress can legislate quickly in a genuine crisis, but that assumes floor schedules, committee bottlenecks, and veto-threshold coalitions will wait for the emergency. They often will not.
[C1.4] Removing the president’s capacity to impose immediate, limited trade measures would advertise to adversaries that the United States needs weeks to answer an economic attack.
[C1.5] Third, the motion is too absolute. It does not demand better triggers, narrower scope, mandatory congressional votes, reporting, or sunsets. It demands elimination. That would discard a necessary emergency tool instead of repairing its flaws.
[C1.6] Presidential tariffs remain politically visible: the president owns the resulting input costs, retail prices, retaliation, and job losses, and Congress retains power to change the underlying authority. Reform the emergency power, yes. Strip it away entirely? That trades constitutional symbolism for genuine economic vulnerability.

If a foreign government withholds critical inputs or manipulates transit, immediate pressure on its export earnings may work before the disruption becomes entrenched. Congress rarely legislates at crisis speed.
[C2.6] Pre-authorization is the only realistic answer—and that is what current statutes provide.
