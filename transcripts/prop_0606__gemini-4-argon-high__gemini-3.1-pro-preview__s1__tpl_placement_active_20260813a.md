[P1.1] A patent is a social contract, not a license to hold public health hostage. States grant temporary monopolies so that life-saving medicines are developed and delivered to patients. When an essential medicine—such as a critical treatment for HIV, hepatitis C, or cancer—is priced beyond a country’s capacity to pay, that contract breaks. Today, although trade rules permit compulsory licensing, exercising that right requires navigating a discretionary political minefield.
[P1.2] Governments face intense diplomatic pressure and legal threats for every single drug they attempt to license, leaving patients to suffer while officials hesitate.
[P1.3] Establishing a defined affordability threshold fixes this broken leverage. Under this policy, countries set transparent ceilings tied to national income, health budget capacity, and clinical value. Whenever an essential medicine exceeds that ceiling, legal grounds for a compulsory license are automatically established. Moving from ad hoc political battles to a clear regulatory trigger transforms the bargaining table.
[P1.4] Pharmaceutical companies can no longer rely on trade intimidation to protect extortionate markups. Instead, knowing a compulsory license is the predictable consequence of pricing above the threshold, manufacturers gain a powerful incentive to voluntarily price their drugs within reach to retain market exclusivity. A bright-line rule forces fair pricing before a license even needs to be issued.
[P1.5] The opposition will warn that this chills research, but that ignores basic market reality. First, compulsory licensing is not confiscation; patent holders still receive reasonable royalties on generic sales. Second, when an essential drug exceeds a country's affordability threshold, the manufacturer is not capturing broad revenue there anyway—they are either pricing the public system out entirely or cannibalizing budgets meant for basic healthcare. Innovators cannot lose revenue that a country cannot afford to pay.
[P1.6] Tying licensing grounds to an affordability ceiling simply replaces low-volume exclusion with high-volume, royalty-generating access. When life-saving medicine already exists, pricing it out of reach does not fund the future—it merely rations survival in the present.
