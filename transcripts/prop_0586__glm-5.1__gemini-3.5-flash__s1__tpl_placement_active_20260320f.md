[P1.1] Sectoral bargaining and wage boards offer what large minimum wage hikes cannot: precision. A $15 or $20 national minimum wage applies the same floor to a fast-food franchise in rural Alabama and a hotel in San Francisco, ignoring the vast differences in local productivity, cost structures, and profit margins. Sectoral bargaining allows wage standards to track actual economic conditions within industries—raising pay aggressively where margins permit, and moderating where survival is genuinely thin.
[P1.2] The employment cost of blunt minimum wage increases falls hardest on low-wage workers themselves. When a single large hike hits, employers in tight-margin sectors face a binary choice: absorb the increase or cut positions. The researchers who find minimal disemployment effects study moderate increases. At the scales now proposed—doubling or more the federal floor—the historical evidence thins out, and economic theory predicts displacement that concentrates among the least skilled workers sectoral bargaining aims to protect.
[P1.3] Wage boards solve this by bringing employers and worker representatives to the table together. Employers have incentive to agree to wages they can sustain because they shaped the standard; workers gain leverage through collective voice rather than legislative imposition that employers then fight through attrition. The result is wage floors with broader buy-in and fewer layoffs as compliance mechanisms.
[P1.4] Unlike federal minimum wage fights, which produce years of stagnation followed by one dramatic jump, sectoral systems adjust continuously, letting wages rise with productivity rather than lagging for a decade then catching up in a shock.
[P1.5] The proposition is not that sectoral bargaining is perfect. It is that it is better than the alternative on the table: one national number, applied everywhere, with employment losses concentrated exactly where low-wage workers can least afford them.

[C1.1] The promise of sectoral bargaining rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of the American regulatory state and labor market dynamics. Rather than protecting vulnerable workers, establishing industry-specific wage boards in the United States would institutionalize a system of regulatory capture, cartelization, and administrative paralysis that destroys entry-level jobs far more aggressively than statutory minimum wage increases.
[C1.2] First, sectoral bargaining invites cartelization. On a wage board, the largest, most capitalized corporate incumbents hold the leverage. These giants can easily absorb higher labor costs through automation or scale. They have every incentive to codify complex wage floors and work rules specifically designed to squeeze out small businesses and startups operating on razor-thin margins.
[C1.3] While a uniform statutory minimum wage applies a simple, predictable floor, sectoral boards weaponize regulation to stifle competition. When local businesses close because they cannot navigate or afford bespoke industry mandates, low-wage workers lose their jobs entirely.
[C1.4] Second, sectoral bargaining creates immense labor market rigidity. In a dynamic economy, workers climb the ladder by moving across industries. Sectoral boards lock workers into rigid, bureaucratically defined job classifications. This turns the wage floor into a ceiling, depressing the wage growth of productive workers who are barred from negotiating individual premiums or flexible roles.
[C1.5] Finally, the administrative complexity is a massive job-killer. Instead of a single, easily understood statutory standard, employers must navigate a shifting labyrinth of rules for different sectors, roles, and regions. For small-scale employers, compliance and legal costs alone trigger defensive hiring freezes.
[C1.6] The comparison to a clumsy national flat rate is a false dichotomy. US minimum wages are already highly localized by state and city. Superimposing a Byzantine system of sectoral cartels will not rescue low-wage workers; it will lock them out of the labor market, insulate corporate giants from competition, and replace mobility with stagnation.
