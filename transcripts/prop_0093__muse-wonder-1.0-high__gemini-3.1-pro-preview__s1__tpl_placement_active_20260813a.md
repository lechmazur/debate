[P1.1] Curb space is the most contested land in a city, and we currently give most of it to the least productive use: storing private cars while they sit empty. A street does not succeed by how many vehicles it can warehouse at the edge, but by how many people and goods it can move through it.
[P1.2] The arithmetic is stark. One on-street space locked up for a workday may serve one driver. The same length of curb as a bus lane, used by buses every few minutes, moves hundreds of riders an hour and keeps them out of traffic. As a bike lane, it carries a steady stream of trips that take almost no space.
[P1.3] As a loading zone, it lets dozens of deliveries happen without trucks double-parking and blocking an entire travel lane. Parking is not access; it is idleness. Buses, bikes, and freight are access in motion.
[P1.4] That reallocation also serves people parking policy leaves out. In dense neighborhoods many households — disproportionately lower-income, younger, older, and disabled residents who cannot drive — do not own a car at all, yet they pay for streets through rent and taxes. Protecting cheap curb parking is a subsidy for car owners extracted from bus riders stuck behind those parked cars, cyclists pushed into traffic, and shoppers whose buses crawl. Businesses gain more from reliable deliveries, bus stops that bring customers, and bike lanes that increase passing foot traffic than from a handful of spaces monopolized all day, often by commuters or residents, not customers.
[P1.5] Finally, prizing parking creates the congestion it claims to solve. Drivers circle hunting for an underpriced space, trucks have nowhere legal to stop, and doors open into bike traffic. Reserving more curb for movement cuts circling, double-parking, and conflicts at the curb itself.
[P1.6] Cities should stop treating public curb as free private storage and start treating it as what it is: scarce infrastructure for moving everyone.
