[P1.1] Dear judges, dating apps are not casinos with profiles; they are trust intermediaries. Their real product is the belief that the next like might become something real. Engagement-maximizing swipe mechanics and paid visibility boosts can quietly betray that belief, and that is why they should be limited when they undermine match quality and user trust.
[P1.2] Consider the incentive. A dating app that successfully pairs users loses them. So the business model often rewards continued searching: more sessions, more swipes, more purchases. Infinite decks and gamified swipe rhythms train fast, shallow judgments.
[P1.3] Paid boosts, super likes, and purchased visibility then turn the match pool into an auction. The person most compatible with you may never appear because someone else bought prominence. Your like may sit unseen unless you pay to lift it. That is not matching; that is selling access to attention.
[P1.4] This damages quality and trust together. Quality falls because exposure tracks payment rather than fit. Trust falls because users suspect the algorithm is hiding good options to extract money. Once that suspicion takes hold, the app becomes a rigged game, and users leave.
[P1.5] Dating apps cannot survive on engagement alone if nobody believes a genuine match is possible.
[P1.6] The proposition is conditional, not puritanical. It does not ban swiping or every paid feature. It says limit these mechanics when they undermine quality and trust. Boosts that genuinely surface compatible people can survive; manipulative scarcity, paywalled likes, and endless swipe loops cannot.
[P1.7] Critics call this paternalism, but the asymmetry is real: users cannot audit hidden ranking or know which profiles were suppressed. A market cannot be trusted when one side controls both the deck and the price of visibility.
[P1.8] The central comparison is simple: an app optimized for engagement keeps you swiping; an app optimized for matches helps you leave. Platforms should protect the second purpose. Otherwise they monetize loneliness and call it love.

[C1.1] The proposition fails because it asks platforms to restrict the very tools that make online dating scalable, legible, and user-directed, based on a standard they cannot reliably measure: “undermines match quality and user trust.” Swipe mechanics are not a defect; they are a fast sorting interface. In a pool of thousands, people need a cheap way to say yes, no, or maybe. Slowing that process or capping it does not create better judgment; it creates frustration, backlog, and thinner markets.
[C1.2] Paid visibility boosts are easier to caricature than to condemn. A new profile, a shy user, or someone in a sparse rural market can buy a brief nudge to escape the cold-start problem. If a boost merely makes a person visible long enough for genuine compatibility to register, it improves matching rather than corrupts it. The proposition assumes visibility is a zero-sum theft, but dating markets are often liquidity markets: more exposure can generate more mutual matches, not fewer.
[C1.3] The deeper problem is enforcement. Match quality is subjective, revealed slowly, and often known only to the users. Trust is similarly contested: some users trust speed and abundance; others trust curation. A rule that triggers when features “undermine” these goods invites arbitrary line-drawing and post hoc moralizing.
[C1.4] Platforms would be pressured to suppress ordinary engagement signals because any time spent swiping can be labeled manipulative. That chill would discourage experimentation with interfaces that help users discover preferences they cannot state in advance.
[C1.5] PRO’s real target is bad design, but the answer to bad design is competition, transparency, and exit, not blunt limits. Users can and do abandon apps that feel rigged. If an app sells too much visibility or hides too many profiles, its reputation suffers and rivals can win by advertising honesty. Better to let users compare, choose, and leave than to throttle the tools that help them meet.
