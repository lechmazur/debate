[P1.1] A cinema ticket should buy a particular experience, not privileged access to a cultural moment. Studios should release more major films simultaneously in theaters and at home, with accessibility built into the home release. The question is not whether theaters deserve to exist. It is whether restricting access should remain the routine way we support them.
[P1.2] For many viewers, theatrical attendance is not merely inconvenient. Chronic pain can make fixed seating unbearable; sensory sensitivities can make a crowded auditorium overwhelming; limited mobility or medical vulnerability can make the trip impractical. Even well-equipped theaters cannot accommodate every body or circumstance. At home, reliable captions, audio description, screen-reader-compatible controls, and the ability to pause can turn an inaccessible film into an accessible experience.
[P1.3] Simultaneity matters because “you can watch it later” is not equal participation. New releases generate conversations, shared plans, reviews, and spoilers immediately. A disabled viewer should not routinely have to choose between physical distress and joining that conversation weeks later. Home access also reaches people constrained by caregiving, distance, or transport costs.
[P1.4] These are audiences a theatrical-only window may fail to serve, not simply customers waiting to be diverted.
[P1.5] The economic objection deserves a concrete answer: simultaneous release need not mean a cheap subscription giveaway. Studios can offer paid home rentals alongside tickets and assess demand title by title. Some theater spending will shift, but some home purchases will come from households that otherwise would not buy during the opening window. Lost ticket revenue therefore cannot simply be assumed to equal lost total revenue.
[P1.6] Theaters still offer scale, sound, atmosphere, and a shared occasion. Those are reasons to attend voluntarily. Our proposal preserves that choice while expanding another; it does not require every film to use the same strategy. Studios should have to justify withholding accessible day-one options, rather than audiences having to justify why they cannot get through a cinema door.
