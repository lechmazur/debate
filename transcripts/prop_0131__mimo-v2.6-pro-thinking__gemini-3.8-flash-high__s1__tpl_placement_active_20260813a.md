Generously paying family members does not liberate them; it institutionalizes their isolation.
[C1.2] First, cash stipends create a regressive economic trap that disproportionately harms women. When the state attaches substantial stipends to domestic care, it creates a powerful incentive for the lower-earning household member—usually a woman—to exit the formal labor market. Family care offers no promotions, no transferable credentials, no workplace safety protections, and no labor unions. A stipend today cannot compensate for permanent career derailment, lost compounding wage growth, and diminished lifelong economic independence.
[C1.3] Rather than relieving gendered expectations, generous pay formalizes them into state-subsidized domestic confinement.
[C1.4] Second, it compromises the autonomy and quality of care for care recipients. High-need older adults and disabled people require specialized support: physical therapy, complex medication management, and professional behavioral care. Familial affection is not a substitute for clinical competence. Moreover, monetizing family care creates toxic financial dependencies within households, discourages independent living, and eliminates the external oversight that protects vulnerable individuals from neglect.
[C1.5] Care recipients deserve professional, rights-based support, not total reliance on kin.
[C1.6] Finally, paying families cannibalizes the universal care infrastructure society actually needs. Direct household payments divert scarce public funds away from building communal day programs, professional home-health services, and accessible respite facilities. It abdicates government’s duty to collectivize care, privatizing it right back into isolated homes.
[C1.7] We do not end exploitation by putting relatives on the payroll to shoulder the burden alone. We end it by building a public system that relieves them of it entirely.
