[C1.1] The proposition mistakes assistance for legitimacy and sets the legal standard too high. A shelter bed is not a precondition for every law regulating conduct in public space. What matters is whether the law targets conduct rather than status, is clear and proportionate, and respects due process.
[C1.2] A person who blocks a wheelchair ramp, leaves waste in a park, pitches a tent across a fire lane, or stores property in a prohibited place has obstructed others, whether a vacant shelter bed exists that night. If the city cannot obtain voluntary placement, may it remove tents or require relocation? The proposition says no unless all three alternatives are genuinely available. That would give obstruction leverage: the more severe the camp, the less likely residents will consent to services, and the less available the shelter, the more immunity from regulation.
[C1.3] Services matter, but as mitigation and prevention, not moral permission. Counting beds does not establish genuine availability. A person may work nights, need pet accommodation, fear congregate shelter, lack identification, or reject treatment. Genuine availability must mean accessible, safe, relevant capacity when enforcement occurs.
[C1.4] If that is the benchmark, governments can evade duties by claiming nominal beds while real pathways remain closed. Worse, shelters can be unsafe or unsuitable, so compelling entry would expose residents to violence or coercion. Justice does not require making that choice.
[C1.5] The divide is not “help offered versus poverty punished,” but between laws preserving public space and laws declaring homeless people criminals merely for existing. A narrowly drawn obstruction rule can be enforced with notice, an opportunity to move, secure storage, and reasonable exemptions, even when no bed is available. Such a law regulates conduct; it does not make shelter a condition of citizenship.
[C1.6] Thus alternatives are one consideration, not the sole source of justification. The proposition’s “only” is false, and it risks protecting severe obstruction while stigmatizing those it claims to assist.
