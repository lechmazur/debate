[P1.1] Unabated coal remains the single largest driver of global carbon emissions, yet our current system of voluntary "phase-down" pledges is failing. The reason is a classic prisoner’s dilemma: no major economy wants to unilaterally retire reliable baseload power while competitors keep burning cheap coal to undercut their manufacturing. Voluntary targets crumble the moment energy markets tighten. Only a binding international agreement with a common timeline and differentiated support can break this stalemate.
[P1.2] First, a binding common timeline eliminates the free-rider problem and transforms global capital markets. When major economies, from the United States and Europe to China and India, lock into a shared legal deadline, competitive paranoia disappears. Industrial policy shifts from protecting legacy coal mines to winning the clean energy race. Just as importantly, a hard treaty deadline sends an unmistakable signal to investors.
[P1.3] Right now, ambiguous diplomacy allows utilities to keep building coal plants with forty-year lifespans, locking in either catastrophic warming or chaotic, uncompensated stranded assets later. A common timeline halts financing for new unabated coal immediately and guarantees synchronized global demand for grid storage, transmission, and renewable supply chains.
[P1.4] Second, integrating differentiated support makes a unified timeline both equitable and achievable for emerging powers. Critics argue that countries like India, Indonesia, or South Africa cannot leave coal on the same schedule as wealthy nations because their coal fleets are younger and their borrowing costs for green infrastructure are punishingly high. That is precisely the gap this mechanism bridges. Today’s ad-hoc climate finance fails because donors fear their money will not yield real emissions cuts, while recipients refuse hard deadlines without guaranteed cash.
[P1.5] A binding treaty solves both problems simultaneously: wealthier economies are legally bound to underwrite early plant retirements and subsidize grid modernization, unlocking the capital developing economies need to hit the shared deadline without sacrificing growth.
[P1.6] The status quo delivers broken promises and escalating climate devastation. A binding pact with differentiated support finally aligns economic self-interest with planetary survival.

[C1.3] The deeper flaw is the 'common timeline.' Coal is not a single switch. In wealthy economies much coal capacity is old and already uneconomic; in India, Indonesia, or South Africa the fleet is far younger and supplies affordable baseload for industrialization. [C1.6] If it is not binding, developing economies are asked to shutter coal and then abandoned.
[C1.7] The real constraint is not a court-enforceable date; it is the absence of cheap, firm, clean power and affordable capital.
