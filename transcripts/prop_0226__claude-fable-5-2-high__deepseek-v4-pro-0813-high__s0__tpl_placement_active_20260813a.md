[P1.1] Picture a family from a low-lying atoll whose freshwater has turned to salt and whose land floods at every king tide. They arrive at a border and tell the truth: no one is persecuting us; our home is simply ceasing to exist. Under current refugee law, that honest answer gets them nothing. The refugee framework was built around persecution, and the sea does not persecute.
[P1.2] That gap is what this debate is about.
[P1.3] The motion asks for something modest: a *limited* legal right. That means a narrow, evidence-based status for people whose home areas have become objectively uninhabitable from sea-level rise or lethal heat, where relocating within their own country is not a realistic option. It does not mean open borders or asylum for anyone with a climate-related hardship. It is a defined backstop for cases where return means sending people back to conditions that cannot sustain life.
[P1.4] There are three reasons to recognize it.
[P1.5] First, the principle already exists and the law just hasn't caught up. Nearly every state accepts that you don't return someone to a place where their life is in serious danger. International human rights bodies have already acknowledged, in principle, that climate conditions could trigger that duty. A limited asylum right makes this predictable rather than leaving it to ad hoc discretion.
[P1.6] Second, the choice is not between movement and no movement. People will leave land that is underwater or too hot to survive working outdoors. The real choice is between orderly, screened, legal pathways and smugglers, visa overstays and permanent legal limbo. A right with clear criteria gives states more control than they have now.
[P1.7] Third, discretion fails exactly when it's needed. Bilateral deals, such as Australia's arrangement with Tuvalu, show managed climate mobility is workable. But goodwill that depends on political mood protects the lucky, not the endangered. A right is what remains when the mood shifts.
[P1.8] Expect to hear "floodgates" and "fund adaptation instead." But the limits are written into the motion, and adaptation is not a substitute for a last resort. [clipped]

[C1.1] That family is not fleeing a persecutor. That is not a technicality; it is the reason asylum exists as a distinct protection. Extending it to sea-level rise and heat converts asylum into a general disaster-relief visa and weakens the legal force of refugee status. Persecution requires a human agent and targeted harm.
[C1.2] A rising tide may be lethal, but it does not single anyone out. Once the target of asylum becomes environmental harm, the principled boundary that justifies special non-return protection dissolves. Non-refoulement was never a promise against all hardship; it bars return to persecution and torture.
[C1.3] “Limited” is not a workable limit. Sea-level rise and extreme heat operate on continuums. Would a resident qualify only when their island is fully submerged, or when groundwater becomes saline? At what number of unliveable heat days?
[C1.4] And who decides that relocation within their own country is unrealistic: the host state, the applicant, or a court? Every rejected claimant can litigate these gradations. A legal right gives applicants the strongest tool to force those questions into courts, producing expansive interpretations rather than a narrow backstop.
[C1.5] The better response is not a right but a menu of controlled tools: bilateral relocation agreements, humanitarian visas, temporary protection after disasters, regional mobility arrangements, and serious investment in adaptation. These allow countries to set numbers, prioritize cases, and maintain democratic control over entry. Australia-Tuvalu-style cooperation is an argument for negotiated migration, not for a universal justiciable entitlement. A legal right does the opposite: it replaces political discretion with contested legal obligation.
[C1.6] The choice is not between rights and chaos. States can create safe, orderly pathways today without redefining asylum or surrendering control. That path protects both the displaced and the institution of asylum.

[C2.3] The line-drawing reply also fails. Asylum asks whether this person has a well-founded fear of persecution—a judgment about individualized risk, not a threshold on a thermometer. [C2.4] They are answered by legislatures setting quotas and criteria in discretionary programs, which can be revised. [C2.5] PRO says courts are already hearing these claims, so a defined right constrains them [P2.6]. The opposite is true. Today a court can grant relief in an individual case but cannot order a state to create a general climate status. A legal right gives every claimant a statutory hook to demand entry, and every adverse decision becomes a rights violation.
[C2.6] That shifts control from elected governments to judges precisely where the numbers are largest.
[C2.7] Finally, PRO’s “person just past the number” [P2.8] is not an argument for a right. Every system has a cutoff; a right does not create infinite capacity. The menu CON offered—bilateral deals, humanitarian visas, temporary protection, adaptation—can be scaled, targeted, and democratically accountable.
[C2.8] PRO’s floor is a universal obligation with no democratic check. That is the worse trade.
