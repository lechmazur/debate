[P1.1] Historic cities are not theme parks. They have finite bridges, lanes, squares, water, air, and housing. When cruise ships and coach fleets arrive in bursts, they consume capacity residents depend on, then leave. The proposition is conditional, not a ban: cap arrivals when crowding, pollution, and housing pressure outweigh tourist revenue.
[P1.2] That is basic public management, not hostility to tourism.
[P1.3] The revenue case is weaker than it looks. Cruise passengers often sleep and eat onboard; bus tours often arrive, photograph, buy a coffee, and depart. Their spending is modest and frequently captured by international operators, while the city pays for waste, traffic control, emergency services, and wear on fragile streets.
[P1.4] Meanwhile, idling coaches fill medieval centers with diesel fumes, and ships concentrate emissions at port. These are not abstract costs; they are asthma, eroded stone, blocked ambulances, and residents unable to cross their own square.
[P1.5] Housing pressure compounds it. Tourist demand fuels short-term rentals and hotel conversion, shrinking the long-term stock. Nurses, teachers, craftspeople, and students are priced out. Once a historic center loses its residents, it loses the very life that makes it worth visiting.
[P1.6] A postcard district of souvenir shops is not a living city.
[P1.7] Caps also target the worst offenders, not all visitors. Cities already ration water, waste, cars, and noise. Tourism should not be exempt. A seasonal limit on cruise berths and large bus slots can preserve overnight tourism, which spreads spending and respects local life, while reducing the day-trip surges that produce the most damage for the least local return.
[P1.8] The opponent must defend a strange position: that even when a city can show resident crowding, pollution, and displacement outweigh revenue, it must keep accepting unlimited arrivals. But no serious city manages traffic by hoping every driver pays enough in parking. When the ledger turns negative, the cap is not arbitrary; it is the only way to make tourism serve the city rather than consume it.

[C1.1] The motion sounds prudent only because it hides a blank check. [P1.1] offers a conditional cap: restrict cruise and bus arrivals once crowding, pollution, and housing pressure "outweigh" revenue. But no city can audit that ledger. Crowding and pollution are real, yet infinitely elastic; "outweigh" has no unit, threshold, or stopping rule.
[C1.2] Real congestion pricing posts a number; the proposition asks us to trust a feeling. In practice, officials facing any complaint can declare the balance negative, converting a temporary safeguard into a standing veto on disfavored visitors. That is not management; it is authority without accountability.
[C1.3] Worse, the cap aims at the wrong target. Large tour buses replace dozens of private cars or ride-hails, cutting congestion and emissions per visitor; restricting them can push those same travellers into separate vehicles and increase total trips. Cruise passengers may eat onboard, but they also pay port fees, take shore excursions, and support attractions that off-season and budget travellers use, smoothing demand across the year. Cutting these visitors to chase "higher-yield" overnight elites narrows the tax base while doing nothing about housing.
[C1.4] Housing pressure is driven mainly by short-term rental conversion, second homes, and investment capital, not by day trippers walking from a coach. Capping buses does not return a single apartment to a nurse. If a city wants fewer idling diesels or cleaner air, it should enforce emission zones, congestion charges, and rental rules—surgical tools that protect residents while preserving open access. A blunt arrival cap merely pushes tourists elsewhere and small businesses under.
[C1.5] The cap must be rejected because the proposition's test is unworkable, its target misidentified, and its tool unnecessarily destructive. Historic cities should remain open, managed by precise pricing and targeted regulation rather than by bans dressed as balance. History is not preserved by emptiness; it is sustained by the visitors whose fees keep the stones standing.
