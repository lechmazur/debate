[P1.1] Expanding legal pathways for seasonal and sectoral work attacks the root cause of irregular migration: persistent labor demand in key industries. Agriculture, tourism, and construction in destination countries require workers, and when legal options are absent or inadequate, migrants turn to irregular channels. Legal seasonal and sectoral programs transform this reality by providing structured, temporary access that satisfies employer demand while removing the need for clandestine entry. Canada's Seasonal Agricultural Worker Program offers tens of thousands of visas annually, tying workers to specific jobs and employers, which has demonstrably reduced unauthorized crossings in agricultural regions.
[P1.2] Similarly, the US H-2A program, despite its limitations, channels agricultural labor demand through legal means, preventing what would otherwise be irregular migration. Germany's sector-specific guest worker initiatives show the same pattern: where legal pathways exist, irregular migration for those sectors declines measurably. Meanwhile, Spain's experience with regularizing seasonal agricultural workers from Morocco demonstrates how legal avenues can rapidly reduce irregular flows in targeted sectors. Border enforcement, by contrast, cannot match this effectiveness because it does not address underlying economic pull factors.
[P1.3] Each new fence, patrol, or detention center merely displaces routes or increases smugglers' profits as they adapt to obstacles with alarming speed. The result is a costly arms race with diminishing returns. In contrast, legal pathways offer a direct solution: they convert irregular flows into regulated, temporary ones that benefit both sending and receiving countries. They are more cost-effective, more humane, and more sustainable than endless enforcement escalation.
[P1.4] Most critically, they address the actual drivers of migration rather than attempting to block them with increasingly expensive and ineffective barriers.

[C1.1] The proposition requires a causal comparison, and its key mechanism is not established. More legal seasonal and sectoral visas do not automatically mean fewer irregular migrants. Such programs cover selected occupations, nationalities, regions, and periods, while unauthorized migration responds to broader pressures. If employers cannot recruit through a legal channel, or workers cannot change jobs, remain with family, or extend visas, demand does not disappear; it is redirected into irregular channels.
[C1.2] A visa program may formalize some journeys while leaving total migration intact.
[C1.3] By contrast, additional border enforcement directly raises the cost and risk of unauthorized entry and can reduce irregular arrivals. Deterrence need not eliminate structural migration to work to beat narrow legalization. If enforcement cuts irregular entries by ten percent while legal programs reduce them by two percent, enforcement is more effective on the proposition’s own metric. “Displacement” does not prove otherwise: routes may change, yet successful unauthorized entries can still fall. Border controls can target repeat smugglers, unauthorized employment, and overstaying, not merely add patrols.
[C1.4] The examples do not establish the counterfactual. Canada’s and the United States’ seasonal programs are quota-limited and employer-bound, while regularization in one Spanish agricultural corridor cannot show that expansion generally reduces irregular flows. These illustrate legal migration, not proof that legal expansion outperforms enforcement.
[C1.5] There is also an incentive problem. Legal programs that cap wages or bind workers to one job can make informal work more attractive, leaving firms reliant on irregular labor and workers dependent on smugglers. Legal pathways work best alongside inspection and sanctions, not as their replacement. On the narrow measure of irregular migration, targeted border enforcement has the clearer causal mechanism: it raises the cost of illegal entry and can immediately reduce successful unauthorized crossings.
[C1.6] Legal admission is valuable, but unless its scale and eligibility match displaced demand, the proposition overclaims.
