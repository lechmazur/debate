[C1.2] Start with absorption. Western weapons are not plug-and-play. Himars crews, artillery crews, and especially armored brigade staffs need months of training, maintenance chains, and spare-parts pipelines that did not exist in Ukraine in mid-2022. Ukraine's 2022 victories — Kharkiv, Kherson — were won with equipment its forces already mastered.
[C1.3] The proof is 2023: even with fully equipped Western-trained brigades, the counteroffensive could not breach prepared defenses. The claim that identical tanks delivered a year earlier, to less-trained units, would have produced deeper exploitation, inverts everything we learned about what actually limits Ukrainian offensive power.
[C1.4] Second, faster escalation cuts both ways. In 2022, Moscow was actively signaling nuclear use and had not yet committed its mobilized reserves. Flooding Ukraine with Western armor that spring plausibly accelerates Russian mobilization, deepens Iranian and North Korean arms cooperation, and spooks the cautious partners — Germany, Turkey's straits posture, the global-south coalition at the UN — whose sustained support is precisely what has kept Ukraine alive. A faster 2022 that fragments the coalition is a worse 2024.
[C1.5] Third, Russia adapts regardless. The depot dispersion Himars eventually forced, and the fortification lines, were responses to any long-range threat, not to delivery calendars. Hitting logistics a few months earlier buys temporary strikes, not a broken army.
[C1.6] Ukraine is where it is because its partners stayed unified and its forces gained real proficiency over time — both products of gradual escalation, not casualties of it. Faster aid was never free speed; it was borrowed against sustainability and competence. The proposition's clean counterfactual ignores those costs.
