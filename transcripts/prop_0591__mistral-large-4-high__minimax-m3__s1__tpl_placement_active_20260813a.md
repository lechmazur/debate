[P1.1] Immigration decisions now hinge on algorithms that score asylum claims, flag visa applicants, and predict flight risk—often without the affected individual knowing why they were denied. When a family is separated or a refugee is deported to persecution because of an opaque risk score from a black box, due process becomes a fiction. We cannot meaningfully appeal a decision we cannot see, nor can courts review what they cannot examine.
[P1.2] A global minimum standard for algorithmic transparency is the necessary floor, not a ceiling. It requires states to disclose the general logic behind automated immigration decisions, the categories of data used, and guarantee meaningful human review with the right to contestation. This mirrors existing obligations in data protection and financial regulation, where high-stakes automated decisions already require explainability and audit trails, proving such standards work.
[P1.3] Critics will invoke national security and sovereignty, arguing that revealing screening methods aids evasion. But transparency does not mean publishing classified watchlists or proprietary source code. It means ensuring that when the state denies entry or asylum, the individual receives a reasoned, contestable explanation and the system is subject to independent oversight. Security through obscurity fails when biased or malfunctioning algorithms systematically exclude legitimate claimants—undermining border control's legitimacy.
[P1.4] Without a global floor, we face a regulatory race to the bottom: jurisdictions compete for security by deploying the least transparent systems, exporting injustice across borders while claiming procedural compliance. Consider a refugee denied protection because an opaque algorithm penalized inconsistent testimony without context—a decision unreviewable because the logic stays hidden. A minimum standard anchors accountability, prevents beggar-thy-neighbor opacity, and affirms that fundamental rights apply equally whether a decision is rendered by a clerk or a machine.
[P1.5] The question is not whether algorithms should assist immigration screening, but whether those who wield state power over human mobility must remain answerable for how they do so. Transparency is the difference between legitimate governance and arbitrary rule.

[P2.1] The charge that a global standard is an unenforceable "press release" misunderstands how international law operates. Minimum standards function as floors, not ceilings—incorporated into domestic statutes, enforced by national courts, and reinforced through treaty monitoring bodies and individual complaint mechanisms, exactly as the Convention Against Torture or CEDAW operate without a global police force. Sovereignty is preserved because states retain discretion over how to meet the floor; they simply cannot hide behind it to avoid accountability. When a state denies asylum, it must explain why to the individual and to reviewing courts—that is not the imposition of an international bureaucrat but the vindication of sovereignty's own legitimacy condition: consent of the governed.
[P2.2] On security, the claim that this publishes the answer key conflates transparency with disclosure. A requirement to reveal "the general logic" and "categories of data"—whether a decision weighed documentation, credibility, or risk factors—does not expose source code, watchlists, or classified thresholds. It exposes the structure of reasoning, not the secrets within it. Smugglers already exploit opacity by coaching clients to game unknown systems; transparency allows affected communities and NGOs to audit for error and bias, which is the actual threat to security—a system that wrongly excludes legitimate refugees while missing real risks because no one can see the flaws.
[P2.3] The concession that transparency does not equal fairness draws the wrong conclusion. Transparency is necessary though not sufficient for fairness, just as due process is necessary though not sufficient for justice. Without visibility into how algorithms weigh testimony consistency or country-of-origin data, bias remains invisible and uncorrectable. The alternative—opaque systems with "robust human review"—fails because reviewers cannot oversee what they cannot see.
[P2.4] Finally, minimum standards do not freeze innovation; they create markets for it. Safety regulations did not stop automobile innovation, and explainability requirements are driving better AI. A floor prevents the race to the bottom where jurisdictions compete by hiding the ball.
