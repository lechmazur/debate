[P1.1] An exemption from anti-discrimination law is permission to do what the law otherwise forbids. It requires a justification, and the justification actually offered for religious exemptions is that a faith community must be able to constitute itself: choose its ministers, teach its doctrine, decide who belongs. That reason has a shape. It reaches the pulpit and the seminary.
[P1.2] It does not reach the parking garage.
[P1.3] The proposition simply insists that the exemption stop where its justification stops. A church may require its priest to be Catholic. A religiously owned hospital chain that fires a lab technician for marrying outside the faith is not constituting a religious community; it is running a business and imposing a creed on someone whose work is pipettes and paperwork. Nothing about the sincerity of the owners changes what the job is.
[P1.4] Notice who bears the cost of each rule. Under a function-tied exemption, religion loses almost nothing it actually claims to need — no congregation is forced to ordain an unbeliever. Under a status-tied exemption, the cost lands on millions of workers in hospitals, universities, insurers, publishers, and contractors who never signed up for a religious test and often don't know their employer claims one until they're dismissed. That asymmetry decides the motion before any abstraction about liberty is reached.
[P1.5] There is also a competitive dimension. When a religiously affiliated firm takes public money and bids for public contracts while claiming a blanket exemption, the exemption stops being a shield for conscience and becomes a publicly subsidized licence to discriminate in an ordinary market.
[P1.6] My opponent must therefore defend a principle: that ownership identity, rather than the activity being regulated, is what triggers the exemption. Ask what that principle would not exempt. A line drawn at core religious functions is administrable and principled. A line drawn at the door of incorporation is no line at all.
