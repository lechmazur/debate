[P1.1] India's labor law does not protect Indian workers. It protects a small island of formal jobs and leaves the vast mainland outside. Roughly nine in ten Indian workers are informal: no severance, no pension, no health cover, no notice period. The rigid rules on the books—government permission to retrench in mid-sized factories, litigation that drags for years—never reach them.
[P1.2] The status quo is not "protection versus flexibility." It is rigidity for a few and nothing for everyone else.
[P1.3] That rigidity is not harmless. Picture a garment maker in Tiruppur with ninety workers who wins a large export order. Scaling to three hundred means crossing thresholds that make every future downsizing a legal ordeal. So the owner does what thousands do: splits the firm into shells, hires through contractors, keeps the books small.
[P1.4] The result is India's notorious "missing middle"—a country with millions of tiny enterprises and strikingly few of the mid-sized manufacturers that lifted Bangladesh, Vietnam, and earlier East Asia. The worker "protected" by the law is the one who never gets hired formally at all.
[P1.5] The better principle is simple: protect workers, not jobs. Denmark hires and fires easily, yet workers are secure because unemployment insurance, retraining, and healthcare belong to the person, not the position. The same logic applies here. Make contracts genuinely flexible—fixed-term employment, simpler retrenchment, fewer thresholds—and in exchange build universal social protection: portable health coverage, pensions, and unemployment support that follow a worker from a factory in Surat to a warehouse in Pune, whether formal or informal.
[P1.6] This is one package, not two separate asks. Flexibility without protection is exploitation; protection without flexibility is unaffordable, because a small formal base cannot fund universal coverage. Only a larger formal economy—generated by firms willing to hire—can pay for it.
[P1.7] India has already moved halfway with fixed-term contracts and higher thresholds in the new labor codes. The protection side is what lags. Completing the bargain is how India stops defending ten percent and starts covering everyone.

[C1.1] The bargain sounds elegant and is dangerous because one half is real and the other is fiction.
[C1.2] Flexibility is immediate, enforceable, and valuable to employers. The permission to retrench, the fixed-term contract, the easier layoff — firms will use them tomorrow. [C1.3] Mountains of construction cess lie unspent, EPF barely covers the bottom half of formal workers, e-Shram registers hundreds of millions with almost no benefits attached. You cannot fund Danish security on an Indian fiscal base without massive, regressive taxation, and you cannot run a portable unemployment system when you cannot even verify who lost a job.
[C1.4] Second, the diagnosis is wrong. The garment owner in Tiruppur does not stay small only because of Chapter V-B. Most requests for closure are actually granted; the law deters on paper more than in practice. He stays small because of delayed GST refunds, unreliable power, lack of scale finance, and a contractor system that already lets him hire flexibly.
[C1.5] India's states that raised retrenchment thresholds to 300 workers saw no surge in formalization or mid-sized firms. They saw more contract workers inside larger factories, less training, shorter tenures.
[C1.6] That is the real exchange on offer: destroy protections for the one small cohort that has them, in return for a promise to 90 percent that the state has no capacity to keep. Protect workers, not jobs, sounds good until you realize it means unprotecting the only jobs with any protection at all, while workers churn through precarious spells with no income insurance.
[C1.7] Build social protection directly. Don't sell it against the right to be fired cheaply.
