[P1.1] Every payment a creator receives from a streaming platform comes from a formula they cannot see, applied to numbers they cannot check, reported in a format no one else uses. That is the status quo this motion ends. The case rests on one principle: if your pay depends on data, you have a right to see that data in a form you can verify and compare.
[P1.2] First, the information asymmetry is total. A songwriter paid fractions of a cent per stream cannot tell whether a low check reflects a small audience, a pro-rata pool diluted by fraud, or an accounting error three intermediaries upstream. A showrunner whose series is "underperforming" cannot test that claim, because the platform alone holds the viewing numbers. Streaming services also need some shows to look cheap: flat buyouts replaced back-end residuals partly because success became invisible.
[P1.3] No other market lets one party set the price, count the units, and grade its own homework.
[P1.4] Second, standardization is what makes transparency real. Platforms already release selective data when it flatters them: a top-ten list, a hit's hours-viewed figure. Selective disclosure is marketing, not accountability. A common format means a creator can compare offers across services, a manager can spot anomalies, and an auditor can reconcile statements without reverse-engineering a proprietary dashboard.
[P1.5] Think of standardized nutrition labels. They did not force companies to reveal recipes. They made products comparable.
[P1.6] Third, this is proven and workable. Book publishers have long sent royalty statements. Europe's copyright rules already include transparency obligations toward authors and performers. The Hollywood writers' strike ended with platforms agreeing to share streaming viewership data with the guild.
[P1.7] The sky did not fall. The only open question is whether that protection belongs solely to groups with enough leverage to shut down an industry, or to every creator.

[C1.8] Royalty statements and guild concessions do not justify a universal requirement. Unit-times-rate accounting is simple.
