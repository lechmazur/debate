[P1.1] Start with the asymmetry. A parking minimum tells a developer: build at least this much car storage even if tenants would pay less without it. A maximum says: build no more than this, but less if you can. The first mandates car infrastructure; the second merely guards against excess.
[P1.2] Even where nearly everyone drives today, that mandate is the bigger mistake, because it hardens car dependence into the built environment and charges everyone for it.
[P1.3] Car-dependent regions are not parking monoliths. A one-size-fits-all minimum forces a 40-unit apartment on a bus route to provide the same stalls as one at the edge of town. The result is higher rents, wasted land, and fewer housing types. Structured parking can cost tens of thousands per space; surface lots consume land that could hold homes or customers.
[P1.4] Bundled parking makes a tenant who cannot afford a car still pay for stalls.
[P1.5] A maximum does not abolish parking. In a car-dependent suburb, a developer who knows residents will drive will still build it, because the market demands it. If demand is weaker—senior housing, smaller units, a job-center location—the developer can build less, and savings appear as lower rents or a project that gets built at all. Government should curb the externalities of excess asphalt—stormwater, heat, induced traffic—not guarantee a space for every theoretical peak.
[P1.6] The real fear is spillover onto neighborhood streets. That is visible and fixable: price curb parking, use residential permits, allow shared parking, or unbundle spaces. The damage from minimums is slow and hidden—empty asphalt, inflated housing costs, locked-in driving. In car country, crowded curbs are not proof that more private parking is needed; they are a reason to manage public space better.
[P1.7] The choice is not parking versus no parking; it is compelling parking versus permitting it up to a sensible ceiling. Maximums let communities build for trips people actually make, not trips a formula imagined.

[C1.4] Proponents claim markets will deliver adequate parking because developers know their tenants. But developers deliver what is profitable in the short term, not what is socially necessary. [C1.6] The alternative is not "less parking and better transit"; it is less parking and a measurable decline in quality of life for everyone within a half-mile radius.
[C1.7] We should not gamble neighborhood livability on the hope that developers will voluntarily build what their tenants need. Keep the floor. Protect the streets.
