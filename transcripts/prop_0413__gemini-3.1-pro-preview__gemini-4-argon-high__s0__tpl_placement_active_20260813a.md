When an adoptee reaches adulthood, the state has no compelling interest in withholding their original birth certificate or identifying information. This is a matter of basic equality and autonomy.
[P1.2] Beyond the psychological harm of an erased identity, the status quo poses severe physical risks. Sealed records sever individuals from their family medical history. In modern healthcare, preventative medicine relies heavily on genetic markers, such as screening for early-onset heart disease, autoimmune disorders, or hereditary cancers. Because they lack access to their records, adoptees are forced to guess about their health risks, often discovering genetic conditions only after symptoms appear.
[P1.3] A legal right to access this information is a medical necessity.
[P1.4] Opponents will inevitably point to the biological parents' right to privacy. But we must distinguish between privacy and state-guaranteed anonymity. [P1.5] However, they do not have a fundamental right to erase another human being's factual origins from the state record.
[P1.6] Historically, the practice of sealing adoption records was driven by the societal stigma surrounding out-of-wedlock births. [P1.7] Adult adoptees possess the same capacity to navigate complex family boundaries and respect personal privacy as anyone else. Granting them access to their own records restores their dignity, protects their health, and returns to them the most basic story of all: their own.
