[P1.1] The United States spends roughly twice as much per person on healthcare as countries like the UK, Canada, or Australia, yet lands near the bottom of wealthy nations on life expectancy and near the top on preventable deaths. That single comparison is the spine of this debate: more private insurance, more money spent, worse results. The proposition isn't that private systems deliver zero value — it's that public systems deliver more health per dollar. That's a claim about efficiency, and efficiency is where private insurance structurally loses.
[P1.2] Three mechanisms explain why.
[P1.3] First, administrative overhead. A multi-payer private system requires every hospital and doctor to negotiate, bill, and fight claims with dozens of different insurers, each with its own forms, rules, and denial processes. Single-payer or heavily public systems collapse that into one payer, one fee schedule, one set of rules. That difference isn't theoretical — administrative costs in the US system run several times higher as a share of spending than in Canada or the UK, and none of that spending buys a single extra vaccine or surgery.
[P1.4] Second, bargaining power. A public payer covering an entire population can negotiate drug and procedure prices the way a single large buyer always can — this is why Americans routinely pay multiples of what Canadians or Europeans pay for the same patented drugs. Fragmented private insurers negotiate separately and weakly, and providers extract higher prices from a system with no unified countervailing force.
[P1.5] Third, universality itself is an efficiency mechanism. Preventive care and primary care are cheap; emergency treatment of advanced disease is expensive. When private insurance leaves people uninsured or underinsured, those people defer care until it becomes a costly ER visit or a hospitalization. Public systems that guarantee coverage push spending toward the cheap end of the curve and away from the expensive end.
[P1.6] Put together: lower overhead, lower unit prices, and earlier intervention. That's not an ideological claim about government being good — it's a claim about what happens when you remove the deadweight losses that fragmented, profit-driven insurance [clipped]

[C1.1] The United States is an outlier, not a test of private insurance. Using its fragmented, employer-tied, fee-for-service system to condemn all predominantly private insurance confuses one designed market with the model itself.
[C1.2] Look instead at systems that rely on competing private insurers under universal rules: Switzerland, the Netherlands, and Germany’s sickness-fund model. They achieve universal coverage, short waits, high patient satisfaction, and strong outcomes on measures like cancer survival and avoidable mortality — outperforming the UK and Canada — without a single payer. If private insurance were structurally inefficient, those systems should be expensive failures. They are top performers, which breaks the link between public financing and value.
[C1.3] The spending comparison also misleads. Public systems look cheap because they suppress what they buy: capped budgets, rationed access, queues for elective surgery and diagnostics, slower uptake of drugs and technology. A hip replacement delivered in three weeks and one delivered after a year of pain, lost work, and worsening mobility are not the same outcome, even if the second appears cheaper on a ledger. When waiting time, foregone treatment, and patients paying privately to escape queues are counted, the efficiency gap shrinks.
[C1.4] Nor do life-expectancy gaps isolate healthcare. The United States carries higher burdens from obesity, opioid deaths, homicide, and road fatalities, and countries differ in inequality, diet, and demographics. Those factors move population mortality even with excellent care. On outcomes more directly tied to treatment — timely cancer care, cardiac intervention, specialist access — private and mixed systems frequently lead, while public systems struggle with delayed diagnosis and capacity shortages.
[C1.5] Finally, low public prices are not efficiency. Monopsony purchasing can drive underinvestment, staff shortages, and facilities, with costs reappearing as queues and deteriorating quality. Competing insurers have incentives to experiment with prevention, care coordination, networks, and payment models that reward results. That dynamic productivity — not squeezing this year’s price tag — is what delivers health per dollar over time.
