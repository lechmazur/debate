[P1.1] The United States is buying too few, too expensive weapons to survive a war of attrition. A significant shift toward cheaper attritable systems — drones, loitering munitions, unmanned vessels, and expendable sensors designed to be risked and replaced — is not a downgrade. It is how to restore mass, resilience, and a sustainable cost curve.
[P1.2] Exquisite platforms concentrate enormous capability — and enormous loss — in a handful of hulls and airframes. Lose one large warship, stealth aircraft squadron, or satellite, and years of production and billions of dollars vanish with no wartime replacement. An adversary does not need to match that sophistication. It needs enough missiles and drones to saturate defenses and force the United States to spend scarce interceptors and irreplaceable platforms against expendable threats.
[P1.3] That exchange favors the attacker.
[P1.4] Attritable systems reverse the logic. Fielded by the hundreds or thousands, they can scout, jam, deceive, strike, and absorb fire while crewed platforms stay at more survivable range. Their loss hurts far less than their absence would. Distributed mass also complicates enemy targeting: there is no single exquisite node whose destruction collapses the operation.
[P1.5] Ukraine has shown how adapted drones and unmanned systems can deny movement, destroy armor, and impose costs far above their price. A Pacific conflict against a major industrial power would magnify the need for numbers the current force cannot provide.
[P1.6] They are also the only part of the arsenal that can be built and improved at wartime speed. Exquisite programs take decades, suffer chronic delays, and can be produced by only a few yards and lines. Attritable systems use modular designs and commercial components, invite competition from many firms, and can iterate in months based on combat feedback. That means surge capacity before a crisis and replenishment during one.
[P1.7] Without that mass, exquisite platforms will not survive long enough to win.
