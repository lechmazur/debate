[P1.1] A university that bans disclosed, accountable AI drafting is not protecting rigor; it is grading students on their ability to hide their tools. Professional work already works this way: lawyers certify assisted filings, engineers ship assisted code, analysts model data and then defend it. The professional norm is transparent, accountable use of powerful aids. The motion aligns coursework with that reality, while keeping the student fully responsible for accuracy and reasoning.
[P1.2] The clash is about what coursework is for. If the goal were unaided prose generation, CON would have a case. But universities should certify competent thinkers who use the best tools responsibly, verify claims, and reason well. Because the student stays accountable, disclosed AI drafting raises the premium on critical reading, source checking, and logical defense.
[P1.3] It assesses the capacities we claim to value, and it forces the process into the open where teachers can actually teach verification.
[P1.4] Prohibition does not create purity; it creates concealment. A ban with no disclosure requirement simply pushes use underground, where errors go uncorrected and misconduct goes unseen. The motion's conditions reverse that: disclosure makes assistance visible, and accountability makes the student own the result. That is like allowing calculators in statistics instead of pretending they do not exist; the safeguard is not prohibition, it is disclosure plus defense.
[P1.5] Consider a policy memo. A student uses AI to produce a rough outline, then must verify every statistic, repair weak reasoning, and defend the argument orally. The tool becomes a prompt for harder thinking, not a substitute for it.
[P1.6] Equity agrees. For non-native speakers, disabled students, and first-generation students, AI drafting support levels a field tilted toward those who can afford human editors. Disclosure keeps it honest; accountability keeps the student responsible.
[P1.7] Allow disclosed, accountable AI drafting in most coursework. Judge the thinker, not the solitude.

[C1.3] A student can fact-check a statistic without ever learning how to build an argument that needs statistics.
[C1.4] Disclosure does not fix this, and accountability is largely fiction. A disclosure line tells a professor a tool was used, not which judgments were the student's. [C1.5] Credentials collapse: an A certifies a good editor of machine prose, not a clear thinker.
[C1.6] The professional analogy gets the order backwards. Lawyers and engineers may certify assisted work precisely because they first learned to draft, calculate, and code without assistance. Coursework is training, not production.
[C1.7] Nor will permission end concealment; it will perfect it. Once machine phrasing is legitimate raw material, no instructor can reliably separate heavy reliance from light editing, and pressure will push every student toward heavier use to keep up.
[C1.8] Judge the thinker, yes — which is why we must require the thinker to do the drafting.
