[P1.1] A constitutional court earns its authority from the public's belief that its membership reflects something more principled than an actuarial table. Life tenure fails that test. Who sits on the court today depends on which justice died in which year, who happened to be president, and whether an aging justice timed retirement under a friendly government. That is not a democratic pedigree; it is a lottery with partisan payouts.
[P1.2] In the United States, one president may appoint no justices and the next three, and a single seat now carries thirty years of consequence, which is precisely why every vacancy has become total war.
[P1.3] Fixed, staggered, nonrenewable terms fix the input problem. When each elected government fills a predictable number of seats, the court's composition tracks election results over time, gradually and without rancor. Vacancies stop being emergencies. The incentive to nominate the youngest plausible candidate disappears.
[P1.4] And losers accept rulings more readily when they know their turn comes through the ballot rather than through a funeral.
[P1.5] The independence objection sounds grave but dissolves on inspection. A judge is compromised by hoping for something from politicians: reappointment, promotion, a lucrative post-court job. A long nonrenewable term with a guaranteed pension and a post-service bar removes those hopes more completely than life tenure does, because there is nothing left to seek. Germany's constitutional court serves twelve-year nonrenewable terms and is among the most respected in the world; France, Italy, Spain, and South Africa all use fixed terms, and none is regarded as a government lapdog.
[P1.6] Most democracies are already running this experiment, and the predicted collapse of independence has not appeared.
[P1.7] Life tenure, meanwhile, corrodes independence from the inside: justices time departures to secure like-minded successors, which is partisan behavior wearing a robe.
[P1.8] So the ledger reads: a structural, observable gain in legitimacy against a hypothetical independence cost that ordinary design features already neutralize. The motion asks which effect is larger. Only one of them is real.

By tying seats to an electoral clock [P1.3], fixed terms formalize the idea that judges are partisan delegates sent to execute a platform on a schedule. That does not cure a legitimacy crisis; it codifies it.
[C1.3] Meanwhile, the damage to judicial independence is severe and structural. Proposing a "post-service bar" [P1.5] ignores reality: a free society cannot place a sixty-year-old former justice under professional house arrest. [C1.4] Only life tenure guarantees that the court is a jurist’s final destination, leaving no future patron left to please.
[C1.5] Moreover, independence is institutional, not just personal. [C1.6] Nor do European examples [P1.5] rescue the proposition. Germany’s court commands respect because appointments require a legislative supermajority that forces cross-party compromise, not because of its twelve-year clock. [C1.7] Fixed terms sacrifice the terminal insulation that makes judges independent, offering in return only the legitimacy of a partisan pendulum.
