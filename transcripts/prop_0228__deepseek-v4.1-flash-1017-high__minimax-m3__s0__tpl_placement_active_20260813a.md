[P1.1] The gig economy’s central trick is not flexibility; it is cost-shifting. A delivery courier or ride-hail driver is often ranked, dispatched, priced, and disciplined by an algorithm, cannot negotiate the fare, and can be deactivated for refusing too many jobs. That is not an independent business selling a product to many clients. It is work under control, with the risks of employment pushed onto the worker and the public.
[P1.2] Reclassifying most such workers as employees by default simply makes the legal label match the economic reality.
[P1.3] The consequences are concrete: injured workers with no sick pay, unpaid waiting time, no bargaining power, and platforms externalizing safety and social-insurance costs. Employee status by default supplies minimum wage, social insurance, safety duties, and liability—not because every gig is identical, but because the burden should fall on the party that controls the work.
[P1.4] The narrow exemption is what keeps this proposal honest. Genuine freelancers who set their own prices, serve multiple clients, refuse work without penalty, and bear real profit and loss remain independent. The test becomes control and dependency, not whatever label appears in a click-through contract.
[P1.5] The likely objection is flexibility. But flexibility and employment are not opposites. Part-time work, variable hours, and multi-platform participation can all be accommodated under employment law. What changes is that flexibility is no longer financed by denying basic protections.
[P1.6] If a platform cannot survive while paying minimum standards, its model depends on a subsidy from workers and taxpayers.
[P1.7] This matters especially across Europe and Asia, where aging populations and incomplete social insurance make misclassification a fiscal and human drain. “Most” is calibrated: it targets firms whose algorithmic control makes employee status appropriate, while leaving genuine freelancers exempt. The question is not whether gig work should exist, but whether it should exist as a loophole. Default employee status closes it.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a genuine scandal for a universal solution. A courier denied accident insurance or minimum pay may deserve employee-like protection. But that does not establish that “most” gig workers, across countries and occupations, are employees, nor that a binary default is the right mechanism. Reclassification should follow the actual relationship; it cannot make the relationship more equal by relabeling it.
[C1.2] PRO’s own test exposes the problem. Control, dependency, and ability to refuse work are relevant, but not self-executing. Platforms set prices, rank, and monitor for reasons that also govern ordinary service markets; genuine freelancers may accept platform terms without becoming employees; and employees may have substantial discretion. The proposed exemption for “genuine freelancers” therefore turns on the very question at issue and invites dispute over who bears the burden of proof.
[C1.3] Intermediate cases—dependent contractors, part-time platform teachers, micro-entrepreneurs who use one marketplace—are forced into a binary.
[C1.4] A continent-wide default also has costs beyond administrative error. It can price marginal work out of the market, removing the low-risk, flexible entry point that makes platforms attractive to students, caregivers, migrants, and people between jobs. Firms can respond by hiding behind staffing intermediaries, nominal cooperatives, or voucher systems; then nominal reclassification changes little for the worker. Conversely, blanket employee status can deny real entrepreneurs access to business protections and make their platform participation legally unstable.
[C1.5] Neither outcome delivers the wage, safety, and insurance floor PRO wants.
[C1.6] Europe and Asia are not one labor market: enforcement capacity, social-insurance systems, informality, and platform regulation differ sharply. A universal rule would produce divergent results without shared administrative capacity.
[C1.7] The defensible alternative is targeted, rebuttable presumptions based on control, dependence, investment, and risk, together with enforceable baseline rights and portable social insurance. That can protect dependent platform workers without treating every app-mediated occupation as employment. Some gig workers should plainly be employees. That concession defeats—not supports—the motion’s sweeping presumption.
