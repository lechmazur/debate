[P1.1] The default on a phone is not a neutral convenience. On the two operating systems that dominate mobile computing, it is a distribution channel no rival can buy. When Apple or Google presets its own wallet, browser, or AI assistant, it converts control of the operating system into control of the next market—without having to win that market on the merits.
[P1.2] People do not comparison-shop at setup. They tap through. The option that appears first captures the habit, the data, and the network effects. A safer wallet, a faster browser, or a better assistant never gets a fair test if the operating system has already chosen its sibling.
[P1.3] That is not revealed preference. It is engineered inertia.
[P1.4] The conflict is structural. The firm that designs the setup flow also sells the product that flow recommends. It can bury a rival one menu deeper, attach warnings to leaving, and make its own option the only one that feels native. Desktop history already showed what follows when a platform bundles its browser into the starting position: rivals stall, and users pay in quality, privacy, and price.
[P1.5] These are not trivial add-ons. A default wallet steers payments. A default browser steers search, ads, and tracking. A default assistant will steer queries, purchases, and, soon, nearly every other app.
[P1.6] Self-preferencing at that layer concentrates data and bargaining power in two firms and starves everyone else.
[P1.7] The remedy is narrow. It does not ban Apple Pay, Safari, Chrome, or Gemini, and it does not stop anyone from choosing them. It only forbids the dominant provider from printing its own name on the ballot before the election. Choice screens and equal placement are enough.
[P1.8] If those products are better, they will still win. If they win only because refusal was made inconvenient, they should not.
[P1.9] Dominant platforms should compete downstream the way everyone else must: by being chosen, not by being preselected.

[C1.2] Take the wallet. A default payment app is not a bookmark; it is bound to secure hardware, biometrics, tokenized card credentials, and a defined fraud-liability chain. That integration is exactly why tap-to-pay is safer than handing a plastic card to a stranger. Mandate equal placement for wallets without that binding and you do not get competition — you get phishing surface, fragmented liability, and breaches the phone maker gets blamed for.
[C1.3] Where is the consumer in that trade?
[C1.4] Take the assistant. That category is barely formed. Barring a phone maker from pairing its own assistant with its own silicon and privacy stack hands equal default access to rivals that must then be granted deep permission to messages, location, and apps to feel native at all. The motion's "level playing field" is really a data-access and integration mandate dressed up as neutrality.
[C1.5] The load-bearing claim on the other side is that defaults seal markets. It is the weakest link in the case. Rival browsers have taken large followings despite preinstallation. Chrome conquered the desktop from a worse starting position than the bundled incumbent it displaced.
[C1.6] Messaging and video apps became dominant with no default slot whatsoever. A default is a nudge, not a moat; installation takes seconds and users switch for a better product.
[C1.7] Finally, the motion is a blanket ban spanning three markets with entirely different competitive conditions. It forecloses integration benefits even where no rival is harmed and no consumer is trapped. The other side must show welfare rises net of that loss. A slogan about ballot boxes cannot carry that burden.
