[P1.1] Access is not an instruction to prescribe. It means that a minor diagnosed with gender dysphoria can receive treatment when parents and qualified clinicians judge it appropriate, with the young person meaningfully involved. The strongest reason to support that proposition is simple: refusing treatment is also a consequential medical decision, not a neutral escape from risk.
[P1.2] Puberty proceeds while adults deliberate. A deepening voice or developing breasts can become lasting sources of distress for an adolescent with persistent gender dysphoria. Counseling can help someone understand and manage that distress, but it cannot prevent those physical changes. Requiring every patient to wait until adulthood therefore imposes an outcome that some patients, families, and clinicians have good reason to avoid.
[P1.3] These treatments must be distinguished. Puberty blockers suppress pubertal progression; their use requires attention to bone health, development, and uncertainties about longer-term outcomes. Hormone therapy produces physical changes, some lasting, and requires explicit discussion of fertility and other risks. Those differences justify different thresholds, age-appropriate assessment, informed consent and assent, and ongoing monitoring—not treating every intervention as equally appropriate, or equally unacceptable.
[P1.4] For appropriately selected adolescents, treatment can reduce distress associated with unwanted physical development and help daily functioning. That benefit is not guaranteed, and the evidence has important limitations. But medicine does not ordinarily demand certainty before allowing individualized care. It asks whether the likely benefits outweigh the risks for this patient, compared with realistic alternatives.
[P1.5] Coexisting mental-health concerns, persistence of dysphoria, maturity, and family support belong in that assessment.
[P1.6] Parental agreement and clinical expertise are not infallible; they provide an accountable framework for decisions that cannot be made responsibly by age alone. Denying access overrides that framework even where the case for treatment is strong. Protecting minors means taking both treatment risks and the risks of unwanted puberty seriously. Carefully governed access does that; categorical exclusion does not.
