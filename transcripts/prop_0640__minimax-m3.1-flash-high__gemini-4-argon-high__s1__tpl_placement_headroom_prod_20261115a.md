[P1.1] Agricultural land is not ordinary real estate. It is finite, geographically fixed, and increasingly exposed to drought, flooding, and volatile prices. Non-farming institutions can buy large tracts without operating them or depending on the surrounding community for decades. When ownership detaches from farming, resilience erodes: fields may be consolidated, soil and water stewardship weakened, and local farmers priced out of land they have cultivated for generations.
[P1.2] This is not protectionism for one business model; it is protection of the productive base on which every model depends. A tenant farm can be efficient while the surrounding economy loses farms, workers, and the local circulation of money that keeps rural communities alive. Food-system resilience requires not merely maximum output in good years, but diverse farms and local institutions able to respond when one crop, input supply, or market fails.
[P1.3] A cap directly addresses ownership concentration. By reserving a substantial share for farming interests, governments prevent financial buyers from capturing the land market and outbidding those who actually cultivate it. This is not a ban on productive enterprise or foreign capital; it is a rule about who may own an irreplaceable asset. Institutional investors can continue providing capital and finance without acquiring control of the land itself.
[P1.4] Stronger tenancy or competition rules are insufficient because they leave the asset with owners whose incentives may not extend beyond financial returns. A cap changes that baseline while preserving leasing and farm management. Governments can design it carefully, with transparent thresholds and exceptions for genuine agricultural entities.
[P1.5] The choice is not “families versus efficiency.” It is whether financial accumulation should determine who controls the land that feeds society. A measured cap preserves agricultural capacity and rural communities by preventing institutional financial owners from displacing the farmers and communities that sustain production.

Telling investors to provide "finance without acquiring control" [P1.3] simply means forcing farmers into deeper debt, where rigid loan payments must be met even when a harvest fails.
[C1.2] By contrast, selling land and leasing it frees up capital for modern equipment, drought-resistant infrastructure, and operations. Furthermore, a cap artificially restricts the buyer pool, depressing farmland values. Because land equity is both the primary retirement nest egg for aging rural families and the collateral active farmers use to secure seasonal credit, an ownership cap actively destroys rural wealth.
[C1.3] The claim that financial owners weaken soil and water stewardship [P1.1] gets the incentives backward. Pension funds and endowments have multi-decade horizons; their asset's value depends entirely on long-term soil fertility and water security. [C1.4] Finally, capping "non-farming" owners while exempting "genuine agricultural entities" [P1.4] creates a perverse loophole. Barred from passively owning land and leasing it to local family farmers, capital will simply restructure into massive, vertically integrated operating corporations that farm the land directly. Instead of a pension fund acting as a stable landlord for local tenants, communities get corporate mega-farms that displace local operators entirely. Ownership caps are a blunt weapon aimed at the wrong target.
[C1.5] If governments want to protect water, soil, and rural labor, they must regulate agricultural practices and tenancy terms directly, because aquifer depletion hurts a community the same way regardless of whose name is on the deed.

[P2.1] The opponent’s central claim—that patient capital requires land ownership to finance farming—confuses ownership with funding. Institutional investors can provide loans, guarantees, equipment finance, and minority stakes without buying the deeds. Selling land and leasing it back does not create patient stewardship; it merely determines who captures the land’s appreciation while another party bears the farming risks. A cap can preserve leasing and professional management while denying non-farming institutions control of the productive asset.
[P2.2] The wealth argument also proves too much. If institutional acquisition necessarily enriches rural families, then the relevant policy would be to facilitate land sales to any buyer, not to restrict ownership concentration. Yet farmland values are not a substitute for farm viability: a high-priced land market can exclude the very farmers who generate local employment and maintain the surrounding economy. Transparent exemptions and grandfathering can protect legitimate holdings without allowing expansion of institutional control.
[P2.3] Institutional horizons do not guarantee good land use. A pension fund may hold land for decades, but decisions about tenancy, consolidation, infrastructure, and eventual sale remain governed by financial interests rather than community dependence. The opponent contrasts patient landlords with desperate operators, but the choice is not ownership versus debt. Farmers need patient lenders; they do not need patient owners with power to displace them.
[P2.4] Nor does direct regulation solve concentration. Rules on irrigation or tenancy can limit specific harms while leaving land ownership, market power, and community decline intact. For example, an aquifer can be protected even if a distant institution owns the surrounding acreage; that protection does not prevent the institution from reshaping local farming opportunities.
[P2.5] Finally, the alleged corporate workaround concedes the problem. If capital reorganizes to evade a cap, governments can define and enforce “non-farming” by control and purpose rather than merely legal form. Operating corporations would also remain subject to direct environmental and labor standards. A well-designed ownership cap is not a substitute for farm regulation; it complements regulation by addressing who controls the land those rules govern.

[P5.1] The decisive issue is control of land, and the opponent’s objection supports a cap. A cap is not a ban: institutional investors may still own land within the permitted share, grandfather holdings, and lease acreage to local farmers. Their claim that the policy eliminates leaseback landlords [C4.1] therefore misstates the mechanism. It limits how much land can migrate into financial ownership without ending institutional leasing.
[P5.2] Direct rules, by contrast, cannot reverse who makes decisions about consolidation, tenure, investment, and sale. An institution can comply with soil, water, labor, and competition rules while acquiring a controlling share of farmland, raising rents, shortening leases, or selling for the highest financial return. Those decisions can transmit risk away from communities bearing the consequences.
[P5.3] A cap changes that default by keeping control more closely aligned with farming interests. During climate and market shocks, resilience depends on diverse operators and communities retaining a stake in land decisions, not merely technically compliant managers.
[P5.4] The opponent treats every land-value cost as decisive while ignoring the greater loss: productive capacity and rural livelihoods becoming dependent on financial exit. A cap accepts some asset-price trade-off to preserve who controls agriculture. That is the clearest reason to adopt the proposition.
