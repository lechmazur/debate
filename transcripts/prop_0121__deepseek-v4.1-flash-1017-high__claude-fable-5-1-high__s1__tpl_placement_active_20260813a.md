[P1.1] At twenty-two, you can vote, sign a lease, consent to surgery, and be trusted with a job—but under current practice you may not be trusted to see a therapist without a parent finding out. That contradiction is the heart of this debate. Privacy here does not mean cutting families out forever. It means the young adult controls when and how family is involved, rather than an insurance form or a clinic receptionist making that decision by default.
[P1.2] Confidentiality is not a bureaucratic nicety; it is part of treatment. Patients censor themselves when they fear disclosure, especially around suicidal thoughts, sexual violence, substance use, LGBTQ identity, or family conflict. A young adult who knows a claim will generate an explanation of benefits, or that a clinic may call the family home, often does the rational thing: skips care or hides the most important details. That is not protection.
[P1.3] It is preventable harm.
[P1.4] The opposition will warn that parents need to know in order to keep young adults safe. But automatic notification is the wrong instrument. It replaces clinical judgment with blanket disclosure. Clinicians can already act when someone is in imminent danger; that is very different from billing systems and front desks deciding by default that a parent must see the visit.
[P1.5] Safety is best served by getting the person into care, not by making disclosure the price of entry.
[P1.6] Young adulthood is a transition, not a switch. Many young adults are still on family insurance or financially entangled with parents. Privacy is precisely what lets them seek help before a crisis becomes visible to everyone. The question is which default produces more care and less harm.
[P1.7] A default of disclosure chills help-seeking; a default of privacy invites it, with targeted safety exceptions. Protect the visit, not the paperwork.

[C1.1] Nobody disputes that a twenty-two-year-old is an adult. The question is who is paying for the visit and who catches the patient when treatment goes wrong. The motion answers both badly.
[C1.2] Start with money. The explanation of benefits exists because someone bought the policy and is entitled to see what it is spent on. If a young adult wants true privacy, that option already exists: pay out of pocket, use a sliding-scale clinic, use free campus counseling, none of which generates a statement to anyone. What this motion actually demands is something stranger: that parents keep funding coverage while being forbidden from knowing it is used.
[C1.3] Autonomy that is financed by someone else and concealed from them is not independence. It is dependence with a gag order.
[C1.4] Now the harder point. Ages eighteen to twenty-five carry some of the highest suicide risk and the worst treatment dropout of any group. The clinician sees the patient for fifty minutes a week. The parent sees the empty pill bottle, the missed shifts, the car keys at two in the morning.
[C1.5] My opponent concedes clinicians may act in "imminent danger" [P1.4], but imminence is only visible to someone in the room. The patient who quietly stops attending after session three is invisible to the therapist and invisible to the family. Secrecy severs the only safety net that operates between appointments.
[C1.6] Consider a twenty-year-old with emerging bipolar disorder. He is diagnosed, starts a mood stabilizer, feels better, stops. His parents watch him unravel over the next months with no frame for what they are seeing, because the one fact that would explain it was hidden by design. Family involvement is not an intrusion on treatment for conditions like this; it is the treatment.
[C1.7] Finally, depression and shame distort judgment. Concealment is often a symptom, not a preference. A default that ratifies isolation at the exact moment a person is least able to ask for help does not protect the visit. It protects the illness.

[P2.1] The billing argument mistakes a ledger for a medical record. A parent may be entitled to know what a policy costs; that does not entitle them to an itemized window into a twenty-two-year-old’s therapy. Insurance can pay without automatically announcing the visit—confidential billing, sensitive-service handling, direct patient billing, aggregate statements. The motion does not demand free secret treatment; it demands that payment systems not be the disclosure system.
[P2.2] And “pay out of pocket” is not a real choice for the young adult on a family plan with no savings. Telling someone they may have privacy if they can afford it is not autonomy; it is a means test for safety.
[P2.3] The safety argument is where CON’s case collapses. The parent sees what the clinician cannot—true for some families, and irrelevant as a default for all. For a young adult with controlling or abusive parents, or one whose family rejects their identity, automatic notification is not a safety net; it is the reason to stay silent. The bipolar example proves too much: if the first visit automatically reaches parents, the twenty-year-old may never get diagnosed, never start the mood stabilizer, never have a clinician to coordinate family involvement with.
[P2.4] Family involvement is valuable—when it is a clinical decision made with the patient, not a billing byproduct.
[P2.5] CON calls concealment a symptom. Sometimes. But forced disclosure does not treat shame; it punishes it with exposure and teaches the patient to lie or disappear. Privacy is what lets the clinician hear the truth early enough to act.
[P2.6] The right comparison is not “secret patient versus watched patient.” It is “patient in care with control over disclosure versus patient outside care because disclosure was the price.” The first default produces more treatment, more honest sessions, and more chances for a clinician to bring family in safely. The second protects paperwork and calls it protection.
