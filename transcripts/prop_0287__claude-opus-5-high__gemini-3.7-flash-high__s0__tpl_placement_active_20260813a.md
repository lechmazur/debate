[P1.1] Start with the strongest fact in this debate: almost every constitutional court designed since 1945 uses fixed terms. Germany's Federal Constitutional Court sits for twelve years, non-renewable. Italy, Spain, South Africa, and most of Latin America and Eastern Europe follow variants of the same design. If fixed terms gutted judicial independence, these courts would be pliant instruments of their governments.
[P1.2] They are not. Germany's court routinely strikes down the legislation of the parties that appointed its members. The life-tenure model is the outlier, not the benchmark.
[P1.3] The reason is simple, and it is the hinge of this debate: independence is threatened by *dependence on a future decision-maker*, not by a calendar. A judge who wants reappointment can be bought. A judge facing removal can be bribed or bullied. But a judge serving one long, non-renewable, unrenewable-by-anyone term has nothing to gain from pleasing anyone from the day of the oath.
[P1.4] Fixed terms done properly — long, staggered, single-term — surrender none of that protection. My opponent must show what a justice in year fourteen of eighteen is afraid of.
[P1.5] Against that near-zero cost sits a large legitimacy gain. Under life tenure, a president's influence over constitutional law is set by the actuarial tables. One president gets three appointments in four years; another gets none in four. Justices time their retirements to hand their seat to a friendly successor — which is itself a partisan act performed by someone who claims to be above partisanship.
[P1.6] Regular, predictable vacancies replace this lottery with something a citizen can recognize as democratic: elections have proportionate, foreseeable consequences for the court.
[P1.7] And here is the part CON should worry about most. Courts have no army and no purse; they have consent. When a court's composition looks like a fluke of mortality, the losing side stops seeing a tribunal and starts seeing a captured prize — and reaches for court-packing, jurisdiction-stripping, defiance. Fixed terms lower the stakes of every vacancy.
[P1.8] That doesn't endanger independence. That is how independence survives.

[C1.2] Once you impose fixed terms, judges inevitably contemplate their next act—whether at corporate law firms, international bodies, political appointments, or university leadership. [C1.3] Second, fixed terms do not de-escalate confirmation wars; they institutionalize and schedule them. By guaranteeing a regular quota of vacancies per presidential term, the court is converted into an explicit, scheduled prize of electoral politics. [C1.4] Third, PRO confuses democratic legitimacy with majoritarian synchronization. A constitutional court is deliberately designed to be counter-majoritarian—a check on the democratic majority, not an echo of it. If court membership mechanically rotates with the political calendar, constitutional doctrine will oscillate with every shifting partisan tide. When fundamental rights and established precedents expire on a predictable conveyor belt, stare decisis collapses.
[C1.5] Citizens will no longer see an independent tribunal applying enduring law; they will see an unelected third legislative chamber enacting temporary party platforms.
[C1.6] Trading the counter-majoritarian shield of life tenure for an auditioning bench and continuous doctrinal whiplash does not save democratic legitimacy. It destroys the very judicial independence that makes constitutional review legitimate in the first place.
