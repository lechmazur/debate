[P1.1] Remote work expands choice, but it does not convert an HQ salary into a portable lifetime subsidy. When workers deliberately relocate to significantly lower-cost regions, location-adjusted pay is the fair, sustainable default rather than freezing pay to high-cost headquarters bands.
[P1.2] HQ bands already bake in extreme local pressures—housing, taxes, services, and competitive talent premiums. A worker who keeps that cash after moving to a place where rent, groceries, and childcare run a fraction as high captures a large real raise without any corresponding increase in value delivered. Colleagues still stuck in the expensive hub absorb the full cost of that lifestyle while receiving the same nominal pay for the same work. That is not equity; it is an unearned transfer from the firm and from higher-cost peers.
[P1.3] Companies cannot run infinite remote policy if every hire can demand San Francisco or New York rates while living in Lisbon, Boise, or Bangalore. Extending premium bands everywhere inflates labor costs, shrinks headcount, and eventually reverses the very remote flexibility workers prize. Location-adjusted scales let firms hire more people, keep remote options open, and still deliver competitive real purchasing power where workers actually live. The labor market has long priced location even for mobile professionals; remote status changes the office, not the underlying cost and supply realities.
[P1.4] The move itself confers massive lifestyle gains—more space, shorter commutes, lower financial stress. Expecting both those gains and full HQ cash is double-dipping. Adjusted pay preserves the genuine win: higher real living standards for employees who choose lower-cost places, and a viable business model that keeps remote work available rather than treating it as an open-ended arbitrage privilege. When the cost gap is large, pay should generally follow the place, not the old headquarters zip code.

[C1.3] Furthermore, the argument that HQ pay reflects local pressures ignores how modern businesses operate. [C1.4] Applying a geographical penalty also incentivizes invasive corporate surveillance. To enforce these pay cuts, companies must track employees' physical locations, turning a benefit of remote flexibility into a mechanism for constant monitoring.
[C1.5] Ultimately, talent is global, and value is location-agnostic. Penalizing employees for optimizing their living situations is a regressive corporate cash grab. The standard must remain equal compensation for equal contribution.
