[P1.1] Curb space is not a parking lot—it is a finite, high-value public asset that cities currently hand over for free to the highest-volume commercial users. Every day, ride-hail vehicles and delivery vans occupy prime curb space for minutes at a time, often blocking bike lanes, bus stops, and crosswalks, while private drivers circle endlessly searching for the same free spots. This is a hidden subsidy paid by the public, extracted from the public through lost time, choked traffic, and dangerous streets.
[P1.2] The alternative is dynamic curb pricing: charging commercial vehicles for the time they occupy scarce curb space, with rates that rise when demand is high and fall when it is not. This is not a tax on driving; it is a price on the use of public space. When a delivery van pays for thirty minutes at the curb, it internalizes the cost it imposes on traffic flow and pedestrian safety. When prices adjust to demand, turnover increases, ensuring that a spot is available for the next user rather than being squatted on all day.
[P1.3] Cities that have moved in this direction—applying congestion charges to ride-hail trips or dynamic pricing to commercial loading zones—see measurable drops in double-parking and circling traffic, along with new revenue streams. Those funds can repair sidewalks, expand bike lanes, and improve transit, reducing the very demand that chokes streets. Keeping curbs mostly free is not a neutral policy; it is an active choice to subsidize Amazon, Uber, and DoorDash with public infrastructure while ordinary residents pay the price in congestion and danger.
[P1.4] The proposition is simple: if curb space has value, it should have a price. Dynamic pricing transforms a chaotic free-for-all into an orderly market that serves the public interest, rather than a giveaway to the companies that profit most from clogging our streets.
