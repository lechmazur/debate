[P1.1] The 2003 invasion did not merely topple a regime; it removed the central political order in Iraq and replaced it with a prolonged security vacuum. Under Saddam Hussein, Iraq was a brutal but territorially intact state that balanced Iranian power and suppressed transnational jihadist infrastructure. After the invasion, the army and Baathist state were dissolved, creating mass unemployment among armed men and a recruitment pool for insurgency. That vacuum produced the core instability the Middle East later suffered.
[P1.2] From that vacuum came an escalatory chain. The insurgency incubated al-Qaeda in Iraq, whose sectarian atrocities against Shia civilians drove Iraq into civil war after 2006. That organization was not a pre-existing threat on that scale; it was a product of the occupation. Its successor, ISIS, seized Mosul in 2014 and erased the Iraq-Syria border, turning local disorder into regional collapse.
[P1.3] Without the invasion, neither al-Qaeda in Iraq’s rise, the Iraqi civil war, nor ISIS’s territorial state has a plausible equivalent.
[P1.4] The invasion also shifted the regional balance. By removing Iraq as a counterweight, it opened space for Iran to deepen influence over Shia militias and political factions, from Baghdad to Damascus to Beirut. Meanwhile, the war inflamed Sunni-Shia polarization and drove refugees and fighters into neighboring states, burdening Jordan, Syria, and others. The result was not one contained dictatorship but a fractured region with failed institutions, sectarian militias, and a new jihadist geography.
[P1.5] That is less stable by any reasonable standard. A contained dictatorship is not stability in any moral sense, but the motion asks about regional stability, and there the causal record is clear. The counterfactual of a contained Iraq under sanctions, however ugly, was more predictable than the spillover the invasion actually produced.

[C1.1] The proposition requires proving a counterfactual: that the Middle East as a whole would have been more stable without the invasion. PRO assumes every later conflict flowed from that decision. That is too simple for a region already divided by interstate rivalry, dictatorship, sectarian tension, and the Gulf War’s aftermath.
[C1.2] Saddam Hussein’s Iraq was odious, but not an unstable vacuum. It maintained territorial unity, suppressed armed sectarian rivals, and contained Iran through state power. The prior decades were violent—the Iran-Iraq War, the 1991 uprising, repression, sanctions, and periodic crises—but “more stable” cannot mean merely “free of conflict.” Continued rule, perhaps under containment, could have left Iraq centralized and prevented some networks that flourished after 2003. PRO offers no basis for dismissing that alternative.
[C1.3] The causal chain is overstated. Al-Qaeda existed before 2003, and jihadists had already operated from Iraq, fought in Afghanistan, and attacked the United States and embassies elsewhere. The invasion enlarged their opportunities; it did not invent the movement. Iraq’s later civil conflict arose from interacting causes: institutional collapse, disbanding the army, sectarian mobilization, and regional intervention.
[C1.4] PRO cannot assign all of those to one decision and call the result inevitable.
[C1.5] Most importantly, regional instability after 2011 cannot be booked solely to 2003. Arab uprisings, Syria’s civil war, conflicts in Libya and Yemen, and foreign intervention in Iraq and Syria each had independent histories. Even if the invasion made Iraq worse than it otherwise would have been—which is plausible—that is not the proposition. The motion asks about regional stability overall.
[C1.6] On a broad, historically realistic counterfactual, Saddam’s coercive but intact Iraq offered a more orderly framework than the fragmented order that followed. The invasion carried enormous costs, but that does not prove the region would have been more stable without it.
