[P1.1] Medicine currently allows sellers to edit science. A company can run five trials, publish the two that look good, quietly file away the three that show no benefit or worrying side effects, and then market the drug as proven. Doctors, guideline committees, and patients only see the winners. That is not scientific uncertainty; that is engineered deception with prescriptions attached.
[P1.2] The harm is not theoretical. For years the apparent effectiveness of widely prescribed antidepressants looked far stronger in journals than in the full set of trials submitted to regulators, because negative studies largely stayed hidden. Similar patterns distorted perceptions of painkillers, flu antivirals stockpiled at huge public expense, and drugs later withdrawn over safety signals that earlier unpublished data might have flagged. Each hidden trial means thousands of patients taking a drug they would not have chosen with full information.
[P1.3] Voluntary disclosure has failed because secrecy pays. Tying disclosure to market access changes the calculation: if you want the privilege of selling to our patients, you must show us what you found when you tested on patients. Companies already compile these results for regulators; publishing a summary is trivial next to the cost of development. What they call confidential commercial information is usually just evidence of limited efficacy — the exact information buyers need.
[P1.4] Finally, trial participants volunteer their bodies on the understanding they advance knowledge. Burying their contribution when inconvenient betrays that trust and corrupts future research, as scientists waste years repeating questions already answered in a drawer. No right to profit includes a right to mislead. Access to a national market is conditional on safety, manufacturing standards, and honest labeling; complete trial disclosure belongs in that same basic bargain.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes regulatory review for public deception. Regulators already examine complete trial dossiers—including every negative result—before granting market access. When they approve a drug, they have weighed hidden failures against demonstrated benefits. Patients are protected by that expert judgment, not by reading raw data.
[C1.2] Mandatory public disclosure, however, would expropriate proprietary research. Developing a new therapy requires billions in sunk costs, and negative trial data represents expensive, hard-won intellectual property. If market access requires surrendering this intelligence to the public domain, we destroy the incentive to innovate. A company that spends a decade discovering that a compound fails in Phase III has effectively paid for the entire industry's education.
[C1.3] Under this policy, competitors could free-ride on those failures, adjusting their pipelines without bearing the cost, while the original innovator faces ruined exclusivity. That is not transparency; it is expropriation that chills investment in exactly the high-risk research patients need, particularly from smaller biotech firms lacking resources to manage disclosure disputes.
[C1.4] Furthermore, raw negative data misleads without expert curation. Trials fail for many reasons—underpowering, flawed design, inappropriate patient populations. Flooding the public domain invites media alarmism and erodes physician confidence in drugs regulators have deemed safe. The proper mechanism already exists: regulators can deny market access based on undisclosed negative data without forcing companies to publish their proprietary research for competitors.
[C1.5] Transparency belongs in the regulatory review, not as a punitive condition that punishes innovation and serves up misleading data.

They need curated, peer-reviewed evidence that synthesizes all available data into actionable guidance. Mandatory public disclosure does not improve clinical decision-making; it floods the information environment with complex datasets requiring expert statistical interpretation.
[C2.2] The antidepressant distortion PRO cites is properly solved not by dumping filing cabinets into the public domain, but by strengthening peer-reviewed publication standards, registry requirements, and regulatory labeling—not by forcing companies to surrender proprietary research intelligence to competitors.
[C2.3] PRO's claim that a failed endpoint reveals nothing copyable fundamentally misunderstands pharmaceutical R&D economics. Learning that a compound failed at a specific dose in a specific patient population is precisely the strategic intelligence that saves competitors billions in development costs. It reveals which molecular mechanisms to abandon, which patient subgroups to exclude, and where the therapeutic window closes. This is not abstract "efficacy" in the pejorative sense PRO implies; it is a detailed, expensive roadmap of where not to invest.
[C2.4] Calling this merely "losing the power to present 40% as 100%" ignores that the hidden 60% represents proprietary knowledge purchased with sunk capital, knowledge that competitors can exploit without bearing any of the risk or cost.
[C2.5] The small biotech argument inverts reality. A startup's failed Phase II data is often its primary asset in acquisition talks or licensing negotiations. Mandatory public disclosure destroys that valuation before the science can be repurposed or pivoted to new indications. Meanwhile, the charge that innovation "needs hidden data" is a strawman: no one proposes hiding data from regulators, who already review complete dossiers under confidentiality.
[C2.6] The proposition demands public surrender of research intelligence as the price of market entry—a punitive tax on failure that chills exactly the high-risk, exploratory trials that drive medical progress.
