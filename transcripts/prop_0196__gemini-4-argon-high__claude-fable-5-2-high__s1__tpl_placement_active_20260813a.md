[P1.1] Every poorly insulated, fossil-fuel-dependent home built today in a wealthy country is a mistake we immediately schedule ourselves to pay a second time to fix. Requiring new residential construction to be net-zero-energy-ready at the point of building is vastly cheaper, more reliable, and fairer to residents than building obsolete homes and hoping someone tears open the walls later.
[P1.2] Consider the basic economics of construction. A net-zero-energy-ready home needs a tight thermal envelope, high-grade insulation, efficient electric heating, and the wiring capacity to add renewable generation later without an overhaul. Installing better insulation, heat pumps, and upgraded electrical panels while the framing is open and crews are already on-site adds only a modest marginal cost—one that shrinks further when mandates standardize the supply chain. Doing that same work as a retrofit years later means ripping out drywall, re-running conduit, scrapping working heating systems, and paying for standalone labor.
[P1.3] Upfront readiness pays once; retrofitting pays twice.
[P1.4] Worse, relying on future retrofits fails in practice because of severe real-world friction. Once a home is occupied, deep retrofits rarely happen at scale. Homeowners dread the disruption of living in a construction zone and rarely have liquid cash sitting around for invasive renovations. In the rental market, the split-incentive problem is fatal: landlords will not pay thousands for envelope upgrades just to lower their tenants' utility bills.
[P1.5] Every sub-standard home built today locks in decades of wasted energy and heavy peak-demand strain on the electrical grid.
[P1.6] Finally, cutting corners at construction does not make housing more affordable; it merely hides the cost. True affordability is the mortgage plus monthly utility bills and future maintenance. Allowing developers to build leaky, inefficient homes simply shifts the financial burden from the builder onto families through decades of inflated energy bills and five-figure retrofit liabilities. Wealthy nations have the capital and technical capacity to stop digging the hole deeper.
[P1.7] Build it right the first time.
