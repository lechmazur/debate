[P1.1] AI deployment is not buying office software; it is rewriting the workplace. A system that scores calls, ranks warehouse speed, generates schedules, screens applicants, or recommends discipline changes workload, privacy, pay, promotion, safety, and job security. Those are collective conditions, not private managerial preferences. Before a large employer embeds such rules in code, the people whose work and bargaining power are reorganized should negotiate through elected worker councils.
[P1.2] The duty is modest: negotiation, not a worker veto over every line of code. Worker councils would get notice, intelligible impact information, and good-faith bargaining over data, metrics, human review, appeals, redeployment, and monitoring. Workplace law commonly treats safety, discrimination, layoffs, and workplace rules as legitimate subjects of collective process. AI is a faster, less visible way of setting those terms.
[P1.3] Voluntary consultation is structurally weak. Managers decide whether, whom, and when to consult—often after the vendor contract and workflow redesign make reversal costly. Individuals lack technical information and fear being labelled obstructive. Elected councils aggregate practical knowledge, press for disclosure, and can flag scheduling models that destroy caring responsibilities or scores that punish accents, disabilities, or complex cases.
[P1.4] Large employers are the right target: one algorithmic policy can govern thousands, and they can run a process.
[P1.5] Consider a call centre adding an “AI assistant” that drafts replies, times pauses, and feeds interaction data into a performance score. Management sees efficiency; agents may see surveillance, speed-up, deskilling, and discipline by opaque error. A council can ask about data, audits, contesting flags, staffing effects, and retesting updates. These are terms of work, not technical niceties.
[P1.6] The claim that this freezes innovation gets the comparison backwards. Dynamic systems need ongoing review, not a trust-me promise. Negotiated deployment catches hidden risk and bad design before they become grievances, errors, or displacement. The law would not ban AI; it would require large employers to treat workers as governed participants rather than raw material for optimization.

Where unions already exist, establishing a separate statutory body for AI fractures collective bargaining over pay, staffing, and discipline. Tellingly, [P1.1] cites AI that “screens applicants,” yet job applicants get no vote on an incumbent council, nor do workers at smaller contractors when large firms outsource tasks to escape bargaining gridlock.
[C1.4] Finally, the concrete harms in [P1.3] and [P1.5], including accent bias, unsafe warehouse speeds, invasive monitoring, and opaque discipline, are unacceptable whether imposed by neural networks, spreadsheets, or human supervisors. Regulate abusive workplace outcomes directly regardless of the tool, rather than subjecting continuous technological adaptation to mandatory collective negotiation.

Good-faith negotiation is not consent: after notice, intelligible impact information, meetings, and a fair chance to resolve material issues, the employer may proceed, including at impasse, subject to bad-faith review. Nor does every cloud patch trigger bargaining. The duty attaches to material deployments or material changes in systems that screen, schedule, score, monitor, discipline, or allocate work; routine security fixes and minor upgrades fit a negotiated change-control protocol.
[P2.2] Continuous AI is an argument for a standing procedure, not for no procedure.
[P2.3] The vendor point [C1.2] misses the object of bargaining. Councils need not rewrite global code; they negotiate the employer’s local use of it: data inputs, thresholds, human review, appeals, staffing, redeployment, and workflow design. If management can switch on a feature or set a score that changes discipline or shifts, it can discuss that decision before lock-in. The legal line is material workplace effect, not a metaphysical fight over the word “AI.”
[P2.4] Uniform standards [C1.4] are a floor, not a substitute. Discrimination and safety laws exist, yet black-box metrics conceal how injury happens: a warehouse score that penalizes bathroom breaks, a call score that punishes complex cases, a scheduler that strips stable shifts. Regulators usually arrive after harm and need evidence; councils can obtain impact information and raise context-specific design problems before deployment. That does not bargain away rights; it makes them usable against the actual system.
[P2.5] The council objections [C1.3] misfire. Existing unions can house the elected council inside collective bargaining; non-union workplaces gain a voice where none exists. Applicants and contractors matter, but workers have stakes in data, benchmarks, pace, promotion, and displacement, and anti-avoidance rules can reach AI used to control outsourced work. The clash is not innovation versus freeze; it is bounded ex ante negotiation over material deployments versus CON’s trust-me model: wait until vendors, metrics, and discipline are embedded, then let individuals and regulators clean up.
