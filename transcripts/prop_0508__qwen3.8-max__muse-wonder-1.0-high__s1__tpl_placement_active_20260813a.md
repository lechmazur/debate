[P1.1] The central question is where the next liberalisation payoff lies. In mature trade blocs, the binding barriers are no longer the border taxes on goods; they are duplicate rules that stop a qualified professional or digital firm from selling across a frontier. A software company, auditor, engineer, or nurse should not have to re-certify, re-test, or re-establish itself in every member state merely because regulators do not trust each other’s approvals. Mutual-recognition deals attack that directly.
[P1.2] First, the economic upside is larger. Tariff cuts on industrial goods often chase the last few points of protection, while regulatory duplication can impose costs equivalent to much larger barriers, especially for services. A small consultancy or fintech startup cannot absorb parallel licensing regimes, local establishment rules, and conflicting audit requirements. If members recognize equivalent credentials and conformity assessments, firms can scale across the bloc immediately.
[P1.3] That expands competition, lowers prices for consumers, and lets smaller participants enter markets that tariff cuts never unlock.
[P1.4] Second, mutual recognition is more compatible with democratic regulation. It does not require forcing one country’s standards onto another. Members can keep their own licensing, consumer-protection, and data-safety rules, then agree that equivalent outcomes count. That is a far more legitimate way to integrate than harmonization or endless tariff bargaining.
[P1.5] It also builds trust: regulators learn which partner systems are reliable, making deeper cooperation possible.
[P1.6] Third, this is where modern blocs are judged. Workers and firms care whether an engineer can move, whether a telemedicine provider can treat patients abroad, whether digital identities and certifications are accepted. New tariff cuts mostly help traded goods; mutual recognition helps labor mobility, services productivity, and the digital economy.
[P1.7] If critics say recognition is hard or imperfect, the answer is not to retreat to easy but marginal tariff cuts. The right priority is the bottleneck. Trade blocs should spend political capital where the returns are greatest: making credentials and digital approvals portable across borders.

Even in mature blocs, protection concentrates where politics is hardest — agriculture, processed food, textiles, vehicles and green-technology inputs — with peaks well above the low average for ordinary manufactures.
[C1.2] Those remaining tariffs tax food, clothing and the equipment for decarbonisation, hurt poorer consumers most, and invite retaliation and supply-chain rerouting. Chasing only the average misses the distortion.
[C1.3] Mutual recognition, by contrast, does not abolish regulation; it asks one state to import another's judgement. For a nurse, engineer, auditor or payments platform, equivalence is not a paperwork detail. Training hours, exams, supervision, insurance, data-protection enforcement and professional discipline differ for real reasons. Recognise them wholesale and the most permissive licensing or weakest privacy enforcement can become the practical entry point to the whole bloc, while citizens who suffer harm are told accountability lies abroad.
[C1.4] That is not preserving democratic control; it is outsourcing it without a vote.
[C1.5] It is also the slower, more capturable route. A tariff cut is reciprocal and verifiable at the border. Recognition must be negotiated profession by profession and platform by platform, then monitored as curricula, cyber threats and business models change. Medical councils, bar associations and financial regulators can delay for years, and large digital firms are best placed to shape “equivalence” around their existing compliance systems.
[C1.6] Prioritising that process spends scarce negotiating capital on indeterminate promises while leaving measurable price barriers intact.
[C1.7] None of this makes credential portability worthless. It makes it secondary and conditional — after safeguards, liability and enforcement are proven comparable. First, remove the taxes every consumer still pays at the border. That is broader, faster, safer and easier for voters to verify.
