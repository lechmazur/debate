[P1.1] Young adults are old enough to vote, work, enlist, and consent to treatment, but the moment a therapist visit appears on a parent’s insurance statement, their legal adulthood collapses. The proposition protects a simple condition: if someone is mature enough to seek help, the system should not force that help to be disclosed to a parent or employer-sponsored policyholder through automatic billing notices.
[P1.2] The decisive point is that confidentiality is not a luxury; it is often the difference between getting care and avoiding it. Many young adults are financially dependent on parents, but dependence does not make disclosure safe. A college student with depression may skip counseling if a parent will receive an explanation of benefits. An explanation need not name the diagnosis to reveal the provider, date, and service—enough to trigger interrogation, punishment, or denial.
[P1.3] A young person in an abusive or controlling household may hide trauma, substance use, or sexuality precisely because disclosure could bring ejection. In mental health, delay is not neutral: untreated anxiety, depression, eating disorders, and suicidal thoughts tend to worsen, and early outpatient treatment is far easier than crisis intervention later. Privacy here is not secrecy from care; it is the doorway to care.
[P1.4] Opponents will likely stress parental concern or financial responsibility. But those interests do not justify automatic revelation. Parents can still be involved when the patient consents, and clinicians can still act when there is imminent danger. What the proposition rejects is a default that turns every appointment into a report home.
[P1.5] If we want young adults to use preventive mental-health services rather than wait for emergencies, we must give them the same privacy we give adults for other medical decisions. Access that depends on a parent’s approval is not reliable access; it is conditional access, and for the most vulnerable young adults, it is no access at all.

[C1.2] If you want private billing, carry your own policy.
[C1.3] Mental health makes parental blindness especially dangerous, not especially compassionate. Depression, eating disorders, substance use, and suicidality are not managed in a 50-minute session; they are managed in the home where the patient sleeps, eats, and stores medication. A clinician who cannot tell parents that their 19-year-old son was just started on medication with a black-box risk, or that their daughter's eating disorder requires meal supervision, is treating with one eye closed. When a crisis comes — missed doses, overdose, self-harm — the very people positioned to intervene will not even know treatment existed.
[C1.4] This matters because "young adult" often means 18 to 20, still living at home, still developing judgment, still navigating first episodes that are notoriously hard to diagnose. An isolated young patient is an easy target for open-ended talk therapy that never ends, misdiagnosis, or online pill mills. Insurance statements and parental awareness are not bureaucratic cruelty; they are the basic audit that catches errors, duplication, and exploitation.
[C1.5] Confidentiality with consent already protects real autonomy, and clinicians already can break it for imminent danger. What the proposition demands is a new default of automatic concealment, where families learn about care only after an emergency room visit. That does not build trust or prevention. It replaces supported recovery with secret-keeping, leaving the patient alone with the illness and the parents alone with the bill.
[C1.6] True independence means paying for your own privacy.

[P2.2] If the goal is oversight, consent-based disclosure and clinician judgment are far more precise than automatic insurance notices.

[P2.5] The safety argument cuts the other way. A young adult who fears disclosure is likely to delay care until symptoms become severe. For depression, suicidality, substance use, and eating disorders, early outpatient contact is usually safer than crisis-stage intervention. [P2.6] That is not protection; it is preventable escalation.
[P2.7] Finally, CON conflates parental awareness with clinical support. Serious treatment can involve families when that is safe and useful, and clinicians can act when danger is imminent. What the proposition forbids is making every visit automatically visible to the policyholder.
[P2.8] The real choice is between supported, voluntary disclosure and a default that deters care.
