[P1.1] Every adult has a basic claim to know the biological facts that shape identity, health, and kinship. Denying donor‑conceived people that knowledge treats them as commodities rather than persons, and it clashes with the universal principle that individuals control information about themselves. The law should therefore grant a legal right to identifying details once they turn eighteen, regardless of the downstream effect on donation numbers.
[P1.2] First, health. A person cannot responsibly manage inherited risk without data on genetic background. Cases of hidden familial cancers, cystic fibrosis, and other hereditary conditions have surfaced when donor‑conceived adults finally accessed records; early detection and preventive care depend on that knowledge. A right to information is a public‑health measure, not a luxury.
[P1.3] Second, identity. Psychological research consistently shows that knowledge of genetic origins reduces anxiety, improves self‑concept, and eases family integration. When the state withholds that data it creates a permanent information asymmetry that no other adult faces.
[P1.4] Third, the claim that anonymity is needed to preserve supply is speculative. Donor programs already function in jurisdictions that have abolished anonymity, such as the United Kingdom and parts of Scandinavia, without collapsing. Supply can be sustained through modest, transparent compensation, better recruitment, and clearer consent frameworks that respect both parties.
[P1.5] Donor privacy can be protected by limited, regulated disclosure—name, medical history, and contact opt‑in—rather than by blanket secrecy that erases the child's right. The trade‑off the opposition proposes pits a conjectural market effect against a concrete, lifelong right. When a law forces a whole class of people to live without essential medical and identity information to keep a market afloat, the balance tips unmistakably toward the individual.
[P1.6] Thus the core of the case is simple: autonomy and health outweigh a hypothetical drop in donations, and the latter can be mitigated without sacrificing the former.
