[P1.1] Consumer wearables do not merely count steps; they map the intimate rhythms of human life—heart rate variability, sleep architecture, stress responses, even early signs of illness before symptoms appear. When health and life insurers gain unfettered access to this stream, they do not simply price risk more accurately; they acquire a continuous surveillance apparatus over the most private corners of a person’s body. The proposition before us recognizes that such power must default to prohibition, permitting use only through genuine opt-in under strict limits.
[P1.2] The case for this default rests on three pillars. First, the coercion problem: health insurance is often legally mandated or economically essential, and life insurance is frequently required by mortgage lenders. In this context, an "opt-in" that is technically voluntary becomes practically mandatory, forcing individuals to choose between affordable coverage and bodily privacy. The power imbalance is structural; the insurer holds the gate to financial security.
[P1.3] Second, the accuracy problem: consumer-grade devices are not medical instruments. They misread heart rates, misinterpret sleep stages, and cannot distinguish between a brisk walk and cardiac distress. Pricing decisions built on this noise amount to arbitrary discrimination against people whose bodies or devices simply behave differently, penalizing the anxious, the chronically ill, or the simply different.
[P1.4] Third, the chilling effect: if insurers can penalize high resting heart rates or poor sleep scores, rational consumers will stop wearing devices or deliberately corrupt their data, destroying the very health-monitoring ecosystem that improves public health.
[P1.5] The proposition does not ban innovation; it channels it. Insurers may still use wearable data when customers freely choose to share it under strict limits—limits that prevent the data from becoming a backdoor for underwriting, coverage denial, or premium spikes based on transient biometrics. Without this default prohibition, we normalize a regime where your morning jog or your restless night becomes a line item in a risk algorithm, transforming instruments of wellness into tools of exclusion that punish the sick and surveil the healthy.
