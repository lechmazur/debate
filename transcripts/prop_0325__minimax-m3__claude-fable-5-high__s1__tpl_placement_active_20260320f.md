[P1.1] Sports broadcasting today is built on a 20th-century assumption: that fans will pay for everything because they have no choice. That assumption is breaking, and the bundle model is what's breaking it.
[P1.2] The proposition is simple: broadcasters should sell what fans actually want to watch. A supporter of one club shouldn't subsidize fifteen others. A Formula 1 viewer shouldn't be forced to fund MotoGP. A cricket follower of the IPL shouldn't carry the cost of a Big Bash subscription.
[P1.3] Team-specific and competition-specific passes match how fandom actually works today.
[P1.4] Three reasons this case is strong.
[P1.5] First, consumer fairness. Granular consumption deserves granular pricing. When a broadcaster forces a single-team fan to buy a bundle of dozens of teams they ignore, that's not a market transaction; it's a captured-customer extraction. The fan pays for content they will never watch, and the broadcaster collects economic rent rather than competitive revenue.
[P1.6] Second, market expansion. Broad bundles exclude price-sensitive casual fans who would happily pay a small fee for their team but refuse to pay a large fee for content they don't value. Lower-priced passes grow the paying audience rather than shrinking it. The bundle is a barrier to entry, not a gateway.
[P1.7] Third, technological feasibility. Streaming platforms can deliver team-specific and competition-specific passes at trivial marginal cost. The bundle is no longer a technical necessity; it is a pricing choice broadcasters make to maximize extraction.
[P1.8] The opponent will likely defend cross-subsidization: that broad bundles fund smaller competitions and women's leagues that could not survive alone. This concedes too much. Cross-subsidization through forced bundles is a corporate accounting choice, not a consumer obligation. If niche sports need support, broadcasters should fund them through advertising, sponsorship, and dedicated public-broadcasting arrangements, not by forcing unrelated fans to pay.
[P1.9] The right test is whether the current model serves fans or exploits them. On that test, granular passes win.

[C1.1] The bundle isn't a relic; it's the discount. Broadcasters pay enormous fixed sums for rights years in advance, and they price bundles to spread that cost across the widest base. Slice the audience into team-specific fragments and the fixed cost doesn't shrink — it just gets divided among fewer payers per slice. The predictable result: each "cheap" pass gets priced near what a devoted fan will bear, which is a lot, because devoted fans are the least price-sensitive customers in media.
[C1.2] Cable unbundling promised savings too; a decade later, à-la-carte streaming has fans juggling five services and paying more than the old bundle ever cost. Granular pricing means granular extraction, not fairness.
[C1.3] Second, sport is an ecosystem, not a catalogue. A league is only valuable because every team in it is funded well enough to compete. Bundle revenue underwrites collective rights deals and revenue sharing that keep small-market clubs viable. Team-specific passes create superstar economics: the big clubs capture nearly all pass revenue, the bottom half of the league starves, competitive balance collapses — and then even the big club's fans have a worse product to watch.
[C1.4] Your team-only pass is worthless without solvent opponents.
[C1.5] Third, on cross-subsidy, PRO simply waves the problem away [P1.8]. "Fund women's sport through advertising and public broadcasting instead" is not a mechanism; advertising rates for niche sport are thin precisely because audiences are small, and public broadcasters are cutting sports budgets everywhere. The bundle is currently the only revenue engine that actually pays those bills. Dismissing it as an "accounting choice" doesn't identify a replacement.
[C1.6] Finally, bundles build fans. People discover new competitions because they're already there — the F1 subscriber who stumbles into MotoGP becomes tomorrow's paying fan. Fragmentation walls off that discovery and shrinks the sport's future audience.
[C1.7] The test PRO offers — serve fans or exploit them — is the right one. Bundles serve fans better: lower effective prices, healthier leagues, broader sport. Fragmentation fails that test.
