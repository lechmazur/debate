[P1.1] A statute is not a spell. It is an instruction to ordinary people about what they may and may not do. The demand here is not for childish laws; it is that non-lawyers bound by law can actually read it. That is the rule-of-law baseline: the state’s demand for compliance is legitimate only when the commanded conduct is knowable.
[P1.2] If a nurse, a small landlord, or a truck driver needs a lawyer to discover basic duties, the law has not bound them fairly—it has trapped them.
[P1.3] The common defense of dense drafting is that precision prevents arbitrariness. But legalistic precision frequently produces the opposite for non-specialists. It moves disputes from public rules to the private manipulation of clauses. A single cross-reference, proviso, or defined term can flip an outcome, so sophisticated actors hire drafters and litigators to exploit exactly that density.
[P1.4] When precision is purchased at the price of comprehensibility, we do not get fairness; we get inequality. The rich buy decoding. Everyone else guesses.
[P1.5] Sacrificing some precision is not the same as embracing chaos. Legislatures can state the core rule plainly, give purposes and examples, and leave residual hard cases to courts and agencies. A worker reading “if you work over 40 hours, you are owed extra pay, with narrow exceptions for salaried managers” grasps the entitlement. Lawyers can still argue the exceptions.
[P1.6] What the tradeoff removes is not all certainty, but the illusion that certainty exists mainly for those who can pay to find it.
[P1.7] The cost of the current regime is measurable in conduct, not just comprehension. People comply with rules they understand; they resent and evade rules they cannot parse. Confusing tax, licensing, or benefit rules push citizens toward expensive preparers or accidental violation. A plain-statute requirement would force lawmakers to make rules clear, improving democratic accountability: voters can see what they are being asked to obey.

[P4.2] CON’s dilemma—either too precise to be plain or too plain to be law—requires plainness to mean deletion [C2.4]. It means relocation: moving the exception from a buried cross-reference to an enacted, readable qualification.

[P4.4] Courts already give an as-applied defense to those lacking fair notice; the valid core stands, and the legislature must reenact clearly before binding everyone.
