[P1.1] Influencer advertising has a feature ordinary commercial ads often lack: the seller is a trusted, visible personality whose audience follows choices, intimacy, and risk-taking. When that personality promotes high-risk gambling—sports betting, casino-style games, or bets featuring uncertain returns and aggressive incentives—to an audience likely to contain minors, lawmakers are not merely regulating a product. They are allowing commercial recruitment of children for an activity whose harms depend heavily on early exposure, repeated exposure, and easy access.
[P1.2] That is why the prohibition should be narrow but firm. It need not ban every gambling advertisement, influencer, or promotion to an exclusively adult audience. It should bar a defined class of high-risk products when the influencer’s audience makes minors a substantial or foreseeable part of it. This is a risk-based rule: the more dangerous the conduct and the more plausible the child exposure, the stronger the public-interest justification.
[P1.3] Disclaimers do not solve the problem. An “18+” label cannot neutralize a creator’s recommendation, excitement, coded language, affiliate link, or on-screen gambling animation. Children can understand branding and simulated risk before they can evaluate odds or chasing losses. Targeting also fails whenever the audience is mixed: algorithms distribute content without knowing who is watching, while users switch devices and undermine audience estimates.
[P1.4] An audience-level prohibition closes that route without requiring proof that every viewer is a minor.
[P1.5] The likely objection is that this threatens adult marketing and free expression. But the motion does neither. Adult-only, low-risk campaigns remain lawful, and creators retain broad freedom to discuss gambling without becoming sales channels for products likely to recruit minors. Conversely, protecting only exclusively adult audiences would leave children least protected, because the influencers most capable of reaching minors often have mixed or young audiences.
[P1.6] Targeted rules should prevent commercial recruitment of minors without censoring adult discussion.

[C1.3] Second, the rule punishes the party with the least control over the harm. A minor cannot legally open a sportsbook account. What stands between a fourteen-year-old and a bet is the operator's identity check, deposit controls, and the product's design — speed, bonuses, loss-chasing loops. Licensed operators already face advertising codes and age-verification duties; those levers bite on the entity that actually takes the money.
[C1.4] Criminalising the promoter for the age mix of an algorithmic feed shifts blame away from the only actor who can verify a customer.

The measurable audience becomes unmeasurable, and the minors least protected are precisely the ones my opponent wants to shield.
[C1.6] "High-risk" compounds this. All commercial gambling has negative expected value. Without a workable definition, the state gets to bless some products and criminalise promotion of others by fiat. Protect children where bets are placed, not where videos are watched.
