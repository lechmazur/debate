[P1.1] Every country asks something of those who join it. The question is whether it may ask for the minimum that makes equal membership real: the public language and the civic rules. It may, and it should.
[P1.2] Start with language. A common public language is not a cultural trophy; it is the highway to jobs, schools, courts, hospitals, and politics. If learning it is optional, the cost is paid by the immigrant who cannot complain to a landlord, a teacher, or a police officer without a translator—and by a society that fragments into mutually unintelligible enclaves. Expectation is not cruelty.
[P1.3] It is the opposite of abandonment.
[P1.4] The same is true of core civic norms: obey the law, reject violence and coercion, accept equal dignity regardless of sex or faith, resolve conflicts through democratic institutions. These are not the private property of any ethnicity. They are the terms of a free society. A nation that says “adopt them if you like” has no shared rules at all—only parallel authorities and quiet inequality.
[P1.5] The people most harmed are often the vulnerable inside closed communities, whose exit is blocked by language and fear.
[P1.6] None of this requires erasing private culture. People can speak another language at home, worship as they wish, cook, marry, and celebrate as they wish. The expectation applies to the public sphere: the language of civic life and the norms that make pluralism safe.
[P1.7] And the expectation is fair because it is reciprocal. The receiving society should offer language classes, civic education, and equal treatment. In return, newcomers commit to the shared minimum. That is not assimilation; it is citizenship as a two-way bargain.
[P1.8] Integration optional means integration failed by design. We should choose the demanding but generous path: a country that welcomes you and asks you to join it.

[C1.5] A devout parent who votes, obeys courts, and dissents from majority views on gender or religion is still a full member; demanding public adoption of those convictions is assimilation with softer branding. [C1.6] Real integration is measured by access, not internal adoption: can newcomers navigate a clinic, start a business, organize, and be heard without passing a cultural exam? If yes, that integrates them far more than demanding they first become cultural replicas. That is integration that respects both the newcomer and the pluralism PRO claims to defend.
