[P1.1] Access to high‑quality internet is no longer a luxury; it is the infrastructure that determines whether a citizen can work, learn, receive health care, or exercise democratic rights. Treating it as a legally enforceable universal service puts it on the same footing as water and electricity, which we already obligate governments to provide because without them modern life collapses. The proposition therefore rests on three pillars: necessity, precedent, and feasibility.
[P1.2] First, necessity. In 2024 more than half of global economic activity depends on digital connectivity. A farmer who cannot check market prices, a student who cannot attend a remote class, a patient who cannot book a tele‑medicine appointment—all are effectively excluded from essential services. When the state already guarantees water because dehydration kills, it must guarantee broadband because digital exclusion kills opportunity and health.
[P1.3] Second, precedent. Many nations already impose universal service obligations on telecoms, subsidising rural phone lines for decades. Extending the legal framework to broadband simply updates an existing model, not invents a new one. The EU’s Universal Service Directive already defines broadband as a basic service; the United States’ Lifeline program proves that targeted subsidies can be administered at scale.
[P1.4] Third, feasibility. The cost of universal broadband is comparable to historic electrification projects. Public‑private partnerships, spectrum auctions earmarked for coverage, and modest universal service funds can finance rollout without bankrupting budgets. Moreover, the economic return is massive: each dollar invested in broadband yields multiple dollars in GDP growth, tax revenue, and reduced social‑service costs.
[P1.5] Opponents will claim market forces are sufficient, but markets have repeatedly left the poorest and most remote communities behind. A legal right forces accountability, creates a clear standard of “high‑quality” rather than vague promises, and gives citizens a remedy when providers fail. In a world where participation is digital, denying that right is equivalent to denying water.
[P1.6] Therefore we must codify high‑quality internet as a universal service, because it is as essential as any utility we already protect by law.

[C1.1] This proposition rests on a fatal category error. Water and electricity are universal physical needs with stable, predictable minimum requirements that do not change hour by hour or user by user. High-quality internet is not like that. One family streaming 4K video while another checks market prices do not consume the same resource, and enforcing a one-size-fits-all legal guarantee will not expand access—it will redirect resources away from the people who need it most.
[C1.2] First, this legal right creates perverse tradeoffs that harm the vulnerable PRO claims to protect. When governments are legally required to deliver consistent gigabit speeds to every last remote cabin, they will divert funding away from providing even basic affordable connectivity to the thousands of low-income urban households currently on waiting lists. This is not hypothetical: every country that has adopted mandatory universal high-speed targets has prioritised hitting rural coverage benchmarks for legal compliance over expanding discounted access for poor urban residents.
[C1.3] Second, there is no valid precedent. The phone lines and electrification programs PRO cites were uniform, low-variance utilities. No one ever sued because their tap ran 20% slower than average. But citizens absolutely will litigate over latency, upload speeds, and outages.
[C1.4] This will not be a right to access—it will be an endless flood of disputes that benefit telecom lawyers, not farmers or students.
[C1.5] Finally, the economic returns PRO celebrates vanish the moment quality is enforced by law. It is cheaper to connect 100 people with functional internet than 10 with perfect internet. Every dollar spent chasing the legal "high quality" bar is a dollar not spent connecting another hundred excluded people. PRO would rather write an impressive legal right on paper than actually get more people online.

The opposite is true: without a legal floor, providers concentrate investment where profit is highest, leaving the poorest both rural and urban unserved. [P2.6] Finally, the economic argument: every dollar spent meeting a quality floor yields higher productivity than a dollar spent on a bare‑bones connection that cannot support remote work or tele‑medicine.
