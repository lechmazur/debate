[P1.1] The world does not need another voluntary climate promise that fades when elections change. It needs a hard rule: unabated coal is finished by 2040, and countries that keep building it face real consequences.
[P1.2] Coal is not one emission source among many. It is the dirtiest large-scale source of electricity, releasing far more carbon dioxide per unit of power than gas and vastly more than wind, solar, nuclear, hydro, or geothermal. Every year unabated coal remains in service locks in emissions that make the 1.5°C threshold practically unreachable and the 2°C threshold far harder. The stakes are not abstract: more heatwaves, droughts, floods, crop failures, forced migration, and instability.
[P1.3] The deadline is ambitious but achievable. Solar, wind, batteries, and grid modernization have become mature technologies, and new renewables are now often the cheapest form of new power in many markets. A 2040 phaseout is not a demand for overnight collapse of electricity systems; it is a demand that the dirtiest plants retire rather than being rebuilt for another 30 or 40 years.
[P1.4] Voluntary pledges have failed because they reward words, not action. Trade and finance penalties change that. A country that continues expanding unabated coal after 2040 would not merely be harming its own people; it would be exporting climate risk to everyone. Enforceable penalties align market incentives with physical reality: carbon has no borders, and neither should responsibility.
[P1.5] The rule should include transition support for lower-income countries so the phaseout is just, not punitive. But fairness cannot mean exempting rich governments that already possess the capital, technology, and institutions to act. A global deadline with teeth turns climate policy from a contest of promises into a floor of responsibility.
[P1.6] Phase out unabated coal by 2040. Make noncompliance costly. The alternative is not stability; it is compounding harm.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds tough, but toughness that never happens is just theater. A global coal ban with trade and finance penalties fails on its own terms at every step: adoption, enforcement, and climate effect.
[C1.2] Start with adoption. No treaty with real teeth gets ratified by the countries that matter. China and India together account for the majority of the world's coal generation, and both have built their industrial rise on it. India still draws well over half its electricity from coal, and its per-capita emissions are a fraction of the West's.
[C1.3] Neither government will accept a binding deadline, enforced by trade punishment, that hands its energy policy to foreign tribunals. A rule the biggest emitters refuse to sign is not a floor of responsibility—it's a press release.
[C1.4] Then enforcement. Trade penalties for energy choices are economic coercion by another name, and the targeted countries will read them that way. We've seen the pattern: when climate policy arrives as sanctions, cooperation collapses and forums fracture. Worse, the penalties strike hardest at lower-income countries with no capital to replace plants early.
[C1.5] PRO says transition support will fix this, but promised finance has a long history of arriving late and short. The "just transition" clause is a fig leaf over a punishment mechanism.
[C1.6] And the climate payoff is smaller than advertised. Coal is already losing to cheap renewables almost everywhere new capacity is proposed. The fastest phaseouts in history—driven by economics and finance, not treaties—happened without a single enforceable penalty. A rigid 2040 deadline doesn't accelerate that; it gives reluctant governments a sovereignty grievance to rally domestic politics around, and gives rich countries a moral club while their own historical emissions dwarf everyone else's.
[C1.7] Deadlines with teeth sound decisive. In practice they produce refusals to sign, punishments on the poor, and fractured cooperation. Choose the path that actually retires plants.

[P2.3] They also argue coal is already losing to cheap renewables. If true, the deadline is nearly free. It stops what market forces alone have not: state-backed financing of plants that would belch carbon for forty years. Without penalties, captive banks and subsidies keep dead economics alive; with them, the arbitrage closes.
[P2.4] Economics can retire a plant; only a rule can stop the next one from being built.
[P2.5] The “punish the poor” objection ignores mechanism. Transition finance can be front-loaded against retirement milestones, not promised after the fact. Border-style penalties can rebate low-income exporters, targeting the fuel rather than the farmer. [P2.6] Nor is this economic coercion. A coal plant exports heat, drought, and flood damage across borders. Penalties internalize a cost the emitter is already imposing on others; sovereignty is not a right to uncompensated harm. Cooperation without consequences is what produced rising emissions in the first place.
[P2.7] The choice is not a perfect treaty versus purity. It is a rule that raises the cost of unabated coal versus pledges that let it ride. The first can fail at the margin; the second has already failed worldwide. Choose teeth.
