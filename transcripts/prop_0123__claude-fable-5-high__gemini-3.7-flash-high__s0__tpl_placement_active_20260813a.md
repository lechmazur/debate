[P1.1] Every day, shoppers make food decisions in about six seconds per product, standing in aisles engineered by companies that spend billions making ultra-processed food look wholesome. Against that machinery, "consumer choice campaigns" — pamphlets, websites, well-meaning ads about balanced diets — are a whisper in a hurricane. Warning labels put honest information exactly where the decision happens: on the package, at the moment of purchase. That is the core of this debate, and it is why the motion stands.
[P1.2] Three arguments carry the case.
[P1.3] First, labels fix an information failure that education cannot. Ultra-processed foods are deliberately designed to obscure what they are — a granola bar marketed as healthy can carry more sugar than a candy bar. A prominent front-of-pack warning, like Chile's black stop signs for high sugar, salt, and saturated fat, cuts through marketing at zero cognitive cost. Chile's experience is instructive: after mandatory warnings, purchases of labeled sugary drinks fell substantially, and Mexico and other countries followed.
[P1.4] Education campaigns, by contrast, have decades of documented mediocrity — knowledge rises modestly, behavior barely moves.
[P1.5] Second, labels change what companies make, not just what consumers buy. Faced with a black warning octagon, manufacturers reformulate to avoid it — cutting sugar and sodium across entire product lines. That improves the diet of every shopper, including those who never read a label. No pamphlet has ever forced a soda company to reformulate.
[P1.6] Third, equity. Choice campaigns reward people with time, literacy, and money — and leave behind shift workers, non-native speakers, and low-income families who bear the heaviest burden of diet-related disease. A stark symbol on a package works for everyone equally.
[P1.7] Notice what labels don't do: they ban nothing, tax nothing, and remove no choice. They simply make choice real by making it informed. If the alternative is relying "mainly" on campaigns that industry can outspend a thousand to one, the honest, effective, and freedom-respecting policy is the warning label.

[C1.2] First, "ultra-processed food" is an ill-defined, sprawling umbrella. Consumers become desensitized, tune out the warnings entirely, and lose the ability to distinguish between genuinely nutrient-poor junk food and affordable, practical nutrition.
[C1.3] Second, warning labels drive perverse reformulation, not genuine health. This leaves consumers with products that are technically unflagged but nutritionally hollow, while penalizing nutrient-dense, shelf-stable options.
[C1.4] Third, health is determined by overall dietary patterns, not the isolation of individual ingredients. Slapping apocalyptic warnings on specific foods demonizes affordable staples that working-class families depend on, raising grocery anxiety without teaching how to construct a balanced diet.
[C1.5] Consumer choice campaigns, backed by transparent and nuanced nutrition facts, treat adults like capable decision-makers. They build holistic dietary literacy rather than reducing nutrition to an all-or-nothing panic. When government replaces comprehensive consumer education with blunt, stigmatizing warnings across the supermarket, it confuses the public, distorts the food supply, and fails to build healthy lives.
