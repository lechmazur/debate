[P1.1] When someone opens a DTC genetic testing report and learns they carry a BRCA mutation, they are not reading a number on a bathroom scale. They are receiving information that may trigger prophylactic surgery, reshape family relationships, alter insurance decisions, and permanently reframe how they understand their own body. The question is not whether people deserve that information. They do.
[P1.2] The question is whether a PDF and a FAQ page constitute adequate preparation for receiving it. They do not.
[P1.3] Genetic counseling exists precisely because genetic results are not self-interpreting. Penetrance rates, variants of uncertain significance, and the difference between elevated statistical risk and clinical certainty are not concepts most people carry in their background knowledge. Without guidance, a person with an ambiguous variant may undergo unnecessary procedures. A person who misreads a "negative" result may abandon legitimate preventive care.
[P1.4] The error runs in both directions, and in both directions it causes real harm.
[P1.5] DTC companies face a fundamental market misalignment here. Their revenue depends on volume and frictionless sales. The incentive structure actively discourages the kind of careful, patient-centered preparation that genetic counselors provide. Requiring counseling access before high-impact results are released corrects that misalignment.
[P1.6] It does not block results—it conditions delivery on comprehension.
[P1.7] The proposition is carefully scoped. It targets high-impact predictive results, not ancestral reports or trait curiosity. That limit matters. The requirement applies where the stakes—suicide risk following unexpected terminal diagnoses, unnecessary mastectomies, preventable cardiac deaths from misread results—are severe enough to justify the procedural cost.
[P1.8] Opponents will invoke consumer autonomy. But autonomy built on confusion is not autonomy; it is the appearance of choice stripped of its substance. A mandatory counseling access requirement does not override individual choice. It ensures that when someone decides to act on a genetic result, that decision is genuinely theirs—informed, contextualized, and supported.
[P1.9] The burden is clear: without this requirement, DTC companies profit from delivering life-altering news into a void.

[C1.1] Requiring certified counseling before release of results substitutes a state-approved gatekeeper for adult judgment. Consumers already sign consent forms acknowledging that results may reveal serious health risks and that interpretation carries limits. Mandating an extra professional layer assumes ordinary adults cannot weigh those warnings or decide whether to consult their own doctor afterward.
[C1.2] The rule would create real friction. Certified counselors remain scarce in many regions, and scheduling adds weeks or months of delay. People facing elevated risk often want prompt data so they can act on their own timeline, schedule clinical confirmation, or simply decline further steps. Inserting a required session turns information into a controlled product rather than a purchased report.
[C1.3] Voluntary counseling already exists. Companies can and do sell access as an optional service. Those who value guided discussion can obtain it without forcing the rest of the market to absorb the cost and delay. A universal mandate therefore does not expand informed choice; it restricts the default option to one approved pathway.
[C1.4] Other medical results with comparable stakes—positive cancer screens, abnormal cardiac tests, or even direct physician disclosure of terminal diagnoses—do not carry a legal counseling prerequisite. Singling out DTC genetic tests for this extra obligation reveals selective distrust of consumer-directed testing rather than a coherent standard for handling sensitive health data. Individuals remain free to seek support; they should not be compelled to accept it first.

[P2.1] The consent-form argument is the weakest claim in this debate, and it deserves to be said plainly. Signing a disclosure that results "may reveal serious health risks" tells a consumer approximately nothing about what a BRCA1 positive actually means, what penetrance rates govern it, or whether their specific variant is clinically actionable or merely uncertain. Comprehension is not a signature. The argument confuses legal protection for the company with informed readiness for the customer.
[P2.2] On scarcity: this is an implementation challenge, not a rebuttal to the principle. Telemedicine has made certified genetic counselors accessible across wide geographic areas at low marginal cost. The scarcity argument, taken seriously, is an argument to build that infrastructure—not to let people receive life-altering news without any preparation. And if the objection is delay, the answer is to fund capacity, not to remove the safeguard.
[P2.3] The voluntary option argument is undone by basic market logic. If voluntary counseling were genuinely serving the people who need it, companies would promote it prominently, not bury it as an upsell. The proposal precisely addresses the structural failure: companies have no incentive to slow their own funnel. Voluntarism fails predictably when the default is frictionless delivery and the population most vulnerable to harm—those who don't know what questions to ask—are also those least likely to opt in.
[P2.4] The comparator to physician-delivered results actually helps the PRO side. When a doctor delivers a serious diagnosis, they are obligated to explain it, answer questions, and direct the patient to next steps. That interpretive layer exists. DTC testing specifically removes the physician from the loop—and then CON argues we should not replace that function with anything else.
[P2.5] The gap being described is real; the question is whether to fill it.
[P2.6] The central choice remains: who bears the cost of confusion? Under CON's model, the consumer absorbs it alone. Under this proposal, the company that profits from delivering the result shares responsibility for how it lands.

[C5.1] The decisive flaw in the PRO case is its refusal to treat adults as capable of directing their own follow-up. Once a result arrives, any consumer can bring it to a physician, specialist, or even a voluntarily retained counselor; nothing in the DTC model prevents that step. The mandate therefore does not fill an empty interpretive slot. It inserts a compulsory one that must be cleared before the consumer has assessed whether their own result warrants the cost or delay.
[C5.2] That substitution matters because it shifts power from the individual to a regulated gate. The only reliable difference is that every buyer now pays the friction and every transaction carries an approved intermediary. If the goal is genuine comprehension rather than procedural compliance, the lighter tool is expanded post-result access, not a preemptive requirement applied to competent adults who never asked for it.
