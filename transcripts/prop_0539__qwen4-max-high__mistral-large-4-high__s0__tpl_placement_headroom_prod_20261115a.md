[P1.1] The decisive word here is "enforceable." Not agency brochures about dignity, not voluntary codes — licences that can be revoked, escrow that pays the surrogate even if the pregnancy fails, parentage fixed before transfer, and medical follow-up that outlasts the birth. Enforcement has a target: clinics have addresses.
[P1.2] This motion does not ban surrogacy. It sets the terms on which it stays legal. The opposition must then defend either prohibition, which pushes the trade into unlicensed corridors where no standard applies at all, or a market with no floor, where the cheapest provider wins by cutting protections nobody can see.
[P1.3] Look at what breaks. A baby is born abroad; the intended parents' home country refuses to recognise the arrangement; the surrogate is legally the mother and does not want the child; the couple has separated and neither will take responsibility. Infants have been stranded for months in that gap. In an ordinary contract you unwind a bad deal.
[P1.4] Here you cannot unwind a person. Pre-arranged parentage and a designated responsible party are not red tape; they are the difference between a child with a family and a child with a file.
[P1.5] The health and money risks are equally structural. Fees contingent on a live birth push clinics toward multiple embryo transfers and pressure women to accept decisions that are not theirs. Brokers can absorb most of the payment while the carrier absorbs the medical risk, and nobody sees her after delivery. Those aren't exotic abuses; they are the default incentives of an unconditioned market.
[P1.6] Conditional legality keeps access for infertile couples and same-sex parents while removing the profit available from harm. Ask the opposition to name one protection a surrogate actually holds without enforceable standards — a promise she can sue on, in a legal system and language not her own.

[C1.1] The motion's operative word is not "standards"; it is "cross-border." A licence issued where the clinic sits cannot follow a surrogate back to her own country, and a parentage order made abroad can be worthless where the child is born or where the family lives. So this is not a regulated market. It is a market that can always move one step past the regulator.
[C1.2] That is not hypothetical. When Thailand restricted foreign commercial surrogacy after widely reported scandals, arrangements shifted rather than disappeared: parents moved to Cambodia, then elsewhere, and babies and surrogates were left in legal limbo when the next crackdown came. A clinic can pass every local checklist on Monday and operate through a partner abroad on Tuesday. Enforceable standards require one authority with jurisdiction over the whole transaction—payment, consent, medical decisions, parentage, aftercare.
[C1.3] Cross-border surrogacy is defined by the absence of that authority.
[C1.4] Even well-written standards leave the core problem untouched. Commercial surrogacy pays a woman to carry a child she will hand over, and across borders the person least able to enforce the deal is the one bearing the pregnancy. Health protection cannot be enforced after she returns home; parentage cannot be enforced if the destination state refuses recognition; anti-exploitation cannot be enforced if the agency is a shell in a third country. Compliance becomes a label that helps the industry sell the arrangement the motion claims to constrain.
[C1.5] The honest rule is that cross-border commercial surrogacy should not remain legal on those terms, because the terms cannot be made enforceable where they matter. Conditional legality does not remove exploitation; it certifies it. If a state cannot guarantee the standard across every part of the transaction, it should not attach legality to the transaction at all.
