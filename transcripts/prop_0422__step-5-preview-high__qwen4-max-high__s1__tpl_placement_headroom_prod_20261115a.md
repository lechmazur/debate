[P1.1] Seating a child next to a parent is not an upgrade; it is a safety requirement and a basic operating condition. When airlines or rail operators impose add-on seat fees or premium fare penalties to keep young children with accompanying adults, they convert a non-negotiable welfare and logistics need into a discretionary upsell.
[P1.2] The central clash is whether family seating is a core safety function or a luxury amenity. PRO says it is core. On aircraft, an unaccompanied toddler or small child in a separate row creates acute risks during turbulence, emergency evacuations. On trains, separated children raise supervision and safeguarding concerns in crowded carriages.
[P1.3] Operators already bear a duty of care for passenger safety; charging extra to fulfill that duty shifts the cost of risk management onto the passenger and punishes families for compliance.
[P1.4] Opponents will claim that seat-assignment complexity and yield management justify fees. But that conflates inventory pricing with safety obligations. A carrier can guarantee adjacent seating for adult-child pairs without collapsing revenue models, because most seats are already sold as paired inventory. The real problem is not complexity; it is that operators have monetized the anxiety of parents.
[P1.5] Consider a typical family of four: two adults, two young children. A £20 or $25 per-seat reservation fee on top of full child fares can add up to $100—enough to force a parent into a middle seat far from a toddler who may need feeding, comforting, or evacuation. Rail operators who offer no free adjacent option are effectively telling lower-income families that supervision is optional if you cannot pay the surcharge.
[P1.6] Seat fees for children are not ancillary revenue; they are a safety tax. Requiring operators to seat young children with accompanying adults at no extra charge aligns commercial practice with operational duty and public welfare.
