[P1.1] A tenant is rejected for an apartment, a nurse screened out of a hospital job, a borrower denied a mortgage — each told only that the system scored them too low. No factor to dispute, no error to correct, no reason to learn from. A limited right to explanation closes that accountability gap where automated decisions significantly affect housing, credit, employment, or healthcare.
[P1.2] This is not a right to source code, model weights, or trade secrets. It is a right to what a person needs to exercise every other right: the principal factors behind an adverse decision, the data relied on about them, whether a human reviewed it, and what could be changed or contested.
[P1.3] Without it, existing protections become decorative. Anti-discrimination and fair-lending laws cannot be enforced if a model quietly penalizes a zip code, an employment gap after caregiving, or a medical billing code and no one can see the pattern. Errors entrench just as easily: a mixed credit file, an expunged arrest matched to the wrong person, a miscoded diagnosis. If people never learn which fact drove the outcome, that error follows them from application to application, at a scale no human gatekeeper could match.
[P1.4] The power at stake justifies judicial recognition. These systems do not merely advise; they sort who gets shelter, work, care, and credit, often with no meaningful human alternative. Courts routinely require reason-giving for consequential decisions to test legality and prevent arbitrary power. Extending that principle to automated gatekeepers does not invent a new value — it prevents technology from becoming a loophole in old ones.
[P1.5] Feasibility confirms the limit works. Credit markets already use adverse-action reason codes without revealing proprietary models or collapsing under compliance. A short statement of top factors, data sources, and a route to correction preserves innovation while forcing firms to keep records, monitor outcomes, and catch indefensible proxies. Opacity protects only the deployer; explanation protects accuracy, equality, and trust.

[C1.1] The motion sounds modest because “limited” does all the work. A judge-made right spanning housing, credit, employment, and healthcare must define “automated,” “significant,” and an adequate explanation, then choose remedies. Those are sector-specific regulatory choices. A vague entitlement invites inconsistent rulings and litigation before it delivers one corrected decision.
[C1.2] PRO’s central mistake is treating explanation as accountability. A system can generate a fluent list of “principal factors” whose clarity exceeds its fidelity to the actual decision process. Even an accurate notice—“high debt utilization, short employment history”—does not establish whether the data were wrong, the model discriminatory, or the decision lawful. The tenant needs the disputed file corrected and the outcome reviewed; a narrative alone changes neither.
[C1.3] The credit example proves too little. Adverse-action reason codes operate within a particular legal regime, alongside specified data and dispute duties. They do not show that courts can generalize that regime to hiring or healthcare. Mandating disclosure of decisive factors can also reward gaming: fraudulent applicants learn which signals to imitate, while deployers optimize notices for defensibility rather than decisions for fairness.
[C1.4] The costs fall unevenly. Individual litigation is most usable by people with lawyers and time. Large deployers can standardize boilerplate and litigate compliance; smaller landlords, clinics, and employers may abandon useful screening tools or avoid hard-to-score applicants. PRO’s promised accountability can therefore produce fuller rejection letters, not broader access.
[C1.5] Existing anti-discrimination law is not “decorative” merely because one proposed enforcement tool is absent. Courts can examine evidence, compel records in litigation, and apply sector-specific duties. Where those duties are inadequate, legislatures and regulators can require audits, correction, human appeals, and tailored disclosures with defined thresholds and resources.
[C1.6] The choice is not opacity versus justice. It is a genuine, enforceable fairness regime versus a judicially improvised right that can make opaque systems sound legible without making them accurate, lawful, or reversible. Reject the motion.

[P2.1] The objection that explanation is merely a “narrative alone” [C1.2] answers a right no one proposed. The right includes the data relied on, the principal factors, whether a human reviewed the decision, and what can be corrected or contested. Explanation is not a substitute for correction; it is the key that makes correction usable. Without knowing a denial rested on an expunged arrest, a mixed credit file, or a caregiving gap coded as instability, a person cannot know what to dispute or whether discrimination is worth alleging.
[P2.2] That exposes the circularity in relying on existing litigation. Discovery helps only after someone files a plausible claim. If the only notice is a low score, the rejected nurse or tenant must sue blind, hoping records reveal a problem. An explanation at denial turns enforcement from a fishing expedition into a targeted check — not proof of illegality, but the starting point for dispute, just as with adverse-action notices.
[P2.3] Imperfect fidelity is not a reason for silence. Courts evaluate human reason-giving despite risks of rationalization and pretext; they test stated reasons against records and outcomes. The same discipline applies here: deployers must state factors that actually drove the output, retain inputs, and face consequences for a generic or false notice. Boilerplate therefore fails, because the explanation must be tied to the individual’s data.
[P2.4] Naming top factors also does not invite meaningful gaming without disclosing thresholds, weights, or source code — a distinction credit markets already sustain.
[P2.5] Sector differences argue for a floor, not paralysis. Courts routinely apply reason-giving and anti-discrimination principles across contexts while tailoring them to the decision. A clinic, landlord, or employer that cannot identify why its system denied a significant opportunity has not been burdened by explanation; it has been caught deploying a tool it cannot defend. Waiting for four legislatures to close the same loophole leaves that loophole open meanwhile.

[P4.2] A2: What remains limited is scope and disclosure: only materially adverse decisions in four domains, only this person's top factors and data, and a route to correction — never source code, weights, thresholds, or system-wide redesign. [P4.3] That turns CON’s dilemma against it. CON says audits, correction, and review are workable replacements, yet each depends on the same records, the same causal judgments, and a decision-maker able to say why this file failed. [P4.4] What CON actually offers the tenant in [P3.1] is still a low score and a hope that blind pleading survives. Our right reverses only that informational coercion: the party that automated suspicion must make it checkable. That is why existing law is sharpened, not duplicated — explanation supplies the individual fact that lets correction, discrimination analysis, and human reconsideration begin. Silence protects arbitrary sorting until someone guesses correctly; a narrow, truthful reason makes power answer for one decision at a time.
