[P1.1] Fragmented service industries—retail, hospitality, food service, home care—employ millions across thousands of small businesses with razor-thin margins. Repeated minimum-wage hikes treat this entire landscape as a single target. They aren't. They're a blunt instrument that distorts behavior, accelerates automation, cuts hours, and still leaves workers behind.
[P1.2] Sectoral bargaining is the sharper tool.
[P1.3] Minimum-wage hikes are set by legislators who lack granular knowledge of what a hotel, a fast-food franchise, and a home-care agency can actually afford. The result is one-size-fits-all pressure that crushes the most vulnerable employers—small franchises, independent operators—while large chains absorb the cost. Workers at those small shops lose their jobs. That's not a floor; it's a ceiling disguised as one.
[P1.4] Sectoral bargaining flips this. It brings together employers and workers from the same industry to negotiate standards reflecting actual conditions. A hospitality sector board can set different wage floors for full-service hotels versus fast-casual restaurants, account for regional cost differences, and pair wage increases with scheduling protections or training requirements. The standard is negotiated, not imposed—so compliance is higher, evasion is lower, and the framework is more durable.
[P1.5] Consider the cycle of repeated hikes. Each one is a shock. Employers scramble, workers see temporary gains then face reduced hours or layoffs, and the cycle repeats. Sectoral bargaining replaces that cycle with a stable agreement that evolves with the industry.
[P1.6] It's not a one-time fix—it's a system.
[P1.7] Places that have tried this—Seattle's hospitality standards, various European sectoral models—show that when workers and employers co-design the rules, compliance improves, turnover drops, and wages rise more sustainably than any legislature could mandate alone.
[P1.8] The choice isn't between doing nothing and raising wages. It's between a hammer and a scalpel. For fragmented service industries, the scalpel wins.

[C1.1] The proposition's core promise—negotiated standards in fragmented industries—collapses on its own premise. Bargaining requires bargainers. Fragmented service sectors are precisely where neither side of the table exists: union density in American retail and food service sits in the low single digits, and there is no employer association that can credibly speak for thousands of independent restaurants, corner stores, and home-care agencies. So who actually fills the seats on a "sectoral board"?
[C1.2] Political appointees and the largest incumbent chains—the only players organized enough to show up. That isn't bargaining. It's regulation with extra steps, less accountability, and a built-in capture problem: dominant firms get to write standards that raise their smaller rivals' costs.
[C1.3] Meanwhile, the minimum wage's supposed bluntness is its greatest strength in exactly these industries. Fragmented sectors are defined by high turnover, informal employment, and weak enforcement capacity. A single, universally known number is self-enforcing: every dishwasher knows what they're owed without a lawyer. Replace that with a lattice of negotiated rates—full-service versus fast-casual, region by region, tier by tier—and you've handed evasion-prone employers a classification game.
[C1.4] "Different floors for different formats" sounds like precision; in practice it's a loophole catalog. Complexity is the enemy of compliance where workers are least able to police their own paychecks.
[C1.5] The doom story about hikes is also empirically stale. A large body of modern research on real-world increases—including substantial ones—finds wage gains for low-paid workers with little to no measurable job loss, because service work is tied to place and can't be offshored. The "shock-layoff cycle" PRO describes is a theoretical fear, not the observed pattern.
[C1.6] Finally, weigh speed and legitimacy. A legislated wage floor delivers raises to millions on a known date, passed by accountable officials. Sectoral boards take years to constitute, get tied up in litigation, and cover whoever the board's definitions happen to reach. For fragmented industries, the simple, universal, enforceable tool wins.
