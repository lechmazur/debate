[C1.1] The proposition asks us to believe that banning short-term rentals is a housing policy with big upside and small downside. It is neither. It is a narrow intervention on a small slice of the housing stock, sold as a cure for a problem — high-demand-neighborhood rent growth — that is overwhelmingly driven by zoning, construction costs, and land scarcity, not by tourists renting spare rooms.
[C1.2] Start with scale. Even in the most STR-saturated neighborhoods, short-term rentals typically make up a small fraction of the total housing stock. Removing a few percentage points of listings does not meaningfully move rents in markets where the real constraint is that almost nothing gets built. Cities that have imposed strict STR limits — plenty have — still see rents climb because the underlying scarcity never touched.
[C1.3] Second, PRO assumes the freed unit re-enters the long-term rental market. That is the weakest link in their mechanism. A landlord who can no longer rent nightly does not automatically hand over a lease to a local family. They may sell to an owner-occupier who removes the unit from the rental pool entirely, convert to a pied-à-terre, leave it vacant awaiting a buyer, or exit the city altogether.
[C1.4] None of these outcomes lowers rent; several worsen availability.
[C1.5] Third, the “concentrated among big operators” framing hides the real victims. Many ordinary homeowners in expensive neighborhoods rely on STR income to cover mortgages that let them stay in the same neighborhood PRO claims to be protecting. Strip that income and you get exactly the displacement PRO warns against — just aimed at owners instead of renters.
[C1.6] Finally, the visitor-spending loss is not neutral. Hotels concentrate revenue in large chains; STRs spread income to cleaners, small hosts, and local shops in residential areas that never see hotel tourism. Restricting STRs doesn't just cost visitors — it starves the very neighborhood economies PRO wants to stabilize.
