[P1.1] A voice is the closest thing to a fingerprint a performer owns: breath, age, accent, emotion. When AI dubs an actor into thirty languages, it is not editing their performance — it is manufacturing new ones they never gave, in words they never spoke. The question is who gets to say yes to that: the performer, affirmatively, or a clause on page forty of a contract signed before the technology existed.
[P1.2] Three reasons opt-in wins.
[P1.3] First, boilerplate is not consent in any meaningful sense. Standard contracts are take-it-or-leave-it, drafted by studios, signed under vastly unequal bargaining power, and written broadly — "all media now known or hereafter devised" — precisely so no one has to ask again. Consent that is buried and purchased before anyone understands what is being sold is a legal fiction, not an agreement.
[P1.4] Second, the stakes are uniquely high. A captured voice model does not expire with the film. It can be reused, repurposed, resold: an actor's voice selling products in a market they never entered, or delivering lines in a genre they have publicly refused. The 2023 Hollywood strikes made AI consent a central fight precisely because performers saw this coming.
[P1.5] Third — and here the opposing case collapses — opt-in costs almost nothing. The proposition does not ban AI dubbing or delay localization by a single day for anyone willing to ask. Studios with legitimate uses will get consent, because performers will grant it at a fair price. That is how markets treat everything of value.
[P1.6] The only use opt-in blocks is the one its owner would refuse. A system that depends on people not being asked is confessing its own illegitimacy. Efficiency built on someone else's identity is not efficiency; it is taking.
[P1.7] The default is simple: your voice is yours until you say otherwise. Opt-in says exactly that.

[C1.1] Nobody on this side is defending an actor's voice being sold to a mattress commercial. So let's find the real clash: not whether performers deserve protection, but whether a per-performer opt-in gate is the instrument that delivers it. It isn't.
[C1.2] Start with what dubbing actually is. When a film is localized, the studio has already licensed the performance for that work. AI dubbing doesn't manufacture a new performance — it carries the authorized one into Spanish or Tamil instead of handing it to a different actor entirely, which is what the industry did for eighty years without asking anyone's permission. The abuse PRO fears — resale, ads, genres the actor refuses — is a *use* problem, and the fix is use restrictions in the contract that no clause can override.
[C1.3] Opt-in doesn't fix it: a performer who checks "yes" at hiring has consented, and PRO's own logic then says the studio may proceed.
[C1.4] That's the fatal asymmetry. Individual consent is exactly what unequal bargaining power is best at extracting. Tell a day player with four lines that the AI clause is optional, and watch how optional it feels when the next audition is a phone call away. Opt-in converts a structural protection into a signature — and signatures are cheap.
[C1.5] A non-waivable collective floor, bargained by a union, is the protection you *cannot* buy out of an actor one at a time. PRO's mechanism trades that for a checkbox.
[C1.6] Then the breakage. An ensemble series has hundreds of speaking roles across seasons. Per-performer, per-language, per-revision clearance means one unreachable estate, one holdout, one lost contact address freezes localization for everyone else — the crew, the translators, the audiences who don't speak English. Music sampling learned this lesson: a clearance regime with veto points doesn't produce fair payment, it produces works that never ship.
[C1.7] "Your voice is yours" is a good slogan. It is not a licensing system. Bargain the terms; don't build a gate that only the powerful can walk through.
