[P1.1] Local journalism is collapsing, and the consequences are measurable. As newspapers shutter and newsrooms shrink, the local accountability infrastructure that once covered city councils, school boards, zoning decisions, and municipal contracts disappears. What fills that vacuum is national partisan media, which has no interest in whether a county commissioner is steering contracts to his brother-in-law. People lose shared facts about their own communities and gain partisan friction imported from elsewhere.
[P1.2] This is the core mechanism. Without local reporting, corruption gets cheaper—fewer eyes, fewer stories, fewer consequences. And without a common local information diet, citizens retreat into nationalized partisan narratives that have nothing to do with whether their drinking water is safe or their school district is competent. Polarization deepens precisely because the local substrate that once grounded people in shared reality erodes.
[P1.3] Public funding with firewall governance directly addresses both problems. Firewall structures—statutory independence, multi-year funding cycles, editorial boards insulated from political appointment, transparent and competitive grant allocation—create distance between the funding source and editorial decisions. This is not a theoretical aspiration. Public broadcasters in democracies with strong firewall protections routinely outperform private media on both trust and independence metrics.
[P1.4] The design constraint matters: the proposition specifies firewall governance precisely because it neutralizes the capture risk.
[P1.5] The opponent must prove not merely that risks exist, but that they exceed the documented harms of the status quo. Right now, the absence of local journalism is not a hypothetical—it is an active engine of corruption and polarization. A well-designed public funding model replaces that engine with something accountable, local, and structurally protected. The comparison is not between perfection and imperfection; it is between a dying system with known harms and a governed system with manageable risks.
[P1.6] The proposition wins because the status quo is already doing what opponents fear public funding might do—except the status quo does it silently, at scale, with no firewall at all.
