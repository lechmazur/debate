[P1.1] The decisive question is not whether AI can nudge output upward, but whether employers should have the power to require workers to live under an always-on companion that watches, prompts, and corrects them all day. They should not.
[P1.2] An always-on AI companion is not a normal tool. A spreadsheet or CRM waits to be used. A companion that monitors pace, keystrokes, tone, screen focus, or “engagement,” then whispers reminders, scripts responses, and flags hesitation, operates continuously on the worker’s attention and conduct. Imagine a customer-service agent whose system listens to every call, suggests phrases, and logs silence.
[P1.3] When an employer requires it, the worker is not choosing assistance; she is submitting to real-time behavioral supervision. Because employment is coercive in ordinary conditions—lose the job, lose income—consent on the shop floor or in a call center is close to meaningless.
[P1.4] This matters because the harms are structural. First, autonomy collapses. If the system can record every pause and prompt every next step, workers become executors of machine-managed scripts, with less room for judgment, craft, and honest recovery from mistakes. Second, dignity and privacy erode.
[P1.5] Always-on coaching infers fatigue, confusion, mood, or effort from fine-grained behavior, creating intimate data that can be repurposed for discipline, scheduling, or dismissal. Third, the pressure is self-enforcing: even a “gentle” nudge becomes a threat when the boss controls the dashboard.
[P1.6] CON will likely say these tools raise productivity or help training. But productivity gains do not justify compelled surveillance, and employers have less intrusive alternatives: clear goals, outcome measures, human coaching, and optional software that workers can open when useful. The proposition bans compulsion, not innovation. If a tool is genuinely beneficial, employers can persuade workers to adopt it, with real consent and an off switch.
[P1.7] A mandatory always-on companion crosses that line.

[C1.1] The proposition asks for a total ban—not disclosure rules, not data limits, not the right to refuse abusive systems, but a flat prohibition on requiring any always-on AI companion, for any worker, in any job. That is the wrong tool for the problem PRO describes.
[C1.2] Start with what employers already lawfully require: fixed scripts in call centers, GPS tracking for delivery drivers, keystroke logging for data-entry teams, checklists that supervisors enforce minute by minute. An AI companion that prompts a nurse to double-check a dosage, or reminds a warehouse picker to rest before a lift, is not a new species of control—it is the same managerial direction employers have always exercised, delivered faster and more consistently. If requiring supervision were inherently illegitimate, we would have banned time clocks and call monitoring decades ago.
[C1.3] We did not, because the employment relationship has always involved directed, monitored labor in exchange for wages. PRO needs a reason this crosses a categorical line that mere supervision does not; "it happens continuously" is a difference of degree, not the sharp break a prohibition requires.
[C1.4] Now weigh what a ban forecloses. In aviation maintenance, trucking, and hospital wards, real-time fatigue and error prompts catch the lapses that human supervisors miss because no supervisor can watch everyone every second. A new hire in a complex job—say, a junior coder or a claims processor—benefits enormously from a companion that flags mistakes before they compound, rather than being quietly fired three months later for underperformance no one explained. Banning mandatory use does not just protect autonomy; it strips away the very mechanism that helps struggling or disabled workers keep their jobs and helps new workers survive probation.
[C1.5] The honest fix for surveillance abuse is regulation: limits on data retention, bans on using coaching data for discipline, required transparency, worker appeal rights. That solves PRO's real harms. A blanket prohibition solves nothing except employer flexibility, and it does so by torching tools that make workplaces safer and more forgiving of error.
