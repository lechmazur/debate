[P1.3] A conduct-based rule is also more effective. If someone uses self-custody to launder money, evade sanctions, fund ransomware, or dodge taxes, existing criminal prohibitions already reach that conduct. Prosecutors can target the transaction, the intent, the proceeds, and the conspiracy. The proposition does not create a safe harbor for crime; it says the lawful default is legality, and the state must show a direct tie to specific wrongdoing.
[P1.4] That is ordinary due process, not radical libertarianism.
[P1.5] Bans fail for a practical reason: self-custody cannot be effectively banned without mass surveillance and severe collateral damage. Keys can be generated offline, written on paper, memorized. A family in Argentina protecting savings from inflation, or an activist whose bank account is frozen, is not a criminal syndicate.
[P1.6] They lose protection while criminal networks adapt.
[P1.7] CON’s logic invites prior restraint: register before you hold, disclose keys, or use only approved intermediaries. That turns innocent self-custody into a permission slip.
