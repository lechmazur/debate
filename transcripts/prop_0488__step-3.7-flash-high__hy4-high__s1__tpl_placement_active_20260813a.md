[P1.1] Extreme concentration in global seed and agrochemical markets is a threat to food system resilience, the capacity of food systems to withstand climate shocks, supply chain disruptions, and crop pest outbreaks. [P1.2] This concentration does two catastrophic harms to resilience. First, it crowds out the diverse, regionally adapted seed varieties that are our best defense against shocks. When stem rust devastated wheat crops across East Africa in 2021, the only available resistant varieties were smallholder-developed open-pollinated lines, but the four dominant firms controlling 90% of seed distribution in the region refused to stock unpatented alternatives, leaving millions of farmers with no access to resilient seeds.

Splitting the top four seed and agrochemical conglomerates into independent, regionally focused firms would force competition on resilience metrics: developing locally adapted seeds, building redundant supply chains, and pricing inputs affordably, rather than extracting monopoly rents at the cost of global food security.

[C1.1] The breakup case confuses a concentration chart with a resilience plan. Resilience means getting tested seed, crop protection, and inputs to farms during droughts, pest outbreaks, and trade shocks. Forced separation can reduce market share on paper while scattering the assets that make fast response possible: long breeding pipelines, germplasm collections, multi-location trial data, regulatory files, seed multiplication contracts, and formulation capacity. A smaller firm with thinner inventories and no global trial network is not resilient; it is brittle when the shock arrives.
[C1.2] Nor does the asserted market power prove the proposed remedy. Competition does not magically fund slow, uncertain breeding for minor crops; capital-starved spinoffs would likely cut that work first. “Regionally focused firms” [P1.4] are worse for transboundary threats: wheat rust, fall armyworm, heat stress, and input disruptions ignore corporate borders. A resistance gene found in one country must still be screened, approved, multiplied, and shipped across others. Fragmenting that chain is an odd preparation for shocks that travel.
[C1.3] The examples offered do not bridge the gap either. If a company refuses to stock a public resistant line [P1.2], the defect is exclusionary dealing, solvable by conduct rules, compulsory licensing, and public procurement—not by dismembering research and agrochemical divisions. If prices spike in a fertilizer shock [P1.3], more corporate logos do not create phosphate, natural gas, freight, or buffer stocks. Breakup litigation and forced asset sales instead consume the managerial and state attention needed for emergency coordination and multi-year investment.
[C1.4] What protects resilience is redundancy and access: public breeding, local seed systems, transparent germplasm licensing, generic input supply, emergency stocks, regional multiplication, and penalties for exclusionary practices. Those tools address bottleneck failures directly. Breakups are slow, blunt, jurisdictionally messy, and irreversible; they target firm size rather than performance under stress. Governments should discipline abuses and build public and local capacity, not shatter the integrated capabilities required to deliver resilient seed and crop protection at scale.
