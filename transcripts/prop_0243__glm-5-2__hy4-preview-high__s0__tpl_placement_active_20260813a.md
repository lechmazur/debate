[P1.1] Ride-hail platforms didn't just arrive in major cities; they reshaped how those cities move, and they did so in a way that systematically adds vehicles to already saturated streets. The core case is simple: before ride-hail, a person taking transit generated zero new car trips. After ride-hail, that same person summons a single-occupancy vehicle that was very likely empty minutes before, will likely be empty again minutes after, and occupies road space for the entire trip plus the deadhead miles surrounding it.
[P1.2] Three mechanisms drive the worsening. First, deadheading: ride-hail drivers spend a large fraction of their working hours circulating without passengers, hunting fares or repositioning. Those are pure overhead—vehicle-miles with no transportation benefit, concentrated precisely where congestion is thickest. Second, mode shift: the most convenient substitute for a would-be rider isn't always transit; in city after city, ride-hail drew riders off buses, trains, and sidewalks and into backseats.
[P1.3] Every trip shifted from a shared vehicle to a private one multiplies road impact. Third, induced demand: the mere existence of cheap, frictionless rides generates trips that otherwise wouldn't happen—short hops, discretionary travel, journeys that would have been consolidated or skipped. A ride-hail vehicle is still a vehicle; it still occupies the intersection, the curb, and the lane.
[P1.4] The "overall" in the proposition matters because defenders will point to reduced drunk driving, first-mile/last-mile gaps, or car ownership declines. But even granting those, the balance sheet in major cities runs negative: more total vehicle-miles, more curb competition, more bottlenecks at peak hours, and more pressure on exactly the arterials that can't absorb it. The question isn't whether ride-hail moved people; it's whether moving them this way, at this density, made getting around worse for everyone else. On the evidence that matters—road time, congestion costs, and mode-shift away from higher-capacity options—ride-hail platforms made traffic in major cities worse overall.

[C1.1] The proposition must show ride-hail made congestion worse overall: net added delay versus what would have happened anyway. PRO's case in [P1.2] fails because it treats every ride-hail mile as new, as if the alternative were a city without cars. The right comparison is ride-hail versus the private car, taxi, or parking search it displaces. On that comparison, ride-hail often removes more road pressure than it adds.
[C1.2] Deadheading is not "pure overhead" with "no transportation benefit." Taxis cruised for fares before apps, and private cars burn time circling for parking—one of the largest sources of downtown congestion. App matching is imperfect, but it can cut aimless cruising by sending the nearest car.
[C1.3] More importantly, a ride-hail vehicle that replaces a personal car trip also removes that household's need to own, drive, and park a second vehicle. Private cars sit parked roughly 95 percent of the time yet impose their heaviest traffic cost while hunting for storage. One high-utilization ride-hail car can absorb trips that would otherwise be several private cars, each adding lane demand and parking search. In dense districts, a driver circling for parking can add more delay than a ride-hail car that stops once and leaves.
[C1.4] Mode shift and induced demand cut both ways. Some riders leave transit, but many trips substitute for driving, taxis, or rentals, and first-mile/last-mile rides make transit usable. So-called induced trips are often short, off-peak, or discretionary rides that would otherwise be driven. Major-city congestion tracks population and job growth, freight, delivery traffic, and flat road capacity—not one app.
[C1.5] Judge "overall" by congestion itself: travel time, speeds, reliability, not gross vehicle-miles. A city can add trips and still be less congested if ride-hail reduced ownership, parking search, and unsafe driving. Absent ride-hail, we would likely see more personally owned cars, more circling, and worse bottlenecks. PRO has not proved net harm.
