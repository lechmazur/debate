[P1.1] Fundamental science fails gracefully under short funding cycles; it succeeds spectacularally under patient money. That asymmetry is the whole debate.
[P1.2] Breakthroughs in fundamental science arrive on decade timescales, not three-year ones. CRISPR grew out of curiosity-driven microbiology on bacterial immune systems that had no application in sight when the work began. mRNA vaccine chemistry was a backwater for decades before it saved millions. Nobody writes "and then a pandemic will make this useful" into a grant application that a panel scores for feasibility. Short-cycle competitive grants systematically select for projects that can promise deliverables within the funding window — which is precisely the kind of project fundamental science usually isn't.
[P1.3] The grant mechanism itself filters out the science we actually need.
[P1.4] Worse, the competitive treadmill consumes the very resource it claims to allocate. Researchers spend enormous fractions of their time writing applications with success rates low enough that most effort is wasted by design, then retooling projects to match what panels will fund. Talented scientists become professional proposal-writers who do science in the gaps. And the incentive is to promise conservatively, publish incrementally, and never take the risky swing — because a failed ambitious project means unemployment at the next renewal.
[P1.5] A scientist on a ten-year block grant can spend three of those years on something that doesn't work, because that's what fundamental exploration looks like.
[P1.6] Yes, block grants trade away per-dollar accountability. But accountability to what? To milestones we cannot specify in advance, for questions we cannot yet frame? That isn't accountability; it's theater.
[P1.7] We would be optimizing the legibility of spending over the productivity of discovery — measuring the lamp post's light because that's where we can see.
[P1.8] The honest comparison is not "accountable versus unaccountable." It is "lots of documented busywork with modest discovery" versus "less paperwork with a real shot at the breakthroughs that competitive scoring structurally punishes." For fundamental science, patience is not a cost. It is the active ingredient.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to trade accountability for patience, but that bargain bankrupts both. Accountability is not paperwork theater; it is the mechanism by which a democratic society ensures that public money follows public value. Block grants sever that link. They create permanent fiefdoms where yesterday's winners collect tomorrow's checks regardless of results, shielded from the competition that forces excellence and weeds out dead ends.
[C1.2] Short-cycle competitive grants are not a treadmill that wastes talent; they are a selection filter. They allocate resources to the sharpest minds and the most promising questions *now*, not to incumbents who captured funding years ago. When a field stagnates, a hypothesis collapses, or a lab drifts into irrelevance, the competitive cycle lets money flow to new entrants and emerging paradigms. Block grants ossify.
[C1.3] They lock capital into existing power structures, making science less responsive to new evidence, new methods, and new talent. The cost of "less accountability per dollar" is not elegance; it is sclerosis.
[C1.4] The proposition claims we must choose between "documented busywork" and "breakthroughs," but this is a false dichotomy. Accountability per dollar is precisely what buys legitimacy for fundamental science. When taxpayers see peer-reviewed progress reports, milestone reviews, and competitive renewals, they see stewardship, not waste. Remove that visibility, and you invite the political backlash that starves science entirely.
[C1.5] The CRISPR and mRNA examples prove that patience pays—but they were funded within a competitive ecosystem that rewarded merit, allowed resources to concentrate where ideas proved fruitful, and permitted failure to be recognized and corrected. Strip out the competition, and you don't get more CRISPR; you get entrenched labs coasting on reputation while bold newcomers and risky new fields starve for lack of a hearing.
[C1.6] Fundamental science needs both patience and pressure. Block grants offer only the former, and the price is a science that answers to itself instead of to the society that funds it.

A block-grant regime gives the wrong incumbent the same protection as the right one for the same decade.
[C2.5] Finally, “accountability to results rather than paperwork” [P2.8] is empty before results exist. Fundamental results arrive late, are hard to attribute, and cannot steer money already spent. PRO asks taxpayers to underwrite a ten-year lottery and trust discovery to justify it afterward.
[C2.6] That is not patience; it is accountability postponed until it cannot matter.
[C2.7] The choice is sharper than PRO allows: if block grants are accountable per dollar, they are short-cycle competitive grants; if they are genuinely long-term blocks, they spend less accountability per dollar. Public science cannot afford the second, and the motion admits it.
