[P1.1] A sixteen-year-old can work a taxed job, leave school for training or employment, and in many places make medical decisions, join the armed forces with parental consent, or be held criminally responsible. She cannot, however, choose the government that sets her minimum wage, funds her college, and decides whether her town gets buses or youth services. That exclusion is not neutrality; it is rule by older voters over people with the longest stake in the outcome.
[P1.2] Competence does not justify the line at eighteen. Voting does not require expertise, driving-level reflexes, or the impulse control needed to resist a fight on a Saturday night. It requires cold, deliberative judgment: weighing values, reading a ballot, choosing between competing claims over time. That capacity is basically formed by mid-adolescence, especially when discussed in school and at home, not made under pressure.
[P1.3] Adults are not tested for news literacy, financial sophistication, or freedom from bias, and many would fail such a test. Singling out sixteen-year-olds for a standard we impose on no one else confuses imperfection with incapacity.
[P1.4] Lowering the age also makes democracy work better, not just fairer. Eighteen is the worst moment to start voting: leaving home, changing cities, starting work or university, re-registering amid chaos. Sixteen is stable. A first vote cast while living with family and enrolled in school can be taught, discussed, and made routine, building a habit that lasts.
[P1.5] Countries and cities that have tried sixteen-year-old voting have generally seen respectable turnout and no collapse in decision quality, suggesting fear outruns experience.
[P1.6] Finally, an electorate that excludes the young systematically underweights the future. Older, higher-turnout voters understandably prioritize pensions and immediate health costs; politicians respond. Education, housing supply, and climate action, whose costs and benefits stretch for decades, get delayed. Adding sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds will not let teenagers run the country, but it forces every candidate to answer, at least a little, to those who will live longest with the result.

[P2.5] To treat proximity as contamination would weaken every dependent adult's claim.

[P4.1] A1: Sixteen is not fixed by cold deliberation alone. It is where several things converge: that deliberative capacity is broadly formed by mid-adolescence, and many societies already assign sixteen-year-olds taxed work, choices about leaving school for training, and elements of medical and criminal responsibility. Fourteen typically sits earlier in that legal and educational transition. Sixteen is still a convention, but not an arbitrary one — it is a less exclusionary convention anchored in capacities and responsibilities that fourteen does not yet generally carry.
[P4.2] A2: Turnout and no collapse do not by themselves prove a lifetime habit. They corroborate an ordinary habit mechanism: a first real vote cast from a stable home and school, with discussion and reminders, is easier to repeat than a first vote amid moving, changing jobs, and re-registration at eighteen. Experience in places that have tried it also answers the competence objection. If sixteen-year-olds can understand one consequential ballot, CON needs a reason the same voter cannot understand another; election type changes salience, not the basic act of choosing representatives.
[P4.3] Together, those answers turn CON's standard against them. They defend eighteen as merely reasonable and conventional, yet demand that sixteen prove permanent habits and transformed policy before adoption. No franchise reform could meet that ex ante test. Sixteen meets the reasonable-basis test better: it is administrable, tied to recognised responsibility, and errs toward including people who will live longest under the result rather than excluding them for two more years.
[P4.4] The remaining objections cut against CON. If home and school discussion is assumed directive for teenagers but enriching for everyone else, independence becomes a presumption based on age, not evidence; the secret ballot then protects the actual choice. And a bloc small enough not to transform national policy cannot simultaneously threaten electoral quality. What remains is inclusion at the stablest starting point, for citizens already bearing consequences — not perfection, but a better democratic line than eighteen.
