[P1.1] The proposition is narrow: apps may sell ads, but they should not let sponsorship become so dominant that ordinary users cannot readily find cheaper, nearby independent restaurants. That line matters because these platforms are not just noticeboards; they are gatekeepers that decide which businesses get seen at the moment of purchase. The deciding question is whether users retain practical access to the full local market.
[P1.2] When a delivery app fills the first screen with sponsored chains and high-commission partners, a customer looking for an affordable meal is no longer choosing from the market. They are choosing from whatever bought placement. The cheaper independent noodle shop or family pizzeria may be closer and better-priced, but if it is buried under paid listings, search costs rise and users often settle for a more expensive option. That is especially true for users without time or transport to compare options offline.
[P1.3] That is a consumer harm: less choice, higher effective prices, and a misleading impression of what is available nearby.
[P1.4] It is also an anti-competitive harm. Independent restaurants cannot match chains’ marketing budgets or absorb large commissions and ad fees. If visibility is auctioned too aggressively, success depends less on food quality, price, or service and more on who can pay the platform. Over time, that squeezes small operators, reduces local diversity, and makes the market more concentrated.
[P1.5] Sponsorship can fund features, but the proposition does not require abolishing ads; it requires preventing a level of privileging that makes discovery difficult. Clear labeling, limits on paid slots, and a genuine option to sort by price, distance, or independent status preserve monetization while protecting the market.
[P1.6] The central test is simple: if a user cannot easily find cheaper nearby independents because the app has crowded them with sponsored results, the platform has traded public trust for ad revenue. That is why apps should not privilege sponsors that heavily.
