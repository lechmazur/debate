[P1.1] A functional market runs on a simple pact: the price tag measures the product, not the person buying it. This motion does not ban uniform supply-and-demand shifts, seasonal sales, or transparent student and senior discounts. It bans a specific predatory practice: using surveillance algorithms to profile individual shoppers and secretly charge each person the maximum they can be squeezed to pay.
[P1.2] First, personalized pricing destroys price discovery and competition. Markets only discipline sellers when prices are public signals that shoppers can compare. When every screen displays a private, algorithmically generated number, comparison shopping dies. Retailers no longer need to compete by lowering costs or improving quality; they compete on who can best profile buyers to extract every dollar of consumer surplus.
[P1.3] Without a common price, checkout stops being a transparent exchange and becomes an asymmetric ambush against a black-box supercomputer.
[P1.4] Second, targeting "willingness to pay" systematically punishes desperation rather than wealth. Defenders often imagine algorithms charging the rich more to subsidize the poor. In reality, an algorithm cannot distinguish affluence from urgency. To a pricing model, high willingness to pay simply means inelastic demand—and nobody has more inelastic demand than someone in a bind.
[P1.5] A parent searching late at night for fever medicine, a shopper buying supplies before a storm, or a low-income resident trapped in a retail desert registers high "willingness to pay" precisely because they lack options. Meanwhile, wealthier, tech-savvy shoppers have the time, transport, and digital tools to game or evade profiling.
[P1.6] Finally, allowing personalized pricing creates a toxic incentive for ubiquitous surveillance. If personal vulnerability directly increases profit margins, retailers are rewarded for harvesting every conceivable data point—from payday schedules and geolocation to browsing habits and device battery levels—just to weaponize it at checkout. Banning this practice preserves honest competition, stops the monetization of human urgency, and keeps the price tag on the item where it belongs.

[C1.1] The motion bans a tool because it might be abused, rather than regulating the abuse. That move fails three times.
[C1.2] First, PRO assumes personalization means secretly raising prices to each shopper's maximum. In competitive retail it mostly means the opposite: targeted discounts off a public list price. The sticker stays visible and comparable, so price discovery survives; the algorithm's job is closing sales that would otherwise be lost by shaving the price for the hesitant shopper who abandons the cart. That is the shopkeeper offering a wavering customer ten percent off — the oldest practice in commerce.
[C1.3] Coupons are willingness-to-pay pricing delivered by mail; this motion bans them only when a computer makes them efficient.
[C1.4] Second, the alternative to personalized pricing is not fairness — it is one high price. A retailer forced to post a single number sets it for the customers who will pay most, and the price-sensitive simply go without. Willingness-to-pay discrimination is exactly what lets sellers serve poorer customers at prices they can manage while earning margin elsewhere — the same logic PRO endorses in their own carve-out for student and senior discounts [P1.1]. That concession gives the game away: the harm is secrecy, not the mechanism.
[C1.5] So mandate disclosure; don't ban the mechanism.
[C1.6] Third, every villain in PRO's story has a narrower remedy. The fever-medicine parent [P1.5] is what anti-gouging laws target — directly, without criminalizing every loyalty discount in the country. The surveillance concern is a privacy problem: data harvesting stays profitable for advertising and inventory even under this ban, so the tracking survives while the discounts die. Monopoly extraction is an antitrust problem.
[C1.7] A ban that destroys pro-consumer discounting to reach harms better handled by gouging, privacy, and competition law is not consumer protection — it is consumer harm with a moralizing label. And it is unenforceable anyway: retailers will keep personalizing through opaque targeted coupons, less transparently and less auditably than a regulated practice conducted in the open.
