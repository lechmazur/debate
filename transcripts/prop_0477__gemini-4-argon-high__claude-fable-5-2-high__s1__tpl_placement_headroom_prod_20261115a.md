[P1.1] Vast majorities of intercontinental data traffic, including daily financial clearing, supply-chain logistics, and military intelligence, travel not through satellites, which possess only a sliver of global bandwidth capacity, but through fiber-optic cables lying largely unguarded on the ocean floor. A nation that buys gleaming squadrons of fighter jets and surface warships while leaving this digital nervous system exposed is building a fortress on sand.
[P1.2] Adversaries understand this asymmetry. In modern strategic competition, the most probable coercion is not a conventional blitzkrieg that triggers clear retaliation, but deniable gray-zone sabotage: a conveniently dragged anchor from a commercial vessel or a quiet submersible severing critical maritime chokepoints, as recent suspicious cuts in the Baltic and Red Sea illustrate. Visible, prestige defense procurements cannot deter this threat because a state cannot easily launch an airstrike over an unattributed seabed outage. Conversely, shifting capital into acoustic seabed sensors, domestic cable-repair fleets, and geographically dispersed redundant routes achieves deterrence by denial.
[P1.3] When an adversary sees that cutting a primary trunk line merely triggers instantaneous rerouting and rapid state-backed repair, sabotage loses its strategic leverage.
[P1.4] Trading a margin of visible hardware for subsea resilience actually multiplies overall defense capability. Modern militaries are entirely network-centric. Fifth-generation aircraft, missile-defense batteries, and naval task forces are operationally blinded without uninterrupted, high-capacity bandwidth connecting theaters of operation to homeland command centers and intelligence clouds. More fundamentally, no military can sustain a war if a coordinated cable blackout freezes its domestic banking system and paralyzes industrial mobilization on day one.
[P1.5] The fiscal trade-off is overwhelmingly favorable. Subsea protection and dark-fiber redundancy cost a fraction of marquee weapons programs. Foregoing just a few high-end combat platforms can fund comprehensive monitoring and repair capacity across entire maritime bottlenecks. Security requires protecting the vital arteries of the modern state before buying sharper armor.
