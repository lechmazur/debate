[P1.1] The market is very good at one thing: allocating resources to where they generate the most measurable return. But healthcare access is not a widget, and the market's blind spot is exactly what this motion asks us to correct. When a rural hospital closes, the spreadsheet looks efficient. On the ground, something else happens.
[P1.2] My central claim is simple: in emergencies, geography is destiny. Consolidation logic assumes patients can travel to the larger regional center. But a heart attack, a stroke, a hemorrhaging childbirth, a farm accident—these are conditions where survival is measured in the minutes before you reach care, not the quality of care once you arrive. A superb regional trauma center ninety minutes away saves fewer of these patients than a modest local hospital fifteen minutes away.
[P1.3] The market cannot price the deaths that occur in the ambulance, because those people never become customers of the efficient regional hub. That is a structural failure of market logic, not an incidental cost, and it is precisely what a subsidy exists to fix.
[P1.4] Second, the closure of a rural hospital is not a clean substitution—it is a cascade. When the hospital goes, the physicians leave, the pharmacy loses its anchor, maternity and emergency services vanish, and the town's economic viability erodes. You do not get "the same care, farther away." You get a medical desert.
[P1.5] Subsidy prevents a self-reinforcing collapse that no later intervention easily reverses.
[P1.6] Third, we already accept this principle everywhere else. We subsidize rural roads, rural mail, rural electricity, and rural fire service despite unfavorable per-capita economics, because we've decided citizenship shouldn't depend on your postal code. Emergency medicine belongs squarely in that category.
[P1.7] The burden I accept is real: I must show these benefits justify the public cost. I will. The comparison is not "efficient care versus wasteful care." It is "care that reaches people in time versus care optimized for an accountant who will never sit in the back of that ambulance."
[P1.8] When lives turn on distance, the government should keep the door open.
