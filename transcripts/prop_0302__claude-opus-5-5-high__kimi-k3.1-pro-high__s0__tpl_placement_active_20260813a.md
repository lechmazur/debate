[P1.1] A problem is structural when it is large, persistent, shows up nearly everywhere, and is produced by how the system works rather than by individual bad luck. Male underperformance in school meets every one of those tests. It should be judged by that standard, not by whether boys still occupy some corner offices.
[P1.2] Start with scale. Across most developed countries, girls outperform boys in reading by a margin that international assessments like PISA have shown for decades, often amounting to a meaningful fraction of a school year. Girls earn higher grades at nearly every level, in nearly every subject, including math and science. Boys are far more likely to be held back, suspended, expelled, or funneled into special education.
[P1.3] And the pipeline shows the cumulative damage: in the United States, women now earn roughly six in ten bachelor's degrees, and men who do enroll are less likely to finish.
[P1.4] Now persistence and breadth. These gaps aren't a single bad cohort or a single country's quirk. They appear across nations with very different cultures and school systems, and they have widened or held steady for a generation. When a pattern survives that much variation, the cause is built into the system, not scattered across individual boys.
[P1.5] Third, the mechanism. Schools reward early self-regulation, sitting still, and verbal fluency, traits that on average develop later in boys. A boy who is developmentally a year behind at six gets labeled a behavior problem, falls behind in reading, disengages, and by fourteen has concluded school isn't for him. That is a conveyor belt, not a coincidence.
[P1.6] Finally, who pays. The heaviest burden falls on working-class boys and boys from disadvantaged minority groups. Pointing to men at the top of the income distribution does nothing for the boy at the bottom of his class who is failing to read.
[P1.7] When girls were underrepresented in science, we treated that as structural and built targeted policy. Consistency demands the same seriousness here. Ignoring a gap this large, durable, and systemic isn't neutrality. It is neglect.
