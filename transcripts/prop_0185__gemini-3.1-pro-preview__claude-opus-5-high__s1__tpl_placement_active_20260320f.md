[P1.1] Leaving fiscal discipline to legislative discretion guarantees structural failure. Politicians face an inherent, inescapable conflict of interest: spending money wins elections, while raising taxes loses them. Consequently, lawmakers routinely run deficits even during economic booms, passing the bill to future generations. This systemic myopia inflates national debt, crowds out private investment, and increasingly diverts taxpayer revenue away from vital public services just to pay interest to bondholders.
[P1.2] To fix this, fiscal discipline must be enshrined in the constitution. A constitutional balanced-budget mandate fundamentally changes the rules of the game. It forces politicians to make honest trade-offs, requiring them to fund new spending through actual revenue rather than the hidden tax of endless borrowing.
[P1.3] Crucially, the motion before us includes a vital safeguard: exemptions for automatic stabilizers. The traditional argument against balanced-budget amendments is that they force governments into dangerous austerity during recessions, cutting spending exactly when the economy needs a lifeline. By exempting automatic stabilizers—like unemployment insurance and food assistance programs that naturally expand during a downturn—this policy neutralizes that risk. The government retains its ability to provide immediate, targeted macroeconomic stimulus precisely when it is needed, without relying on sluggish, politically fraught discretionary spending bills.
[P1.4] What we achieve is a structurally sound fiscal framework: strict discipline during economic expansions and guaranteed, predictable relief during recessions. Under the status quo, discretionary deficit spending is a blank check for political convenience, masking the true cost of government and inviting sovereign debt crises. By moving to a constitutional requirement with stabilizer exceptions, we eliminate the permanent political temptation to overborrow, protect our future economic sovereignty, and ensure that our safety nets remain fully functional when crises hit.

[C1.1] The proposition's safeguard is its fatal flaw. Automatic stabilizers are the shock absorbers for a pothole, not for driving off a cliff. In 2008 and again in 2020, unemployment insurance and food assistance expanded automatically — and it was nowhere near enough. What stopped a depression was discretionary action: bank recapitalizations, furlough and payroll-support schemes, emergency transfers to households and firms whose incomes vanished overnight.
[C1.2] None of that is an automatic stabilizer. A constitution that permits only stabilizer-driven deficits is a constitution that forbids the exact policies that saved the last two decades. Governments would face a choice between economic catastrophe and open constitutional violation — and they would choose violation, which teaches citizens that the constitution is a suggestion.
[C1.3] Second, ask who enforces this. A judge cannot write a budget. So enforcement runs through forecasts, and forecasts are the softest thing in government. A "balanced" budget is balanced on projected revenue, which ministries have every incentive to inflate.
[C1.4] The predictable result is not discipline but disguise: off-budget vehicles, state-owned enterprises loaded with debt, public-private partnerships that book costs decades out, unfunded mandates shoved onto cities and regions, and asset sales counted as income. Europe's fiscal rules were repeatedly breached, then suspended, then rewritten. Germany's debt brake did not eliminate borrowing; it produced special funds sitting beside the budget while bridges and rail decayed. Constitutionalizing the target moves the deception upstream and makes public accounts less honest, not more.
[C1.5] Third, a constitution is the wrong shelf for a macroeconomic theory. Constitutions are meant to be hard to change; fiscal doctrine needs to change as interest rates, demographics, and growth prospects change. And a strict annual balance treats a grid upgrade financed over forty years as identical to this year's payroll — punishing exactly the investment that makes future debt sustainable.
[C1.6] The choice is between a system that sometimes overborrows and a rule that, at the worst possible moment, either binds catastrophically or is openly broken. Discretion, disciplined by markets, fiscal councils, and voters, fails smaller.
