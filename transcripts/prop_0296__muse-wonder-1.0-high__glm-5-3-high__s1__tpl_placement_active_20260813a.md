[P1.1] A street name is not a history lesson. It is a plaque the state asks people to live under, learn under, and celebrate under. When that plaque primarily honors a colonizer, a slaveholder, or a dictator, keeping it is not neutrality — it is a continuing public endorsement.
[P1.2] That distinction between remembering and honoring decides this debate. History belongs in museums, archives, classrooms, and memorials where context, victims, and debate are possible. Honor is different. A school named for a Confederate slaveholder does not teach a child about slavery; it puts that man’s name on her report card.
[P1.3] A national holiday for a colonizer does not invite reflection; it instructs citizens to celebrate. A dictator’s avenue does not preserve the past; it keeps his claim to public space alive long after his victims can object.
[P1.4] Democratic governments, unlike empires and dictatorships, owe equal standing to all citizens. Many of these names were imposed to deny that standing — under colonial rule, segregation, or authoritarian intimidation. Historical continuity therefore is not an innocent value here. It is continuity with a decision made without, and often against, the people now forced to repeat the name in their addresses, school chants, and civic holidays.
[P1.5] Renaming does not erase history, as defenders imagine, and we know this from ordinary practice. Cities rename streets for new leaders, disasters, and development all the time without losing the ability to teach what came before. Post-Franco Spain could remove regime symbols while debating Franco more openly, not less. Germany has no Adolf Hitler schools to help students learn about Nazism; it teaches the horror more seriously because public honor is reserved for its victims and its democracy.
[P1.6] The motion is also carefully limited. It does not target every flawed figure or every old name. It targets places where the primary public meaning is domination: conquest, enslavement, or tyranny. When that is what a name celebrates, the state should stop celebrating it.

[C1.2] Nearly every nation's founding generation fails the test this motion sets. Washington and Jefferson held slaves. Churchill presided over colonial famines. Belgium's streets honor a king who presided over atrocities in the Congo.
[C1.3] If the principle is that governments should strip honors from slaveholders and colonizers, the principle does not stop at the easy cases — it devours the founders of democracies themselves. PRO will say the motion only targets names whose "primary public meaning is domination," but who decides that? A name like Washington's carries a hundred meanings, and the committee that keeps its job by finding one more disgrace will never run out of work. Once renaming becomes an official instrument of moral judgment, it becomes a political weapon, swung by whichever faction holds power this decade — and reversed by the next.
[C1.4] That is not reconciliation; it is a perpetual culture war conducted through maps.
[C1.5] Notice also what renaming does not do. It moves no wealth, funds no schools, returns no land, compensates no victims. It is the cheapest possible gesture of redress, which is precisely why governments love it. Spain and Germany teach the horrors of their pasts seriously through education, law, and memorial — not because they swapped street signs.
[C1.6] Meanwhile, continuity of names carries real civic value: shared reference points that let a community argue about its past together, rather than watch each era erase the previous one's choices and call that progress.
[C1.7] The alternative to renaming is not endorsement; it is contextualization — plaques, honest curricula, museums, as PRO itself concedes. The state can teach everything about a name without pretending the only two options are celebration or deletion.
[C1.8] Governments should keep the names and do the harder work.
