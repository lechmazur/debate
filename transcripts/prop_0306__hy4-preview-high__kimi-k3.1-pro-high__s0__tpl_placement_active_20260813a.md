[P1.1] The test is clear: if immersive digital tools and habitat protection can deliver conservation and education with less captivity, then keeping wild animals in zoos is no longer ethically justified. That test is met.
[P1.2] Conservation is the weaker leg for zoos. Habitat loss, not a shortage of captive births, drives extinction. Protected areas, community conservancies, and anti-poaching work attack the cause; a zoo enclosure does not. Captive breeding helps a handful of flagship species, yet reintroduction is slow, expensive, and often fails to establish self-sustaining wild populations.
[P1.3] Meanwhile the space and money absorbed by a single large-mammal exhibit could secure far more habitat. Zoos may donate to field projects, but that simply proves habitat protection is doing the conservation; the cages are not.
[P1.4] Education is no rescue. A visitor’s typical zoo experience is a glimpse of an animal lying down, a sign, and a gift shop. Empathy does not require proximity to captivity. High-definition livestreams, virtual reality, and interactive simulations let students follow a real herd’s migration, hear forest soundscapes, and see predation, disease, and drought without confining anyone.
[P1.5] We no longer need to keep orcas in concrete tanks to teach ocean ecology; a remotely operated camera in a marine reserve shows hunting, play, and family bonds more truthfully. Digital tools reach millions, including classrooms that could never travel to a zoo, and they can be updated as science changes.
[P1.6] The ethical remainder is the animals themselves. Captivity imposes restricted movement, social disruption, boredom, and, for many species, shortened lives. Even enriched enclosures are simplifications of ecosystems. Once we can pursue the same conservation and educational aims with materially less captivity, continuing to confine sentient animals is not a tragic necessity; it is a choice to retain captivity when we no longer need to.
[P1.7] A society that can protect habitats and teach through screens and field trips has no ethical warrant to keep prisoners as teaching aids. That is why the proposition stands.

[C1.1] The proposition proves, at most, that zoos must change. It does not prove that they have lost ethical justification. “Less captivity” does not mean “no justified captivity,” and a digital substitute for part of education is not a substitute for an entire conservation system.
[C1.2] PRO treats conservation as habitat protection alone. Habitat is indispensable, but protection cannot always rescue a population already reduced to a handful of animals, devastated by disease, or facing threats that cannot immediately be removed. Managed breeding helped bring species such as the California condor and black-footed ferret back from the brink. These programs did not compete with habitat recovery; they preserved the animals while recovery became possible.
[C1.3] Zoos contribute not merely enclosures, but veterinary expertise, genetic management, husbandry, emergency capacity, and reintroduction experience. When a species’ habitat is insecure, the ethical response is to secure it—not discard the insurance population first.
[C1.4] A livestream can reveal a migration; it cannot maintain a viable breeding population, rehabilitate injured wildlife, or provide animals for reintroduction. Nor does digitizing education make zoos redundant. Modern zoos can combine living encounters with livestreams, simulations, and field-based learning. Digital access is valuable, especially for distant classrooms, but it supplements rather than demonstrates the worthlessness of direct encounters.
[C1.5] PRO’s portrait of every visit as an inert animal, a sign, and a gift shop simply assumes away the educational practices modern institutions can provide.
[C1.6] Captivity undeniably imposes moral costs. It therefore demands excellent welfare, meaningful conservation purposes, and willingness to close inadequate facilities. But those costs are not automatically decisive against every institution. Preventing suffering and irreversible extinction also carries moral weight.
[C1.7] Nor is every zoo dollar naturally transferable to habitat protection: visitor revenue, specialist staff, and long-term institutional capacity do not become equivalent field conservation merely by closing the gates.
[C1.8] The real choice is not cages versus forests. It is whether carefully justified captivity, alongside forests and screens, can prevent losses that neither forests nor screens can prevent alone. Where it can, abandoning that tool is not ethical progress; it is moral purity [clipped]

[P2.2] Con is right that a screen cannot breed a condor; the alternative pairs habitat protection with closed breeding centers, not public cages. The expertise Con prizes—veterinary care, genetics, reintroduction—can be housed in those centers, field stations, and habitat agencies without turning animals into exhibits. That is the decisive terrain: the proposition is about zoos as education and conservation attractions, not about whether any captivity anywhere could ever be useful.
[P2.3] On education, Con says screens supplement encounters. Supplement, perhaps—but the proposition asks whether they can do the job with less captivity, and they can. Livestreams of hunts, migrations, and droughts teach ecology more accurately than a pacing animal in an enclosure. Modern zoos can add VR and cameras only by caging animals for visitors; detach the tools from the cages and deploy them in schools, museums, and reserves.
[P2.4] The cage is not pedagogically necessary; it is an entertainment choice.
[P2.5] Con's funding point proves too little. If zoo revenue depends on displaying animals, then the money is tied to exhibition, not conservation. [P2.6] "Less captivity" is not a rhetorical trick [C1.1]; it is the ethical test. Con's "alongside" model asks us to keep the cages and add better tools. [C2.1] PRO’s rebuttal concedes the decisive point: some captive conservation remains necessary. Their alternative is not captivity-free; it is captivity relocated behind closed doors. A “closed breeding center” still confines animals, manages breeding, and restricts choices. If conservation purposes and good welfare can justify that, the question becomes whether public display adds unjustified harm.
[C2.2] PRO never shows that suitable animals in well-designed public habitats necessarily fare worse than animals in nonpublic facilities. Removing visitors is not, by itself, a welfare improvement.
[C2.3] The observation that most displayed animals are not bred for release also proves less than PRO needs. Conservation value cannot be measured by counting releases alone: assurance populations, specialist expertise, field support, and public engagement can matter even when a particular animal will never be reintroduced. Where animals already require lifelong human care, educational display need not mean breeding additional animals for exhibition. PRO treats every exhibit as an animal captured for amusement; that is an assertion, not an analysis of modern zoo practice.
[C2.4] On education, PRO simply declares equivalent outcomes. A livestream can reveal ecological processes more fully; it does not follow that it produces equivalent attention, emotional connection, or conservation commitment for every audience. Conversely, a poor zoo visit does not indict a well-designed one. The honest comparison is not PRO’s ideal reserve camera against a caricatured gift shop, but integrated digital and direct learning against digital learning alone.
[C2.5] PRO has demonstrated a valuable supplement and sometimes a substitute—not that living encounters add no justifiable educational value.
[C2.6] Nor does calling revenue “tied to exhibition” answer the funding argument. Visitor income and audiences are real resources; saying veterinarians could work elsewhere does not show that budgets, facilities, and public support would automatically follow them.
[C2.7] Thus PRO establishes duties to reduce unsuitable captivity, improve welfare, and invest in habitats and digital access. It does not establish that modern zoos lack ethical justification. “Less captivity” is a direction of reform, not proof that justified public conservation institutions must disappear.
