[P1.1] When a doctor prescribes a drug, she believes she is acting on the full evidence. Too often, she is acting on a curated highlight reel. Pharmaceutical companies have every incentive to publish trials that succeed and quietly shelve trials that fail — and decades of experience show they do exactly that. The result is a medical literature systematically biased toward making products look safer and more effective than they are.
[P1.2] Requiring public disclosure of negative results as a condition of market access fixes this at the only leverage point that reliably works.
[P1.3] Three pillars anchor the case. First, informed consent is impossible without complete information. Patients in failed trials accepted real risks — needles, side effects, placebo arms — on the implicit promise that their participation would advance knowledge. Burying their results betrays them twice: it wastes their sacrifice and endangers future patients.
[P1.4] The history of antidepressants illustrates the stakes: when unpublished trials were finally examined, the apparent benefit of several drugs shrank dramatically, and pediatric suicide-risk signals that had sat in company files came to light only after years of prescriptions.
[P1.5] Second, hidden negatives corrupt the entire scientific enterprise. Researchers repeat failed experiments, payers reimburse weaker drugs over stronger ones, and meta-analyses — the gold standard for guidelines — are built on a skewed sample. You cannot do evidence-based medicine on half the evidence.
[P1.6] Third, the mechanism matters. Voluntary registries and journal pledges have been tried; compliance stays poor because there is no real penalty. Tying disclosure to market access aligns incentives perfectly: the company that profits from selling the drug bears the cheap, simple duty of posting all its results. No disclosure, no market.
[P1.7] That is not a burden — it is the price of asking the public to trust your product.
[P1.8] Expect arguments about trade secrets and regulatory cost. Note what they share: they ask patients to bear hidden risks so firms can keep convenient silence. That trade should be unacceptable in any country that takes medicine seriously.

[C1.4] Then there's the competitive reality. Drug development costs billions and fails nine times out of ten. [C1.5] A country that demands more transparency than its neighbors risks becoming a market entry afterthought rather than a research priority.
[C1.6] Finally, "negative results" is not a clean category. A trial can fail its primary endpoint while generating valuable exploratory data. [C1.7] Transparency matters. But this mechanism punishes patients, exports R&D, and assumes a clarity that doesn't exist. There are better ways.
