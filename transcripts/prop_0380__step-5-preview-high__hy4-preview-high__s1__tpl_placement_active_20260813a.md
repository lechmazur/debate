[P1.1] The age of majority is a convenience, not a cognitive threshold. We let sixteen-year-olds drive on public roads, hold jobs, pay taxes, and in many places face adult criminal responsibility. Yet we tell them their voice in electing the government that writes those laws, sets those tax rates, and shapes their climate future must wait. That is not a principled line; it is an arbitrary one.
[P1.2] At sixteen, the capacities that matter for voting are already in place. Cold, logical reasoning and knowledge acquisition are largely mature. What still develops in late adolescence is psychosocial maturity under peer pressure and emotional heat—capacities relevant to impulse control in the moment, not to the slow, reflective act of choosing a representative. Voting is not a barroom brawl; it is a considered civic judgment.
[P1.3] Treating sixteen-year-olds as unfit to deliberate while trusting them to drive, work, and pay taxes exposes the maturity argument as a pretext, not a reason.
[P1.4] Lowering the voting age also repairs a structural flaw in our democracy. Eighteen is precisely the moment of maximum transition—leaving home, starting work or university—when voting habits are hardest to form. Sixteen-year-olds are still embedded in stable civic institutions: school, family, community. Enfranchising them at that moment builds a durable habit of participation rather than a one-off scramble at eighteen.
[P1.5] Countries that have moved in this direction have not descended into chaos; they have widened the circle of stakeholders.
[P1.6] The opposing view rests on protecting democracy from the uninformed. But exclusion is not protection; it is disenfranchisement. A teenager working a shift, paying sales tax, and breathing the air our climate policy will inherit has a more direct stake in elections than many passive adult voters. The franchise is not a reward for reaching a birthday.
[P1.7] It is a recognition of stake and capacity. Both point to sixteen.
