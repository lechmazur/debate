[P1.1] Critical public functions should never depend on infrastructure a government does not control. When states route sensitive AI workloads through private cloud giants or foreign data centers, they hand the keys to national capacity to boards and geopolitical rivals who owe them nothing. Sovereign compute is not a luxury; it is the minimum requirement for strategic autonomy in the twenty-first century. A nation that cannot run its own defense, health, or emergency models on hardware it governs is not digitally independent; it is a tenant.
[P1.2] The vulnerability is concrete and severe. A foreign provider can suspend service overnight in response to sanctions, trade disputes, or simple corporate restructuring. During a pandemic, a cyberattack, or a natural disaster, the algorithms coordinating hospital triage, power grids, or food distribution cannot freeze because a terms-of-service clause changed or because a server farm sits across a border that just closed. Private clouds optimize for quarterly revenue, not for guaranteed access during existential crises.
[P1.3] Sovereign capacity ensures continuity when markets panic. Lives are measured in minutes of grid failure or delayed medicine.
[P1.4] Beyond survival, there is alignment. Public-interest AI—climate modeling, social-welfare fraud detection, epidemiological forecasting—often lacks the profit margins that drive private investment. Left to the market, these workloads face deprioritization, opaque pricing, and vendor lock-in that siphons public funds into perpetual rent. Domestic infrastructure keeps both the data and the strategic upside under democratic law, subject to citizens rather than shareholders.
[P1.5] It also cultivates domestic engineers who serve the public interest rather than extracting rent.
[P1.6] The opponent will protest cost. But the alternative is worse: a state that cannot guarantee its own emergency response, that ships citizen data to jurisdictions it cannot regulate, and that mortgages its AI future to the highest bidder. Building sovereign compute is expensive; surrendering sovereignty is catastrophic. We choose security over subscription, resilience over rent, and public control over precarious convenience.
