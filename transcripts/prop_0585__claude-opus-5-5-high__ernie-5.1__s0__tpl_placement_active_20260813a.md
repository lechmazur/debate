The decisive constraint on reliability and decarbonization is the mismatch in time—weeks of low wind, days of cloud cover—that no amount of interconnection can erase. [C1.2] First, speed and certainty. Building a high‑voltage line from the Plains to the East Coast routinely takes a decade, tangled in permitting, land‑use lawsuits, and NIMBY opposition. By contrast, utility‑scale iron‑air or flow‑battery projects can break ground in two to three years and scale in modular blocks. When the climate deadline is 2035, we cannot wait for a transmission corridor that may never be built; we must deploy firm, clean capacity now.
[C1.3] Second, resilience to climate shocks. Transmission corridors are themselves climate‑vulnerable: wildfires, ice storms, hurricanes can knock out the very lines the proposition relies on. Distributed storage sits behind the meter or at substations, immune to a single line failure. After Texas's 2021 freeze, the state's isolated grid suffered not because it lacked interconnection, but because it lacked on‑site, dispatchable power that could have kept the lights on.
[C1.4] Third, cost dynamics. Storage technologies follow a learning curve similar to solar: each doubling of capacity has driven costs down roughly twenty percent. Targeted subsidies accelerate that curve, creating a self‑reinforcing market that eventually makes storage cheaper than building new transmission. Transmission, by contrast, offers diminishing returns; each new line serves a smaller marginal load as the easiest corridors fill.
[C1.5] Finally, firm clean capacity. Even a perfectly integrated market cannot guarantee power when the whole continent is wind‑still for a week. Long‑duration storage provides that firm, zero‑carbon resource, eliminating the need for fossil peakers and unlocking higher renewable penetration. The primary investment must go where the bottleneck is—time, not distance.
