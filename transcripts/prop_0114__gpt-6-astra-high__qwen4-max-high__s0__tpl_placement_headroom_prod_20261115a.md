[P1.1] When a worker cuts paid hours to care for a parent with dementia, society still receives essential work. Governments should stop treating necessary care as an empty space in a working life.
[P1.2] The policy is straightforward: publicly funded pension or social-insurance credits for periods of substantial, verified care for a dependent family member. Credits should protect a basic level of entitlement, reflect reductions in paid work, and be available regardless of gender or marital status. This is not an unlimited salary for every household task; it is protection against a specific gap in social insurance.
[P1.3] The central justification is that contribution is not synonymous with a paycheck. Feeding, supervising, and assisting someone who cannot live independently meets a real social need. Without family care, some of that work would require paid provision; some would simply go undone. Recognizing it does not require pretending every hour saves the treasury money.
[P1.4] It requires recognizing that caregivers are doing useful work, not merely choosing leisure.
[P1.5] Credits also reduce a predictable injustice: people who shoulder care, disproportionately women, can emerge from years of responsibility with weaker independent retirement rights. Leaving them dependent on a spouse or later poverty assistance is not fiscal neutrality. It shifts the consequences of a shared social need onto the person who answered it.
[P1.6] Cost and employment concerns justify careful design, not invisibility. Cap credits, verify eligibility, and preserve access to formal care so families retain choices. Credits cannot replace childcare, disability services, or respite; they address the retirement penalty those services do not always prevent.
