[P1.1] Solitary confinement beyond fifteen consecutive days is not discipline. It is the deliberate use of isolation to break a person psychologically, and it should be abolished as torture.
[P1.2] Picture the practice plainly: a person locked alone in a small cell for 22 or more hours a day, meals through a slot, exercise alone in a cage, no meaningful conversation, work, education, or touch. Days blur without natural markers. That is not merely being alone; it is engineered sensory and social starvation imposed on someone powerless to leave.
[P1.3] The human mind predictably deteriorates under those conditions. Anxiety, insomnia, panic, depression, paranoia, hallucinations, and self-mutilation are common responses, not aberrations. People with prior mental illness decompensate fastest, young people are especially vulnerable, and many emerge with lasting trauma, impaired cognition, and an inability to tolerate ordinary social life. A punishment that foreseeably drives people toward breakdown and suicide, and leaves damage after the cell door opens, meets the core of psychological torture: severe mental suffering inflicted by the state on a captive.
[P1.4] It also fails on its own claimed terms. Extended isolation does not produce safer prisons or safer communities. It removes every opportunity to practice self-control, resolve conflict, or earn trust, then returns a more agitated, withdrawn, and dysregulated person to a general population or to the street. Short-term separation to stop an immediate fight is one thing; warehousing someone for weeks until they deteriorate is the opposite of rehabilitation or deterrence.
[P1.5] Abolition beyond fifteen days does not require leaving staff defenseless. A dangerous person can be separated, closely supervised, restrained during an emergency, and placed in a secure unit — with daily human contact, mental-health care, out-of-cell time, regular review, and a step-down plan. What it forbids is using total isolation itself as the tool.
[P1.6] Fifteen days is therefore a hard ceiling, not a target. Beyond it, justification fades and cruelty becomes the method. A system that knows this harm and continues anyway is not maintaining order; it is torturing by calendar.

[C1.1] The fifteen-day line is a slogan dressed as a standard. It doesn't ask who the prisoner is, what he did, what threat he poses, or what conditions exist inside the cell. It doesn't weigh safety on either side of the wall. It just counts days.
[C1.2] A gang coordinator running a smuggling operation from his bunk, an inmate who has just attacked three officers in a week, a witness who must be separated for his own survival — these cases have nothing in common with each other, and a single calendar cutoff helps none of them.
[C1.3] More importantly, "torture" is not a synonym for "harmful." Under any serious legal or moral definition, torture is severe suffering inflicted for a forbidden purpose — to break the will, extract a confession, or punish dissent. Managed isolation, even for weeks, with mental-health screening, daily human contact, meaningful review, and a documented security justification, is not that. It can be cruel.
[C1.4] It can be overused. It can leave lasting harm. But the category of torture requires an intent and a moral equivalence this motion has not earned, and collapsing the two makes the word useless the next time a regime actually does torture someone.
[C1.5] The alternatives the proposition offers are not as clean as they sound. What does a warden do on day sixteen with an inmate who has already slashed three cellmates and threatened two more? Return him to general population and accept the next stabbing? Handcuff a violently resistant inmate to a chair for the rest of the day?
[C1.6] Solitary is sometimes the least-worst tool available in a violent, understaffed, real-world prison. Abolition removes that option without supplying one that actually works under pressure.
[C1.7] The right reform is tighter statutory limits, independent judicial review, better conditions inside the cell, meaningful step-down programs, and expanded mental-health staffing — not a bright-line ban that will not survive contact with the next genuine crisis inside a real prison.
