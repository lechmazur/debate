[P1.1] A job should come with wages and responsibilities, not a claim on the worker’s next job. Yet noncompetes let employers control people after the employment relationship—and after the paycheck—has ended. The right default is therefore simple: workers may leave and compete; employers must protect legitimate interests with tools targeted at those interests, not with a general ban on earning a living.
[P1.2] The harm begins before any lawsuit. Many workers cannot afford to litigate whether a clause is technically enforceable, so a threatening contract is enough to keep them in place. A nurse barred from nearby hospitals, a technician excluded from an entire industry, or a software engineer told not to join a vaguely defined “competitor” loses leverage even when staying put. Wages, conditions, and career mobility suffer because the employer no longer has to outcompete other employers for that person’s labor.
[P1.3] Broad employer exceptions magnify this imbalance: categories such as “confidential information,” “customer relationships,” or “specialized training” can describe ordinary experience in almost any skilled job.
[P1.4] Those interests do not justify noncompetes. Genuine trade secrets can be protected through confidentiality obligations and trade-secret law; customer lists through nonsolicitation terms; expensive, unusual training through reasonable repayment agreements. These tools target the threatened asset while preserving the worker’s ability to use general skill and knowledge. If an employer says narrower tools are harder to enforce, that is not a reason to impose the broader restraint.
[P1.5] It often means the employer wants the cheap certainty of stopping competition altogether rather than proving actual misuse.
[P1.6] The comparison is not worker freedom versus economic chaos. It is targeted protection versus contractual labor-market lock-in. When talented people can move, start firms, and join rivals, knowledge circulates and employers must compete through better pay and management. Nearly universal restriction also prevents the exceptions from swallowing the rule: any truly extraordinary case must be narrowly justified, not presumed across whole occupations or industries.
[P1.7] Government should stop employers from purchasing immunity from competition in the fine print of an employment contract.

[C1.1] Noncompetes are not sold in a vacuum; they are a priced term in a bargain. The engineer who signs one gets the lab, the roadmap, and the salary premium; the dental associate gets the patient book and the training. PRO treats that term as pure extraction and would ban it for nearly every worker. "Nearly all" is the fatal phrase, because legitimate noncompetes are not rare exceptions; they are ordinary value-creation.
[C1.2] First, noncompetes buy investment. Firms train people, share clients, and open know-how only when assured the investment won't walk across the street in month eighteen. Research links enforceable noncompetes to more firm-sponsored training; remove the assurance, and rational firms share less, train less, and pay less. A universal ban doesn't free workers from the bargain — it prevents the bargain.
[C1.3] Second, the term is priced. Workers who sign noncompetes knowingly, up front, tend to earn more; research on physicians finds they pay more, with no clear harm to patients. A ban doesn't transfer value to workers; it confiscates a chip they could sell. You cannot be paid for exclusivity the law forbids you to offer.
[C1.4] Third, the narrower tools in [P1.4] are strictly weaker. Trade-secret suits demand proof of actual misuse that is almost impossible to obtain — the secret is knowledge in a head, not a file you can photograph. Nonsolicitation cannot stop clients from following the person they trust; patients aren't bound to the practice. The result is more trade-secret litigation, a game only big legal departments win.
[C1.5] Large firms will substitute golden handcuffs and deferred compensation; startups cannot. Near-universal restriction is regressive across firms.
[C1.6] A filter already exists: courts void clauses overbroad in time, geography, or scope; the sandwich-shop horror stories are already unenforceable. The real question is the default — "enforceable when reasonable," or "banned for nearly all." Abuse argues for sharper filters and penalties, not for abolishing a tool that funds training and pays the very workers who sign it.

[P4.1] A1: Because confidentiality protects information, while a noncompete excludes the worker from a job. Those are different restraints with different scopes. A confidentiality obligation can leave someone free to join a rival, use general skills, and earn wages immediately; it prohibits disclosure or misuse, not employment itself. Overbroad confidentiality drafting should also be confined to genuinely protected information, but no legal tool makes abusive drafting impossible.
[P4.2] The relevant comparison is whether the default leaves a viable next job. Targeted protection does; a noncompete deliberately does not.
[P4.3] A2: The employer must justify an exceptional restraint under narrow criteria set in advance, and the employer—not the worker—bears that burden. Most importantly, ordinary workers need not litigate anything to receive the motion’s protection: their clauses are restricted by default. CON treats any possible exceptional proceeding as if it recreated today’s system for everyone. It does the opposite.
[P4.4] A rare, demanding justification cannot become an occupation-wide exception, and uncertainty is shifted away from the person least able to afford it.
[P4.5] That answers CON’s “clear fence” argument too. A visible eighteen-month fence is still eighteen months barred from using one’s livelihood. Its clarity may help an employer issue a credible threat; it does not supply the missing proof of misuse. And noncompetes are hardly litigation-free: workers can still dispute whether an employer, market, or role counts as “competitive.”
[P4.6] The remaining clash is therefore not certainty versus fog. It is whether employers may impose the cheaper restraint merely because proving a narrower wrong is difficult. CON’s strongest examples—merger plans, equity, genuinely negotiated executive terms—show why extraordinary justification may remain available, not why ordinary hiring contracts should carry broad exceptions. Investment can be protected around the asset; mobility should be restricted only around an extraordinary necessity.
[P4.7] CON keeps asking every worker to surrender outside options so exceptional bargains remain easy. PRO makes those bargains justify themselves while restoring competition for everyone else’s labor.
