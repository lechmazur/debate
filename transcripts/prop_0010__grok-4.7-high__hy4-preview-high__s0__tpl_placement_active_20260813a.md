[P1.1] Workplace secrecy has a legitimate place. Trade secrets and unfinished products can stay confidential. Harassment, discrimination, and abuse are different. They are harms done to people, often unlawful, and the public has a direct stake in their exposure.
[P1.2] An NDA that buys silence about those harms should not be something a court will enforce.
[P1.3] The mechanism is simple. A worker is harassed or mistreated. The employer offers money only if the worker agrees never to describe what happened. Under financial pressure and already harmed, the worker signs.
[P1.4] The perpetrator keeps the job, the reputation, and a clean record. The next hire walks in blind. Repeated across workplaces, that is not peaceful resolution. It is a market for impunity.
[P1.5] Unenforceability is the right fix because it is narrow and it respects real choice. No one is forced to speak. A survivor who wants privacy can stay silent. What disappears is the damages threat and the injunction that turn a private preference into a legally compelled gag.
[P1.6] If silence is genuinely voluntary, it needs no lawsuit to sustain it. The clauses that require a court are the ones used to punish speech after the fact.
[P1.7] Settlements will not collapse. Employers settle because liability, evidence, and reputation already create pressure, not because a judge will forever police a victim’s mouth. Parties can still agree not to volunteer details. They simply cannot conscript the state to punish a later decision to warn others.
[P1.8] Ordinary business confidentiality remains intact. Only secrecy about harassment, discrimination, and abuse loses the force of law.
[P1.9] Public policy already refuses to enforce contracts that conceal serious wrongdoing or defeat protective rights. These cases belong in that category. Courts should not be the enforcement arm of workplace abuse.

[C1.1] To forbid enforceable NDAs in cases involving workplace harassment, discrimination, or abuse is to misunderstand why victims sign them. They are not, in the main, state-imposed gags. They are contracts that let a victim convert a painful, uncertain grievance into guaranteed money, a positive reference, and closure on her own timetable.
[C1.2] PRO says "No one is forced to speak" [P1.5]. But stripping enforceability does not leave choice untouched; it removes what makes the choice valuable: the ability to sell silence for compensation. An employer pays precisely because confidentiality has legal teeth. Remove the remedy for breach, and settlement offers drop or vanish.
[C1.3] A harassed server may accept $60,000 under confidentiality rather than $5,000 for a bare release and a public fight; void the clause, and the employer simply keeps the difference. Victims lose the exit ramp from litigation. Instead of a guaranteed settlement, they face a trial with discovery into their emails, dress, and relationships—or accept a nuisance-value release. That is not empowerment.
[C1.4] It transfers value from victims to employers and lawyers.
[C1.5] The motion is also wildly overbroad. It voids confidentiality in any case merely "involving" discrimination—a performance dispute ended by a general release, mutual non-disparagement protecting both sides, the settlement amount, even a deal a victim begged for to spare her family publicity. We do not need this blunt instrument. Doctrines of duress, unconscionability, and public policy already void gag clauses used to conceal crime or block reporting to the EEOC or police.
[C1.6] The narrow fix exists; the nuclear option is unnecessary.
[C1.7] The state’s job is to punish coercion, not to leave victims unable to bind their own silence for value. Enforceable NDAs give victims leverage, privacy, and a remedy on the timeline they choose; unenforceability strips the remedy and the leverage. Keep NDAs enforceable, and victims keep the bargaining chip that buys them a future.

[C2.2] PRO says only the “increment that purchases impunity” vanishes [P2.2]. But there is usually no clean line to sever. The consideration is the package: cash now, no admission, confidentiality later. Strip enforceability, and the employer discounts what it cannot control.

[C2.4] A survivor can decline interviews [P2.4], but she cannot sell a silence the law will not enforce. The value of confidentiality is precisely that it binds; without teeth it is not “the ability to sell silence for compensation” [C1.2]—it is nothing. Unenforceability does not hand the victim her timetable; it gives leverage to whoever can wait, usually the employer that litigates or the public that gets her story for free.
[C2.5] The motion is still overbroad. PRO protests it targets “that secrecy, not every employment settlement” [P2.7], but it voids NDAs in any case merely “involving” discrimination or abuse: confidentiality over the amount, mutual non-disparagement, even mixed performance settlements. Existing doctrines already strike gag terms used to conceal crime or block EEOC or police reporting [C1.5]. If those carve-outs are incomplete, strengthen them: protect truthful warnings to regulators or coworkers where danger is shown.
[C2.6] That is surgery. Abolishing enforceability in every “involving” case destroys the victim’s chip and rewards whoever can outlast her.
