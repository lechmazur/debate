[P1.1] When an aging parent cannot pay for basic care, the cost does not disappear. It lands on the parent as unmet need, on taxpayers, or on whichever adult child is willing to shoulder it. A legally enforceable duty simply decides that adult children with the capacity to help must contribute according to their means before the parent is left in avoidable hardship or the public is forced to absorb the whole bill. This is not a command to move home, abandon careers, or bankrupt oneself; it is a proportionate contribution duty with hardship, abuse, and estrangement exceptions, and a public backstop for what no family can bear.
[P1.2] The core principle is reciprocity. Parents routinely sacrifice income, time, and security to raise children; a decent society does not treat that as a one-way transfer that ends the moment the child turns eighteen. Nor is this alien to law: child support and spousal support already make family dependency a legal matter when someone cannot provide for themselves. Adult children are often the closest people with the knowledge, the moral relationship, and sometimes the inheritance that makes contribution fair.
[P1.3] Where they genuinely cannot pay, the duty is capped or waived.
[P1.4] Enforceability matters because moral exhortation fails exactly where need is greatest: neglectful children, family conflict, and siblings who free-ride while one daughter does everything. Without a legal duty, the responsible child is punished and the absent child is rewarded. A legal duty gives caregivers and agencies a fair basis for cost-sharing, encourages early planning, and protects vulnerable parents from abandonment.
[P1.5] The autonomy objection proves too much. We do not let autonomy mean leaving a dependent spouse or child destitute, and we should not let it mean leaving a frail parent to go without care while an adult child with means pays nothing. The law need not police love. It sets a floor: contribute what you can, with dignity and exceptions, before the vulnerable parent or the taxpayer bears everything.

[C1.1] A legally enforceable duty for adult children to fund parental care is a blunt instrument aimed at a problem that already has better solutions—and it creates harms the proposition never adequately confronts.
[C1.2] First, the proposition assumes family relationships are transparent enough for courts to adjudicate fairly. They are not. Contribution capacity, parental sacrifice, sibling dynamics, estrangement causes—these are deeply private, contested, and unverifiable. What follows is litigation that tears apart the very families it claims to protect.
[C1.3] Imagine a court ordering one sibling to pay while another is exempt: this doesn't resolve free-riding, it formalizes resentment. The legal apparatus becomes a new battlefield, not a safety net.
[C1.4] Second, reciprocity is a moral intuition, not a legal principle. Raising a child is not a loan. No parent signs a contract at birth promising future care in exchange for investment. Millions of adult children were raised by neglectful, abusive, or absent parents.
[C1.5] Forcing them to pay for care on the grounds of "reciprocity" punishes the vulnerable child, not the irresponsible parent. The proposition's own exceptions for abuse are admissions that the rule is fundamentally unjust in many real families—exceptions that will be fought over in every courtroom in the country.
[C1.6] Third, the proposition conflates "the cost must be paid" with "adult children must pay it." We already fund elder care through progressive taxation, Medicaid, and social insurance. These pooled systems spread risk fairly across society rather than singling out individuals based on bloodline. A legal duty to children is essentially privatizing a public problem and offloading it onto families with wildly unequal resources—a single mother earning minimum wage versus a sibling who inherited property.
[C1.7] The real question is not whether aging parents deserve care. They do. It is whether the state should compel specific family members to pay, or whether we invest in universal systems that protect dignity without weaponizing family ties.

The proposition punishes the responsible child with paperwork and the irresponsible child with a judgment he may never satisfy.

Who audits, who collects, who adjudicates disputes? The cost of that machinery falls on the same vulnerable parents and overstretched courts the proposition claims to protect. Universal pooled systems already spread this risk; the proposition replaces efficient collectivization with inefficient, adversarial individualization.
