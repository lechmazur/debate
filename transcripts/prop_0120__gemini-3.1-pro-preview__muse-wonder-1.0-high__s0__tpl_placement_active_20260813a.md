[P1.1] The modern tax system is fundamentally blind to how immense wealth operates. By relying exclusively on income and consumption taxes, we allow the ultra-wealthy to legally opt out of contributing their fair share, shifting the fiscal burden onto everyday working households. An annual tax on large net worth is essential to restore fundamental equity.
[P1.2] First, income taxes fail to capture true economic power at the top. For the working class, wealth means heavily taxed wages. For the ultra-wealthy, wealth is asset appreciation. Through the well-documented "buy, borrow, die" strategy, billionaires finance lavish lifestyles using low-interest loans secured against their appreciating stocks and real estate.
[P1.3] Because loans are not legally classified as income, they pay virtually no income tax on this newly accessible wealth. A net worth tax bypasses this structural loophole by taxing the underlying reservoir of wealth directly, aligning taxation with a person's genuine ability to pay.
[P1.4] Second, consumption taxes are inherently regressive. Working families spend almost everything they earn on basic necessities, subjecting the vast majority of their resources to sales taxes. The ultra-wealthy, by contrast, physically consume only a microscopic fraction of their multi-billion-dollar net worth. Relying on consumption taxes mathematically guarantees that the richest households face the lightest effective tax burden relative to their total financial capacity.
[P1.5] Finally, a wealth tax promotes economic dynamism. The current tax code rewards the passive hoarding of assets. An annual net worth tax introduces a holding cost on stagnant capital. To outpace the levy, the wealthy are heavily incentivized to deploy their capital into highly productive, growth-oriented investments rather than parking it in low-yield shelters or dynastic trusts.
[P1.6] We cannot maintain a system that relentlessly targets the paychecks of the middle class while entirely ignoring the untaxed fortunes of the richest few. We must tax wealth where it actually resides.

[C1.1] A tax on what you earn or spend taxes a measurable event. A tax on what you own on paper taxes a guess, every year, whether you have cash, profit, or even a buyer. That is why a wealth tax fails in practice, however appealing it sounds.
[C1.2] First, valuation and liquidity make it unfair and unworkable. A listed share has a price; a family business, startup stake, farm, partnership, or patent does not. The state must appraise such assets annually, inviting disputes, discounts, and audits. Paper wealth is not cash.
[C1.3] A founder whose company is valued but pays no dividend, or a land-rich, income-poor farmer, must borrow or sell part of the business each year to pay tax on an unrealized estimate. Control passes not to better managers but to those with liquid cash, often large corporations and foreign buyers.
[C1.4] Second, large fortunes are the most mobile and easiest to restructure. Owners can move residence, shift assets into trusts, add debt to shrink net worth, or convert wealth into hard-to-value forms. Several European countries that adopted wealth taxes later narrowed or repealed them after disappointing revenue, high costs, and capital departure. The result is not a reliable levy on billionaires but a subsidy for avoidance advice.
[C1.5] Third, it punishes saving and productive investment. Wealth built from already-taxed income is taxed again merely for being retained. In a year when assets fall, the levy can still be due, consuming capital itself. That discourages long-term investment in businesses, housing, and innovation, while rewarding spending or risky short-term bets to outrun the tax.
[C1.6] If the problem is borrowing against assets or inheriting gains untaxed, fix that directly: tax gains at death or transfer, and tighten estate and loan rules. Target the actual escape, not all saved wealth with an annual guessing tax.
