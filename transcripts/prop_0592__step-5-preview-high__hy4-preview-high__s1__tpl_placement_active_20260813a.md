[P1.1] Unanimity is not unity. It is a veto handed to the slowest member, and in foreign policy that veto is a weapon pointed at Brussels by every aggressor watching the clock. When the EU debates sanctions, it does not deliberate in a vacuum; it deliberates while tanks move, while banks launder, while civilians bury their dead. The problem with unanimity is not procedural elegance.
[P1.2] It is that deterrence decays with delay. A sanctions package announced after a ceasefire is a press release; a package announced before an invasion is a deterrent.
[P1.3] The motion accepts a simple trade. We give up the theoretical right of any single member to block action, and we gain the ability to move at the speed of crisis. Qualified majority voting does not abolish consultation. It abolishes blackmail.
[P1.4] It tells every capital that the union will act on its merits, not on the idiosyncrasies of one energy contract or one domestic election cycle.
[P1.5] Consider the mechanics of modern aggression. Authoritarian states do not wait for Europe to reach consensus. They probe for the weakest link, time their escalation to the slowest sanction cycle, and calculate that by the time twenty-seven capitals align, the territory is already annexed. Unanimity does not protect small members from being overruled; it exposes them to being abandoned.
[P1.6] The counterargument will invoke sovereignty and coalition cohesion. But cohesion built on unanimity is brittle. It forces the majority to dilute measures until the most reluctant member says yes. The result is the worst of both worlds: action too late and too weak.
[P1.7] Qualified majority voting lets the EU maintain a credible threat, because a threat everyone knows can be vetoed is not a threat.
[P1.8] The question is not whether Europe should agree. It is whether one government should be able to tell the others that genocide must wait for their convenience. The EU should replace unanimity with qualified majority voting because sanctions that arrive too late are not diplomacy. They are an obituary.

[C1.1] The motion is sold as a simple trade: surrender the veto, gain speed. But speed is not the scarce good in sanctions. Enforcement is. Unanimity is slow because it forces every capital to own the measure; that ownership is what turns a Brussels decision into port inspections, asset freezes, and export-license denials across twenty-seven jurisdictions.
[C1.2] Qualified majority voting can produce a faster communiqué, but it risks producing paper sanctions: adopted over the objection of a state that must still police its banks, ports, and borders. A dissenting member that is outvoted does not become a loyal enforcer; it becomes a loophole. Sanctions are not self-executing; they depend on national authorities that can slow-walk or narrow them.
[C1.3] That is the fatal flaw in the PRO’s deterrence story. When they say “a threat everyone knows can be vetoed is not a threat,” they confuse the credibility of a vote with the credibility of implementation. Aggressors study enforcement, not press conferences. They route transactions through the least enthusiastic jurisdiction, mirror trade through third countries, and test whether the union will actually sustain pain at home.
[C1.4] The 2022 energy and oil-price-cap measures were not slow because diplomats enjoyed delay; they were slow because carve-outs had to be calibrated to avoid legal challenge and humanitarian blowback. Rushed majority votes would have delivered headlines and invited evasion. A qualified-majority vote can be unraveled by one customs authority or one court.
[C1.5] Unanimity also protects small states not by empowering blackmail, but by forcing the majority to address real exposure—energy dependence, proximity, security ties—before adoption. Outvoting those concerns does not erase them; it relocates them to implementation, where they resurface as delays, legal fights, and quiet non-compliance. The choice is not slow unity versus fast action, but fast words versus durable pressure. Aggressors do not fear a ballot count in Brussels.
[C1.6] They fear a union that still holds after months of shared pain.
