[P1.1] This motion is already the compromise. It asks for no blanket exemption: faith-based dress expectations are protected only where participation is genuinely voluntary and realistic alternatives exist. To win, the other side must defend something far more radical — that a freely chosen, easily exited community of faith may never ask its members to visibly embody its commitments. That claim should not survive scrutiny.
[P1.2] Religious schools and religious employers exist to transmit a way of life, not merely to deliver math lessons and paychecks. Dress — a headscarf, a kippah, modesty norms — is often how a community teaches, witnesses, and holds itself together. An Islamic school where no adult models Islamic dress is like a French-immersion school where no one speaks French: the medium contradicts the message.
[P1.3] The two conditions do the moral work. Where a teacher freely chooses a faith school over the public district down the road, and can leave for comparable work if her conscience changes, the dress rule is not coercion but the terms of an association she may exit. We accept this logic everywhere: law firms require suits, vegan restaurants forbid fur, orchestras require black. Consent plus exit converts rules into choices.
[P1.4] Religion deserves the same logic, not special suspicion.
[P1.5] And who loses under a total ban? Not the secular majority, which never needed such spaces, but religious minorities — Muslim, Jewish, Sikh communities — whose institutions would be flattened into secular sameness. Neutrality that forbids every community from expressing itself is not neutral; it is secularism made compulsory.
[P1.6] Expect stories of trapped employees and captive students. Those are exactly the cases where voluntariness fails or alternatives are unrealistic — cases this motion already excludes. They will be arguing against a motion that does not exist.
[P1.7] The question is simple: may a community of faith ask freely joined members to dress the part when walking away is genuinely possible? The only answer compatible with freedom of association — and with real pluralism — is yes.

[C1.1] The motion is not a compromise; it is a permission slip with an impossible test. “Genuinely voluntary” and “realistic alternatives” sound like safeguards, but in religious schools and employers they are often formal fictions. A child does not choose her school; her parents do. A teacher in a rural town may have one faith-based school and no comparable job within an hour’s drive. A low-wage worker at a religious hospital or charity may need that paycheck far more than she needs a dress-code fight.
[C1.2] Calling that “exit” is not consent; it is a penalty for conscience.
[C1.3] Nor is the analogy to suits and black concert dress honest. A suit is fungible; a headscarf, kippah, or modesty rule is not. These expectations encode religious and often gender orthodoxy. When an institution imposes them, it does not merely ask employees to look professional; it makes some bodies and beliefs welcome and others suspect.
[C1.4] And “some room” has no limiting principle: schools and employers will define “faith-based” broadly, apply it to non-members, and discipline dissenters.
[C1.5] The conditions also fail administratively. Voluntariness is judged before the harm, but coercion often appears after: the probation review, the parent-teacher pressure, the threat of dismissal. A rule that says “only when truly voluntary” invites institutions to declare consent and leaves the most vulnerable to prove otherwise. For many, the realistic alternative is not another school or employer; it is stigma, lost income, or exclusion from one’s community.
[C1.6] Religious association matters, but it cannot be purchased by putting dress expectations on people who did not choose the faith, cannot leave, or are children. The state should protect conscience, not license institutions to test it.
