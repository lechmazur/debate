[P1.1] The motion does not ask whether AI can replace thinking. It asks whether universities should permit AI-assisted drafting under two strict conditions: students must disclose their use, and they remain fully accountable for accuracy and reasoning. That framing matters, because the realistic alternative is not an AI-free campus. It is a campus where AI use is universal, hidden, and ungoverned.
[P1.2] First, prohibition is already failing. AI detectors are notoriously unreliable, producing false accusations that disproportionately hit non-native English speakers, while sophisticated users evade them easily. A ban therefore punishes honesty: the student who admits using AI is sanctioned; the student who hides it sails through. That inverts every incentive a university should want.
[P1.3] Disclosure flips it back—students who are transparent operate in the open, and instructors can actually see and shape how the tool is used.
[P1.4] Second, accountability is the real engine of learning, and this policy strengthens it. When a student must defend every claim in a draft, explain the reasoning, and own every error—including AI hallucinations—the cognitive work doesn't disappear; it shifts toward verification, judgment, and revision. That is precisely the skill graduates need in workplaces where AI drafting is already standard in law firms, hospitals, and engineering teams. A university that pretends otherwise prepares students for a world that no longer exists.
[P1.5] Third, the motion is carefully bounded. "Most coursework" leaves room for exceptions: introductory writing courses, language acquisition, proctored exams—anywhere unaided fluency is itself the learning objective. The proposition isn't a free-for-all; it's a default of regulated transparency with carve-outs where faculty judge them necessary.
[P1.6] So the comparison for this debate is simple. On one side: honest, disclosed, accountable use that universities can teach, audit, and assess. On the other: a blanket prohibition that is unenforceable, rewards deception, criminalizes a professional skill, and erodes trust between students and institutions. Integrity is better protected by sunlight than by a rule everyone quietly breaks.
