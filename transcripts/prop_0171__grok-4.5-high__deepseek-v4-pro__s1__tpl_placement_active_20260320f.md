[P1.1] When animal models cannot replicate human disease with fidelity, controlled human infection studies offer the only rigorous path to evaluate vaccines and treatments before widespread risk. Regulators should permit them more broadly for that reason, even while ethical concerns demand rigorous safeguards.
[P1.2] Animal models frequently fail for pathogens tuned to human biology—HIV, many respiratory viruses, malaria strains. Trials then stumble: vaccines that protect mice collapse in people, or promising candidates never surface because the model misled. Challenge studies close that gap. Healthy volunteers receive a controlled, low-dose exposure under constant medical watch; researchers track protection in real time, dose-response, and correlates of immunity with unmatched precision.
[P1.3] For diseases that kill hundreds of thousands annually, that speed and accuracy translate into earlier, better tools.
[P1.4] Ethical objections remain serious: deliberate infection carries risk of harm, even if remote and mitigated. Volunteers may feel pressure; there is residual uncertainty. Yet these studies already operate under intensive oversight—ethics boards, informed consent, exclusion of the vulnerable, immediate rescue therapy, insurance. Participants are not mere instruments; they are autonomous adults who choose to contribute to collective protection.
[P1.5] The alternative—slow, sequential human trials after flawed animal data—exposes far larger populations to unmitigated risk and delayed relief.
[P1.6] Broader permission means clearer criteria: disease severity high, animal models inadequate by demonstrated criteria, risk-minimization protocols ironclad. Deny that path and we accept preventable loss of life for the sake of purity that spares a few screened volunteers at the expense of the many. Public health requires calibrated risk-taking, not absolute aversion when the tools exist to do it responsibly.

[P2.1] CON claims that unknown risks make genuine consent impossible, so broader permission must be refused. That standard would ban every first-in-human trial: every novel compound begins with uncertainty about rare or delayed harms. Challenge studies are more controlled than those open-ended phase I trials—fixed low dose, continuous monitoring, prepositioned rescue—yet volunteers still accept residual risk. Adults already make that judgment for ordinary research; denying them the same agency here treats them as children rather than ends in themselves.
[P2.2] The alternatives CON offers do not close the fidelity gap. Organoids and chips capture fragments of human biology but miss systemic immunity, multi-organ dynamics, and correlates that only emerge in a living host. Field studies and adaptive designs remain slower and noisier precisely because exposure is uncontrolled and delayed. When animal models have already failed, waiting on imperfect surrogates simply lengthens the period in which hundreds of thousands die without better tools.
[P2.3] “Permit more broadly” does not equal routine free-for-all. It means transparent criteria—high disease burden plus demonstrated model inadequacy—under the same intensive boards, exclusions, and insurance already used. One publicized adverse event may shake trust; continuous preventable mortality from stalled vaccines will erode it far more lastingly.
[P2.4] The decisive comparison remains scale and choice. A screened volunteer who chooses residual risk under watch is not morally equivalent to the vastly larger population that never consents to delayed protection. Calibrated expansion respects both autonomy and urgency; absolute refusal sacrifices the many for the appearance of clean hands.
