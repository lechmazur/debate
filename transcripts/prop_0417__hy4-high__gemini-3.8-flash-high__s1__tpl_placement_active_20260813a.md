[P1.1] Weekend late-night transit extensions reduce nightlife disorder and drunk-driving risk more effectively because they treat the trip home as part of the night out. Earlier closing treats the clock as the problem. The real hazard is a synchronized, intoxicated crowd with no reliable way to disperse.
[P1.2] Closing at 1 instead of 3 does not put a sober person behind the wheel at 1. It creates a surge: people leave together, queue for scarce taxis, argue at the curb, crowd takeaways, hunt for cars, and drift into residential streets. The impairment remains; it is merely compressed in time and shifted from managed premises to less supervised space.
[P1.3] Frequent late trains and buses change the environment before disorder forms. They let people leave gradually, split crowds across routes and stops, carry hospitality workers and sober companions, and make the safe choice visible when someone is deciding whether to drive. Transit is not a reward for drinking; it is infrastructure that prevents a bad decision from becoming a car journey.
[P1.4] Earlier closing may shave off a final round, but it does not solve the trip after several rounds have already been consumed. If the last train is gone, the options left are a long wait, an unlicensed ride, a risky walk, or the car. Extensions align the safe option with the closing window: people can leave before they are more impaired, or stay later without driving.
[P1.5] Earlier curfews also relocate disorder into house parties, store queues, and neighborhoods without door staff, lighting, or transport. Extended transit drains entertainment districts steadily along lit, staffed corridors and reduces fights fed by queues and ride scarcity. The metric is harm across the whole evening and journey home, not the hour printed on a licence. Extensions reduce unsafe trips and uncontrolled crowds together; earlier closing dumps the same impaired crowd into a worse setting.

[C1.1] PRO treats intoxication as a fixed condition that merely needs to be shuttled away. But intoxication is fundamentally a function of time. Extending trading hours increases total drinks consumed and spikes blood alcohol levels; closing venues earlier cuts off the tap. Restricting closing times is the decisive intervention against nightlife violence because peak impairment—and the catastrophic loss of impulse control that fuels assaults and drunk driving—accumulates during the latest, most exhausted hours of the night.
[C1.2] When cities roll back trading hours, assaults and alcohol-related hospital admissions drop sharply. A crowd departing at 1:00 AM is markedly less intoxicated, more cognitively intact, and vastly easier to manage than that same crowd ejected at 4:00 AM after hours of continuous binging. Earlier closing does not simply move the clock; it prevents severe intoxication before it starts.
[C1.3] In contrast, late-night transit extensions fail to address the root cause and actively worsen the problem. First, all-night transit acts as an infrastructure subsidy for marathon drinking: guaranteeing a train home at 4:00 AM encourages patrons to stay later and drink heavier. Second, transit does not dissolve disorder; it relocates it into confined train carriages, isolated platforms, and bus corridors where door staff are absent and transit workers face the brunt of violent abuse.
[C1.4] Third, rail networks rarely serve the outer suburbs where the motorists most likely to drive drunk actually reside.
[C1.5] Earlier venue closings intervene at the source by capping alcohol intake and halting the night before acute intoxication peaks. Transit extensions merely attempt to manage the fallout of an extra two hours of heavy drinking, shifting violence onto public platforms while leaving the drivers who actually risk fatal crashes on the road.
