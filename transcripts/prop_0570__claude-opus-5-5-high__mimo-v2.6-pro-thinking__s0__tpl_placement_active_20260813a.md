[P1.1] The motion describes a division of labor: let machines do what they do best so teachers can do what only humans can. That division is the whole case.
[P1.2] Start with mastery practice. Solving linear equations, balancing chemical reactions, conjugating French verbs, recalling the causes of the First World War: these tasks have right answers and improve through repetition with immediate correction. An AI tutor is ideal here. It never tires of the fortieth fraction problem, it notices when a student keeps making the same sign error, and it serves up another problem at exactly the right difficulty.
[P1.3] A teacher with thirty students cannot give every child that kind of instant, individual response. For convergent skills, AI tutoring is a clear win.
[P1.4] Writing is different in kind, not just in degree. A good essay has no single correct answer. Writing is communication with a real reader, so the most valuable feedback is a reader's honest reaction: "I got lost here," "this example convinced me," "you're hiding your actual opinion." AI can imitate that response, but its feedback pulls toward the statistical average: tidy topic sentences, safe transitions, polished but generic prose.
[P1.5] A fifteen-year-old with a strange, promising voice gets smoothed into competent blandness. When AI is the main source of feedback, students learn to satisfy the model, not to persuade a person.

[P1.7] Writing development does not, because the thinking is the product.
[P1.8] Third, writing feedback is how teachers come to know their students' minds. A teacher who reads drafts sees who is confused, who is coasting, and who has an idea worth pushing. Outsource that reading and you lose the diagnostic heart of teaching.
