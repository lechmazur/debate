[P1.1] The motion asks a question of priorities, not a rejection of technology. In early secondary grades—roughly ages 11 through 14—schools face a choice: pour resources into one-to-one device programs, or invest in the developmental work those years actually require. Three reasons settle it.
[P1.2] First, attention is a finite resource that early adolescents are still building. The capacity to sustain focus, resist distraction, and think deeply is not innate at twelve; it is trained, or it is eroded. Constant access to devices in every class conditions students to switch tasks every few minutes, skim instead of read, and expect stimulation on tap. Schools that hand out devices without protecting attention are outsourcing a core academic outcome to a known distractor.
[P1.3] Prioritizing attention means structuring classrooms—long reading blocks, discussion circles, sustained writing—where students practice the focus that later learning demands.
[P1.4] Second, early secondary is the engine room of social development. This is when children learn to read a room, repair a friendship after a fight, work in a group without a moderator, and build an identity outside their family. None of that happens through a screen. A one-to-one device program puts a private portal between every student and every peer, all day.
[P1.5] Prioritizing social development means recess, group projects, oral debates, clubs, sports—activities that build people skills no software teaches.
[P1.6] Third, the promised gains of one-to-one programs are thin. Ordinary classroom experience and the broader pattern of evidence show that device saturation correlates with weaker writing, shallow research, and more off-task behavior, while equity and test-score gains are inconsistent. Schools chasing the device promise divert money from counselors, smaller classes, arts, and play—exactly what early adolescents need.
[P1.7] A twelve-year-old does not need a laptop to become a better student. They need a classroom that demands their presence. Prioritizing attention and social development is not nostalgia; it is the foundation every later device program depends on.

[C1.1] Pitting student attention against one-to-one devices rests on a caricature: that issuing a student a laptop means forcing them to stare at an open screen during discussion circles and recess [P1.3, P1.5]. In reality, a laptop lid closes in one second. One-to-one programs are not a mandate to replace human interaction; they are foundational public infrastructure, like textbooks or libraries. When public schools deprioritize that infrastructure in early secondary grades, they do not rescue attention or social growth—they sabotage both for the students who need public education most.
[C1.2] First, deprioritizing one-to-one devices in grades six through eight locks in severe educational inequality. Affluent middle schoolers already have personal computers at home to write essays, revise drafts, and navigate digital tools. Low-income students rely on the school's one-to-one program for that baseline access. If public schools retreat from universal devices until high school, disadvantaged fourteen-year-olds walk into ninth grade functionally behind in the basic mechanics of modern schooling—typing, digital research, and managing online coursework—right when grades begin counting toward graduation.
[C1.3] Second, universal devices actively protect classroom attention and social dignity through individualized support. Early secondary classrooms contain wide skill gaps: a seventh-grade room routinely mixes advanced readers with students navigating dyslexia, learning delays, or limited English. Without devices, a struggling student either zones out—destroying attention—or suffers the social humiliation of receiving visibly remedial paper handouts in front of peers. With a one-to-one device, every student uses the same hardware while privately accessing text-to-speech, adaptive math scaffolding, or instant translation.
[C1.4] That protects peer belonging and keeps struggling learners engaged.
[C1.5] Finally, digital focus cannot be taught through abstinence [P1.2]. Twelve-year-olds already encounter screens outside school without guardrails. Early secondary is the safest window to teach disciplined digital work habits inside a filtered, teacher-monitored environment. Deprioritizing one-to-one programs does not build presence; it simply rations modern educational tools by family income.
