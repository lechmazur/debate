[P1.1] Posting creative work online is an invitation to an audience, not an open-ended surrender of economic agency. When an artist, writer, or musician uploads their work to a public platform, the bargain has always been visibility in exchange for distribution. Converting that creative portfolio into training data for commercial generative-AI systems fundamentally shatters that bargain. It transforms the creator’s output into the raw material for a commercial engine built to replicate and compete with them.
[P1.2] First, public visibility is not public domain. Reading an essay, viewing an illustration, or listening to a track online is an act of individual consumption; scraping millions of copyrighted works to train a commercial model is an industrial extraction process. Creators share their labor to build an audience, not to subsidize proprietary models that generate direct market substitutes at zero marginal cost.
[P1.3] Second, the absence of a strong default right traps creators in an impossible dilemma. Independent artists must either retreat from the modern internet—destroying their ability to reach audiences—or post their work and actively feed the systems engineered to displace them. Fragmented creators have virtually zero leverage to negotiate terms of service against trillion-dollar platforms. A passive opt-out framework places the entire administrative burden on the victim of extraction, while platforms silently profit from inertia.
[P1.4] Third, a protective default establishes an equitable foundation for innovation. Technological advancement cannot be built on the uncompensated appropriation of human labor. Requiring platforms to obtain affirmative consent simply shifts the burden to where the resources lie, incentivizing genuine licensing partnerships rather than unilateral exploitation.
[P1.5] Defending this right does not halt generative AI; it ensures that the future of technology respects the agency of the people who make culture possible. Platforms must not be permitted to take by default what they never bought.
