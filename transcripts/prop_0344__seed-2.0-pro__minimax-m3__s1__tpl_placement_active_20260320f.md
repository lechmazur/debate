[C1.1] The motion asks us to choose between attention and devices. That is the wrong question, and accepting it is how this debate gets lost.
[C1.2] A one-to-one program is not a rival to attention training or social development — it is the infrastructure that delivers both. Personalized learning software exists precisely because attention is scarce and heterogeneous. Adaptive readers, math tutors, and writing assistants let a teacher meet thirty different attention spans at once instead of losing the rest. A student who cannot focus on a print textbook can often focus on a short video, an interactive simulation, or a story built around their own choices.
[C1.3] The device is the attention tool, not the obstacle to it.
[C1.4] The same is true socially. The motion treats "social development" as if it only happens face-to-face at lunch. But adolescent social life is hybrid. A student who cannot collaborate on a shared document, contribute to a class discussion board, or give structured peer feedback is socially underdeveloped by the standards their future workplaces and friendships will use.
[C1.5] Pulling a device out of a thirteen year old's hand does not return them to 1962 — it returns them to a smaller, less capable version of 2025.
[C1.6] The "you can roll it out next year" line is weaker than it sounds. The developmental window that early secondary opens is not just attention — it is digital fluency. A sixteen year old who has never managed a school email account, navigated a learning platform, or done basic research online is not behind on social development — they are behind on citizenship. That gap is exactly as hard to close later as the one PRO is worried about.
[C1.7] So the question is not "attention OR devices." It is whether we will use the device to teach attention. Schools should integrate them, and the motion, taken seriously, would prevent that.
