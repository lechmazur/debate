[P1.1] Copyright was never a prize for being first to ask. It rewards the human decisions that make expression what it is: what to keep, what to cut, how lines fall, how meaning lands. A prompt is an instruction to someone — something — else. Unless a person can show substantial creative control over the final expression, there is no author to protect, only a lottery ticket to enclose.
[P1.2] That distinction matters because scale has changed everything. A human novelist writes one book a year; a generative system can emit thousands of stories, songs, and images per hour at near-zero cost. If each output arrives copyrighted by default, we invite an unprecedented land grab on culture. Prompt farms and large model owners could fence off vast territories of plausible expression, forcing independent creators to license back what a machine happened to spit out first.
[P1.3] The public domain — the raw material every future artist needs — would shrink by automation.
[P1.4] Critics will call a documentation requirement burdensome, but it is actually a permission slip for real AI-assisted art. A photographer using AI to draft backgrounds, then selecting, masking, repainting, arranging, and revising until the final image says what she means — she can document that control and earn protection. The person who typed five words and claimed the result cannot. That line preserves incentives where they work: reward labor, judgment, and vision, not button-pressing.
[P1.5] Without this rule, ownership becomes incoherent. Was the “author” the prompter, the model developer, or the thousands of uncredited human creators whose work trained the system? Requiring proof of substantial control answers that question cleanly: the copyright goes to the human whose creative choices actually determined the expression. No control, no copyright — the work stays free for all to use, remix, and build upon.
[P1.6] That is exactly where AI outputs belong until a human makes them her own.

[C1.1] The proposition turns copyright into an audit. It does not merely ask whether a work is original or whether a human contributed expressive choices; it imposes a special paperwork regime on every AI-assisted creation. That is the wrong test. Copyright has always looked at the work and the human authorship reflected in it, not at whether an author kept production logs.
[C1.2] A painter need not preserve sketches, a novelist need not save every draft, and a photographer need not document each exposure choice before protection attaches. Singling out AI for a documentation prerequisite creates a two-tier system in which identical expressive results are treated differently based on tools.
[C1.3] The standard is also unworkably vague. What counts as “substantial creative control”? How many prompts, edits, masks, or rejected outputs must be preserved? Small creators, freelancers, and hobbyists will not know how to comply, while large studios will hire lawyers to manufacture convincing records.
[C1.4] The result is not cleaner authorship; it is more litigation, more platform gatekeeping, and more pressure to route creativity through services that can store and certify every step. The rule would punish the very artists PRO claims to protect: the independent designer who iterates quickly, the writer who uses AI to brainstorm and then fixes the final text, the musician who transforms generated material but cannot reconstruct every intermediate command.
[C1.5] Worse, denying protection when documentation is missing does not free culture; it transfers leverage from creators to platforms and bad-faith copiers. Unprotected AI-assisted works can still be locked behind contracts, terms of service, watermarks, and trade-secret control, but individual authors lose the ability to license, attribute, or stop predatory copying. If the concern is overreach, the answer is to deny protection to genuinely nonhuman, mechanical outputs and evaluate human contribution under ordinary originality standards. The proposition goes much further: it makes fragile, burdensome proof the price of protection.
[C1.6] That is bad law and bad policy.
