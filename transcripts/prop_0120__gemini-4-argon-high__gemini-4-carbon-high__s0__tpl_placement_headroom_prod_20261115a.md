[P1.1] A tax system built almost entirely on income and consumption operates on a broken fiction: that economic power is measured by a paycheck or a checkout receipt. For working families, that is true: every dollar earned is taxed as wages, and nearly every dollar kept is spent and taxed again. For the ultra-wealthy, however, paychecks are irrelevant and personal spending is a rounding error. Their fortunes expand through compounding, unrealized gains in equities, private businesses, and real estate.
[P1.2] Under the current rules, a billionaire can watch their net worth surge by hundreds of millions, sell nothing, report minimal taxable income, and cheaply borrow against their appreciating assets to fund a lavish lifestyle tax-free. By taxing only realized cash flows rather than the massive stock of wealth, we allow the richest households to effectively opt out of the fiscal contract.
[P1.3] Relying on consumption taxes only deepens this distortion. A tycoon cannot possibly buy enough consumer goods to match the effective tax burden of a nurse or mechanic who must spend their entire income just to live. Nor can traditional income taxes close the gap when capital gains can be deferred indefinitely, sheltered in trusts, or passed to heirs. Only an annual tax on large net worth aligns taxation with true ability to pay, capturing economic power as it actually accumulates.
[P1.4] In fact, we already tax net worth annually, yet only for ordinary families. Local property taxes levy a recurring charge on the single largest asset middle-class households own: their homes. Taxing a family's modest house every year while exempting a billionaire's sprawling financial empire is indefensible. An annual tax on extreme net worth restores basic fairness, raises vital public revenue without penalizing work or everyday commerce, and checks the runaway, compounding wealth concentration that corrodes democratic equality.

[C1.2] Comparing billionaire fortunes to local property taxes [P1.4] conceals why annual wealth taxation fails in practice. Municipalities value ordinary homes using thick markets of comparable neighborhood sales. [C1.3] Worse, paper value is not cash. Taxing unrealized net worth forces founders of cash-poor, growing firms to dump shares, surrender control, or drain capital from hiring and research to pay taxes on paper gains that may evaporate in the next downturn.

Most advanced democracies that tried annual net worth taxes repealed them after capital fled, administrative costs ballooned, and meager collections failed to offset lost investment and income-tax revenue. Economically, an annual wealth tax also distorts incentives backward: a billionaire who liquidates productive capital for a chartered superyacht vacation shrinks their taxable net worth, whereas an entrepreneur reinvesting patiently in risky innovation gets taxed repeatedly on the same principal. Reformed income and consumption taxes capture elite spending and generational windfalls cleanly when wealth is actually monetized or transferred, achieving fairness without crippling investment or drowning tax agencies in annual appraisal wars.
