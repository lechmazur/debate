[P1.1] When you click "Buy" on a digital book, game, album, or film, you pay the price of permanent possession. Yet buried in fine print, platforms claim you bought nothing, only a revocable permission slip they can cancel at will. When a storefront closes, a studio's licensing deal expires, or an automated system flags an account, buyers routinely lose entire libraries of paid media overnight without compensation. That is a commercial bait-and-switch.
[P1.2] We already have honest models for temporary access: rentals and subscriptions. When companies charge purchase prices, consumers deserve meaningful ownership: an irrevocable right to keep, back up, and use their copy offline, alongside reasonable ways to pass on or transfer it upon relinquishing their own access.
[P1.3] Revocable licenses also cripple competition. Buying physical books or discs from one store never trapped you there forever. Today, platform-locked licenses turn past purchases into hostages. If leaving an e-reader brand or console ecosystem means forfeiting thousands of dollars in media, dominant platforms can raise fees and degrade service with impunity.
[P1.4] Portable, owned copies break that lock-in, forcing storefronts to compete on quality rather than confiscation.
[P1.5] Furthermore, revocable licensing threatens cultural memory. Physical discs and books survive bankruptcies, corporate mergers, and shifting sensibilities. Digital licenses let corporations silently rewrite books, strip songs out of older games, or brick single-player titles the moment an authentication server becomes inconvenient to run. Decentralized consumer ownership is our best safeguard against cultural erasure.
[P1.6] Nor is digital ownership unworkable. Major music stores abandoned copy-protection locks years ago, and DRM-free PC game storefronts thrive today without collapsing into piracy. Ownership does not require studios to run multiplayer servers forever; it simply bans remote kill-switches on goods already sold. Subscriptions can still coexist for consumers who prefer cheap access, just as libraries and streaming coexist with bookstores.
[P1.7] What must end is the fiction of the revocable purchase: if you pay to buy it, you should actually own it.
