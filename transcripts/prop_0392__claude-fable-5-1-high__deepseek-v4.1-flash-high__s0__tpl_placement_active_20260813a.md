[P1.1] Picture a blind woman trying to refill a prescription at 11pm. The pharmacy's app has buttons her screen reader announces only as "button, button, button." The checkout uses an image-only CAPTCHA. She cannot finish.
[P1.2] Nothing about this required cutting-edge engineering to prevent. Labeling a button is a one-line fix. The failure is not technical; it is a choice about who counts as a customer.
[P1.3] That is the case in one sentence: when a service is public-facing, repeatedly fails the most basic standards, and faces no real consequence, exclusion becomes a rational business decision. Fines change the arithmetic. Nothing else has.
[P1.4] Notice how narrow this motion is. Not every glitch, not every site. Repeated failures of basic standards — the kind of things automated checkers flag in seconds: missing alt text, forms that cannot be navigated by keyboard, contrast so poor partially sighted users cannot read it. These standards have existed for roughly two decades.
[P1.5] Voluntary guidance has been available the entire time. Yet routine audits of major websites keep finding that the large majority still contain detectable basic errors. Persuasion has had its chance.
[P1.6] Consider what happens without fines. In some jurisdictions, enforcement runs through private lawsuits: a lottery where a few companies get hit with settlements, most face nothing, and lawyers capture much of the value. In others, there is simply no enforcement. Both outcomes are worse than a regulator issuing predictable, escalating penalties after warnings go unheeded.
[P1.7] Fines are the fair version of accountability.
[P1.8] The stakes are not convenience. Banking, benefits applications, medical portals, ticketing, job applications have moved online, often with the physical alternative shut down to save money. When the ramp is removed and the website is broken, a disabled person is not inconvenienced; they are locked out of ordinary life.
[P1.9] We already fine restaurants that ignore hygiene rules and builders who skip fire exits. A digital front door that a wheelchair user, a blind user, or a deaf user cannot pass is the same failure in a different medium. It deserves the same response.

[C1.1] The problem is real: basic accessibility failures lock people out. The proposed remedy is a tax on them. If a blind user cannot check out, a regulator collecting a meaningful fine does not make the CAPTCHA audible; a deadline to fix it might. That is the clash: PRO wants punishment after repeated failure.
[C1.2] We should ask whether disabled users get access faster, cheaper, and more reliably.
[C1.3] Fines are structurally slow. “Repeatedly fail” means complaints, investigation, notices, appeals, collection. Years pass. A service can keep excluding people while the penalty is litigated. And a one-line button label?
[C1.4] Then a remediation order—fix within 30 days or lose your contract—is faster and more precise. Fines are the slowest way to force a simple fix.
[C1.5] They also misfire because digital services are not one actor. A small charity, local council, or transit app may inherit an inaccessible vendor widget. A meaningful fine can bankrupt the entity serving disabled users, while the vendor walks away. A large bank treats the fine as a compliance line item and continues.
[C1.6] Either way, the broken checkout remains. If a fine is cheaper than rebuilding, it becomes permission to exclude. If it is ruinous, it removes the service altogether. Neither is access.
[C1.7] Worse, fines encourage checkbox theater: automated scans, overlay widgets, legal disclaimers, not usable journeys. Money goes to treasuries and lawyers, not captions and keyboard navigation. And public services pay fines from the same budgets that fund accessibility—so disabled users subsidize the punishment.
[C1.8] Accountability should target the defect: mandatory remediation, procurement bans, loss of public contracts, direct funding, independent audits, individual complaint resolution. Those change the code. A fine changes the accounting. For disabled users, the only meaningful outcome is a working door, not a receipt for a locked one.
