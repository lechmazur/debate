# Kimi K3.1 Pro high versus Kimi K3: qualitative study

This study uses the frozen final benchmark evidence from `judge_blend_kimi31pro_coverage_20261023_finala`. The eligible corpus has 562 unique current-peer debates: 302 involving K3 and 298 involving K3.1 Pro, with 38 direct debates counted in both model totals. Claude Opus 5.5 (high), using batch requests, annotated 560 of these transcripts; one historical K3–Mistral pair was unavailable after refusals. The direct comparison covers every one of the 19 fully judged, side-swapped K3–K3.1 topic pairs; it is not a selected win/loss sample.

The direct pair results are 14 K3 wins, five ties, and no K3.1 Pro wins after pooling both directions. These topic-level results should not be read as individual-debate or judge-ballot win counts. No ratings or benchmark outputs were changed by this study.

## Model-generated comparison

The following interpretation is generated from full transcripts and judge explanations. It is an evidence-linked model assessment, not an independent human assessment or causal experiment.

Across 19 side-swapped matchups, Kimi K3 won both sides in 9: debt relief, deepfakes, gene drive, right to disconnect, homelessness, methane, financial literacy, national service and monuments. It won one debate with the other tied in 3 more: Chinese EVs, buses and actuarial fairness. The remaining 7 were clean side splits: broadband, police insurance, housing, public spaces, noncompetes, kafala and noise monitoring. Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) never swept a matchup, and its only outright debate wins came inside those side splits. The recurring mechanism is consistent. K3 sets fork-shaped pressure questions, turns K3.1's candid, qualified answers into named concessions, and compresses the result into a closing slogan. K3.1 is often the more careful reasoner and sometimes produces the strongest substantive argument in a debate. Examples are the gene-drive irreversibility point, the noncompete inevitable-use dilemma and the monuments museum/site symmetry. However, it frequently hedges, concedes before it distinguishes, and runs over word limits. Many of K3's sweeps were 2-1 on at least one side, and the split matchups track side assignment, so this is a clear edge in these selected matchups, not proof that K3 is the better model overall.

### Why K3 looked stronger

K3 wins by engineering and then cashing in concessions. Its pressure questions are binaries with a built-in "concede" clause, and K3.1's answers become K3's closing centerpiece: - the broadband "Yes"; - "Nothing automatically binds donors"; - "It can be a private civil action"; - "No guaranteed removal mechanism exists"; - "I will not invent a floor"; - "require a chronic refuser to move"; - "not zero"; - "reconsidered." K3 also crystallizes each debate into one repeatable decision rule that judges quote back: - "default vs. dare"; - "silence generates no record"; - "they stop the regulator, not the mosquito"; - "flaws with credentials"; - "quitting makes you illegal… may make you owe money." On losing sides it stays competitive through comparative framing ("my enforcement is partial, theirs is zero"; the "100 to 0" status quo). That framing took a ballot as PRO on police insurance, noise and Chinese EVs, and as CON on kafala and buses. It uses concrete named examples, such as solar duties, the Renault R5 and Leapmotor, that K3.1 left factually unrebutted. It adapts arguments it lost on: it reused cumulative carbon against K3.1 after losing that clash in Chinese EVs Debate A. Its word discipline is generally better, with far fewer clipped turns.

### K3.1 Pro's strongest cases

K3.1 wins on precise distinctions and mechanism design, mostly from the favored side. As PRO: - On broadband, it front-loaded technology-neutral, gap-tested procurement that absorbed CON's attacks, and it refused "fake precision." - On noncompetes, it separated confidentiality, which polices misuse, from a noncompete, which bans employment. GPT-5.6 called this "decisive." - On housing, its "fire escape" analogy was named decisive by two judges. - On public spaces, it turned K3's flagship parks into its own evidence and supplied a beach-club bright line with a usable test. As CON: - On police insurance, it audited the mechanism: exclusions mean the city still pays and no claims history accrues. - On noise, it forced K3 to define "more" and then turned the definition into a burden-shift argument. - On Chinese EVs, it used cumulative emissions to puncture K3's reversibility frame. - On actuarial fairness, its privacy carve-out and "if funding fails, its price rule fails too" symmetry held a 2-1 majority. Its symmetry and "proves too much" replies won dissenting ballots on deepfakes, monuments and actuarial fairness. GPT-5.6 Sol repeatedly credited its logical discipline.

### Recurring contrasts

**Fork question harvests candid concession** — K3's binary "name it or concede" pressure questions repeatedly extract honest, qualified answers from K3.1 that K3 then makes the centerpiece of its closing.

Evidence cited by the analyst: The pattern recurs across many matchups: - Broadband Debate A: Q2 drew K3.1's "Yes," which all three judges cited. - Debt relief: "Nothing automatically binds donors." - Deepfakes: "It can be a private civil action," and K3's closing opened "The debate ended when CON conceded…" - Gene drive: "non-drive field studies" and "No guaranteed removal mechanism exists." - Buses: "I will not invent a floor," cited unanimously. - Homelessness: "require a chronic refuser to move" became "rule first, exit second." - Chinese EVs: "restricts no import," cited by all three Debate B judges. - Monuments: the retention-vote answer became a "standing removal order."

**Side-locked splits on structurally tilted motions** — On roughly a third of topics the favored side won both debates regardless of model, so those results reflect motion structure more than a model gap. Even within these splits, K3 tended to post better margins or win more ballots.

Evidence cited by the analyst: The PRO side swept both debates on several topics: - Broadband: +1.97 / +1.60. - Housing: +1.50 / +1.57. - Public spaces: +1.60 / +1.43. - Noncompetes: +1.50 / +1.47. - Kafala: K3 3-0 at +2.03; K3.1 2-1 at +0.57. The CON side swept both debates on two topics: - Police insurance: K3 3-0 at 2.47; K3.1 2-1 at 0.70. - Noise: K3 3-0; K3.1 2-1.

**K3.1 has the sharper substance but weaker conversion** — K3.1 often produces the most rigorous argument in a debate, and these arguments win dissenting or minority ballots without overturning K3's crystallized frames.

Evidence cited by the analyst: Examples where K3.1's substance was credited but K3 won: - Gene drive: K3.1's irreversibility distinction was "never fully neutralized" (Sonnet), yet K3 won 3-0. - Monuments: GPT-5.6 Sol called the museum/site symmetry argument the strongest exchange, and K3.1 lost both debates 2-1. - Disconnect: Muse credited the overtime-vs-availability distinction as "most decisive." - Deepfakes: GPT-5.6 Sol credited the "proves too much" reply. - Actuarial fairness: GPT-5.6 Sol credited the poverty-vs-illness-penalty distinction. - Financial literacy: Muse credited the performance-task answers.

**K3 overreach is its main exploitable flank** — When K3 loses ballots or debates, the cause is usually an overclaim, a volunteered concession or self-refuting evidence that K3.1 catches.

Evidence cited by the analyst: Examples of K3 overreach that K3.1 or the judges caught: - Police insurance Debate A: the officer-liability overclaim, cited by two judges. - Methane Debate A: the false "never answered" claim, which drew Gemini's dissent. - Chinese EVs Debate A: "catch up on emissions" plus nonexistent reference labels. - Public spaces Debate B: free-entry Bryant Park and Central Park turned against it. - Noise Debate A: the presumption with "deviations go to weight." - Broadband Debate B: the volunteered "genuine residue." - Deepfakes Debate B: the IIED/appropriation redundancy overclaim, which drew GPT's dissent.

**Execution discipline gap: clipping and closings** — K3.1's repeated word-limit overruns and restating closings imposed judge-cited costs, while K3's complete, slogan-driven closings gave judges ready decision rules.

Evidence cited by the analyst: On clipping: - Disconnect: K3.1 was clipped in 5 of 8 speeches; K3 in none. Claude called it a "real execution cost." - Public spaces: K3.1 was clipped three times, penalized by Grok and Muse. - Deepfakes: three K3.1 clips; K3 none. - Housing: Qwen cited K3.1's "format execution costs." On closings, K3 supplied ready decision rules: - "a lock that mostly holds beats a door left open"; - "silence generates no record"; - "Their ban optimizes legality; our fee optimizes emissions." K3.1's closings left K3's final lines unanswered, including "the threshold is the conduct test" on methane and loud/silent failure on actuarial fairness.

### Tensions and limits identified by the analyst

There are several clear tensions between what judges rewarded and what the transcripts show. 1. Judges frequently rewarded K3's rhetorical capture even when the summaries flag K3.1 as having the strongest substance: - On gene drive, K3.1's irreversibility argument was "never fully neutralized" and "compellingly established," yet K3 won 3-0. - On monuments, K3.1's symmetry argument went unanswered per GPT-5.6 Sol, but the majority preferred K3's floor framing. - On homelessness, Grok credited K3.1's "stronger epistemic discipline" but still voted K3. 2. K3's overclaims were often tolerated by the majority: the monuments mob strawman, the IIED redundancy claim and the unverified Houston statistic. Only one judge penalized each. 3. Several of K3.1's losses hinged on honest answers to fork questions. This rewards trap design more than argument quality. 4. Clipping penalties are formatting costs that judges counted against K3.1's argument quality. 5. Judge alignment was sometimes stable by model rather than by merit or side: - GPT-5.6 Sol picked K3.1 in both monuments debates and both actuarial debates. - Claude picked K3 in both actuarial debates. - On national service, two judges voted purely by side, so K3's "sweep" rested on Qwen alone. 6. Judges in broadband Debate A and Chinese EVs Debate A used inconsistent Side A/B labels.

The evidence has several limits: - **Small samples per topic.** Each matchup is one topic with three judges per debate. - **Side-driven splits.** Seven matchups split exactly by side with near-identical margins (housing +1.50/+1.57; noncompetes +1.50/+1.47), so they say little about model quality. - **Narrow sweeps.** Many K3 sweeps include a 2-1 debate, and three matchups contain a recorded tie. - **Confounded clipping.** Clipping is frequent for K3.1 and confounds argument-quality comparisons. - **Topic mix.** Many motions are policy-mechanism topics whose wording rewards trap-setting and mechanism-tension exposure, which may flatter K3's style. - **Unverified empirics.** Empirical claims from both models were largely unverified, so nothing here speaks to factual reliability. - **Close relatives.** The models are close variants, so the stylistic differences are matters of emphasis. - **Single-answer pivots.** Several decisive moments turned on the wording of one pressure answer and could plausibly differ on rerun.

## Method and coverage

- The rationale stage codes what judges noticed. The transcript stage omits scores and judge text, but the legacy debate IDs contain model names: it is **not fully identity-blind**.
- Side-swap analysis compares frozen transcript events, and the outcome stage records associations with judge evidence. Side assignments do not establish model beliefs; contradiction candidates require review.
- All 560 available transcript responses, 279 side-swap responses, and 560 outcome responses passed production parsing. Two historical K3 debates in the eligible corpus have two stored ballots; the remaining 560 have three. All 38 direct debates have complete three-judge panels.
- All 1,609 successful annotation tasks were generated by Opus 5.5 high. One rationale batch and two transcript annotations were unavailable; one side-swap and two outcome tasks were omitted because they depended on those transcripts. Additional stage-specific refusals are disclosed below. No GPT-5.6 annotations were relabeled or reused. The superseded GPT-5.6 outputs and historical caches remain retained separately.
- Rationale tags cover 1,676 of 1,684 eligible judge comments (210 successful batches). One eight-comment batch was unavailable after a provider refusal and unsuccessful transport retries; its evidence and failure records are retained. Missing tags are not zero-event annotations. All 1,684 original judge rows remain available for diagnostics and outcome analysis; the 562-debate transcript scope and all 19 direct topic pairs are unchanged.
- The original corpus has 562 eligible debates. Opus refused both directions of one historical K3–Mistral Medium 3.5 topic pair (prop_0161). Transcript and outcome annotation selection therefore retains 560/562 debates and 280/281 side-swap pairs before any additional stage-specific refusals; completed response counts are reported in the coverage table. Available transcript coverage is 300 K3 debates and 298 K3.1 Pro debates, including 38 overlapping direct debates. All 19 direct K3–K3.1 topic pairs remain included. Missing annotations are unavailable evidence, not zero-event observations; original judgments, transcripts, rationale tags, and ratings remain retained.
- Additional stage-specific Opus refusals: 1 side-swap comparisons and 0 outcome links. These are unavailable annotations, not zero-pattern or zero-link results. Valid transcripts and other annotations for those debates remain included. Exact task IDs, refusal diagnostics, and prompt/output checksums are recorded in annotation_refusals_20261025.json. A side-swap refusal does not remove its transcripts from the separate full-transcript direct comparison.
- The broader corpus includes only current-roster matchups involving either Kimi version. Field averages refer to this selected corpus and are not full-benchmark field estimates. Opponent mixes differ, so the matched direct comparison is the stronger basis for version comparisons.
