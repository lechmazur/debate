## Kimi K3

### Style

Kimi K3 is an engagement-first debater. In blind transcript review, direct engagement appeared in 298/300 debates (99%) and question use in 293/300 (98%). Weighing showed up in 238/300 (79%) and confident specificity in 195/300 (65%). Its typical move is to accept the opponent's frame and argue it can meet it. In one debate it took on PRO's burden directly: "The honest expectation is prices roughly unchanged for the median household — precisely the burden PRO insists I must meet." It also dismantles showcase examples one at a time rather than contesting the mechanism in the abstract, for instance offering a confounder for Tokyo's flat prices. Repetition was observed in 188/300 debates (63%). That is a frequency of the behavior, not a verdict on it.

### Strengths

Judges scored K3 above the current field on every diagnostic axis.
- **Rebuttal quality** is its standout: 8.12, or +0.53 versus the field and +0.98 [+0.83, +1.12] versus opponents.
- **Rhetorical effectiveness** is its highest mean at 8.36 (+0.89 versus opponents).
- **Argument strength** is 8.03 (+0.78 versus opponents).

Direct engagement was the most-mentioned judge category, appearing in 572/898 judge rows (63.7%). Panels reached a majority on it in 203/300 debates. Argument construction followed at 48.3% of rows. These figures show the categories appeared, not that judges approved. Judges did, however, give explicit praise, such as "excellent economic analysis on land values and macro headwinds."

### Weaknesses

- **Grounding is its thinnest edge.** It scored 7.75, only +0.22 versus the field (+0.41 versus opponents). Evidence use was tagged in just 11.2% of judge rows.
- **Concessions become liabilities.** This is the most consistent concrete weakness. Across sides, its concessions were "turned into admissions" by opponents, and its walk-backs only partly recovered. One judge credited the opponent for "leveraging Side B's concessions about a Miami" glut.
- **Possible inconsistency, unconfirmed.** Contradiction candidates appeared in 86/149 side-swap groups (58%). These are review flags, not established inconsistencies.

### Execution

Across 300 debates and 1,500 turns, K3 averaged 248.8 words per turn and used 86.7% of the word limit. It complied with Q/A labels 100% of the time and had no missing claim IDs. Clipping is a modest issue: 5.5% of turns were deterministically truncated, which suggests it occasionally overruns its budget.

### Side Effects

**Judge scores.** K3 scores slightly higher as CON on four of five axes. The largest gap is rebuttal: 8.25 as CON versus 7.99 as PRO. Rhetoric is identical at 8.36 on both sides.

**Behavior across side swaps.** Stable behavior appeared in 147/149 groups (99%) and advocacy flexibility in 130/149 (87%). Side-specific behavior appeared in 53% of groups and evidence asymmetry in 39%. One example: as CON it recast Austin's rent decline as mean reversion, while as PRO it cited the same decline as proof that building lowers prices.

### Overall Read

K3 is a strong, highly responsive rebuttal debater that judges consistently rate above its peers.

**What holds it back:**
- Grounding that lags its rhetoric.
- Tactical concessions that opponents exploit.
- Flexible reuse of evidence across sides.

**How outcomes line up:** Outcomes aligned with the transcript analysis in 286/300 debates (95%). Judges disagreed in 33% of debates, and links were conflicted in 24%. These are associations, not causes.

**Coverage and caveats:**
- Response coverage is near-complete: 560/562 transcripts and 279/281 side-swap comparisons.
- Model names appear in debate IDs, so the judge rationales were not fully identity-blind.
- All comparisons depend on which opponents were scheduled.

## Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)

### Style

Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) is a **clash-first, test-demanding debater**. Blind transcript annotation covered all 298 current-peer debates, and every debate contained at least one tagged event. Direct engagement appeared in 295/298 (99%), questioning in 292/298 (98%), weighing in 242/298 (81%), burden setting in 212/298 (71%), and strategic concession in 193/298 (65%).

A recurring move is reframing the burden around necessity rather than benefit. In one example, it argued that the opponent "assumes what must be proved: that the proposal is needed, rather than merely profitable." Side-swap groups describe the same habit on both sides: it presses for operational tests and separates surface claims from what the motion actually requires.

### Strengths

- **Rebuttal and grounding are judge-perceived edges.**
  - Rebuttal quality averaged 7.72, or +0.14 versus the field. The within-judge advantage over opponents was +0.31, with a 95% CI of [+0.14, +0.49].
  - Grounding/epistemics averaged 7.69, or +0.16 versus the field. The opponent advantage was +0.31, with a 95% CI of [+0.19, +0.42].
  - Both intervals exclude zero.
- **Engagement is what judges discuss most.** Direct engagement was tagged in 497/890 judge rows (55.8%) and reached a panel majority in 172/298 debates. These figures show salience, not approval. Still, they pair with the rebuttal advantage above.
- **Consistency across sides.** Stable behavior appeared in 146/148 event-bearing side-swap groups, and advocacy flexibility in 133/148.

### Weaknesses

- **Rhetoric lags.** Rhetorical effectiveness averaged 7.58, or −0.22 versus the field. The opponent gap was −0.23, with a 95% CI of [−0.36, −0.10]. This is its only clearly negative axis.
- **Originality and argument strength are near field level.** The differences were −0.06 and +0.02, and their intervals cross zero.
- **Concessions can cost it.** Concession was tagged in 23.8% of judge rows. That count includes favorable and unfavorable cases. One judge comment, however, described a concession that "effectively granted the normative core of the motion." The same comment noted that a later burden distinction "never fully escapes having conceded." Side-swap notes describe the intended pattern as granting a narrow point and contesting its significance. When that line slips, judges notice.
- **Contradiction candidates were flagged in 21/148 groups (14%).** These are prompts for review, not confirmed inconsistencies.

### Execution

- **Turn length:** 253.5 raw words per turn on average, using 86.4% of the word limit.
- **Clipping is material:** 25.8% of turns were deterministically clipped.
- **Clean formatting:** Q/A label compliance was 100%, and there were no missing claim IDs.
- **Format compliance in judge comments:** this category appeared in 34.5% of judge rows, with a panel majority in 100 debates. The tag records mentions, not a failure rate. Clipping may plausibly contribute to the rhetoric deficit, but the source does not establish that link.

### Side Effects

- **CON scores higher on most axes.** Rebuttal was 7.87 on CON versus 7.58 on PRO. Grounding was 7.74 versus 7.64, and argument strength 7.67 versus 7.55.
- **Rhetoric reverses the pattern.** It scored 7.68 on PRO versus 7.49 on CON.
- **Some asymmetries appear in side-swap groups.** Side-specific behavior showed up in 48% of event-bearing groups, evidence asymmetry in 17%, and moral-standard asymmetry in 16%.
- **Possible interpretation:** the model may be most natural attacking an unproven necessity claim from CON. This is a reading of the pattern, not an established finding.

### Overall Read

This is a disciplined, rigorous rebutter. It wins judge credit on engagement and epistemics but trails peers on persuasive polish, and it clips about a quarter of its turns.

**Caveats:**
- Outcome links align in 95% of debates, but judge disagreement appears in 39%.
- Rationale tags cover 890 of the 894 judge rows used for scoring, and 866 of those 890 rows contain tagged events.
- The corpus is not fully identity-blind.
- Field comparisons depend on the scheduled opponents.
