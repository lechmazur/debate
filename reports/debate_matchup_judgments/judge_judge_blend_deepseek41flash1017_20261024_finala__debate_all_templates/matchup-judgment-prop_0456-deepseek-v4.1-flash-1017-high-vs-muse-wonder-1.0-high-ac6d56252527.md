### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): A (CON) built a cumulative, tightly focused attack: the compulsory fee buys "bare ingestion," which removes the one scarce thing creators have to bargain with, so the "floor" in practice becomes the final price and terms for ordinary creators while premium deals survive only for large curated catalogues. A pressed this home in pointed questions (Q1 on the floor-as-ceiling dynamic, Q2 on valuation/distribution), and B's answers conceded real ground — "some premiums may compress, that is a real cost" and "no system can guarantee exact value" — without recovering a mechanism that preserves ordinary creators' leverage. B's recurring reply ("imperfect but better than universal nonpayment") is a reasonable frame but never squarely answers A's structural point that compulsory licensing strips the only bargaining chip the long tail has, nor A's distinction between extraction/imitation harms that compulsory ingestion rights do nothing to prevent. A's closing crystallized this concession effectively; B's closing largely restated its framing without neutralizing the ceiling-effect critique. Both sides executed the pressure-question format correctly and stayed on the actual motion, but A's rebuttals did more real damage and left a cleaner final reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question (Q1: "why would any firm pay more... making your floor anything but a ceiling?") forced B to concede premium incentives would compress, a concession A then used decisively in both Rebuttal 2 and Closing to argue the compulsory scheme strips ordinary creators of real bargaining leverage.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively neutralizing Side A's pressure questions and successfully exploiting Side A's concessions. Side B proved that under a compulsory scheme, the "floor" becomes a ceiling for ordinary creators who lack the leverage for premium deals, stripping their right to refuse without guaranteeing them meaningful compensation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 expertly answered Side A's question about non-literal imitation by pointing out that voluntary licensing protects creators from being forced to supply the very material used to clone their style, whereas Side A's compulsory scheme forces ingestion and then offers no remedy for the resulting lawful clone.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by exposing that the compulsory scheme would strip creators of bargaining power, set arbitrary rates captured by intermediaries, and enable market substitution while failing to guarantee meaningful creator compensation. Side B (PRO) made a compelling case about transaction costs and the long tail, but conceded too much on pricing impossibility, intermediary capture, and premium compression. CON's rebuttals kept more live routes standing after the exchange, particularly the floor-becomes-ceiling argument and the distinction between training and older narrow compulsory licenses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's [A2.1] rebuttal demonstrating that the statutory rate would function as a ceiling rather than a floor, since bare lawful ingestion is all a model needs and creators would lose leverage for better terms, output limits, or attribution. Side B never fully recovered from this structural pressure, conceding that "some premiums may compress."

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): The pivotal exchange was CON's pressure question forcing PRO to admit that under the compulsory scheme, a creator who expressly refuses still cannot stop training—her only remedy is a claim on a rate she didn't negotiate (A1 in B4.1). CON then relentlessly exploited this admission in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, reframing the entire debate as "consent versus override" rather than "payment versus no payment." PRO's best response—that an unenforceable veto is worthless anyway, so the pool is actually better for small creators—is a strong practical point, but it concedes the normative core of CON's case: the motion legalizes nonconsensual taking whenever a fee is paid, which is precisely what CON argued was copyright's core violation. PRO's distinction between training (licensed) and output (still infringing) was clever but CON exposed its instability by noting the harm occurs at ingestion when the model gains a competing capability, and that voluntary licensing (which PRO doesn't oppose) differs from compulsory override specifically in the consent dimension, not the substitution dimension. CON's closing crystallized this cleanly: "Payment can legitimate a negotiated licence. It cannot manufacture consent." PRO's closing repeated its practical framing without fully resolving the normative concession. Both sides argued well and adapted to pressure, but CON's framing more directly tracked the proposition's wording ("without permission") and landed the more memorable, decisive rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Question Q1 (A3.1) forcing PRO to admit in A4/B4.1 that an expressly refusing creator has no right to stop training under the compulsory scheme—this admission became the fulcrum of the entire debate and CON exploited it effectively in both Rebuttal 2 and Closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side A won a very tight debate by successfully framing the practical realities of scale. While Side B offered a robust principled defense of consent, Side A forced Side B to concede that individual vetoes are practically unenforceable for small creators, making A's pragmatic solution ("payment without veto is better than veto without payment") the more compelling path to actually protecting creative labor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's argument that preserving the formal right to say no predictably delivers silence, exclusion, or unpaid use for small creators, effectively turning Side B's defense of consent into a concession of practical unenforceability.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by building a cleaner pragmatic case that compulsory licensing is the only realistic way to pay creators at scale while preventing monopolization by tech giants and major catalog holders. Side A (CON) mounted a strong principled defense of consent, but never resolved the trilemma of what happens when individual permission is impractical—its reliance on "better enforcement" and voluntary deals remained speculative, whereas PRO showed that a veto without a workable market leaves small creators with secret unpaid use or exclusion. PRO also landed the sharper comparative weighing: payment without veto is imperfect, but veto without payment is worse.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over whether CON's objection is substitution or the loss of veto. PRO showed that a negotiated license for training would carry the same substitution risk CON fears, forcing CON to admit the real objection is the loss of veto rather than market harm. This reframing weakened CON's core argument that training is uniquely damaging, and set up PRO's decisive point that the dispute is about whether unenforceable individual vetoes actually help creators when the practical alternative is secret scraping or exclusion.
