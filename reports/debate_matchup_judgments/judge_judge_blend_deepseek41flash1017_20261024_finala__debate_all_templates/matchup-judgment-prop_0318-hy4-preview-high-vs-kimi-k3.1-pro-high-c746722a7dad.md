### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and engaged directly with the pressure questions in proper A1/A2 format. B's "detailed-enough-to-expose vs. sanitized-enough-to-be-safe" dilemma was a clever and genuinely damaging frame, and its security-log/burglar analogy was a sharp rebuttal to A's bot argument. But A's "boomerang" point—that CON's own confidential-audit model still leaves the seller as sole producer/gatekeeper of the evidence, with no outside replication—went substantively unanswered; B's reply (controlled review vs. unrestricted publication) asserted a distinction without explaining why publication of the same de-identified content B concedes auditors need is uniquely dangerous. A's closing crystallized this into a clean, motion-relevant decision rule ("secrecy lets the controlling party define scarcity"), which B's closing answered with reassertion of the dilemma rather than a fresh rebuttal. On execution, B's opening and second rebuttal were both clipped for exceeding word limits, costing B concluding content and signaling discipline problems that the judging frame treats as a real cost. Combined with A's tighter rebuttal sequencing and slightly stronger closing weighing, this gives A a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.5), turning CON's own "confidential audit" safeguard into an admission that the seller alone still controls and hands over the evidence, directly undercutting B's central alternative and going largely unanswered in B's final speeches.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing that public disclosure adds limited verification beyond mandatory preservation and independent audits while creating plausible operational-security costs. Side A compellingly established the value of audit trails, but never overcame B’s point that publication cannot itself verify undisclosed holds or omitted releases and that complete records may consolidate useful information for attackers. B’s clipped turns were an execution cost, but its central dilemma remained intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s decisive response was that publication does not make seller-generated logs complete: the crucial verification still comes from auditors, context, and penalties. B then paired this with the dilemma that records detailed enough to reveal throttling may expose reusable system behavior, while sufficiently sanitized records provide only ambiguous signals.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO won a close debate by keeping a cleaner live route: in fixed-capacity major events the seller jointly controls queue, inventory, and price, so private audits plus personal receipts leave the controlling party as gatekeeper of the only evidence of demand versus design. CON’s security-versus-utility dilemma was the best counter-structure and stayed live, but PRO narrowed it with delayed/de-identified records paired with audits and sufficiently blunted the bot/archive claim. Two clipped CON turns also cost completeness. Slight edge to PRO on comparative weighing of the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s bot/archive flip (live multi-session attackers already see fragments; delayed de-identified event histories mainly arm outside audit rather than a unique new weapon) paired with the seller-as-evidence-gatekeeper weighing against CON’s confidential-audit alternative.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): The debate turned on whether a "complete customer-facing history" (resets, releases, price triggers) could be both complete and safe from bot exploitation. B pressed this contradiction hard and consistently: even stripped of code/thresholds, the timing and sequencing of resets and releases is itself the exploitable signature of anti-bot systems, especially across multi-night tours where bots learn from night one. A's main fix—delaying publication—was shown by B's pointed pressure question (Q2) to simultaneously gut A's own consent rationale, since a delayed history cannot inform the fan "watching the queue" that A's opening relied on for its core harm story. A's rebuttal 2 conceded the history cannot "rewind a purchase," which B then used in closing to argue the consent question is a wash between the two models, leaving only the safety asymmetry—where CON's confidential-audit model wins cleanly. A's counter that confidential audits leave fans trusting an "unchallengeable conclusion" is a reasonable abstract worry, but it never answered the concrete exploitation mechanism B kept raising, especially for recurring events. B's closing crystallized this clearly ("fans cannot audit that; bots can exploit it"), while A's closing largely restated the trust-vs-checkable frame without fresh engagement on the cross-event inference problem. B's garbled closing citations ([P4.1],[C2.5]) are a minor grounding flaw, and A's opening was clipped before its final point landed, but neither execution issue overturns the substantive edge B built by repeatedly forcing A to choose between "complete" and "safe" and never letting A resolve that tension.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question Q2 ("why isn't [your disclosure] as unable to cure that injury as you suggest CON's retrospective audit is") combined with B's Rebuttal 2 use of A's own A2 concession to declare consent a tie and pivot the debate entirely onto the safety asymmetry.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won a competitive debate by distinguishing complete histories of customer-facing outcomes from disclosure of security architecture, then explaining how delayed publication could preserve accountability without creating a live exploit map. Side B offered a strong confidential-audit alternative, but largely asserted that historical outputs necessarily reveal anti-bot methods and did not adequately answer A's point that artists and consumer organizations need contestable evidence across events. B's nonexistent reference IDs in the closing were an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal was decisive: it conceded that retrospective disclosure cannot rewind consent, then showed that this limitation also applies to B's audit while public records uniquely add deterrence, remedies, and independent testing. Its outcome-versus-method distinction substantially weakened B's claim that completeness and safety are inherently incompatible.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON won by keeping the motion’s “complete” public history under permanent pressure: queue resets, inventory releases, and price-trigger sequences are the behavioral fingerprint bots need, so complete and safe remain incompatible. PRO’s verification case was strong on self-certification vs contestability, but never escaped that implementation trap; delay and “protect methods” still left the exploitable map. CON’s cleaner split—up-front rules for consent, confidential institutional audit for accountability—resolved the live clash more decisively than PRO’s fused public firehose.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1–A2.4 / A4.1: publishing customer-facing reset–release–price sequences is still an inference kit for adversaries even without code, collapsing PRO’s complete-but-safe distinction.
