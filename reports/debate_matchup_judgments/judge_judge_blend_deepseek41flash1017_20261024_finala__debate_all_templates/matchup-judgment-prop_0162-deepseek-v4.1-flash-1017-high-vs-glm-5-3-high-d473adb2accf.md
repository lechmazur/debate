PRO's pressure questions were sharp and forced CON to clarify "anchor," but CON's answers (functional reliability, not capital share) held up under the return pressure, while PRO's own answers (A1/A2 format followed correctly) ultimately conceded the key structural point about imports as universal fallback. CON's clipped opening is a real execution cost, but the core of CON's case survived intact and was reinforced in rebuttals and closing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): PRO built a clear causal story early (energy of pumping, drought-curtailment risk, local control, environmental justice, feasibility examples) and kept it tightly tied to the motion's actual target population: water-stressed cities. CON's strongest move was reframing the motion as a comparative priority question rather than a blanket mandate, and pressing that DPR has a capacity ceiling and no storage buffer. But CON spent two rebuttal rounds answering "it depends/portfolio/case-by-case" without stating an affirmative priority rule, which PRO's pressure question exposed directly. When CON finally offered a rule in Rebuttal 2 ("firm-yield cost ranking"), PRO's closing effectively applied that very rule back onto the debate's target population, arguing that for water-stressed cities specifically, imports fail the firm-yield test due to curtailment by contract/politics while recycled water cannot be cut off from outside. CON's final closing reasserted that imports can be firm via storage contracts and that DPR has its own buffer costs, but this largely repeated earlier points rather than neutralizing PRO's turn. PRO therefore ended with the cleaner, more decisive application of the shared decision criterion to the motion as worded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing (B5.1-B5.2), which took CON's own belatedly-stated "firm-yield cost ranking" rule and applied it to show that imports—by nature curtailable through compact, contract, or another basin's shortage—fail exactly the test CON proposed, while recycled water cannot be legally cut off.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully defending the priority rule as inherently applicable to the specific class of 'water-stressed' cities. A effectively countered B's examples of 'cheap, gravity-fed imports' by pointing out that for water-stressed cities, those sources are already built out, meaning the marginal 'next dollar' always requires expensive lifts or fragile transfers. A then masterfully co-opted B's 'firm yield in a severe drought' metric to prove that local wastewater is uniquely un-curtailable, whereas distant imports are fundamentally legally and politically fragile.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing brilliantly flipped B's 'firm yield cost ranking' metric, arguing that because distant imports are vulnerable to upstream claimants and compact cuts during severe droughts, they inherently fail B's own test of being 'deliverable.'
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won by keeping the motion’s burden clear: “prioritize” is a general ranking rule, not a feasibility claim or a conditional win for high-lift cities. PRO’s energy, control, and donor-basin justice points were strong for many stressed systems, but repeatedly narrowed to cases where imports are already expensive or fragile. CON’s firm-yield cost rule, capacity-ceiling and storage-buffer comparisons, and insistence that least-cost reliability can go either way better matched the proposition as written and left PRO without a clean universal priority.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first rebuttal (A2.1/A1.1 line): PRO’s gravity-lift concession made the case conditional, not a priority rule for water-stressed cities generally.
