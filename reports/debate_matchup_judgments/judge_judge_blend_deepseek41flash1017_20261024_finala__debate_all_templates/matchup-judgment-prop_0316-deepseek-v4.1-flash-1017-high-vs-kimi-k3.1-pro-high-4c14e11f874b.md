### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by framing notice as a low-cost condition of informed participation in a consequentially curated service. Side A sharply showed that disclosure cannot diagnose individual suppression and that harm-specific duties may be more actionable, but those points did not establish why users should be denied the basic information alongside those remedies. Its gaming and boilerplate objections were plausible yet insufficient against B’s middle-ground disclosure of broad signal categories without weights or code. Side A’s two clipped turns were an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B’s answer to the boilerplate-versus-gaming dilemma: broad categories and material effects can inform users without exposing weights, thresholds, or live triggers. A’s reply that payment disclosure supplies most of the actionable value was strong, but it did not neutralize B’s separate consent and transparency rationale.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully separating material commercial terms (like paid placement) from the technical implementation (AI). By conceding that payment terms should be disclosed while proving that an AI-specific notice fails to give users actionable agency, Side B neutralized Side A's best impacts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal decisively exposed that Side A's most compelling example—users needing to know if payment affects placement—does not require or justify an AI-specific right, as it is a commercial term that applies regardless of the technology used.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side B built a more compelling and morally resonant case that the fact of machine allocation is itself a material term of the matchmaking service, effectively turning Side A's concession on commercial disclosures into a reason to mandate AI disclosure. Side B also handled the "arbitrary trigger" pressure well by distinguishing between preventing semantic evasion and acknowledging gaps in broader regulation. Side A made strong points about targeting actual harms rather than technical labels, but was weakened by clipped speeches and a less persuasive defense of keeping the implementation technique secret from users.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it trapped Side A's concession that users deserve to know when payment buys placement, arguing that if allocation is the product, the fact that a machine is allocating it is equally material and cannot be hidden.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that the right provides material knowledge without needing to diagnose every ranking outcome or reveal exploitable model details. Side A’s mechanism-versus-outcome distinction was sharp, but it imposed an overly demanding usefulness test: disclosure can inform purchases, app choice, and interpretation of silence even without granting control. B also established that audits and outcome data complement rather than replace notice. The clipped opening was a minor execution cost, but B still made the choice clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal decisively answered A’s dilemma with a concrete notice that revealed AI’s functional role without weights or signals. It then used A’s concession that bare notice creates no new manipulation capability to substantially neutralize the gaming objection.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully defending a modest but highly impactful interpretation of the motion. A proved that even "boilerplate" notice provides essential psychological and consumer value by breaking the illusion that a lack of matches is a pure romantic verdict. Side B demanded that the notice provide precise causal leverage (outcome data), but A correctly pointed out that this sets an impossibly high bar designed to protect concealment rather than foster basic understanding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 provided a precise disclosure sentence that successfully navigated B's dilemma between "useless boilerplate" and "gameable blueprint," proving that simple notice can inform users without compromising security.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B successfully proved that knowing AI is involved prevents users from internalizing algorithmic suppression as personal romantic failure, a specific harm that Side A's "outcome data" alternative fails to solve. Side B also provided a highly effective, concrete disclosure sentence in Rebuttal 2 that perfectly balanced informativeness with security, effectively dismantling Side A's claim that any meaningful notice would either be useless boilerplate or a gaming roadmap. Side A relied too heavily on a pedantic text trap regarding the word "when" and failed to adequately distinguish opaque AI ranking from explicit user filters.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, specifically A2, where they provided a concrete disclosure sentence ("In Discover, AI ranks your profile and helps decide which members are offered it...") that directly answered Side A's pressure question and neutralized the dilemma between useless vagueness and dangerous specificity.
