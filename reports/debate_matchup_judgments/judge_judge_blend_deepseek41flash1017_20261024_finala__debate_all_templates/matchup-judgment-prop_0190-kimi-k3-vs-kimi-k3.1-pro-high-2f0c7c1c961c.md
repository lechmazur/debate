Overall A narrowed the clash more effectively and closed by crystallizing the single question (who bears cost first) in a way that was harder for B to dislodge, while B's closing, though solid, repeated already-made points without decisively defeating A's concession-based victory narrative. This is a clear but competitive win for A, not a blowout, since B's needs-based/entitlement framing remained a live, coherent alternative throughout.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B built the sharpest through-line of the debate: once A folded income-based subsidies into its own case for the legal cap, B pressed the dilemma relentlessly — if subsidies do the protective work, the cap is redundant; if they don't, the cap is hollow. A's best answer (the cap enforces a non-negotiable right that illness cannot raise price, independent of subsidy adequacy) is a legitimate philosophical move, but B's "symbolic vs real" framing and especially the "loud failure vs silent failure" comparison (transparent prices fail visibly and are fixable; capped systems fail invisibly through narrow networks and covert selection) gave the debate a cleaner, more concrete decision rule that A's closing never directly rebutted. B's pressure questions also landed more precisely, forcing A into defending a largely conceptual answer about rights, while A's questions, though reasonable, were answered adequately by B's "both systems need oversight" symmetry argument. Compounding this, A's Opening and Rebuttal 2 were both clipped for exceeding word limits, costing A completed arguments and polish at exactly the moments the dilemma needed fuller resolution. B stayed within limits, kept answers clean and well-labeled, and consistently tightened the comparison round over round.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 move — "That rebuttal conceded the core of this debate... either the subsidies do the work, making the cap redundant, or they don't, making the cap insufficient" — set the frame that dominated the rest of the debate and was never fully neutralized by A.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that subsidies and premium limits address distinct disadvantages: poverty and elevated health risk. Side A’s targeted-subsidy alternative was credible, but its redundancy claim ignored that community rating itself reduces sick people’s exposure through risk pooling. Its covert-selection argument also overstated the case by treating avoidance as necessary despite Side B’s risk adjustment and regulatory mechanisms. The clipping costs Side B somewhat, but its core path remained clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal most decisively answered Side A’s dilemma: a cap independently prevents illness from raising the essential premium, while subsidies handle remaining income-based affordability. Side A then largely restated that subsidies did all the work and did not fully answer this distinction.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively isolating the specific effect of the premium cap apart from the shared mechanism of subsidies, proving the cap acts as a regressive, means-blind tax. Side A also successfully framed the cap as converting covert cherry-picking from an option into a necessity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in Rebuttal 2 to Side B's pressure on cherry-picking, where Side A strategically conceded the word 'entirely' to argue that capping premiums turns covert avoidance from a mere option into a survival necessity for insurers.
