### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but PRO built a more complete comparative mechanism: credential escalation requires no complementary investment while opportunity-creation does, making inflation structurally more likely to outrun genuine opportunity, and PRO applied this consistently against CON's examples (noting that even "winning" graduates pay an inflated price, and that CON's hiring-reform rebuttal identifies a remedy rather than erasing an already-incurred cost). CON's strongest material—counterfactual exclusion, career-long and societal benefits of expanded professional access—was substantive but repeatedly reduced to "you haven't proven the comparison" without building an equally concrete mechanism for why opportunity's reach matches or exceeds inflation's reach; its late attempt (career/societal multiplier effects) came only in the final rebuttal and wasn't fully developed before closing. CON also suffered two mid-sentence clippings (Opening and Rebuttal 1) that cut off its final points, a real execution cost per the rules. PRO's pressure question on asymmetric crediting of employer behavior was also more incisive and went materially unanswered beyond restating the burden gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 answer in Rebuttal 2 ("If expansion deserves credit when employers use that pool productively, it cannot be insulated when they use it to raise unnecessary barriers") directly exposed an inconsistency in CON's framework and was never fully neutralized, since CON's reply mostly reasserted that the comparison remained unproven rather than rebutting the joint-causation logic itself.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by providing a decisive structural mechanism for why credential inflation outweighed genuine opportunity: the asymmetry of effects. B successfully argued that genuine opportunity requires complementary investments, while inflation requires only an employer raising a cutoff, allowing inflation to spread further and faster. Side A argued well but was weighed down by minor clipping penalties and a reliance on hypothetical future hiring reform to mitigate past harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's argument that if expansion gets credit for employers using graduates productively, it must also bear the blame when the same graduate oversupply allows employers to impose unnecessary screening barriers.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins the comparison the motion requires. It concedes genuine professional openings but gives a mechanism for predominance: raising thresholds across existing work costs employers nothing, while turning more degrees into better work requires capabilities, demand and funded complements, plus it shows inflation burdens winners as well as excluded non-graduates. CON (A) effectively shows real gains beyond first jobs and that hiring reform could preserve education while removing wasteful filters, but never provides a counter-scale and repeats existence-not-outweighing without its own weighing, with two openings clipped for length.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2 – joint causation (abundant pool makes screening easier, so credit for productive use implies responsibility for barrier-raising) and the asymmetry that opportunity needs investment while inflation does not, neutralizing the hiring-reform insulation claim.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): PRO built a genuinely compelling signaling-theory mechanism (cheap screening, rising floors, excluded school-leavers) and landed sharp lines distinguishing capability from eligibility. But PRO never closed the logical gap CON repeatedly exposed: that a plausible mechanism for inflation does not by itself establish predominance over genuine capability gains. CON's consistent framework—count deployable capability and widened access against unnecessary barriers, and don't assume cheapness of screening equals magnitude of harm—survived PRO's pressure questions cleanly, with CON's A1/A2 answers directly engaging rather than dodging. PRO, by contrast, partially retreated under CON's Q2 by discarding its own apprenticeship counterfactual rather than defending it, slightly undercutting its opening comparison. CON's closing crisply reiterated the unresolved burden ("less conditional does not mean greater"), which PRO's closing answered with conviction but not with new logical resolution. Compounding this, PRO's Opening, Rebuttal 1, and Rebuttal 2 were all clipped for exceeding word limits, repeatedly cutting off its own closing punches—a real, repeated execution cost the rules instruct to weigh against it. On balance, CON's cleaner discipline and firmer hold on the unresolved predominance burden give it a narrow but real edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point that "cheapness to employers is not a measure of frequency, social cost, or comparative magnitude" directly punctured PRO's attempt to convert the screening mechanism into proof of predominance, and PRO's closing never fully answered this specific logical gap.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by demonstrating superior logical discipline and systematically dismantling Side B's route to proving predominance. While Side B offered an elegant framework—that screening is 'less conditional' than genuine opportunity—Side A successfully exposed this as conflating a mechanism's ease with its comparative magnitude.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing brilliantly neutralized Side B's core comparative mechanism: pointing out that an employer's ability to 'cheaply' raise credential filters does not mathematically prove those harms outweighed the massive expansion of genuine capability.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON gave the cleaner weighing path. It separated debt/underemployment from credential inflation, conceded a real loss for excluded school-leavers but defined a test counting no new opportunity where eligibility is merely repurchased, and showed PRO's core move confuses cheap screening with large predominance. PRO's asymmetry claim and ticket metaphor were memorable but lacked aggregate comparison, relied on clipped openings, and misdescribed CON's counting rule after A4.3 was explicit. With no quantitative aggregate from either side, CON's burden to block predominance was met more convincingly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 A1/A2 - counting rule that unchanged-task degree buys no opportunity, plus cheapness does not equal frequency/magnitude, directly neutralizing B's automatic-inflation asymmetry.
