### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A offered the more original framing: controls may accelerate substitution, weaken Western research, and prematurely spend a crisis lever. But it overstated uncertain effects as certain and permanent while dismissing security friction as modest merely because its duration was unquantified. Side B more carefully established that frontier equipment remains a real bottleneck, that delay can create valuable defensive time, and that major commercial costs do not defeat the motion when military advantages may be irreversible. B also answered both pressure questions directly and supplied a usable failure standard. A’s repeated clipping was a modest execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest response was that dependence is useful only if governments eventually withhold tools or servicing. A sharply distinguished crisis-time leverage from peacetime controls, but B persuasively answered that this strategy depends on perfect warning and coordination while continued transfers increase adversary capability. That made restriction now the safer use of a bottleneck that may not last.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON won a competitive debate by making the decisive comparison clearer: unmeasured, leaky friction against a less decisive slice of military AI versus certain major commercial harm plus permanent creation of a rival tool industry. PRO’s bottleneck-and-insurance case was coherent and answered pressure honestly, but never pinned down why that friction outweighed a cost the motion itself calls major, while CON’s leverage-timing and captive-market turns kept narrowing the clash and left PRO defending peacetime preemption over crisis deterrence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s leverage paradox (Rebuttal 1 into Rebuttal 2): if the bottleneck is deep enough to buy delay, dependence is durable crisis leverage that peacetime controls spend early and permanently convert into self-sufficiency.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): CON (B) won the weighing clash. PRO concedes major permanent commercial loss and eventual substitution [A4.1, A2.4] but insists unquantified friction is valuable, and sets a failure test that even rival fielding chips wouldn't count [A4.3], making benefit non-falsifiable. CON's dilemma - if substitution is hard, dependence is durable leverage best held for crisis rather than spent early - and its mature-chip / Ukraine example reframes the bottleneck to the decisive slice of military AI, was never squarely neutralized. PRO's crisis-timing reply gambles less than CON's permanent competitor cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1-2 leverage-timing turn: if tools are hard to replicate, dependence is crisis leverage; blanket peacetime controls trigger early import substitution, creating a permanent rival industry PRO concedes may never return, versus leaky front-loaded friction on frontier compute.
