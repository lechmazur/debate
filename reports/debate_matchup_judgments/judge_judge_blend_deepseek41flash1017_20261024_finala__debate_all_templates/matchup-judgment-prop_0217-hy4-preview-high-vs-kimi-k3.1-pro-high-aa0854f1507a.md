### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built the more durable line of attack: it reframed "universal restriction" as something that can be staged and targeted at discretionary uses rather than an equal cut to everyone, which blunted PRO's central contrast. More decisively, CON's reliability critique — that a steep price leaves the pivotal choice with the customer, so an affluent user can "lawfully purchase" water the system cannot spare, while a ban creates an immediate obligation — was never squarely answered. PRO's own admission that a "rescue ban" may still be needed to catch the residual peak effectively conceded that restrictions remain the dependable backstop; PRO's reply ("that's PRO's hierarchy, not a refutation") relabeled the problem rather than resolving it. PRO also weakened its own "the meter does the work" simplicity claim by layering on occupancy-adjusted lifelines, submetering, landlord accountability, and suspense procedures — a concession CON exploited well in Rebuttal 1. PRO's case remained well organized and consistent (steady pressure, equity-by-design, standing incentive logic), but it never neutralized CON's reliability/timing objection or the "lawful purchase vs. violation" framing that anchored CON's closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's "paying the bill is not a shortage violation" point (A4.2-A4.4), combined with "a policy that needs restrictions to rescue it has identified its dependable core" (A4.7), was the most damaging exchange — PRO's answer that this is merely "PRO's hierarchy" never actually resolved why pricing should be trusted to deliver timely, sufficient reductions before restrictions must step in.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): A won a competitive debate by matching the motion’s “main reliance” wording more cleanly and keeping pressure on CON’s quiet upgrade from blunt universal cuts to tailored outdoor bans. B never fully neutralized the rebound and hidden-use problems of act-based rules, so A left the clearer comparative route to the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s A4 answers to B’s pressure Qs: affluent payers still shrink demand and fund fixes, with bans only as narrow backup; safeguards default-protect rather than punish first—turning B’s “rescue ban proves restrictions are core” into a hierarchy win for main reliance on prices.

It showed universal need not mean equal essential cuts – a drought-stage ban on irrigation targets the same heavy discretionary use PRO claims – and exposed that sharply rising prices leave the pivotal choice with the customer: affluent compliance is lawful but can still drain supply, revenue does not return water, and occupancy/master-meter/leak safeguards undermine “the meter does the work.” PRO’s engine/backup hierarchy collapsed when rescue bans were admitted as necessary.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON (A) won the decisive clash by consistently reframing the debate around the physical irreversibility of withdrawal during a shortage: "a pool filled this week cannot be unfilled by next month's bill." A repeatedly exploited B's own concessions ("may pay more," "may keep some irrigation yet water less," price only "discourages the next irrigation cycle") to show that pricing offers no guaranteed, immediate quantity reduction, whereas restrictions forbid withdrawal now and are enforceable as violations. A also answered B's strongest pressure question (the "what do you ban next" dilemma) by extending the restriction logic to other visible, enforceable uses rather than collapsing into intrusive household tiering, blunting what could have been a decisive attack. PRO (B) had a genuinely strong fairness and continuous-incentive case and asked sharp pressure questions, but its answers in Rebuttal 2 effectively conceded the core "this week's water" problem rather than resolving it, and B's case was clipped for exceeding word limits in three separate turns (opening, Rebuttal 1, Rebuttal 2), an execution cost that cut off potentially important closing content each time. A's discipline, sharper exploitation of concessions, and more memorable framing give it the edge in a close, competitive debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 / Closing turn exploiting B's concession that heavy users "may pay more" and "water less" rather than guaranteeing reduced withdrawal, crystallized in the line "a pool filled this week cannot be unfilled by next month's bill."
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): B won a competitive debate by locking the decisive metric as immediate, systemwide water kept in storage during shortage and keeping live the claim that sharp heavy-user prices mainly make excess lawful once paid. A’s fairness, lifeline, and “reaches every excess” case was strong and exposed B’s narrowing to visible outdoor lists, but A never neutralized inelastic wealthy response or the timing problem after conceding users may pay more and keep irrigating. Clipped A turns also left some comparison work unfinished.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s sustained reply that an ignored ban is a stoppable quantity violation while an ignored rising price is just a completed lawful withdrawal (pool/this-week water already gone).
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON kept the metric on water actually kept in storage now and exposed PRO's concession that heavy users may pay more and keep some use. PRO's price-as-immediate-deterrent was less credible than visible bans, and its repeated word-limit clipping cost clarity. A answered the intrusiveness pressure by extending blunt rules to commercial visible uses, while B never fully solved the timing problem - a pool filled in days.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.2 vs B3.1: A distinguished prohibition (illegal, observable, stoppable) from a price (lawful upon payment) to answer why fines deter and rising bills can be absorbed, directly undercutting B's claim that pricing is a stronger quantity control.
