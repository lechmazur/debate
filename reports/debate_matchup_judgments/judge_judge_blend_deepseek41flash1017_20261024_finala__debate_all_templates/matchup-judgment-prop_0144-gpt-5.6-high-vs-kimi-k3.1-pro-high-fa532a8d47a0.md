### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but B's rebuttal chain was tighter and more damaging. B's early dilemma ("if safeguards are insufficient, opt-in doesn't fix them; if sufficient, they can accompany a suggestion") was never cleanly resolved by A, who retreated to a "layered, not magical" defense that implicitly conceded the underlying product remains equally dangerous for everyone who opts in — including vulnerable searchers B had already flagged in its pressure question. B also successfully reframed the autonomy argument (newcomers can't choose what they don't know exists) and converted A's "changes behavior vs. meaningless" bind into a non-issue by distinguishing increased initiation from increased harm. A's strongest material — the claim that gradual, cumulative dependency evades single-message conduct rules — was raised repeatedly but met with B's "govern patterns, not messages" answer, which, while not airtight, was a live, adequate response that A did not further dismantle. Execution costs matter too: A's opening and closing were both clipped, costing it a clean final crystallization, while B's closing landed intact and delivered the sharper final frame ("safety wherever users enter versus obscurity plus the same residual risk"). On balance, B made the clash easier to resolve in its favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's dilemma in [B2.1] ("if protections are too incomplete to recommend responsibly, opt-in doesn't repair that; if they're sufficient, they can accompany a restrained suggestion") was the most decisive exchange — A never fully escaped it, eventually conceding via "layered, not magical" that opted-in users still face the same unaddressed risk, which B then exploited directly in Rebuttal 2 and the closing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON won the decisive clash: the motion’s remedy (block default suggestion) does not interrupt the harm PRO itself prioritizes—gradual, hard-to-detect dependency inside ongoing interaction. PRO strongly framed residual risk, distinctive reciprocal attachment, and “promotion ≠ access,” but never escaped CON’s dilemma that incomplete conduct rules remain incomplete after opt-in, while adequate rules can accompany a neutral labeled tile. CON also better separated awareness gains from harm causation and kept answering pressure with direct A1/A2 structure. Clipped openings/closings slightly hurt both; overall CON made the cleaner vote path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON R1 dilemma [A2.1]/A2.2]: if blackmail/exit/label rules are incomplete, opt-in does not repair them; if complete, they can sit beside a restrained suggestion—later sealed by [A4.4]–[A4.6] that initiation ≠ harm when design is governed.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO kept the motion narrow – no ban, just no auto-suggest by default – and identified a distinctive risk of simulated reciprocal attachment that games/feeds lack. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly, but B's core dilemma—"if dependency mechanisms are controlled, one labeled card poses no plausible danger; if uncontrolled, friction barely helps someone who opts in anyway"—was never squarely dismantled by A. A's best move was reframing CON's "best case" (onboarding selection of romance) as itself "user-initiated discovery" compatible with PRO, which B correctly identified in A2 as a concession narrowing PRO's own rule to cases without prior selection. From there, A had to defend why even non-selecting newcomers deserve a categorical default ban, resting mainly on "platform shouldn't take the first step"—an assertion more than a causal mechanism, since B had already argued notice ≠ intimacy and that disclosure/controls can accompany the suggestion itself. B's pressure questions (Q1 on the directory/suggestion line, Q2 on mechanism after controls) forced A into answers that were serviceable but ultimately reduced to reasserting the user-initiation distinction rather than closing the gap on why bare suggestion-without-entry causes harm. A's pressure questions were good but B's A1/A2 answers (implicit in B4.1-4.2, though not formally labeled A1:/A2:) handled them adequately, costing B a small execution point for not following the required A1:/A2: format explicitly, but not badly damaging the substance. Overall B had the tighter causal story and better exploited A's structural concession, giving B a clear but not dominant edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's A2/B2.1 dilemma (controlled mechanisms render one suggestion harmless; uncontrolled mechanisms make friction insufficient) forced A to retreat to assertion rather than mechanism, and A's concession that CON's onboarding-selection example already satisfies "user-initiated discovery" (A4.1-4.2) let B argue PRO had effectively conceded the central case.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping a narrow live rule—no platform-initiated romantic/sexual steering of newcomers by default—while absorbing Side A’s strongest onboarding example as already user-directed. B’s reversible-friction vs avoidable-exposure weighing, plus clear answers on initiation and burden, outpaced A’s otherwise strong causation dilemma that never fully justified preserving unsolicited auto-promotion once access, directories, and product controls remain.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2): treating explicit “romance” selection as user-directed discovery compatible with PRO, then pressing that unsolicited nudges still lack consent and marginal benefit.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO defined a narrow, user-initiation vs platform-initiation line and kept costs asymmetric: a brief, reversible delay vs avoidable intimate steering of new users at maximal informational disadvantage with conflicted incentives. CON's best case—explicit selection of romance then contextual card—is user-initiated discovery compatible with PRO, as B exposed in pressure and R2. CON's dilemma that controls fix product or extra search fails without controls was answered by PRO's complementarity point: safe-design rules remain needed but do not make the default nudge necessary, and cannot be assumed perfect. That leaves CON without consent or marginal benefit for true default nudges.
