### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side A won a competitive debate by distinguishing accountability for a finished document from evidence that the student developed the reasoning and drafting skills being assessed. Side B made a strong enforcement comparison, but never fully answered the novice-learning problem or showed how unverifiable disclosure prevents permitted AI assistance from becoming substantive substitution across most coursework. The clipping of two B turns was a minor additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest response was that B’s enforcement symmetry was incomplete: disclosure creates a claim, but not reliable evidence of how much work the model performed. This undercut B’s claim that disclosure itself meaningfully secures authorship, while preserving A’s distinction between verifying accuracy and certifying student competence.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully decoupling "accountability for accuracy" from "accountability for authorship," proving that the motion certifies the document rather than the student's reasoning. Side A relied heavily on the premise that take-home writing is inherently unobservable, but Side B successfully countered that process-based assignments (staged drafts, notes) make it observable—and that making AI the default for most coursework undermines those very safeguards.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Turn 8 Rebuttal 2, which pointed out that if Side A relies on process artifacts to verify disclosures, those same artifacts enforce a ban much more cleanly ("missing process evidence is itself a violation").
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully dismantling Side A's core enforcement mechanism. While Side A argued that disclosure creates a falsifiable claim that deters cheating, Side B convincingly demonstrated that this claim is only falsifiable through process evidence like staged drafts or vivas. Side B then executed a decisive comparative pivot: those same process tools are available under a ban, but under a ban, the default is unaided work, making missing process evidence a clear violation. Under Side A's model, the default is AI-assisted, meaning the university must actively disprove a student's vague disclosure label, effectively laundering the cheating. Side B's distinction between certifying the document versus certifying the student's cognitive process crystallized the pedagogical stakes perfectly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it answered the pressure questions by turning Side A's "falsifiable claim" argument against it, proving that disclosure acts as camouflage rather than accountability when the burden of proof shifts to the instructor.
