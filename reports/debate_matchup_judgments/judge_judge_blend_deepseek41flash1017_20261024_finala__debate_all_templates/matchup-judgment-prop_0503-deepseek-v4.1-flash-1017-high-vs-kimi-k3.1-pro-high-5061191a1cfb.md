### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully framing the resolution around the trajectory of transition rather than its final completion.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's argument that Side B's standard makes any steadily diversifying economy a failure until its final stage, demanding arrival before recognizing the journey.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO won a competitive debate by locking the motion to a transition standard—real non-oil assets, commercial durability, and declining dependence over time—rather than full fiscal replacement. CON’s strongest weapon, activity-versus-substitution and the Dubai-exception/enclave critique, damaged pure optimism but never restored the harder claim that this is mostly PR once concrete operating hubs and costly reforms were conceded. PRO’s “oil money buying assets proves too much” and “don’t judge the bridge by whether it has already reached the far bank” answers neutralized CON’s purity test more cleanly than CON neutralized PRO’s commercial-durability route. Format clipping of PRO’s first rebuttal was a real cost, but not enough to flip the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s pressure answers and Rebuttal 2: distinguishing commercial returns from permanent life support, rejecting full national substitution as the motion’s bar, and turning CON’s enclave concession into partial substantive success.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing the debate around the nature of an economic transition. Side A convincingly argued that using oil wealth to build non-oil assets is the very mechanism of diversification for a petrostate, dismantling Side B's core objection that oil-funded assets inherently fail to count. Side A also effectively exposed Side B's standard as demanding a completed transition rather than a credible trajectory, culminating in a highly effective closing analogy about a bridge. Side B made strong points about the rentier bargain and the distinction between activity and substitution, but ultimately conceded the assets and reforms were real. This undermined the "public-relations" prong of the motion, and Side B failed to prove the efforts were unlikely to succeed over time, instead relying on a purity test that Side A systematically dismantled.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they clearly defined the falsifiability of their position (a sustained fall in hydrocarbon revenue share over time) and distinguished commercial durability from state life support, effectively neutralizing Side B's pressure questions and shifting the burden back to CON.
