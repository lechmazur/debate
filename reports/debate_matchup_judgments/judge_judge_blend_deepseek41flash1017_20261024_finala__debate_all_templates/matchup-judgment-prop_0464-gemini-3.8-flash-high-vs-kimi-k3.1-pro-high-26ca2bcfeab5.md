A's two clipped speeches are a real execution cost and slightly blunt the force of CON's case, which is why the margin is moderate rather than large, but the substantive architecture of the debate still favors CON.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent narratives, but B more directly engaged the motion's "even if withdrawal" conditional and built a cleaner comparative frame: the veto doesn't actually prevent invasions or great-power conflict, it only blocks collective non-military responses (sanctions, investigations, humanitarian mandates), so its "insurance" value is illusory while its cost (guaranteed paralysis) is certain. CON's League of Nations/nuclear-brinkmanship chain (abolition → withdrawal → bloc war) was vivid but never fully bridged the gap between losing a legal veto and losing actual military restraint, and A's attempt to spin B's necessary concession (no system compels a nuclear power to stop fighting) into a decisive "gotcha" was preempted by B's closing, which noted the same limitation applies to the status quo veto. B's pressure questions exposed a real false-dichotomy in A's "impotent or partisan" framing, and B's answers (A1/A2) directly dismantled it by distinguishing coercive sanctions from military coercion. A's answers restated assertions about existential interests without fully resolving why non-military measures necessarily trigger catastrophic withdrawal. B's clipped opening is a minor execution cost, but its substance and later speeches still carried the stronger comparative logic.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A2 in B4.3-4.4), distinguishing coercion via sanctions/jurisdiction from military invasion, directly dismantled A's "impotent or partisan" dilemma and reframed the central clash in B's favor for the rest of the debate.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built the more resilient comparative. CON's core live route was universality as precondition for any authority and League-collapse if P5 withdraw. PRO neutralized it by distinguishing non-military Council actions (condemnation, investigations, targeted sanctions) from war against a nuclear power, and by showing the veto does not prevent invasions but guarantees blocked response - illustrated by Russia/Ukraine. PRO answered CON's impotence/partisan dilemma directly in B4 and A3 answers, while CON never showed how veto actively averts WWIII. Even conceding damage from withdrawal, PRO's weighing - possible collective action among willing states vs guaranteed obstruction for privileges - survived.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 answers to A3 Q1/Q2: admitting current Council also cannot enforce peace against a nuclear power, separating sanctions via domestic banks/ports from invasion, and exposing CON's League sequence as assumed not proven.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B built a more compelling and logically cohesive case by systematically dismantling Side A's core premise that the veto prevents great-power war. B effectively argued that the veto only prevents the UN from responding to P5 aggression, not the aggression itself, rendering the "emergency brake" analogy hollow. B also handled the withdrawal impact better, arguing that an institution purchasing universality through total impunity is worthless ("paralysis with prestige"), while A struggled to defend the unique value of a permanently paralyzed universal table over a functional, albeit narrower, coalition. Side B's rhetoric was sharper and their comparative weighing was more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of the "emergency brake" analogy in Rebuttal 1 and 2, specifically pointing out that the veto only stops the UN from acting, not the aggressor, and that keeping rivals at a table where one can unilaterally nullify scrutiny of their own conduct is not meaningful diplomacy.
