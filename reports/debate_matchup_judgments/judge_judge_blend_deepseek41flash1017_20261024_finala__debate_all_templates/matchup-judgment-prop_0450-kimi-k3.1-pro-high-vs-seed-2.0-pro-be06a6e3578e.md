### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): A's strongest move was turning B's own restart-vs-new-build distinction against it: citing the real-world trajectory of Japan's post-Fukushima restarts (most licensed reactors still not operating over a decade later) directly undercut B's claim that restarts are fast and low-risk. B's reply—"govern restarts better with clear timelines and inspections"—never specified what concretely changes the track record, and A pressed this gap unanswered into the closing ("none ever showed a single change"). B's conceptual framework (insurance, selectivity, zero-sum skepticism) was sound and its pressure questions about fossil-fuel backfill during delay were sharp, but A's answer (temporary gas plus efficiency/demand flexibility versus permanently sunk nuclear capital) was an adequate rebuttal. B's second rebuttal was also cut off before completing its final rebuttal thread, costing it some closing force. A relied on several oddly precise, unverifiable statistics (70% failure rate, 19 trillion won, 13% peak cut) that hurt its grounding score, but its core empirical/historical argument about restart failure rates was the more decisive, less-rebutted thread by the end of the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's turn 4 rebuttal (A2.1-A2.2), using Japan's actual 54-to-10 reactor restart trajectory to refute B's claim that restarting existing plants is fast and distinct from new-build timelines, was the most damaging exchange, and B never fully neutralized it beyond calling for unspecified "better governance."
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) keeps the decisive path: restarts are existing assets, so selective safe restarts displace imported fossil now, while expansion provides firm low-carbon later. CON (A) proves implementation friction – low restart rates, sunk-cost risk – but concedes the gap is filled by more gas + trust that efficiency/renewables arrive, directly worsening both security and emissions, and its most specific budget claim (19tn won cut last quarter) is brittle precision. PRO's rebuttal distinguishes restart vs new-build timelines, exposes CON's asymmetric delay test, and turns CON's case into an argument for discipline, not prohibition. B's clipped R2 is an execution cost but does not erase that weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2 distinguishing fast restarts of built, grid-connected reactors from 12-year new-build timelines and challenging the automatic zero-sum budget assumption, plus calling out CON's asymmetric treatment of nuclear vs renewable delays.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A built a highly compelling, empirically grounded case focusing on the actual post-Fukushima track record of nuclear restarts in Japan, effectively demonstrating that the "fast restart" premise fails in reality due to political and legal hurdles. Side A's weighing of sunk-cost risks in nuclear versus temporary fossil use while scaling renewables was decisive. Side B struggled to provide a concrete mechanism to overcome the documented failure rate of approved restarts, making their "insurance" framing collapse under Side A's sharp rhetorical and factual pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which sharply distinguished the financial risk of delayed renewables (where you can stop spending) versus delayed nuclear (where 90% of the budget is already sunk), effectively dismantling PRO's claim that nuclear delays are comparable to renewable delays.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON (A) built the more disciplined case by consistently separating "restart" from "expand," forcing PRO to justify the harder half of the motion. PRO's bridge from restart-urgency to expansion-necessity rested almost entirely on an unsubstantiated "40% baseload gap" and an unverified claim about future reactor retirements; CON directly neutralized the latter in Rebuttal 2 by noting that eventual retirements create a replacement need, not a presumption that replacement must be nuclear specifically. PRO's closing never engaged that point, instead repeating the "unrefuted fact" framing and inaccurately claiming CON offered no alternatives, when CON had repeatedly detailed a diversified portfolio (efficiency, demand response, storage, grids, rooftop solar). PRO's rhetoric (coal/gas replacement line, mortality comparison, "that is abdication") was vivid and the uranium-stockpile/security reframe was a clever point CON had to work to answer, but PRO's substantive case leaned too heavily on one repeated, uncorroborated statistic and conflated two different timelines (restart vs. new build) without ever closing that gap. CON's clipped Rebuttal 1 is a real execution cost, but CON recovered the key distinctions cleanly in later rounds and ended with the sharper, more precise statement of the actual burden under the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point that "eventual retirements create a need for replacement capacity, not a presumption that replacement must be nuclear" directly undercut PRO's attempt to use restart-urgency to justify expansion, and PRO's closing never answered it.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): PRO won the central clash on speed and security. B exposed that CON argued only against new-build while ignoring restart of idle units that deliver in 12-18 months, and reframed dependency as multi-year uranium stockpiles vs weekly LNG exposure. CON never filled the long-term gap PRO labeled as 40% even under perfect renewables, conceding some restarts may help but failing to carry its burden that expansion is unjustified, with its first rebuttal clipped for length.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 [B2.1]-[B2.3] - the restart-vs-expansion distinction plus the stockpile argument that uranium can be stored for years on site while LNG cannot, turning CON's security claim against itself.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side B effectively defended both halves of the motion (restart and expand) by linking short-term restarts to long-term expansion needs, noting that existing reactors will retire by the 2030s. Side B anchored their case on a 40% baseload gap derived from official planning scenarios, which Side A failed to effectively rebut, relying instead on dismissing it as a mere "assumption." Furthermore, Side B's arguments on energy security—specifically the ability to stockpile years of uranium on-site versus relying on continuous LNG shipping—were highly persuasive and tailored to the geographic realities of Japan and South Korea. Side A made solid points about portfolio diversification and concentrated outage risks, but ultimately could not dismantle Side B's core mathematical and temporal claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they directly answered the pressure questions by explaining why restarts logically necessitate expansion (the 2030s retirement cliff) and defended the 40% gap as a product of official models that already assume the perfect, on-schedule delivery of all of Side A's preferred alternatives.
