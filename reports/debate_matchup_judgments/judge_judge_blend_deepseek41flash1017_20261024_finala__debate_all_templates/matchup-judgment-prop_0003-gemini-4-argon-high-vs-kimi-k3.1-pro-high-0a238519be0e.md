### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and honored the format, but the pivotal exchange was A's pressure question forcing B to concede that demolition of a significant building is permissible once necessity for needed housing is demonstrated [A3.1, B4.1]. That concession effectively granted the normative core of the motion, which itself is conditional ("when they block needed housing density"). A's closing exploited this directly, arguing B's remaining "last-resort, independently reviewed" standard is a poison pill that recreates the very blockage the motion targets, while A's own conditioned, district-wide relaxation (binding affordability, one-for-one replacement, tenant right-of-return) offers a workable mechanism to actually deliver housing. B's response—reframing the dispute as "who bears the burden of proof" and distinguishing a proven exception from blanket relaxation—is a defensible distinction but never fully escapes having conceded the substantive tradeoff the motion demands, and B's claim that bounded appeals prevent paralysis was asserted rather than demonstrated. A's vivid framing ("habitat for living people," "weaponized nostalgia," "freeze in amber") also made the stakes more memorable, while B's more cautious, hedged style was intellectually careful but slightly less decisive in resolving the final clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Q1 in Pressure Questions [A3.1], which forced B's direct concession in A1 [B4.1] that demolition is permissible once necessity is proven—later weaponized in A's closing [A5.1-A5.2] to argue B had effectively conceded the motion's logic while only disputing implementation mechanics.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): CON won by keeping the motion’s real decision rule clear: not whether housing can ever outweigh heritage, but whether cities should adopt standing/district-wide relaxation rather than demonstrated last-resort exceptions. PRO was rhetorically strong on scale, fairness, and workability, and forced a useful concession that destruction can be justified in principle. But PRO overclaimed that concession as victory and never showed that by-right weakening is necessary once reformed review, affordability conditions, and non-demolition capacity are on the table. CON answered the pressure honestly, preserved the burden-of-proof distinction through the close, and left the cleaner third-party reason to reject the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s A1/A4.1 answer to B’s trap question: allowing a narrowly tailored, proven-necessity exception is not conceding district-wide relaxation; that burden-of-proof distinction then decided the closing clash.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO won the central burden trade. CON admitted in A4.1 that a proven, mixed-income, net-new project can justify demolishing a significant building, conceding the principle that shelter can outweigh heritage. PRO then made the decisive comparison workability vs paralysis: binding targets, one-for-one replacement and right-of-return answered CON's speculative-gain worry, while CON's call for exhaustive alternatives analysis elsewhere never answered B3.2 about how mixed-income cross-subsidy works in fully built-out districts without scale. The debate narrowed to narrow exception vs predictable district-wide relaxation, and PRO gave the cleaner reason to vote that urgency requires by-right permission, not bespoke vetoes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure Q1 forcing the last-resort concession in A4.1, then crystallized in B5.1-5.2 as proof that case-by-case review is a poison pill - the most damaging exchange.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and followed the format rules well, but B built a tighter, more concrete causal chain. B's opening move—that historic districts are a small fraction of urban land while vast non-historic parcels sit underused—forced A onto the defensive about whether "alternatives genuinely fail" is ever a real-world scenario. B then supplied a concrete economic mechanism (demolishing occupied, expensive historic buildings is slower and costlier than redeveloping vacant lots, so relaxation mainly serves prestige-premium luxury development rather than net housing) that A never fully neutralized; A's "asymmetry" reply asserted non-historic capacity is equally uncertain but didn't explain why a developer would prefer the costlier historic-demolition path absent the premium motive. Decisively, in Rebuttal 2 B exposed a circularity in A's safeguard: A had conceded political refusal to upzone can't justify demolition, yet A's own trigger ("entitled" projects failing to meet demand) is satisfied exactly when cities withhold entitlements — effectively smuggling the disclaimed justification back in. A's closing shifted the trigger word from "entitled" to "feasible" without squarely resolving this tension, and B's closing pressed the point further by walking through the only two ways alternatives could "fail," cleanly mapping back onto its throughline. A's case remained principled and well-organized but more abstract and reliant on an unproven residual scenario, while B's mechanism-level arguments gave the debate a sharper, more resolvable throughline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 exposing the circularity in A's "entitled projects" safeguard (B4.3–B4.4), which A's closing could only partially patch by substituting "feasible" for "entitled" without closing the loophole.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won by forcing PRO into a residual case, then collapsing it: after reuse/infill/ordinary upzoning are credited, alternatives "fail" only via political refusal to entitle non-historic land (which PRO concedes does not justify demolition) or developers skipping entitled lots for prestige demolitions. CON’s stronger live route was efficiency and displacement—occupied historic stock is slower, costlier, and more disruptive than surface lots/strips—plus the developer-premium loophole under parcel-by-parcel proposals. PRO argued skillfully on infinite-weight vs high-threshold and that zoning maps are not homes, but never made the residual housing-need case concrete enough to outweigh irreversible civic loss and admitted tenant harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 (especially A4.3–A4.5): PRO’s “entitled/realistic” trigger is city-controlled, so refusal to upzone ordinary land auto-triggers landmark sacrifice; prestige premium, not corridor need, explains skipping empty lots for occupied historic corners.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO kept a live, conditional route: relax only after credible assessment shows entitled, buildable infill plus ordinary upzoning cannot deliver needed net homes in time, giving heritage a high threshold but not infinite weight. CON's strongest efficiency/premium argument never answered timely-delivery pressure from B3.1, retreating to "physical capacity never runs out" [A4.1] which concedes the motion's residual case, while PRO directly answered A3's displacement/political-manufacture questions and admitted preference for underused lots. That asymmetry made PRO's path easier to vote for.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B2.1/B4.4/B5.2 chain – "unbuilt alternatives are not housing" and "line on a zoning map is not a home" – directly damaged CON's core guarantee that non-historic land suffices, exposing that CON treated cheap lots as automatically available while heavily auditing historic redevelopment.
