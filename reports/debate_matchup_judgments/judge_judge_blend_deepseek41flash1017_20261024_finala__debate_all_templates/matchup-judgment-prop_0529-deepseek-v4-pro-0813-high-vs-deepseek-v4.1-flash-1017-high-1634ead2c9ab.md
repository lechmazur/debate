### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): The debate came down to one question both sides agreed was decisive: does public compute actually unlock scrutiny of a refusing firm's closed frontier model? CON pressed this with a clean distinction—owning compute is not the same as owning or accessing a competitor's weights—and PRO's Rebuttal 2 conceded the point directly ("Nothing in the compute proposal automatically subpoenas private weights"), then tried to recover with a "leverage" and "credibility" argument that never explained the actual causal mechanism by which a public cluster forces a non-cooperating firm to disclose anything. CON exploited this concession cleanly through Rebuttal 2 and the closing, consistently returning to "somewhere else to train, not the power to inspect." PRO's opening case (telescope/CERN analogy, waste reduction, incentive critique of corporate research) was reasonably strong, but PRO let the debate narrow onto the access/audit framing CON set up via pressure questions, and PRO's answers there were the weakest link in the debate. CON's pressure questions (on enforcement mechanism and on "countries" narrowing to "like-minded governments") exposed real tensions that PRO only partially resolved, while CON's own answers to PRO's questions (regulatory leverage via licensing/procurement/liability) were more coherent as an enforcement theory than PRO's compute-as-leverage claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (A1) conceding "the compute proposal does not automatically subpoena private weights," followed by CON's Rebuttal 2/Closing exploitation of that concession as fatal to PRO's core causal mechanism.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B won because it preserved the motion’s broader benefit: independent frontier-scale research capacity, agenda-setting, replication, and technical leverage outside corporate permission. Side A convincingly showed that public compute alone cannot unlock a proprietary model, but that establishes insufficiency for one audit scenario, not that coordinated public capacity is undesirable. B also made the stronger comparison between transparent, plural public allocation and opaque firm-controlled access, while A overstated both the necessary centralization of coordination and the contestability of corporate access.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange concerned private weights. A sharply established that compute does not compel disclosure, but B answered that the proposal creates independent frontier research and the technical capacity needed to execute separate audit mandates. That preserved B’s broader route to the motion despite A winning the narrower point.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A decisively won by identifying a fatal analytical flaw in Side B's causal mechanism: building public compute allows academics to train open models, but does not grant access to the closed, proprietary models that actually require auditing. Side A successfully argued that regulatory and legal authority, not parallel hardware, is the necessary leverage to compel access, rendering Side B's expensive public compute proposal orthogonal to the core governance problem. Side B's concession that public compute does not automatically subpoena weights effectively collapsed their case under Side A's rigorous cross-examination and closing synthesis.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2, specifically the argument that "compute does not unlock private models" and that handing academics GPUs without the firm's weights only lets them train open models, leaving the corporate gate intact. This cleanly neutralized Side B's core premise.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built a tighter comparative case: public funding plus enforceable contracts and competitive private provision, preserving exit and current hardware while avoiding the governance/speed/security costs of a standalone public compute system. PRO (B) opened strong with the telescope/public-good analogy and leverage argument, but under pressure its "coordination" kept sliding between (a) mere negotiated public funding, which A pointed out collapses into CON's own model, and (b) a standalone public option, which remained vulnerable to A's speed, governance, and security critiques. B's own concession at B4.3 that lagging public capacity "could not reproduce a current frontier training run" significantly undercut the stated purpose of checking current frontier claims, and B never cleanly resolved this equivocation. A's pressure questions and answers (A1 on enforcement via contract/courts, A2's "yes" on funding frontier-scale runs through reserved capacity) directly neutralized B's key challenge, while B's closing reasserted "exit among gatekeepers isn't real exit" without rebutting A's sharper point that a fired vendor can be replaced while a political allocator cannot. A's closing line distilling the comparison ("fire the provider, keep the grant") crystallized a clear, undefeated reason to prefer CON's framework.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4), especially A4.3-A4.6, where CON directly answered PRO's pressure questions on enforcement and frontier-scale funding, then used PRO's own concession (that a consortium could buy current-generation capacity in tranches) to show PRO's distinct "coordination" claim collapses into CON's model, leaving only a weaker political-allocator add-on exposed to CON's original governance and speed critiques.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): B won a competitive debate by showing that coordination can include competitive contracted clouds, distributed public nodes, transparent allocation, and appeals rather than the single bureaucratic megacluster A attacked. A offered strong contractual remedies and provider exit, but its model still left academics dependent on the same concentrated industry and did not establish why adding a public fallback would eliminate commercial choice. B therefore supplied the more credible route to independent capacity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest response was that every access system allocates scarce compute, so the relevant comparison is transparent, contestable public allocation with multiple nodes versus unilateral private rationing. This undercut A’s central claim that only the public model contains a gatekeeper.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively trapping Side B on the hardware refresh cycle. A forced B to concede that a public cluster would either lag behind the frontier (failing the motion's core purpose of checking current safety claims) or require purchasing current-generation capacity from the very private firms the motion targets. A brilliantly argued in Rebuttal 2 that B's "coordinated procurement" was functionally identical to A's "public funding with private provision," but with an added political allocation board. A's closing crystallization of "dispatchable exit" (firing a vendor) versus "abstract answerability" (petitioning a committee) sealed a clear and compelling victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it seized on Side B's admission that pooled procurement buys current-gen capacity, pointing out that this collapses B's model into A's model (public money, private provision) while retaining the political gatekeeper, effectively neutralizing B's core distinction.
