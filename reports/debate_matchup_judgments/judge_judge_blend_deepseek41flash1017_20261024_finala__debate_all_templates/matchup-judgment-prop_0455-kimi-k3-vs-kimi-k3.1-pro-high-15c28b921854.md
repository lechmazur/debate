### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued at a high level, but A (CON) built a more granular causal case: associated-gas shut-ins destroy real output, marginal import substitution likely comes from dirtier foreign suppliers, and strict cliff-edge thresholds create a visible-flare/invisible-venting asymmetry that a fee avoids. A's "deferred geology is not supply" and "guarantees illegality, gambles on invisibility" lines crystallized a genuine instrument-choice comparison (price vs. command-control) that B never fully dislodged. B's strongest counter was the fee "fork" (stringent fee = same shut-ins, mild fee = continued pollution), but A answered it with a plausible efficiency argument about fees sorting cheap vs. costly abatement sites, which B did not substantively rebut. B correctly noted the motion textually accepts short-term supply costs, and handled pressure questions with clean A1/A2 formatting, but its responses to the carbon-leakage and monitoring-asymmetry points leaned on general assurances ("enforcement doesn't require omniscience," "phased implementation") rather than mechanisms as concrete as A's. Both sides suffered a clipped speech, roughly canceling that execution cost. On balance, A gave the clearer, more mechanism-driven path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 response to B's fee "fork" (B4.1-B4.2): A reframed quantity vs. price instruments, arguing a fee sorts sites by abatement cost to achieve similar reduction with less shut-in supply and less dirtier-import backfill—an economic point B's closing did not directly answer.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by establishing a clear mechanical advantage for their alternative (fees) over the motion's ban, successfully arguing that a strict threshold incentivizes invisible venting and forces dirtier global imports. Side A cleanly defeated Side B's best counter-attacks by explaining how a fee sorts by abatement cost to prevent supply cliffs, and distinguishing between measuring visible flares and detecting diffuse venting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (A4.4) brilliantly dismantles Side B's attempt to fork the fee, explaining how a fee sorts sites by abatement cost to avoid the shut-ins and dirtier imports caused by a uniform ban.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B won the central trade-off: PRO accepted short-term supply loss but never secured the climate gain. B's two mechanisms — associated-gas shut-ins backfilled at the global margin by high-flaring producers, and the flare-visible/vent-invisible asymmetry that makes a ban pay to hide emissions — directly turned PRO's purpose. PRO's strong license objection and "measurable to price = measurable to regulate" trap were met by B's sorting argument (fee lets cheap sites capture, dear sites pay and keep supplying) and billing-grade vs enforcement-grade distinction. PRO's "manageable temporary pressure" did not answer that temporary domestic loss can mean durable global emissions increase.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.4-B2.5 and B4.2-B4.4: flares are satellite-visible and billable under a fee, venting is diffuse and invisible, so a cliff-edge ban creates incentive to go dark at 3 a.m., while a fee degrades gracefully and sorts by abatement cost — blunting A's "ban also bans venting" and "permanent license" attacks.
