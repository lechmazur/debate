### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): PRO (B) built the more coherent path to victory. Its opening grounded the harm concretely and preempted the "narrowing speech" objection by analogizing to existing narrow categories (defamation, true threats, NCII). Its Pressure Questions round created a genuine fork: either CON's preferred remedy is itself a private right of action (conceding the motion) or it is some weaker tool that cannot reach identifiable abusers. CON's answer in Rebuttal 2 ("It can be a private civil action—and that concedes nothing") walked straight into this trap, and PRO's closing exploited it decisively, noting that CON's own emphasis on litigation costs and chilling effects applies with equal force to its own proposed remedy. CON (A) raised legitimate concerns about practical enforceability, pre-judgment chilling of journalism, and the open-endedness of "even if," and its dilemma framing in the opening was sharp, but the opening was clipped (an explicit execution cost), and CON never clearly articulated an alternative remedy that both reaches abusers and avoids the very costs it criticizes. PRO's closing crystallized the clash more cleanly, while CON's closing mostly reasserted its distinction between "process cost" and "substantive narrowing" without fully escaping the self-undermining concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question Q1 ("if yes, you've conceded the motion; if no, what reaches the abuser?") and A's answer "It can be a private civil action—and that concedes nothing," which B's closing then used to show CON's own remedy carries the same litigation-chill costs CON spent the debate condemning.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side A won by pairing severe, concrete victim harms with a narrow civil remedy and showing that Side B's own private-action alternative creates the same litigation, disclosure, and chilling pressures it criticized. Side B's authentic-footage example was credible, but it did not establish that this residual risk outweighed the remedy's benefits, especially once ordinary safeguards were allowed. Treating the motion as a blank cheque overstated what “even if” requires.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange was the fork forcing Side B to admit its remedy could be a private civil action, then arguing that such an action necessarily carries the same disputed-authenticity and process costs. Side B drew a useful distinction between litigation costs and substantive speech protection, but never fully explained how its alternative avoids the practical chill central to its own case.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side A wins by trapping Side B in a contradiction regarding the speech costs of civil litigation. Side B argues that A's proposal chills speech primarily through process costs (discovery, meritless suits, defense costs). However, when pressed by Side A's Q1, Side B concedes they would support a "carefully bounded" private civil action. Side A brilliantly points out that any civil action inherently carries those exact same process costs, meaning Side B's alternative also accepts the marginal speech narrowing that the motion proposes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Turn 9 closing, which crystallizes the trap set in Q1: by admitting that a private civil action is the right remedy, Side B concedes the very mechanisms of chill (defense costs, discovery) they used to attack the motion.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON built a tight structural case ("wrong defendants, wrong victims, wrong way to narrow speech") and sustained it across every round. The decisive exchange was CON's exploitation of PRO's own concession that the right reaches "identifiable ex-partners, local creators, knowing distributors" — CON showed these are precisely the solvent, identifiable defendants already covered by IIED, appropriation, and existing intimate-image/criminal law, leaving the statute redundant where it works and powerless against the anonymous/foreign perpetrators who typify the harm. PRO's pressure-question answer (the "solvent local producer" example) walked directly into this trap, and PRO's closing never recovered by substantively defending why appropriation/IIED fail to cover that example (e.g., the commercial-use limits of appropriation tort), leaving CON's "circularity" framing unanswered. CON also cleverly flipped PRO's own "jurisdictional patchwork" question back on them, noting civil judgments are themselves jurisdiction-bound while criminal law has extradition/MLAT reach. Both sides handled the Q&A format correctly, but B's opening and first rebuttal were clipped for exceeding word limits, a real execution cost, while A stayed disciplined throughout. PRO's harm narrative (persistence despite disclosed falsity, copyright/defamation gaps) was strong and under-rebutted in isolation, but CON's redundancy trap undercut the practical case for a *new* civil remedy, which is the actual burden the motion requires PRO to meet.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1/A4 in Rebuttal 1 and 2, where CON turns PRO's own concession (which defendants the right "can reach") into proof the remedy is redundant against reachable defendants and absent against the real-world modal perpetrator — a trap PRO's closing never escaped.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side A won a competitive debate by showing that existing doctrines are uneven fits and that an explicit right offers predictable standing, injunctions, compensation, and victim control even without reaching every perpetrator. Side B powerfully identified litigation costs, publicity risks, and demand-letter chilling, but its central redundancy claim depended on treating variable doctrines such as IIED and appropriation as reliably comprehensive. Its alternatives also carried many of the same enforcement and chilling problems. A's clipped turns were an execution cost, but its comparative case survived.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest response was that B's chilling and accessibility objections prove too much: privacy claims, intimate-image laws, and injunctions also generate defense costs and imperfect access, while none supplies a reliable claim where existing doctrine excludes synthetic or openly labeled material. B's courtroom-versus-mailbox framing was sharp, but did not establish that a narrowly defined explicit right creates uniquely intolerable costs.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling the marginal utility of the proposed right. B successfully proved that the right is redundant against solvent defendants (who can be sued via IIED or appropriation) and impotent against insolvent/foreign ones, leaving only the cost of a chilled speech environment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 provided exceptional answers to Side A's Pressure Questions, successfully arguing that IIED and appropriation cover Side A's "local producer" example, and brilliantly distinguishing the bright-line nature of authentic intimate-image laws from the contestable, ambiguity-monetizing nature of deepfake laws.
