### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A won on overbreadth. It conceded that individualized unionization prediction could be prohibited while showing that quitting and productivity forecasts can enable timely raises, flexibility, or coaching. Its disclosure, purpose-limit, logging, and burden-shifting alternative directly answered misuse without erasing auditable evidence. Side B compellingly described scaling, feedback loops, and contestability harms, but never established that these benefits were truly replaceable or that banning documented models would outperform regulating their uses while informal predictions remain.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange answered Side B’s proof problem by placing the burden on employers when logged scores enter prohibited decisions, then contrasted that auditable record with invisible managerial hunches. This materially weakened Side B’s claim that prohibition necessarily creates greater accountability.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal and closing effectively neutralized B's regulatory defense by pointing out that an access log only proves a file was opened, not how an assigned future shaped a manager's unreviewable discretion.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully framing the core clash around the unchallengeable nature of predictive scores and the asymmetry of workplace power. While Side B made a strong case that banning AI leaves unaccountable human "hunches" and prevents proactive retention, Side A decisively rebutted this by arguing that employer-held documentation does not equal worker accountability, and that "help does not require prophecy." Side A also won the pressure exchange by exposing Side B's lack of a lawful purpose for individual unionization predictions, forcing Side B to effectively concede that part of the motion. Side A's epistemic critique of self-fulfilling predictions and its realistic view of workplace enforcement made its case more convincing than Side B's reliance on idealized access logs and regulatory presumptions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's "access log" and "accountability" claims, specifically the point that documentation held by the employer does not equal accountability for the worker, and that an algorithmically assigned future cannot be falsified by the employee. This neutralized Side B's core claim that AI makes management more accountable than human hunches.
