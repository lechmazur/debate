### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): A built a coherent throughline: click-through consent cannot specify unforeseeable future inferences/combinations, so purpose-based prohibition is the cleaner safeguard. A's sharpest move was turning B's central "no technical line between beneficial and exploitative use" claim back against B — if the same signal can serve either purpose, that is precisely why disclosure-based consent can't be trusted to sort them, which favors a bright-line purpose ban over B's opt-in model. B never squarely answered this reversal in Rebuttal 2 or Closing, instead re-asserting the same accessibility examples and "double standard with other behavioral data" point without engaging A's purpose-vs-signal distinction. B's strongest unaddressed point was the plausible textual tension that the motion bans "personalization" outright, which could sweep in B's accessibility examples — A's response narrowing this to "commercial" personalization was not fully grounded in the motion's text, leaving a real soft spot. But on balance A controlled more of the decisive exchanges, answered pressure questions directly in proper A1/A2 format, and ended with a tighter closing frame ("a protection need not abolish every abuse to prevent another from becoming entrenched") that B's closing did not rebut, only restated. A's case suffered real execution cost from being clipped twice (opening and Rebuttal 2), losing some concluding force, which tempers but does not erase its advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1/Pressure Q1 turning B's "no technical line" argument into a reason consent screens cannot meaningfully specify future commercial uses — B never recovered from this reframe and kept re-asserting the same examples rather than directly refuting the purpose-based regulation logic.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): A won narrowly by establishing that activating a neural sensor does not meaningfully authorize opaque future advertising and pricing inferences, then weighing that structural consent failure against the acknowledged loss of some beneficial personalization. B exposed genuine overbreadth and accessibility costs, but repeatedly overstated the ban as eliminating whole tools and treated surviving non-neural surveillance as if it erased the ban's incremental protection. A's clipped turns were an execution cost, but its responses were more comparative and complete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange answered B's double-standard argument: other behavioral surveillance remaining lawful neither validates neural exploitation nor makes an incremental prohibition pointless, while active sensor opt-in still fails to specify downstream commercial uses. B's beneficial-personalization objection remained significant but did not neutralize that distinction.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): PRO maintained the live route that click-through consent cannot specify future inferences, combinations, and pricing experiments for brain data, making it leverage rather than choice. CON's strongest pressure - no technical line and "other data also abused" - was turned: PRO showed a purpose-based legal line can distinguish commercial steering from device function, conceded and weighed limited loss of neural personalization versus hidden manipulation, and answered affordability (discount = costlier refusal) and opt-in (collection ≠ downstream commercial purpose). CON repeated collateral-damage and whataboutism without neutralizing the consent-failure core.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's R1/R2 move that "no technical line" favors a purpose-based prohibition, not disclosure - enabling a ban on advertising/pricing uses while preserving manual and non-neural adaptation, directly undercutting CON's central tradeoff claim.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): B won the substantive exchange. A's opening landed vivid, emotionally resonant examples (preconscious craving detection, crisis-hotel pricing), but its core structural moves were vulnerable: it tried to narrow "personalization" to "commercial extraction," a phrase absent from the motion, which B correctly flagged as an unargued amendment rather than an interpretation of the text as written. A's consent argument (you cannot consent to an unperceived internal state) was directly undercut by B's analogy to widely-accepted consent for sleep-stage and heart-rate-variability inference, which A never distinguished. Most decisively, A's late enforcement argument ("brain data makes evasion undetectable, so ban everything") boomeranged: B showed in Rebuttal 2 that a ban creates no more of an audit trail than targeted rules, since companies willing to hide prohibited pricing could equally hide prohibited advertising use. A's closing ignored this symmetry point entirely, relying instead on unsupported assertion ("that is not what companies build"). B also answered both pressure questions concretely (a locally-processed ad-supported example; consent to purpose rather than omniscience of every signal), while A's answers to B's questions relied on textual claims not actually in the motion. B's position — targeted prohibition of exploitative inference-based pricing/manipulative targeting plus strong safeguards, rather than a blanket ban — survived the exchange as the cleaner, more defensible frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 turn on enforcement ("if a company can disguise stress-based pricing as demand, it can disguise a prohibited advertising pipeline—a ban creates no special audit trail") was the most damaging unanswered blow, since it neutralized A's strongest justification for an absolute ban over targeted regulation, and A's closing never addressed it.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing the motion’s overbreadth and showing that Side A’s limiting phrase, “personalization for commercial extraction,” was absent from the proposition. B also answered the consent objection with purpose-specific, revocable authorization and showed that A’s enforcement argument undermined the claimed unique effectiveness of a ban. A vividly established serious risks, but mostly asserted that every listed use is manipulative and that safeguards will always fail.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it supplied a concrete beneficial advertising use, directly answered why consent need not require perceiving every datum, and showed that covert evasion would undermine a categorical ban just as readily as targeted regulation.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): CON won the definitional and consent clashes. PRO claimed focus/sleep adaptation is not "personalization" but the motion contains no such exception, and PRO added "commercial extraction" language absent from the text. CON's sleep-stage analogy undercut the claim that unperceived states can never be consented to, and its free, locally-timed ad example directly answered PRO's demand for a beneficial advertising use. PRO's enforcement argument that brain data evasion is undetectable was turned around - a ban has the same audit problem, while targeted controls on segregation, logs, and prohibited inferences preserve user-valued services. PRO never showed all three uses are necessarily exploitative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 + Rebuttal 2 combo on overbreadth: personalization in ordinary sense includes adaptive features, PRO's reassurance is an amendment not an interpretation, plus A1 example of less-disruptive ad timing and A2 sleep-stage consent purpose/risk framing that neutralized PRO's impossibility claim and enforceability claim.
