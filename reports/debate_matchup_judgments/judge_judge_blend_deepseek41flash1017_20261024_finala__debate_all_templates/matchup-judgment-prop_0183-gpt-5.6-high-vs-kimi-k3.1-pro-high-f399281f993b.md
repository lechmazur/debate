### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built the cleaner, more durable distinction between preservation (which it conceded freely, without owner-hunts) and public access/distribution (which it argued should be risk-calibrated rather than immunized). This framing let CON absorb PRO's best preservation arguments while still contesting the motion's actual claim—exemption from "traditional copyright enforcement," which PRO's own language extended to "cultural access." PRO's defense progressively narrowed into a tribunal-based, good-faith, market-substitution-sensitive system that, as CON repeatedly pointed out, requires the same kind of individualized judgment PRO called impossible under CON's "risk-based" model—undercutting PRO's claim that blanket institutional immunity is necessary. CON's rebuttal that irreversibility cuts both ways (premature release can spawn uncontainable downstream copies, just as deletion is unrecoverable) was a genuinely sharp symmetry argument that PRO never fully neutralized, mostly reasserting that suspension is less harmful without directly engaging the downstream-copying point. PRO's case stayed substantively strong on transaction-cost and scale arguments, and its closing framed error costs memorably, but the format clip cut off its final crystallizing sentence at the most consequential moment, compounding the sense that CON ended with the tighter, more resolved position.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's [A2.1]/[A4.5] point that PRO's "mission test" tribunal (assessing commercial availability, substitution, good faith) requires the same kind of case-by-case judgment PRO calls an impossible bottleneck under CON's model, exposing that PRO's exemption collapses into a regulated access regime rather than true immunity.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON won by keeping the decisive comparison on the motion as written: whether institutional exemption from traditional enforcement is necessary for preservation. PRO’s irreversibility and archival-scale cost arguments were strong, but CON repeatedly separated pure preservation (copy/migrate/emulate) from public distribution, showed dark-archive and categorical risk rules can protect records without market-substitution immunity, and forced PRO to keep adding mission tests, tribunals, and rapid access restrictions that look like calibrated archival law rather than the claimed exemption. PRO’s best systemic point—who bears uncertainty—stayed live, yet CON’s opt-out/monitoring and irreversible-release counters made the narrower means cleaner and more decisive by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2 (A4.5–A4.8): PRO’s tribunal/good-faith answers recreate the same market/use judgments while flipping copyright to creator opt-out after distribution begins.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON won by narrowing the debate to preservation vs public distribution. Both sides agreed archives should be allowed to copy, migrate, and back up without per-work clearance. PRO needed to justify why immunity must also cover public cultural access, but its error-cost asymmetry was neutralized by CON's counter that premature release is also irreversible downstream. CON's calibrated alternative - categorical preservation permission plus risk-based access presumptions with tailored remedies not existential damages - answered PRO's bottleneck while avoiding opt-out monitoring costs on authors. That left PRO without a live reason why full institutional exemption from traditional enforcement is required to preserve heritage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 - showing PRO's tribunal test reproduces the same work/market/use judgments it calls impossible and shifts uncertainty onto creators to police opt-out distribution, plus downstream copies cannot be recalled by mere suspension.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built the more decisive structural attack: that PRO's safeguarded "function-based exemption" collapses into the same conduct-specific safe harbor CON proposed all along, making the exemption either redundant or (if broader) dangerous to creators' markets. This dichotomy was raised in the opening, sharpened through rebuttals, tested in pressure questions, and never cleanly escaped by PRO. PRO's strongest move—shifting the default from "permission needed" to "protected unless substitutive"—is a real distinction, but CON directly neutralized the practical stakes of that distinction by specifying that a statute could pre-authorize immediate capture/storage/migration with no owner search required, leaving only broader access gated, which answered PRO's own pressure question about delay risk. PRO's closing reasserted the "default matters" point but didn't concretely show what work the label does once CON's model already includes automatic preservation plus a substitution-based enforcement boundary. CON's closing crystallized this cleanly ("either nothing or danger"), giving the easier decision. PRO's clipped opening is also a real execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 opening ("PRO's rebuttal concedes the central issue...that is exactly CON's model") combined with A's Rebuttal 2 answer to B's own pressure question (statute auto-authorizes capture/storage/migration, burden only on broader access) together neutralized PRO's core distinctiveness claim and were never fully recovered from.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A won by converting PRO's preservation urgency into targeted safe harbors while forcing the proposition's "exemption from traditional copyright enforcement" into a lose-lose: either it collapses into CON's enforceable exceptions, or it adds only weakened remedies against market-substituting access. A answered pressure directly, retained immediate capture/migration without owner clearance, and better weighed irreversible access harms against reversible legal restraint. B kept a strong irreversibility frame but could not show practical heritage gain beyond a relabel once courts, boundaries, and substitution tests returned.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2 collapse move: PRO's function-based exemption is either identical to a conduct-specific safe harbor (so the motion fails) or only freer access risk with no extra preservation benefit.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON wins the default debate. Both sides end up permitting immediate capture, storage, and migration without permission and punishing substitutive distribution. A shows that heritage can be saved inside copyright via an automatic preservation safe harbor where the copy stays preserved while access is restrained, answering B's Q1 delay/irreversibility claim. That leaves B's exemption either identical to CON's exception - failing the proposition - or broader and riskier for specialist licensing markets. B never rebuts that nonprofit status doesn't prevent substitution and that 'non-substitutive' begs the question.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R1-R2 collapse that PRO's 'function-based exemption with judicial boundaries' is just CON's targeted exception renamed, and its A1 answer that endangered files can be automatically captured without clearance while access waits, neutralizing PRO's core irreversible-loss asymmetry.
