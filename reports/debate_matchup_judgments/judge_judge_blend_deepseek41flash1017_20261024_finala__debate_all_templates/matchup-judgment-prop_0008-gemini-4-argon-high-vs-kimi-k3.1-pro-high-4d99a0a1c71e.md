### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but CON (A) ultimately won the comparative framing. PRO's peer-supply mechanism and pragmatic grandfather-clause argument were compelling in the opening, but CON consistently pressed the crucial dilemma: if the prevention tools PRO relies on to make demand "vanish" actually work that well, they work without lifetime prohibition; if they don't, generational bans leave the same leakage/resale problems PRO claims to avoid. CON's closing crystallized this tension cleanly and exposed a real disanalogy in PRO's lead-paint/asbestos comparison (those are forward-looking product bans, not permanent birth-year exclusion from an otherwise-legal product). PRO's rebuttal that CON's proxy-enforcement defense "contradicts" its resale skepticism was rhetorically sharp but slightly overstated, since CON never claimed perfect enforcement, only that both systems face leakage. CON's equality argument also survived reasonably well against PRO's "no right to buy cigarettes" rejoinder, since CON reframed the issue as comparable treatment absent adequate justification rather than a literal right. CON's opening was clipped by the word-limit format note, which is a real execution cost, but CON recovered the lost ground in later rebuttals without needing new evidence, limiting the damage. Overall CON produced the cleaner decision rule and better final weighing, giving it a narrow but clear edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's closing dilemma ("if prevention works, the ban is unnecessary; if it doesn't, the ban still leaks") most decisively reframed the debate's central clash and went unanswered in the transcript, while PRO's "contradiction" charge in rebuttal 2/closing was the sharpest counter-punch but slightly overstated CON's actual claims.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by identifying the ban’s distinctive mechanism: the legal purchasing cohort steadily ages out of youth social environments, reducing both late initiation and convenient proxy supply. B replied that supplying minors remains illegal, but did not overcome A’s practical distinction between formally prohibited sharing among close peers and the much higher friction of sourcing from increasingly older cohorts.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side A built a more concrete and persuasive causal mechanism regarding the "social pipeline" and "rite of passage," effectively arguing that conventional age limits inherently replenish the peer supply chain. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built a sharper and more sustained case. Its core contradiction—that PRO simultaneously claims generational bans avoid black markets because "legal access remains for existing adult users" while admitting age restrictions already leak through proxy purchases—was never fully resolved by PRO. PRO's "stock vs. flow" distinction asserted that leakage wouldn't sustain demand but never explained the mechanism, especially given CON's compounding-leakage point: unlike youth-only leakage that ends at adulthood, generational-ban leakage grows every year as the excluded adult population expands. CON also caught a meaningful shift in PRO's justification: PRO's opening grounded the policy in preventing engineered youth addiction (unaddicted adults shouldn't be covered by that logic), but PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer broadened the justification to "ending legal recruitment" generally, which CON correctly flagged as an inconsistency undermining the original framing. CON's equal-citizenship/autonomy framing and its "carding fifty-year-olds" enforcement critique were pressed consistently and never fully neutralized; PRO conceded the point was merely an "administrative inconvenience" without rebutting the scale problem. PRO's rhetoric about "perpetual recruitment" was compelling and the gradualism framing was a reasonable policy design, but it didn't survive the proxy-market and shifting-justification pressure. PRO also suffered an execution cost: its Opening and Rebuttal 1 were both clipped for exceeding word limits, meaning potentially important content was never seen by the judge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2 rebuttal connecting PRO's own admission that age-restriction leakage occurs with PRO's claim that generational bans avoid black markets because "legal access remains" was the pivotal exchange; PRO never cleanly resolved this tension, and CON pressed it through to the closing with the compounding-leakage point.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side A won a competitive debate by framing the comparison as manageable transition costs versus the permanent renewal of legal tobacco recruitment. Side B compellingly identified unequal adult treatment, prolonged ID checks, and diversion risks, but did not establish that illicit demand would replace the mass legal market rather than merely create leakage. A also answered the pressure questions directly and showed that imperfect enforcement is not uniquely fatal to a generational ban. The clipping of two A speeches was an execution cost, but the central case remained clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it conceded some diversion and middle-aged ID checks, then explained why neither must be eliminated for the phase-out to reduce scalable legal supply. Its double-standard response weakened B’s attempt to treat leakage as fatal while defending conventional restrictions that also leak.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully framing the debate around the structural harm of the tobacco industry's "perpetual recruitment" pipeline. While Side B mounted strong practical attacks regarding black markets and the absurdity of carding middle-aged adults, Side A effectively neutralized these by comparing them to the accepted leakage of current age limits and arguing that commercial supply drives mass addiction. Side A's closing weighing mechanism—contrasting a policy whose difficulties shrink over time with one whose central concession never ends—provided the cleaner path to victory. Side B's defense of conventional tools was undermined by Side A's point that such tools can operate alongside a generational ban, leaving Side B to defend the status quo's endless legal pipeline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to the black market and enforcement pressure in Rebuttal 2, where they conceded the administrative burden of carding older adults but reframed it as a manageable transition cost, while effectively turning Side B's tolerance for current age-limit leakage against them.
