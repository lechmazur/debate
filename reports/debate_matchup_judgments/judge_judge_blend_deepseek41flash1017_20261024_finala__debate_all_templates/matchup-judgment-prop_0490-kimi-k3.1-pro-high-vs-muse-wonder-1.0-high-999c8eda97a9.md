### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): A built a tighter, more adaptive case around a single decisive asymmetry: certification freezes mean public code gives well-resourced attackers an offline rehearsal window that defenders cannot match, while the supply-chain gap (source ≠ running binary) means real verification still runs through controlled, credentialed experts regardless of openness. B's strongest material was the normative claim that scrutiny should not depend on institutional permission, and B scored real points in Rebuttal 1 (secrecy doesn't prevent reverse-engineering; obscurity is not security). But B never squarely answered A's "niche, unglamorous, attackers are paid, volunteers aren't" resource-asymmetry point, and in the Pressure Questions exchange B's own answers (manifests, hashes, custody, independent experts reproducing builds) effectively conceded that meaningful verification still runs through controlled expert channels — a concession A explicitly and sharply exploited in Rebuttal 2 ("the one thing experts do not need and attackers do: unrestricted access"). B's closing reasserted the permissionless-scrutiny frame but did not neutralize that concession or explain what unrestricted public access adds beyond attacker benefit once controlled, plural, compellable review is granted. A's closing crystallized this cleanly. B also suffered a minor execution cost from two clipped speeches that cut off concluding points.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.5–A4.6), which used B's own Pressure-Question answers (manifests, hash checks, independent experts reproducing builds) to show that B's model still depends on controlled, permissioned verification — leaving universal publication adding only attacker advantage, not additional assurance.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON won a competitive debate by locking the decisive comparison onto what universal publication uniquely adds. PRO’s democracy-and-inspectability frame was clear and consistent, but CON showed that PRO still needs permissioned experts, manifests, paper contingencies, and audits for real verification while the public dump mainly lowers attacker friction against legally frozen systems. CON protected the existence-vs-exploitability and certification-freeze routes through the exchange; PRO never fully neutralized the silent-adversary asymmetry or the source-vs-running-system gap. PRO’s “verify ballots and rules” weighing stayed philosophically strong, yet operationally softer after R2 answers that leaned on controlled experts rather than public review alone. Minor clip costs and thinner operational closure cost PRO more than CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2/closing turn of B’s A1/A2 answers: verification still runs through named experts and paper, so openness mainly supplies the attack rehearsal CON flagged.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully defending the necessity of *public* review over Side B's proposed *controlled* review. A effectively neutralizes B's strongest practical argument (the certification freeze dilemma) by pointing out that private vendor discovery creates the exact same operational dilemma, meaning openness doesn't uniquely cause the harm. A's closing crystallization—that democratic verification requires conclusions that do not depend on continued permission from the system being inspected—provides a decisive, principled reason to prefer the proposition. Side B argued well on the asymmetry of attacker/defender resources, but ultimately conceded the need for expert review, failing to overcome A's accountability critique of permissioned scrutiny. Side A receives a slight execution penalty for clipped turns, but the argumentative superiority remains clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's neutralization of the "certification freeze" argument in Rebuttal 2, pointing out that a vendor's private discovery of a flaw creates the exact same legal and operational dilemma as a public discovery, thereby collapsing Side B's claim that public disclosure uniquely jeopardizes frozen systems.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): CON built a tighter structural case: the necessity/sufficiency dilemma (paper audits either make universal disclosure unnecessary or, if absent, disclosure alone is insufficient) was never fully dissolved by PRO, who mostly answered by stacking additional requirements (reproducible builds, toolchain controls) that implicitly concede code publication alone cannot do the verifying work. CON's pressure question on licensed components forced PRO into a costly bite-the-bullet answer (B4.3: "yes," exclude otherwise audited, paper-verifiable systems over an undisclosable driver), which CON then correctly exploited in Rebuttal 2 as "disclosure as an end in itself, not election security" — a point PRO's closing never directly answered. CON also repeatedly flagged PRO's unexamined assumption that sophisticated attackers "already have full access," which PRO reasserted without defending against the asymmetry CON raised. PRO's case remained rhetorically strong and consistent ("verification vs. mere trust," "the loser can check"), and its answers to pressure questions were honest and on-format, but it did not neutralize CON's dilemma or the exclusion-cost concession. CON's opening being clipped for length is a real execution flaw and cost it some polish, but the substantive architecture of its case survived intact across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question on licensed/undisclosable components (A3.2), and PRO's direct "yes" answer (B4.3) biting the bullet on excluding an audited, paper-verifiable system — which CON then turned into its sharpest closing indictment that the motion makes disclosure an end in itself rather than a security measure.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): CON won by keeping the proposition’s absolute “all/fully” burden live and showing open source is neither necessary nor sufficient for checkable elections. The paper-audit dilemma, capacity/remediation gaps, and especially the licensed-driver exclusion made disclosure look like an end that can override verified outcomes. PRO had the cleaner legitimacy frame and memorable loser-can-check rhetoric, but kept enlarging the mandate and never neutralized CON’s comparative decision rule. Clipped CON opening was a real cost, yet later rounds recovered and narrowed the clash more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s R2/closing on licensed components: excluding an audited, paper-verifiable system solely for an undisclosable driver showed disclosure overriding verification rather than serving it.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully distinguishing between verifying an election's outcome (achieved via paper audits) and diagnosing its technical mechanism. Side B effectively weaponized the prompt's "all" and "fully" requirements, forcing Side A to concede that jurisdictions must reject otherwise secure, paper-verifiable systems if they contain a single closed-source driver. Side A's rhetoric about the "black box" and the public's right to inspect was strong, but Side B's logical dismantling of the necessity and sufficiency of open-source code, combined with the practical realities of patch cycles and procurement, provided a more convincing and grounded path to victory. Side B's minor execution cost (a clipped opening) did not materially damage their core case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled Side A's A2 answer by showing that rejecting a paper-verifiable system over an unpublished driver prioritizes ideological disclosure over actual election verification.
