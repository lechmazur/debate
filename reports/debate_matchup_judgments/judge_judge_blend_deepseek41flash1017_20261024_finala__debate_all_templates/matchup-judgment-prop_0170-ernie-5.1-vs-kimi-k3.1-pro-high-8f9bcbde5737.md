### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B won by consistently distinguishing desalination’s usefulness from the motion’s demand that it receive primary investment across water-scarce regions. A never established why this ordering should apply to inland or fiscally constrained regions, and its claims that growth inevitably erases conservation and desalination is cheaper than repeated crises were largely asserted. B’s clipped opening was an execution cost, but not enough to outweigh its superior comparison and burden analysis.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal decisively answered A’s pressure: emergency rationing addresses tail risks rather than proving a permanent supply deficit, and the absence of a universal conservation percentage undermines A’s universal priority rather than B’s locally adaptive sequence. B then showed that A’s inland transmission answer compounds the disputed costs instead of resolving them.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON won the burden clash. PRO showed desalination can be useful (Israel, falling RO costs) but never justified why it should be PRIMARY across diverse water-scarce regions. CON kept focus on sequencing and opportunity cost: leakage repair, efficient growth, and staged spending can free water faster/cheaper before locking in energy, brine, transmission, and debt. CON answered pressure (rationing = tail-risk contingency, no single % needed) while PRO sidestepped the inland/fiscal and budget-crowding questions by reasserting coastal examples and adding costly transmission.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 on primacy vs usefulness — [A2.6]/[A4.5] that PRO defends desalination as insurance/useful but the motion requires it as main investment, plus A4.4 showing B's inland fix (transmission, modular offshore) magnifies the cost problem it was meant to solve.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively controlling the core clash: the distinction between desalination being a "useful" tool and it being the "primary" investment. B effectively argued that committing the largest share of scarce capital to the most expensive, energy-intensive option before exhausting cheaper demand-side measures (like fixing leaks and efficient development) is fiscally irresponsible. Side A's rhetoric about changing the "numerator" was memorable but failed to overcome B's logical point that conservation and efficient new development can accommodate growth without requiring desalination as the universal first resort. B also handled the pressure questions better, exposing A's overgeneralization and defending the need for localized audits before megaproject commitments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where B consistently forced A to defend desalination as the primary investment rather than just a useful supplement, and systematically dismantled A's assumption that conservation has a hard ceiling that cannot accommodate population growth.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): B won narrowly because A persuasively showed that rationing cannot create water after conservation reaches its floor, but did not establish that large-scale desalination should therefore receive primary investment across geographically and financially diverse water-scarce regions. B preserved the stronger investment-priority comparison: pursue cheaper permanent savings first, especially where coastal desalination and conveyance are impractical, while retaining desalination as a supplement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest exchange was that subsidies can distribute shortage fairly but cannot create missing water. It substantially damaged B’s rationing case, though B’s distinction between proving a role for desalination and proving its primacy ultimately survived.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO won the central supply-vs-demand clash. CON showed conservation is cheaper per cubic metre but never solved the floor problem: when reservoirs empty, allocating a shortage does not create water. B exposed this in Pressure Qs and in R2 with the subsidy-vs-creation distinction and the hydrology-as-structural-constraint vs energy-as-plannable weigh. A's answers re-badged reuse and temporary curtailment as conservation and left essential deficit unexplained. Geography/cost points kept it competitive, but PRO gave the cleaner reason to vote for primacy as backbone + efficiency to shrink it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 answers + B2.6-B4.6 – subsidy can distribute shortage fairly, it cannot create missing water; capital/energy/brine constrain projects that add water, hydrology constrains CON's strategy entirely – directly neutralized CON's cost-per-litre ledger.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by establishing a decisive structural advantage: conservation has a physical floor, whereas scarcity does not. When Side A pressed on what happens when that floor is reached during a multi-year drought, Side B was forced to rely on emergency restrictions and curtailment, which Side A effectively framed as institutionalized deprivation. Side A also successfully neutralized Side B's equity and example-based arguments by distinguishing between municipal and agricultural water needs and highlighting that the poor suffer most from physical shortages, which subsidies cannot magically create. Side B's cost arguments were strong, but they failed to overcome the fundamental hydrological reality that efficiency cannot replace missing supply.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in Rebuttal 2 regarding equity and the physical limits of conservation, specifically the crystallizing point that "a subsidy can distribute a shortage fairly; it cannot create the missing water," which cleanly dismantled Side B's reliance on targeted rationing as a permanent, equitable solution.
