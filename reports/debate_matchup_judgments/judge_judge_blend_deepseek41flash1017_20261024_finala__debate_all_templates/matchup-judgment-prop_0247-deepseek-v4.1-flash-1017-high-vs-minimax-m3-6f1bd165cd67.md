### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Both sides argued competently with plausible mechanisms and no fabricated evidence, and both suffered one clipped speech, roughly canceling that execution cost. The decisive difference is that CON kept the clash tethered to the motion's specific demand—a rate "high enough" paired with accepted disproportionate burden—and used the pressure round to expose a real tension: PRO's own answer ("lowest rate that meaningfully shifts consumption, monitored and adjusted") collapses the distinction between a modest, harm-calibrated tax and the "high" tax the motion requires defending. PRO's reply ("enough to bite, not the maximum the state can extract") was a reasonable clarification but was never pressed back into the explicit comparison CON demanded, and PRO's closing did not resolve the tension CON reiterated. CON also sustained a live, unanswered point about the asymmetry between a certain, immediate cash loss and an uncertain, delayed health benefit—PRO's population-level response was plausible but abstract and didn't engage the individual-household uncertainty CON kept raising. PRO's strongest material—burden is avoidable by switching, disease burden is not, revenue recycling as a design option under the "even if" clause—landed well but didn't fully neutralize CON's narrower, more disciplined attack on the specific "high rate" mechanism. The result is a close, competitive debate where CON's tighter focus on the motion's actual wording gives it a modest edge.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): B set the decisive frame early: the motion itself accepts disproportionate burden as the price of a behavior-changing tax, so any defense resting on "perfect compensation" is defending a different policy than the one on the table. B's rebate dilemma (a transfer big enough to protect poor households is big enough to cushion the very price pressure the tax needs to work) was a sharp structural point that A never fully dismantled, instead asserting without fully explaining the lump-sum/substitution-effect economics that would have neutralized it. The pressure questions were well-used by both sides, but B's Q1 forced A into a real concession (A would defend the tax even with zero rebate), and B's rebuttal 2 and closing converted that concession into the debate's central weighing claim: gross incidence becomes final incidence once compensation is optional, against diffuse and leaky health benefits. A's case remained coherent and economically plausible (externalities, revenue use, substitution toward water), but its closing was cut off mid-argument, leaving its final synthesis incomplete, a real execution cost per the judging frame. B's closing, by contrast, was clean, crystallized the clash, and ended on a clear decision rule. Overall B made the comparison easier to resolve and more directly engaged the motion's own wording.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 "cash is fungible" dilemma ([B2.1]-[B2.2]), later weaponized after A's concession in the Pressure Questions answer ([A4.1]) that the tax should be defended even absent any rebate — B's Rebuttal 2/Closing turned that concession into "gross incidence is final incidence," which A's clipped closing never fully answered.

Side B sharply exposed that compensation was not guaranteed, but never adequately established that the immediate burden outweighed reduced consumption, reformulation, revenue, and avoided public costs. Its claim that alternatives defeat the tax imposed an unwarranted necessity test. Side A's clipped closing narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's answer to the rebate dilemma was decisive: compensation can cushion household income while soda remains more expensive relative to water. Side B acknowledged this could hold in theory, then relied mainly on fungibility without showing that the price incentive disappears.

After PRO conceded it would still defend a meaningful tax with no rebate, CON converted that into final incidence on lower-income households and made compensation look like an optional bolt-on rather than part of the defended policy.
