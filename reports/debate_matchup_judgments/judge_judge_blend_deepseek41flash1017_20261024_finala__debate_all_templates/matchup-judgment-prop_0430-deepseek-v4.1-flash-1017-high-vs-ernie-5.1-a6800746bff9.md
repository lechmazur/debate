### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): A won by showing that PRO's clearest cases of coordinated harassment or measurable school disruption already fall under existing school-nexus rules, while the proposed expansion chiefly reaches ambiguous invitation decisions. B offered plausible benefits from reporting and training, but did not establish that an anti-bullying label supplies the missing context or outweighs misclassification and surveillance risks. Its claims that the status quo has failed and interventions almost never occur were also insufficiently grounded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response that screenshots can prove cruel messages but not whether an absent invitation reflects ostracism, a falling-out, or a safety retreat directly weakened B's analogy to ordinary cyberbullying. B named pattern, intent, harm, and safeguards, but those criteria largely reproduced the existing nexus standard rather than demonstrating a distinct, workable new duty.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON won by forcing the decisive choice: either PRO collapses into existing school-nexus coverage of disruption/harassment, or it creates a new anti-bullying category whose core “wrong” is often non-invitation—hard to see, easy to misread, and asymmetric in enforcement. PRO’s structural-duty/status-quo-failure case was real but never cleanly solved the adjudication gap; CON kept returning to that burden with sharper comparison and better answers under pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s repeated absence-of-invitation frame (esp. A2.4/A5.1): screenshots show messages, not why a smaller chat formed, so anti-bullying machinery attaches discipline to private association guesses rather than proving cruelty.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing the fundamental epistemic and practical flaws in Side A's proposal. B successfully argued that the "wrongful act" in private digital exclusion is often merely the absence of an invitation, which schools cannot reliably investigate without intrusive social surveillance. B also trapped A in a logical dilemma: if the motion only targets severe, disruptive campaigns, it is redundant with existing school-nexus rules; if it targets ordinary exclusion, it is unworkable and chills private association. A relied heavily on the emotional weight of the harm but failed to provide a workable mechanism for distinguishing targeted bullying from normal friendship formation, leaving B's practical objections largely unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's persistent attack on the epistemic problem of investigating an "omission" (the absence of an invitation) rather than an "action" (a cruel message), culminating in the closing argument that applying anti-bullying machinery to private chats merely "lends procedure to a guess" and forces schools to arbitrarily adjudicate friendship.
