### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): The decisive clash was whether capping enrollment is a necessary mechanism for redirecting funding toward shortage fields, or whether it merely imposes a definite access cost for a speculative benefit. CON pressed this gap relentlessly and memorably ("An enrollment penalty is not a clinical placement"), repeatedly noting that a binding budget/funding priority could reserve resources for placements and instructors without denying students public seats, and that PRO never showed why formula-selected programs were the least valuable cuts. PRO's safeguards (multi-year review, protected core, revisable metric) blunted some objections about metric fragility and equity reversal, and the scarcity argument had real force, but PRO never cleanly closed the gap CON kept reopening: that scarcity justifies priorities, not specifically this enrollment penalty. CON's equity point also landed hard — the cost of the cap is definite and immediate, while the benefit (a poorer applicant actually gaining a nursing seat) remains contingent on completion, aptitude, and retention. PRO's pressure questions were reasonably answered but didn't fully dislodge CON's alternative of direct budget rules. Both sides were clipped equally in Rebuttal 1, so that execution cost is a wash. Overall CON sustained a sharper, more consistent causal critique through to the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 response distinguishing "a binding funding priority" from "an enrollment penalty" ("An enrollment penalty is not a clinical placement"), which PRO never fully neutralized in the final rebuttal or closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by framing the marginal public seat as the relevant allocation decision. It answered transferability concerns by explaining that redirected money funds placements and instructors for different qualified applicants, not necessarily students denied other majors. Side A exposed forecasting, equity, retention, and implementation risks, but its direct-investment alternative never established a better funding tradeoff under scarcity. Both clipped first rebuttals incurred similar execution costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its answer to the claim that targeted investment makes caps unnecessary: without identifying new money or defensible alternative cuts, Side A could not explain how its earmark would expand shortage capacity while preserving every existing subsidized place.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing university enrollment as a zero-sum funding mechanism, proving that without caps, cheap-to-run programs will continuously absorb subsidies at the expense of costly shortage fields. Side A argued well that caps impose definite access costs for contingent gains, but struggled to explain how to fund expensive clinical bottlenecks without eventually cutting into other enrollments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1-B4.2) cleanly answered Side A's Q1 by explaining that because cheap programs naturally expand to absorb available subsidies, a hard cap is the only way to effectively reserve funding for expensive shortage capacity.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases, but B's central distinction—between a "budget brake" (freezing new lines, not auto-refilling departures, redirecting new money to bottlenecks) and an "enrollment bar" that forcibly excludes willing students—proved decisive and was never fully neutralized. The pressure questions exposed a key concession from A ([A4.4]) that caps "need not place every displaced student" into a shortage field, which B then repeatedly leveraged to show the cap's only distinctive function is exclusion, not actual production of nurses or engineers, while carrying a larger irreversibility risk (hollowed majors/faculty pipelines) than a reversible budget freeze. A's closing, which needed to resolve this gap, was cut off by the word-limit clip before completing its final argument, an explicit execution cost under the judging rules, while B delivered a clean, within-limits closing that crystallized the clash with a memorable line ("a stop rule aimed at the student cannot staff the ward"). A's case remained strong on opportunity-cost framing and procedural safeguards (gradual triggers, teach-outs), but never cleanly showed why a cap outperforms the freeze-and-redirect alternative B offered, leaving B's comparative advantage live at the end of the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to A's Pressure Question (A1 in Rebuttal 2: "freeze new lines, do not refill departures automatically...") combined with its later reuse of A's concession that "the bar need not place anyone in the shortage field" ([B4.5], [B5.4]) was the most decisive exchange, isolating exclusion as the cap's only distinctive and riskiest feature.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by establishing that shortage funding and enrollment caps perform distinct functions: one expands constrained capacity, while the other stops repeated public recruitment into persistently weak outcomes. Side A sharply exposed forecasting, lobbying, and irreversibility risks, but often treated a gradual cap as abolition and never fully answered why disclosure and a budget brake adequately protect successive cohorts or change recruiting incentives. B's clipped closing was an execution cost, but its central comparison remained intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3-B4.7 was decisive: B conceded that closing a seat does not itself create a nurse, then showed that perfect reassignment was unnecessary because the cap supplies the stop rule that disclosure and shortage funding lack. This directly weakened A's repeated claim that the policy fails unless displaced students enter shortage fields.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by cleanly decoupling the funding shift from the enrollment cap, arguing that bottlenecks can be funded without a national veto on student choice. Side A's defense of natural capacity limits over outcome-based caps—and the asymmetric harm caps cause to a discipline's pipeline—left Side B struggling to prove why exclusion itself was necessary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 perfectly exploits Side B's answers to the pressure questions, isolating the cap's only unique function as "exclusion" and landing the decisive blow: "A stop rule aimed at the student cannot staff the ward."
