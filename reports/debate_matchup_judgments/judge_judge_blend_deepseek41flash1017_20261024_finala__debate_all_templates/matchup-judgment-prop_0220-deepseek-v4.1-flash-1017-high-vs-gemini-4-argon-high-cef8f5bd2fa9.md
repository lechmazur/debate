### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but A built a more coherent and ultimately more resilient causal story: deductions are a price incentive that only helps people who already have cash and already act, while auto-enrollment attacks the actual binding constraint (inertia) and creates participation "from zero," which A tied cleanly to the motion's "long-run" compounding metric. B's strongest material—liquidity risk, debt, and downward anchoring—landed hard in Rebuttal 1 and the pressure-question exchange, especially the "inertia contradiction" (if workers are too inert to enroll, why assume they'll promptly opt out or pause when cash-strapped?). But A's answer (modest defaults, calibrated opt-out/hardship safeguards as a design choice rather than inherent flaw) was a reasonable, if not airtight, resolution, and A kept returning to the comparative point B never fully rebutted: a deduction does nothing for the non-saver, while even an imperfect default produces some balance. B's claim that pretax deductions give "immediate" withholding relief also muddied the distinction, since that mechanism isn't unique to voluntary deductions. A's closing crystallized the decisive margin (assets accumulated by at-risk households over decades) more cleanly than B's closing, which mostly restated the balance-sheet framing without fully closing the participation gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Pressure Questions exchange on the "inertia contradiction" (B3.1/B3.2 vs. A4.1/A4.3) was the most decisive clash — B exposed a real tension in A's model, and A's answer (modest defaults + hardship safeguards as calibrated design, not compulsion) blunted but did not fully neutralize the point, leaving a live but manageable weakness in A's case.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): B kept the decisive comparison clear: the proposition turns on moving at-risk non-savers from zero to cumulative assets, and a default does that while a deduction mainly rewards those already contributing cash. A’s cash-flow/balance-sheet critique and inertia-contradiction hit hard and forced B to shrink defaults and lean on design, but A never showed how larger voluntary deductions convert the same households or beat a small leaking balance against true zero. B answered pressure more cleanly on the participation margin and weighed long-run security as broad asset-building rather than paper enrollment alone.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s inertia contradiction (workers too inert to enroll yet prompt to opt out/pause under cash stress) was the sharpest single hit; B blunted it by separating default-driven start from crisis-triggered exit, then re-weighed small balance vs nothing.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side A built a much stronger comparative case by broadening "financial security" to include the avoidance of high-interest debt, effectively neutralizing Side B's narrow focus on retirement account balances. Side A's explanation of how pre-tax payroll deductions immediately reduce the out-of-pocket cost of saving dismantled Side B's "cash first" critique. Furthermore, Side A sharply exposed a fatal contradiction in Side B's case: relying on inertia to keep workers enrolled while simultaneously assuming they would proactively opt-out or pause escalation during cash-flow crunches.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but A's pressure questions extracted a decisive concession: B admitted that expanding deductions "often doesn't" help workers below existing contribution caps and "wouldn't" overcome the inertia that keeps disengaged savers from acting. Since most underprepared households are already below existing deduction caps and are precisely the disengaged population B relies on inertia-arguments to explain, this concession gutted B's constructive mechanism, leaving deductions looking like they mainly benefit already-secure, high-bracket savers maxing out limits—exactly A's original regressivity critique. B's cleverest move, the "inertia can't enroll people and then freeze them" bind in Rebuttal 1, was a strong original point, but A defused it in Rebuttal 2/closing by falling back to the baseline comparison (a frozen 3% default plus match and compounding still beats the zero savings that disengaged workers produce under deductions), and by noting raising contributions later is easier than starting from scratch. B's late critique that A's defense relied on "plan design beyond the bare opt-out mechanism" (auto-escalation, auto-IRAs) was fair but didn't fully neutralize A's simpler fallback mechanism, which stayed squarely within the proposition's wording. A's closing crystallized the clash more cleanly by directly quoting B's own concessions, giving the clearer reason to vote PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure questions in Turn 5, forcing B's Rebuttal 2 concessions ("it often doesn't," "it wouldn't") that expanding deductions fails to move either below-cap middle earners or disengaged non-savers—undermining B's entire intensive-margin mechanism and handing A's closing its decisive talking point.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO won the decisive clash on who the policies actually move and whether that movement creates new security. CON’s adequacy-vs-enrollment frame was live and sharp, but under pressure CON conceded that expanded deductions often do nothing for a middle-income 5-to-8% move already under existing caps and will not break inertia for the disengaged—leaving only already-secure high earners who mainly shelter existing wealth. That undercut CON’s intensive-margin route. PRO’s remaining case—default participation, match capture, and early compounding for non-savers—still beat “headcount only,” even after CON’s fair critique that bare opt-out enrollment is not auto-escalation. Comparison favored turning at-risk non-savers into savers over subsidizing rearrangers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Pressure Q1/Q2 on already-full deductibility under caps and inertia’s inconsistency, answered with CON’s “It often doesn’t” / “It wouldn’t,” then crystallized in B’s closing.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B wins solidly by exposing a fatal flaw in Side A's core mechanism. Side A argued that increasing tax deductions incentivizes the "intensive margin" (e.g., moving from 5% to 8% contributions) for middle-income workers. Side B's first pressure question brilliantly pointed out that contributions at those levels are already fully deductible under existing caps, meaning "increasing deductions" (e.g., raising caps) does nothing for workers below the cap. Side A conceded this point in Rebuttal 2 ("It often doesn't"), effectively abandoning their primary comparative advantage. Side B capitalized on this concession in the closing to show that Side A's policy only benefits affluent households already maxing out limits, while Side B's policy at least generates baseline savings and employer matches for the disengaged. Side A's late pivot to attack Side B's reliance on specific default rates or public IRAs was less persuasive than Side B's structural takedown of Side A's tax deduction mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Pressure Question 1 and subsequent Rebuttal 2, which forced Side A to concede that increasing tax deductions does not lower the marginal cost of saving for middle-income workers operating below existing contribution caps, thereby neutralizing Side A's "intensive margin" argument.
