### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but B built a more coherent, consistently maintained case: housing is the only intervention that actually ends homelessness, enforcement is a necessary-but-not-primary backstop, and this framing survived every challenge. B grounded its claims in plausible, appropriately-hedged examples (Houston, Finland) and used them under direct pressure, while A's case remained largely conceptual, never naming a city or metric where enforcement-primacy succeeded even when directly asked (A's A1 answer to B's pointed Q1 reframed "victory" rather than engaging the empirical ask, a real evasion). A's strongest move—distinguishing durable exits from immediate safety duties—was answered sharply in B's closing fire-code analogy ("no city makes the fire department its primary fire policy; codes are primary, the crew is the backstop"), which reused A's own analogy against it and clarified why "primarily" tracks mission/budget/metric rather than moment-to-moment triage. A's closing rebuttal to that reframe ("while the building burns, prevention cannot command the emergency") was a reasonable comeback but didn't fully dismantle the distinction, and repeated earlier conceptual claims rather than closing the empirical gap. B's progression across rounds was tighter and better resolved the central clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's closing fire-code analogy, turning A's own "fire crew" metaphor into proof that prevention (housing) is properly primary while enforcement is a necessary but secondary backstop, combined with A's inability in the Pressure Questions exchange to name any city or metric where policing-primacy actually shrank street homelessness.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won by distinguishing an essential emergency backstop from the primary policy framework. Side A convincingly established that cities must respond immediately to fires, assaults, and obstruction, but never showed why this makes policing the main strategy for homelessness rather than a necessary response to particular conduct. Side B also absorbed the hardest cases into the proposition’s service-access component and emphasized that enforcement lacks a durable mechanism for reducing street homelessness. Its opening relied somewhat heavily on unverified approximate outcome claims, but its comparative case remained stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s closing turn on the fire analogy was decisive: emergency crews can be indispensable without constituting the primary prevention policy. That exposed Side A’s central inference from policing’s immediate necessity to policing’s policy primacy, which Side A’s final response did not overcome.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): B won by locking the motion on what “primarily” means—money, mission, and metric—and forcing A into a necessity case rather than a primacy case. A’s best live route (time-horizon mismatch and immediate safety) was real and well pressed, but B repeatedly showed those emergencies are answered under both frames while only housing durable-exits the street population. A never named a city or metric where policing shrank the problem, and B’s closing fire-department turn crystallized that A proved a needed backstop, not a primary policy. Clear but competitive win for PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s fire-analogy inversion in closing: a crew is necessary but never primary; primacy belongs to what removes the cause, which turned A’s strongest urgency rhetoric against the CON case.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but the decisive moment came in the Pressure Questions / Rebuttal 2 exchange. A forced B to state an end state for the chronic refuser, and B's answer ("Cities can prohibit camping, clear unsafe sites, and require a chronic refuser to move") conceded that enforcement operates first at every real decision point. A seized on this immediately, reframing it as "rule first, exit second" and arguing that a purpose that only governs policy in the abstract, while a rule decides every actual case, cannot claim primacy. B's recovery attempt in closing (distinguishing "tool used in an encounter" from "purpose governing the policy") was conceptually reasonable but arrived late and did not fully erase the rhetorical and substantive damage from the earlier concession. A also sustained a cleaner throughline across rounds (NY as enforcement-enabled-destination, California as housing-first's empirical failure, coercion as the only path for the chemically unable to consent), while B's case, though causally sound, relied more on reasserting mechanism than directly dismantling A's NY reinterpretation or California critique. A's closing crystallized the clash more memorably and left B's own words as the strongest evidence against B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3-A4.4), which explicitly names B's concession in answering Pressure Question 1 ("Cities can prohibit camping, clear unsafe sites, and require a chronic refuser to move") and reframes it as proof that enforcement, not purpose, decides every real case — a point B's closing could blunt but not fully reverse.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): B won by distinguishing the first tool used from the strategy that governs outcomes. A compellingly established that cities need enforceable public-space rules, especially for chronic refusers, but repeatedly treated immediate sequencing as proof of policy primacy. B showed that clearance identifies where someone cannot stay, while housing, shelter, and treatment capacity determine whether homelessness actually ends. A's California and coercion claims did not establish that policing produces durable exits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest response was that A's lawful destinations, mandated treatment, detox, and medication all depend on service capacity: where capacity exists, services produce the exit; where it does not, enforcement merely shifts the person to custody or another street. This directly undercut A's claim that clearing first makes policing primary.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON won a close, high-level debate by owning the hard live cases—chronic refusers, missing beds, and next month’s tent—and by turning PRO’s conceded clearances/camping bans into a practical sequence of rule-first, exit-second. That crystallization made the clash easier to decide than PRO’s elegant but more abstract “purpose vs first tool” frame. PRO answered pressure honestly, kept stronger epistemic discipline, and never fully collapsed, but after the hybrid concessions their distinct “housing-primary” route no longer outworked CON’s order-primary division of labor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2: taking B’s playground-clearance answer and converting it into a sequence concession (“clear this week, place eventually”) as primacy in practice.
