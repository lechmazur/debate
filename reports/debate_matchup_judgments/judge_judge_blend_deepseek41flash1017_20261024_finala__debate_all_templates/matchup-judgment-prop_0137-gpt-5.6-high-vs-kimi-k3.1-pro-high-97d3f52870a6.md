### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly within the motion's conditional structure, properly used the Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 format, and avoided fake citations. CON's strongest weapons were the vagueness of "flagship"/"strong" and the claim that the rule achieves nothing because employers can simply go quiet while leave remains weak. PRO neutralized the second point by explicitly biting the bullet: the motion only conditions reputational acclaim, not substantive policy, so "quieting down" is a legitimate, not evasive, compliance path, and coverage/information survive either way. PRO also exploited a real tension in CON's own argument—that salience is commercially powerful enough to shift applicant and competitor behavior yet supposedly carries no signaling content about workplace priorities—crystallized in the line "accuracy about policies is not accuracy about priorities." CON's vagueness pressure was real but PRO's functional test (showcase/emblem vs. disclosure) gave a workable, if imperfect, standard, which is adequate given the no-citation, policy-debate context. CON's population-specific argument (women without partners, medical urgency) was effectively defused by PRO noting access and information are untouched. On balance PRO built the more internally consistent case and answered the single most dangerous CON objection most directly, giving PRO a clear but not dominant edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response in Rebuttal 2 that "salience cannot be commercially powerful but socially inert," combined with the closing line "accuracy about policies is not accuracy about priorities," most decisively undercut CON's claim that truthful disclosure fully cures the signaling concern, while also reframing CON's "cheaper escape" objection as consistent with the motion's narrow design rather than a failure of it.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully separating the standalone value of egg freezing from comprehensive family support, arguing that employers deserve credit for incremental benefits. Side A effectively exposed the flaw in Side B's remedy, demonstrating that forcing employers to quiet their marketing doesn't create better leave, but merely reduces awareness of a valuable option.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing argument that "acclaim for providing egg freezing is not unearned merely because acclaim for comprehensive family support would be" directly neutralized Side B's core "unearned reputation" framework.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side A successfully framed the debate around the distinction between providing a benefit and marketing it as a flagship emblem of women's advancement. This allowed A to neutralize B's primary argument—that the motion harms women by removing a valuable option—by clarifying that access and transparent information remain, while only the unearned reputational acclaim is conditioned on broader family support. A's rebuttal to the "cheaper escape" (that employers will just hide the benefit rather than improve leave) was decisive: if they hide it, they lose the unearned progressive branding, which is exactly the motion's intent. B's arguments were competent but relied too heavily on the assumption that regulating prominence equates to suppressing the benefit itself, a conflation A expertly dismantled.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's "cheaper escape" argument in the closing and second rebuttal. When B argued employers would simply stop advertising the benefit rather than improve leave, A cleanly bit the bullet, explaining that stripping the employer of unearned "women-friendly" acclaim without curing the weak leave is a successful outcome of the motion, as it stops the mismatch between the company's progressive branding and its actual family support.
