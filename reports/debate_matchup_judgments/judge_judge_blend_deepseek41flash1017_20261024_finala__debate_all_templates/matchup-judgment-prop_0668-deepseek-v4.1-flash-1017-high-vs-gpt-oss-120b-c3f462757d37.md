### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): CON built a consistent, well-defended comparative frame: a ban does not eliminate capability, only the exporter's leverage, audit trail, and ability to detect/punish misuse, while substitution (unchecked vendors) keeps the dissident identifiable regardless. Crucially, CON turned PRO's own Xinjiang example into a self-inflicted wound via a sharp pressure question (A3.1), since it shows mass surveillance built entirely outside Western export controls — directly undercutting PRO's "chokepoint" mechanism. PRO's answer (A1 in R2) never resolved this; it just reasserted that losing Western kit "delays" capacity without engaging why the cited example shows otherwise. PRO also conceded, in effect, that its "objective list" is deliberately blunt and cannot distinguish reformist states from police states, which validates rather than answers CON's categorical-exclusion critique. PRO's case leaned on precise-sounding but unsupported statistics (0.1% arrest rate, 10 million false positives, "2020-2022 surge") that read as brittle fake precision, and PRO's opening and first rebuttal were both clipped for exceeding word limits — a real execution cost that left parts of its core case underdeveloped. CON's conditional-licensing alternative (named end-users, audits, revocation, sanctions for diversion) was clearly and repeatedly defended as the more targeted, leverage-preserving option, and CON's closing crystallized the clash cleanly: "surveillance we can see and condition" versus "surveillance handed to vendors who answer to no one."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Question (A3.1) forcing PRO to explain why a Western ban reduces repression when PRO's own Xinjiang example shows mass surveillance built via non-Western suppliers — PRO's answer never actually closed this gap, just reasserted the delay claim.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): B won the live comparison: ban causes substitution plus lost audits/revocation, so it does not clearly help the dissident more than conditional licensing. A’s choke-point story was undercut by its own Xinjiang example and never recovered into a decisive reduction-of-repression claim. A also paid costs for clipped turns and brittle fake precision on policing efficacy. B answered pressure directly, kept weighing on leverage vs. abdication, and made the vote easier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s turn of A’s Xinjiang example (Western ban is not a choke point) plus the A1/A2 answers framing imperfect visible leverage over invisible substitutes.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side B (CON) won by more effectively undermining the central premise that a ban reduces repression. B's use of the Xinjiang example demonstrated that mass surveillance can be built without Western technology, and B successfully framed the choice as "imperfect leverage versus no leverage." B's rebuttals directly engaged A's choke-point theory, showed that lower-grade substitutes still identify dissidents, and exposed the overinclusiveness of a blanket ban. A's case was weakened by brittle fake precision in its rebuttal, two clipped speeches that truncated key arguments, and an inability to fully justify why categorical exclusion outperforms targeted conditional engagement. B's closing crystallized the clash memorably: a ban makes the remaining transaction invisible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal of PRO's choke-point argument (Turn 4 and Turn 8), where B used A's own Xinjiang example to show mass surveillance can be assembled outside Western controls, and reframed the debate around the loss of audit trails and leverage rather than a reduction in repressive capability.
