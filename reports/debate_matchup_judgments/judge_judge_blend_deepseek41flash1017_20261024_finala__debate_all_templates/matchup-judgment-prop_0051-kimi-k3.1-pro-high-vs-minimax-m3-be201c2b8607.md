### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON built a consistent, clarifying distinction between "controlling access" (which platforms have) and "competence to verify safety/labor conditions" (which they lack), and sustained this through every round. PRO's strongest move—framing responsibility as following control over storefront access—never squarely answered how fraud-prone seller documentation becomes reliable safety evidence, or how subcontractor mapping is feasible without inspection powers. When pressed directly (Q1 on thresholds), PRO's answer was essentially "test it and see," which CON correctly exposed in Rebuttal 2 and Closing as architecture without comparison—PRO's own evidentiary burden left unmet. PRO's rhetoric ("untraceable harm is a business model") was vivid, but rhetorical force without resolving the mechanism gap wasn't enough to overcome CON's cleaner, better-defended comparative case. PRO's speeches were also clipped twice for exceeding word limits, a real execution cost, while CON's speeches stayed disciplined throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.5), pointing out that PRO's comparative "evaluation" test from the pressure-question answer was never actually applied—PRO asserted a decision rule but supplied no evidence satisfying it, leaving the core burden of the motion unmet.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing platform duties as necessary complements to close jurisdictional gaps in cross-border commerce. While Side B cleverly capitalized on Side A's unforced 'harm reduction per dollar' metric, Side A's core argument—that platforms control the transaction chokepoint and that document fraud justifies penalties rather than inaction—remained logically decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's argument that the potential for document fraud justifies penalties and targeted checks, rather than reverting to collecting only a name and waiting for injuries.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by successfully arguing that cross-border e-commerce creates jurisdictional gaps that only platforms can fill as gatekeepers. While CON effectively highlights the limits of platform inspection capacity and the risks of audit theater, PRO counters that transparency and traceability are necessary prerequisites for any enforcement, and that platforms control the access point. PRO accepts the trade-offs of slower shopping and seller friction, aligning well with the motion. CON relies too heavily on domestic regulators, which is less convincing in a cross-border context. PRO receives a slight penalty for clipped turns, but the substantive advantage remains.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO effectively dismantled CON's reliance on state regulators by highlighting jurisdictional limits and framing platform duties as complementary, layered enforcement rather than a replacement for state inspection.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, adapting cases, and both suffered symmetric clipping penalties in early speeches. The decisive clash became "reactive, evidence-triggered enforcement" (A) versus "proactive baseline transparency as the precondition for any detection" (B). B's pressure questions forced A to specify a concrete alternative, which A did credibly (seller/importer verification, record preservation, complaint-triggered suspension, customs inspection). A's pressure questions forced B to admit its "proportionate" regime still requires a substantial baseline (site/subcontractor disclosure, lot traceability, hazard documentation, grievance channels) even for ordinary low-risk sellers, which A then used to show that B's framework is costlier than advertised while its proactive benefits remain speculative. A's closing crystallized this into a clean, memorable cost/benefit asymmetry ("costs certain, benefits speculative... an experiment billed to shoppers"), while B's closing restated the "junction/timing" leverage argument without fully answering why baseline costs on safe, ordinary sellers are justified rather than reactive, evidence-triggered duties. This gives A a modest but clear edge on comparative weighing and rebuttal sharpness, though B's leverage/control mechanism and tiered-duty framing kept the debate genuinely close throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal 2 distinction that B's leverage argument "wins the case for acting on detected danger... not comprehensive transparency before ordinary goods may be sold" [A4.5-A4.6] was the sharpest moment, narrowing the live clash and forcing B's closing to lean on a "timing/junction" defense that didn't fully dislodge A's cost-certainty framing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully framing Side A's ex-ante compliance regime as an expensive tax on safe goods that fails to prevent bad actors from faking paperwork. Side B capitalized on Side A's definition of a 'light' baseline, proving it was still highly burdensome for small sellers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing argument perfectly neutralized Side A's 'timing' point by showing that ex-ante paperwork doesn't detect danger upfront, but merely serves as 'downstream evidence collected in advance'—meaning consumers pay for it on every transaction without guaranteed ex-ante protection.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A won by drawing a sharp and decisive distinction between ex ante supply-chain validation and reactive, evidence-triggered enforcement. While Side B argued effectively that platforms have the leverage to enforce safety and that "timing" requires upstream records, Side A devastatingly countered in the closing that a pre-existing record does not equal pre-purchase detection, framing PRO's regime as merely "downstream evidence collected in advance" at a high cost to consumers and small sellers. Side A also provided a concrete, workable alternative when pressed in the pressure round, and used highly effective rhetoric to crystallize the core policy tradeoff.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing response to Side B's "timing" argument, which perfectly dismantled the idea that ex ante paperwork guarantees upstream safety and reframed it as a costly advance-collection of downstream evidence.
