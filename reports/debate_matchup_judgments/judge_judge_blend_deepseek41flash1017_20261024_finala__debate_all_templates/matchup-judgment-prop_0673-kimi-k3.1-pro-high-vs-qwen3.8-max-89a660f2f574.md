### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): The debate turned on two cruxes: whether "reduced reliance" is satisfied by partial substitution, and whether "fraudulent claims" can be defined to include economically-motivated but legally pursued claims. On the first, PRO's reading that the motion only requires reduction (not elimination) is textually sound, and CON's barrier-based objections, while plausible, never established that reduction wouldn't occur, only that it might be incomplete. On the second, decisive crux, CON's entire fraud case rested on redefining "fraudulent" to mean "filed chiefly for economic motive" or "legally insufficient," which PRO repeatedly and correctly identified as an evasive reframing that would make the proposition untestable and conflate weak claims with dishonest ones. CON never recovered from this charge in rebuttal or closing, instead reasserting the sequencing narrative without resolving the definitional problem or offering evidence of actual increased dishonesty, only a theoretical incentive shift. PRO's pressure-question answers (A2 especially) cleanly isolated dishonesty as the operative fraud standard, which CON's closing never directly rebutted. CON's rhetorical framing ("pays first, invites the wrong claims") was memorable but rested on a shaky conceptual foundation. PRO's case suffered an execution cost from being clipped in three separate speeches, which is a real formatting/discipline problem, but the substantive content that was visible consistently outmaneuvered CON on the decisive definitional fight, giving PRO a clear, if not overwhelming, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's repeated insistence (B2.4, B4.2) that "a fraudulent claim involves dishonesty, not simply an unsuccessful or economically-motivated claim" directly dismantled CON's core mechanism for the fraud prong, and CON never offered a clean answer beyond restating the sequencing theory without evidence of actual increased dishonesty.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO won a competitive dual-burden clash by keeping the motion’s ordinary meaning intact: “reduce reliance” covers measurable cuts in amount, intensity, or duration of aid, and “fraudulent” means dishonest claims rather than weak, mixed-motive, or unsuccessful ones. CON’s sequencing/pull-factor story was coherent and well-framed, but it repeatedly had to expand fraud and demand near-total independence the text does not require, while staying speculative on net caseload effects. PRO’s direct earnings-substitution mechanism plus the point that delayed authorization already exists made the residual fraud claim look more like redefinition than proof. Clipping cost A some polish, but the decisive definitions and weighing still landed cleaner for A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s fraud/definitional rebuttals (esp. A2.4–A2.6 and A4.1–A4.2): forced CON’s “legally insufficient / permit-driven = fraud” expansion into the open and kept screening on the merits, not forced idleness.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) kept the live route to victory: earlier lawful earnings directly reduce amount/duration of aid, and fraud remains defined by dishonesty about persecution, not by mixed motives. CON (A) stretched fraud to include merely weak or work-motivated claims without evidence of material increase in deception, and relied on speculative queue-cost arithmetic that PRO answered – added filers also earn sooner. B’s pressure questions exposed that definition shift, and B answered A’s questions directly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s R2 A1/A2 distinction – reliance falls when amount/intensity/duration falls, not only at full independence, and fraudulent = dishonest filing, not unsuccessful – plus rebuttal that pay stubs/leases don’t weaken asylum screening.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, closely contested cases, but PRO executed more cleanly across the full exchange. PRO's core mechanism (a work ban guarantees no lawful income, so even partial labor-market entry reduces aggregate reliance) stayed consistent and was reinforced by a workable materiality standard for fraud (status unchanged, no boost to claim success, incentive weak relative to the "prize" of durable protection). CON's strongest move was the "asymmetric accounting" charge—that PRO treats earlier wage access as decisive for savings but trivial for fraud incentives—but PRO directly answered this in Rebuttal 2/Closing by distinguishing per-applicant wage substitution from the weaker pull of a temporary, revocable permit on claim fabrication, which blunted rather than resolved but still meaningfully addressed the tension. CON's case, while analytically sharp, was undercut twice by being clipped for exceeding word limits (Opening and Rebuttal 1), an explicit execution cost under the judging frame, and its fraud-materiality standard (filing volume, processing cost) remained somewhat undefined and never quantified any more than PRO's employment assumptions were. Both Pressure Questions rounds were handled with proper A1/A2 format and real engagement, but PRO's closing more crisply crystallized why CON's burden (proving material fraud increase) was unmet, giving the cleaner final reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 reframe ("a work ban guarantees no lawful paycheck") combined with its Rebuttal 2 answer distinguishing wage-substitution effects from weak fraud incentives did the most to neutralize CON's asymmetric-accounting pressure, even though CON's question exposing that asymmetry (Q1/Q2 in Rebuttal 2) was the sharpest moment of the debate.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): CON won a competitive dual-burden debate. PRO’s enforced-idleness story was clear and structurally plausible on assistance, but it never securely established net caseload savings plus no material fraud rise. CON repeatedly forced the conjunctive motion, exposed the permit-is-not-a-paycheck gap, and landed the decisive asymmetric-accounting critique: the same earlier-wage incentive cannot be treated as fiscally transformative yet behaviorally trivial without proof. PRO reduced fraud materiality to permanent status/success and called induced filings “speculation,” while still assuming enough employment to carry the fiscal half. Clipped CON turns were an execution cost, but the live clash still favored CON’s ledger and materiality framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2/Closing asymmetric-accounting strike: earlier lawful earnings cannot be counted as certain assistance savings while dismissed as too weak to induce weak filings.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON wins on PRO's double burden. PRO shows some authorized workers likely earn and need less aid, but never establishes net whole-caseload savings or that a months-long lawful wage is too weak to attract work-motivated fraudulent filings. CON's core distinction — fraud can be material via filing volumes and processing/support costs even when claims are ultimately denied — survives, exposing asymmetric accounting where PRO counts gross wage gains but defines away filing burdens. Feedback loop of induced filings lengthening the earning window went unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 A2 and closing on materiality: a denied sham claim still costs screening, adjudication, support and enforcement, so PRO's test of altering outcomes is too narrow; same wage window matters to different people for assistance vs fraud, breaking PRO's no-contradiction defense.
