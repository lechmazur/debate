### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases and engaged each other's strongest points rather than talking past one another. CON's trust-layer/fraud framing was vivid and consistently pressed (accessibility/SMS permission capture, "multiple bureaucracies" after harm), and it never collapsed under pressure. But PRO's structural argument—that conduct regulation (anti-steering, fee rules) is a treadmill the gatekeeper can evade through review delays, technical friction, or substitute fees, and that only credible exit via sideloading disciplines that behavior—was the sharper, more decisive point, and CON's answer ("courts and agencies can enforce transparent review timelines and bans on retaliation") was generic and did not squarely rebut the asymmetry-of-power logic. PRO also handled the pressure questions cleanly (A1/A2 format), distinguishing permission from endorsement and reframing CON's "single accountable desk" as a preservable option rather than a reason to foreclose alternatives. PRO's case suffered a real execution cost: three speeches were clipped for exceeding word limits, cutting off its own closing synthesis each time, which is a legitimate ding on discipline and polish. Still, the substantive core of PRO's regulatory-evasion argument survived unrebutted in its strongest form, giving PRO the cleaner path to the decision despite the format penalty.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's recurring point that fee/anti-steering regulation lets the gatekeeper evade through delay, technical barriers, or replacement fees, versus CON's comparatively generic reply that "courts and agencies can enforce conduct rules," was the most consequential exchange, since it went to whether CON's "narrower alternative" can actually substitute for structural exit.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by separating preservation of the store’s safe default from compulsory use of that store. Side A made distributed accountability and normalization of risky flows credible concerns, but repeatedly overstated the mandate as forcing transactions into fragmented channels. Side B answered those risks with layered safeguards and showed why Side A’s conduct regulation lacks the discipline of credible exit. Repeated clipping modestly reduced Side B’s execution quality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its answer that centralized refunds and review are valuable services without being sufficient justification for compulsory control. It then contrasted targeted safeguards with Side A’s vulnerable strategy of continually policing new gatekeeper workarounds.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON won a competitive debate by keeping the decisive clash on ordinary-user accountability and mobile-specific trust architecture after harm, while treating PRO’s exit story as a regulatory shortcut that normalizes riskier install and checkout paths. PRO’s useful-default-versus-compulsory-monopoly frame and fee-evasion pressure were strong and clarifying, but later answers scattered responsibility across processors, banks, developers, and device revocation without a clean consumer recovery path. Clipped PRO turns and a somewhat optimistic distributed-accountability model left CON with the cleaner reason to reject a blanket mandate for most apps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2/closing reply that PRO’s “choose the store perimeter” answer fails once outside installs and checkouts are normalized, leaving fraud victims chasing multiple parties with no single refund/revocation desk.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-reasoned cases, but B executed the comparative work more precisely. B strategically conceded ground on payment reform to isolate the weakest link in PRO's bundle—mandatory sideloading—and then exploited it with two sharp moves: (1) exposing that PRO's safeguards ("signing, insurers, shared liability") are not actually guaranteed by the motion's text, just asserted as possible ("calling responsibility assignable is not assigning it"), and (2) the closing's enforcement-symmetry point, that if regulators can't reliably discipline one dominant gatekeeper (PRO's own premise for needing exit), they are even less likely to police a diffuse ecosystem of alternative stores, direct downloads, and payment processors. PRO never squarely closed that loop, instead repeating that "spread accountability is less brittle" without rebutting the diffusion-of-responsibility problem B raised. PRO's "toll vs. gate" framing and exit-as-discipline argument were strong and kept the debate close, but B's Pressure Questions and Rebuttal 2 did more damage to PRO's live route than PRO's answers did to B's. B's opening was clipped for exceeding the word limit, which is a real execution cost, but the substantive content lost was minimal (one trailing sentence), so it only modestly tempers B's advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3 exposure): B points out that PRO's claim the motion "can require" signing, redress, and shared liability is not the same as the motion actually requiring it, since the proposition only mandates allowing sideloading, not building the accountability architecture PRO relies on to make it safe. Paired with B's closing enforcement-symmetry argument, this left PRO's safety mitigation resting on unstated assumptions rather than anything in the motion itself.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by separating payment reform from the bundled sideloading mandate and forcing Side B to justify the latter's additional security and redress risks. Side B established real benefits from exit and contestability, but its accountability model remained speculative and enforcement-dependent. Side A's opening was clipped, yet its later rebuttals directly answered the central structural case and exposed the tension between B's distrust of targeted regulation and its reliance on broader regulation across many distributors.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange was A4.3-A4.5: it answered B's proposed shared liability by noting that direct downloads may involve no store, insurer, or processor, then compared enforcement burdens. The line that B rejects enforcement against one gatekeeper while relying on enforcement across an expanded ecosystem materially weakened B's safety answer.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON won by forcing the decisive burden onto PRO’s bundled remedy: even if independent payments are defensible, compulsory sideloading for most consumer apps still needed separate necessity proof, and PRO never fully earned that second half. A kept narrowing to enforcement realism, weak exit under ecosystem lock-in, and the gap between “safeguards can be required” and the motion as written. B had a coherent contestability story and cleaner slogans, but its pairing logic and layered-security model remained more asserted than comparatively secured against A’s multiplied-accountability attack. A’s clipped opening was a minor cost, not enough to reverse the cleaner path to reject the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 enforcement turn: PRO condemns targeted rules as too hard to police against one gatekeeper, yet its sideloading model requires identity/scanning/takedown/redress across more diffuse actors, while the motion itself only mandates access rather than those duties.
