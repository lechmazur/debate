- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built a tighter, more consistent case around the "tollbooth on the emergency exit" and network-effects framing, and sustained it across rounds with sharper analogies (lifeboat vs. motorized lifeboat) and concrete funding alternatives when pressed. CON (A) raised a real economic concern (revenue funding moderation) and a plausible fraud-scaling worry, but never squarely answered B's strongest point that a weak, bypassable "basic" tier still poisons the shared matching pool for paying users too, and its closing largely recapped rather than engaging B's rebuttal that paywalled verification is an imperfect barrier regardless (undercutting the "fake-document flooding" defense). B's pressure-question answers (A1/A2) were more responsive and offered concrete non-safety revenue alternatives, while A's answers restated assertions without fully resolving the tension B exposed between "basic tools stop most harm" and "losing premium revenue guts even basic tools." Overall B controlled the central comparison (who bears the cost of irreversible harm) and left CON's rebuttal structurally incomplete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 reframing of "basic vs. premium" as "the lifeboat is free but the motorized one is premium," combined with B's closing point that an imperfect, stolen-card-bypassable paywall cannot simultaneously be touted as a serious anti-fraud barrier, directly undercut CON's core revenue/fraud-prevention justification and was never fully answered.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by framing safety as network-wide infrastructure and showing why weaker protection for nonpaying users leaves the entire matching pool exposed. Side A’s funding case depended heavily on unsupported assertions that premium safety tiers are the primary moderation budget and that free verification would enable mass fake accounts. B offered plausible alternative monetization and directly answered both pressure questions, while A never established that paywalling safety was necessary rather than merely convenient.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B decisively exposed Side A’s conflation of blocking with reporting: a free user-side block does not itself flood moderation queues. B then paired abuse safeguards with alternative funding options, undercutting both the implementation and revenue objections.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the motion’s actual target—the best verification, blocking, and scam-response tiers—front and center, and by making network effects plus “who bears irreversible risk” the decisive comparison. B stayed competitive on second-order cost and queue effects, but repeatedly softened the motion into “basics already free” and asserted funding necessity more than it proved alternatives impossible, so A left the cleaner reason to prefer free-by-default best safety.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R1 collapse of B’s free-basic vs paid-enhanced distinction (selfie/capped block vs gov-ID/unlimited/priority queues), extended in R2 answers showing paywalls bar vulnerable users more than determined scammers.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built a more durable causal story: a free-by-default mandate does not conjure funding, it only removes the price signal that pays for expensive capacity (investigators, device bans, appeals), so the predictable equilibrium is cheap, spoofable compliance — a "trust halo" that can be worse than no verification at all. This point was introduced early and never squarely neutralized by PRO. PRO's case leaned heavily on the sympathetic widow example and plausible-sounding revenue figures, but its rebuttals mostly repeated "free is better than nothing" without engaging the mechanism by which mandated "free" could degrade quality or create a false sense of security. PRO's answers to pressure questions leaned on vague, under-grounded fixes ("shared verification consortia," FTC after-the-fact enforcement) that function as hope rather than a funding source or quality floor, and its Rebuttal 2 was clipped for length, a real execution cost. CON's pressure questions (funding source, enforceable quality floor) exposed PRO's structural gap, and CON's answers to PRO's questions directly addressed the "zero access" claim and the subsidy fiction without overclaiming evidence. CON's closing crystallized the clash cleanly ("investigator before the transfer, not a regulator after it"), while PRO's closing mostly recapped without resolving the trust-halo rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 point that a free-by-default mandate does not redirect funding and instead creates a "trust halo" (spoofable free verification making users feel falsely safe) was the pivotal exchange — PRO never adequately answered why mandated free tools would retain quality rather than be rationed to cheap compliance.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by separating nominally free access from adequately funded operational capacity. Its trust-halo, rationing, and niche-platform arguments remained live, while Side B never supplied a convincing funding mechanism or quality floor. Side B also relied on brittle claims about subscription revenue being pure profit, verification consortia costing pennies, and existing enforcement guaranteeing quality. Side A did not prove that current paywalls cross-subsidize free users, but answered that pressure honestly and showed that the proposition itself does not redirect funds toward safety. Side B’s clipped second rebuttal was an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to Side B’s funding case was decisive: making tools free does not itself finance investigators, appeals, or device bans, while cheap automated compliance may create false confidence. Side B’s answers—budget reallocation, inexpensive APIs, market discipline, and after-the-fact FTC enforcement—did not resolve that capacity problem.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the decisive clash on capacity versus nominal access: unpaywalling the best tools does not create investigators, device bans, or appeals staffing, and can produce cheap compliance plus a trust halo. A’s vulnerability and anti-profit framing stayed live, and A correctly pressed the weak cross-subsidy story, but A never secured a concrete funding/quality floor and lost execution points when Rebuttal 2 was clipped. B answered pressure more cleanly and crystallized the vote better in the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s trust-halo / free-does-not-conjure-capacity reply to the widow example (esp. B2.3–B2.4 and the later A1/A2 answers), which flipped “imperfect free verification is better than none” into a rationing and false-security risk.
