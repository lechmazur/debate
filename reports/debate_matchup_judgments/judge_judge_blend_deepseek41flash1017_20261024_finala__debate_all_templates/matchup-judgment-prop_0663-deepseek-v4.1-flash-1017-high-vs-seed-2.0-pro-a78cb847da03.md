### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built the debate around a sharp, well-defined clash: does stronger verification access deter the first confidential report, or only deter obstruction after a credible signal? CON's chilling-effect mechanism was intuitive and consistently argued, but after PRO introduced the confidential-channel/public-obstruction-marker distinction, CON mostly reasserted "there is no clean line" rather than directly dismantling the refined threshold (weeks of refused access vs. a few days of confirmation). PRO's answers to pressure questions were more directly responsive: it conceded that any public label will read as alarming (A1) but turned that concession into a reason the label should be reserved for actual obstruction, and it gave a concrete answer to how WHO would learn of outbreaks absent the first official call (clinicians, labs, neighboring states, media). CON's answers to PRO's pressure questions were valid but more evasive on the comparative safety question (Q2), largely repeating its own case instead of engaging PRO's "visible stall vs. invisible stall" framing. PRO's closing crystallized the comparison most cleanly ("an alarm that can reveal a covered fire vs. one whose only operator is the person covering it"), while CON's closing repeated its central claim as "unrefuted" without fully grappling with PRO's weeks-vs-days distinction. This gives PRO a clear, if not overwhelming, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 answer in Rebuttal 2 ("Yes—any public WHO label of delay will be read as alarming... That is precisely why it must be reserved for obstruction, not uncertainty") was the most decisive moment: it converted CON's strongest pressure point into a structural justification for PRO's design, and CON's closing never directly rebutted this reframing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by systematically neutralizing Side A's core offense. While Side A argued persuasively that a strict verification regime would deter governments from making the initial confidential report, Side B successfully pointed out that the WHO also relies on alternative outbreak signals (clinicians, labs, media). Side A failed to adapt to this point, repeating in the closing that the government's call is the "only signal." Side B also correctly identified that Side A's model offers no solution for a state burying an outbreak after making an initial private report.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 successfully bit the bullet on the alarm nature of a delay notice while devastating Side A's "first call" monopoly by listing alternative ways the WHO learns about outbreaks.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A built a much stronger and more nuanced case by clearly distinguishing between confidential preliminary reporting and public verification obstruction. Side B relied heavily on the absolutist claim that states would never report early signals, which Side A effectively countered by pointing out alternative signal sources and highlighting that Side B's model allows states to use the initial grace period to indefinitely suppress outbreaks without external trace. Side A's weighing of failure modes—imperfect but visible third-party judgment versus state-controlled invisibility—was decisive and logically sound.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they successfully defended the line between reasonable delay and obstruction by arguing that imperfect, contestable third-party judgment is fundamentally safer than leaving the decision entirely to the state under scrutiny, effectively neutralizing Side B's core "no clean line" objection.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON built a sharper, more sustained mechanism argument: that mandatory verification does not remove the first-mover's existing costs (travel bans, stigma) but adds a new one (loss of narrative control), so the incentive to delay or keep evidence informal survives or worsens under the proposed rule. PRO never squarely rebutted this asymmetry, instead reasserting that "uniform" rules remove penalty without addressing why the first country to be verified still absorbs disproportionate real-world costs. CON also landed a durable point that a public "verification ongoing" notice functions as a de facto announcement, directly undercutting PRO's claim that sovereignty over the first message is preserved — PRO's answer that this is "neutral" was asserted rather than defended. The pressure-question exchange favored CON: PRO's answer (A1) conceded "enforcement is not the point," a concession CON exploited cleanly in both rebuttal and closing. PRO's strongest asset — concrete historical failures (Ebola, Covid, mpox) and the "bodies vs. hypotheticals" framing — was rhetorically powerful but did not engage the specific incentive-mechanism critique, and PRO's early "72-hour delay doubles deaths" claim edges toward brittle false precision. CON's closing crystallized the clash more precisely ("you cannot verify data frightened institutions never generate"), while PRO's closing mostly reasserted its burden-of-proof framing without resolving the live mechanism objection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 (A2.1–A2.4) showing that uniform verification duty does not erase the first-mover's real-world costs and that a public verification notice is read as confirmation, which PRO never fully neutralized in later rounds.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's mechanism. While Side B effectively highlighted the massive status quo harms of delayed reporting, Side A successfully proved that stripping narrative control would only incentivize earlier concealment since material consequences like travel bans would still fall on the reporting state.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal, where they used Side B's concession that enforcement 'is not the point' to prove that access fundamentally relies on cooperation, which the proposed loss of messaging control would destroy.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's causal mechanism. While Side A relied heavily on the historical toll of the status quo and asserted that uniform rules would prevent hiding, Side B convincingly argued that constraining national messaging control would incentivize local officials to keep early signals informal and undocumented. Side B's core thesis—"you cannot verify data frightened institutions never generate"—directly neutralized Side A's reliance on WHO's new legal access, showing that access is useless without underlying data generation. Side B also effectively handled the pressure questions, admitting the novelty of the policy while grounding the incentive shifts in observable behavior, whereas Side A's dismissal of market reactions to "verification ongoing" notices felt logically incomplete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it directly answered Side A's pressure questions by exposing the flaw in "uniform" rules (consequences remain unequal for the first reporter) and explaining why a "neutral" verification notice is practically read as confirmation by markets, thereby collapsing Side A's distinction between verification and announcement.
