### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and followed format cleanly, including proper A1/A2 compliance. CON's strongest structural move was the reversibility asymmetry: privacy losses can be repaired, security failures (death, trafficking) cannot. But PRO's pressure question directly targeted this claim, forcing CON to concede it could not name a single modern democracy that has fully rolled back bulk surveillance once granted. That concession gutted CON's central weighing mechanism, since it showed privacy losses from security overreach are, empirically, not reliably reversible either. PRO capitalized on this decisively in closing, framing it as "the whole debate," while CON's closing pivoted to reframing PRO's position as having "retreated" to a shared no-bulk-surveillance stance rather than directly repairing the reversibility damage. CON's parallel point about privacy shielding private predators (abusers, traffickers, landlords) remained live but was answered adequately enough ("investigate and target, not bulk dragnet") to avoid being fatal. On balance, PRO's pressure question did more real work, exposed a genuine contradiction in the opponent's core claim, and PRO used the resulting concession more effectively than CON recovered from it, giving PRO the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question asking CON to name a democracy that fully rolled back bulk surveillance, and CON's forced concession ("No, I cannot name one"), which undercut CON's central reversibility argument and was leveraged hard in PRO's closing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): A won a competitive debate by making the free-society ranking turn on irreversibility and accountability: B openly conceded no modern democracy fully rolls back bulk surveillance, which left B’s “good security under law” fix looking wishful once privacy is already surrendered. Side A capitalized on this in the closing, proving that the loss of privacy is a "one-way door" that destroys the very accountability mechanisms Side B relied upon. Furthermore, Side A successfully dismantled Side B's pressure questions by distinguishing between targeted, warranted investigations (which A accepts) and permanent bulk surveillance, exposing B's reliance on emotional edge-cases to justify systemic overreach.
