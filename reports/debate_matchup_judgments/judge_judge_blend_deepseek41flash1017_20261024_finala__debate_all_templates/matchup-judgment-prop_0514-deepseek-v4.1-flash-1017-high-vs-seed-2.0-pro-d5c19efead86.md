### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built vivid, plausible mechanisms—CON's tacit-knowledge/checklist-capture story and PRO's renewal-leverage story—but PRO did more of the comparative work demanded by the format. PRO explicitly narrowed the motion (not banning suites, not demanding zero switching cost), turned CON's small-vendor argument around with a clear causal mechanism (published interfaces let locals bid without begging the incumbent), and gave a workable, honestly hedged answer to the pressure-question demand for a threshold ("migrate core data within one budget cycle without incumbent permission") rather than retreating into vague assertion. CON's strongest rhetorical lines ("you cannot order someone to care," "two kinds of lock-in") were memorable but rested on sweeping, unsupported generalizations about how procurement always works, and CON's closing restated the lock-in-is-inevitable framing without directly engaging PRO's sharpened "who controls the renewal" question. PRO's progression across rounds was tighter: each speech answered the live point and re-centered the debate on a single decisive comparison, which the judging frame rewards.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 turn on small vendors ([B2.4]: local integrators can build to a published interface rather than begging the incumbent) directly inverted CON's strongest structural claim and was never fully neutralized, since CON's later rebuttals reasserted checklist-capture without addressing why open interfaces specifically disadvantage locals more than proprietary APIs do.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by distinguishing a preference for tested portability from a maximal interoperability mandate. It showed that an integrated prime contractor can remain accountable while core data, configurations, and interfaces stay transferable. Side A raised a meaningful concern about tacit workflows and compliance burdens, but relied on sweeping claims that every preference becomes rigid and that vendor care requires costly exit. It also repeatedly attacked portability being weighted above operational fitness, which the proposition did not require. Side B gave the cleaner comparative: portability need not eliminate all switching costs to preserve meaningful leverage at renewal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange exposed that Side A’s promised vendor care depended on customers being costly to leave, then answered the accountability objection by retaining one responsible prime contractor while requiring a documented exit. Side A’s concession that export and transition terms were individually acceptable further weakened its categorical opposition.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): PRO kept the motion as a preference for documented interfaces, export, and transition assistance compatible with a prime contractor, making renewal leverage the decisive clash. CON never solved who controls renewal and conceded quality tied to lock-in, while PRO's fire-exit weighing survived.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2 turning the 120 customizations and small-vendor claim — arguing tacit tweaks are where lock-in hides and that open standards remove the incumbent veto, preserving accountability while bounding exit cost.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Both sides opened strong, but the decisive clash came down to the mechanism question: does a procurement "preference" actually deliver portability, or do enforceable contract terms (live migration drills, tested exports, penalties, accountable prime vendor) do the real work regardless of architecture? CON pressed this from the opening and never let go. PRO's Pressure Questions were sharp, but its own answer in Rebuttal 2 narrowed "favor" to a mere tiebreaker among "equally qualified" bids and conceded that accountability failures ("no one is accountable") are generic to enterprise IT, not specific to locked stacks. CON exploited both concessions directly: tie-breakers are exactly where hidden integration risk is least visible to scorers, and if accountability is a universal problem, PRO's architecture preference doesn't solve it while still saddling under-resourced buyers with integration and blame-diffusion costs. PRO's closing reasserted the vendor-asymmetry narrative forcefully but never answered this specific damage, leaving CON's final reframe ("the mechanism that buys portability beats the one that hopes architecture delivers it") as the last word on the core clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3-A4.4, where CON turns PRO's own "tiebreaker only" and "no one is accountable" concessions from Rebuttal 2 into evidence that the preference neither targets the real risk nor delivers the protection PRO claims, directly undercutting PRO's unanswered closing argument.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by distinguishing tested portability from an architectural label. Its live migration tests, defined exports, transition assistance, and prime-vendor accountability directly targeted lock-in while preserving the operational advantages of integrated systems. Side B never adequately resolved the integration burden or accountability problem and relied heavily on unsupported universals such as contracts failing every time. Its late claim that favoring open standards was merely a tie-breaker also narrowed the proposition and strengthened A's point that nominally equal bids can hide materially different integration risks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both pressure questions directly, distinguished boilerplate exit clauses from enforceable migration testing, and showed that Side B's tie-breaker could reward standards compliance without proving real portability or continuity.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): CON won the weighing. PRO relied on fatalism that contracts always fail, but CON distinguished boilerplate from enforceable portability (live drills, defined schemas, priced transition) and shifted cost to low-capacity buyers: integration, patching, and blame-diffusion. PRO's answers to pressure hurt its case — conceding "No one is accountable" on multi-vendor failure [A4.3] and narrowing "favor" to only a tie-breaker [A4.1] — while CON answered PRO's historical-scandal question directly. Label vs leverage framing made the mechanism that buys portability beat the architecture that hopes for it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B R2 A1/A2 - reframed scandals as enforcement failures not proof contracts can't work, and exposed PRO's accountability concession on four-component blame, turning PRO's lock-in story into a case for testable portability rather than a standards label.
