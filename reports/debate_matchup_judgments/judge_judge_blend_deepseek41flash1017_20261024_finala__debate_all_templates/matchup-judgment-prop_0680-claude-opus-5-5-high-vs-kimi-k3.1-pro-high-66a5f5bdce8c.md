### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by exploiting the proposition’s categorical breadth. Side B compellingly established that arrest-driven systems can create discriminatory feedback loops, but repeatedly generalized that mechanism to all incident-based models. Side A showed that a model using serious victim-reported incidents would remain covered by the ban even if independently validated, auditable, and shown not to increase stops. Side B’s response largely declared every measurement compromised and incorrectly suggested that external validation would move such a system outside the motion. Side A also answered both pressure questions directly and honestly. Side B had stronger imagery and grounded its case in present harms, but did not justify prohibiting the constrained successful case rather than regulating for it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s decisive rebuttal was that excluding enforcement-sensitive arrests does not remove a system from the motion when historical serious-incident data remain its primary inputs. This defeated Side B’s definitional escape and forced it to defend banning even an independently validated model; its answer that all validation was compromised was too sweeping and did not overcome Side A’s external-testing comparison.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully arguing that the structural flaws in the data create a self-confirming loop, and by forcing Side B to admit their defense relies entirely on an unproven hypothetical. Side A effectively weaponized Side B's concessions about data blind spots to show that any system relying primarily on such data cannot reliably correct its own bias.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing brilliantly capitalized on the Pressure Questions, using Side B's inability to name a successful real-world example and admission about unseen crime to collapse B's hypothetical defense.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): CON wins the overbreadth clash. PRO’s strongest loop—data sees policing, prediction confirms itself—was met with a concrete regulatory alternative: exclude enforcement-sensitive offenses, separate victim reports, demand disclosure, phased trials against independent victimization/medical data, and reject models that merely mirror patrols. PRO’s attempt to define that constrained model out of the motion fails, so PRO must justify banning even that version, but never identifies residual harm beyond distrust of measurement, while CON openly admits limits (no city example, no method sees unseen crime) and shows why prohibition adds cost beyond rejecting failures. That leaves PRO defending possibility as impossibility.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 admission-plus-reframe on Q2: conceding no system sees fully unreported crime, then showing hotspot maps and resident-driven deployment share that limit, and that independent validation breaks circularity—undermining B’s closing claim that CON’s success relies on outside data.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that the categorical ban also captures transparent systems limited to serious victim-reported violence, while leaving patrols, coercive encounters, underreporting, and human reliance on the same history intact. Side B established real risks from biased inputs but did not justify prohibiting the regulated counterexample or explain why less explicit human ranking would improve outcomes. Repeated clipping modestly hurt A's execution but did not obscure its decisive case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A most decisively exposed Side B's distinction between a spreadsheet that generates recommendations and one that merely records operational decisions: the same incident data can produce the same patrol deployment, but the ban removes the more explicit and testable process. - **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically demonstrating that Side A's proposed alternatives would rely on the exact same historical data and result in the same patrols, but with less transparency. Side B successfully neutralized Side A's feedback-loop argument by focusing on victim-reported violent crimes, which Side A conceded weakened the loop.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal, which capitalized on Side A's admission that a spreadsheet recording human decisions is allowed but one generating recommendations is banned, proving the ban targets transparency rather than the actual data or patrols.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON won the central comparison: a ban removes the auditable ranking, not the patrols, stops, or historical-data reliance. PRO conceded the arrest feedback loop is substantially weakened for victim-reported serious violence yet still demands prohibition, and could not sustain the distinction between a spreadsheet that generates recommendations versus one that records a reviewable decision based on the same shootings data. PRO's precautionary burden — prove added prevention before deployment — was turned by CON's point that every night is already an unmeasured experiment and a ban makes measurement permanent uncertainty.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's turn on B2.1/B4.1 — documented operational needs are historical incident data, so the prohibition hides the method rather than the history, leaving coercion while removing accountability.
