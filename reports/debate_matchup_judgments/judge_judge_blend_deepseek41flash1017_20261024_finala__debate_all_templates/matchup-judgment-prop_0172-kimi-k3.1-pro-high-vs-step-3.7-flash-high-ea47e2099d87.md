### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO built a coherent precautionary framework from the start: learning is a trigger for scaled review, not proof of sentience, and the "same frameworks" clause was explicitly defined as proportional procedure (necessity, harm minimization, endpoints) rather than identical treatment to a mouse. This let PRO absorb CON's "category error" attack by conceding that no sentience is being asserted while insisting that uncertainty itself is the reason review-type scrutiny is needed. CON's strongest material was the sentience-foundation argument and the RL-algorithm analogy, but CON's defense collapsed into circularity under pressure: when asked what safeguard assesses harm to the organoid itself, CON answered "there is no such harm to assess," which denies the premise of the question rather than answering it—exactly the move PRO had already flagged as question-begging. PRO's pressure-question answers, by contrast, offered a concrete (if modest) evidentiary marker (reproducible adaptation plus aversion-like responses) for scaling scrutiny, which is a more substantive response than CON's restatement that falsification "would require evidence of a subjective perspective" without specifying what that would look like. PRO's closing crystallized this into a clean asymmetry (bounded cost of false alarm vs. unexamined risk of missed harm) that CON's closing never directly rebutted, instead recapping the category-error claim. The clipped opening is a real execution cost for PRO, but it did not prevent PRO from fully developing and defending its framework in later rounds, where the decisive clash was won.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing (A5.2) explicitly naming CON's "there is no morally relevant harm to assess" as circular, following CON's evasive answer to PRO's Q2 pressure question about what safeguard examines organoid-specific harm—CON never recovered from this in its own closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B won by distinguishing a trigger for proportionate review from proof of sentience or identical treatment. Side A repeatedly asserted that organoids categorically cannot have welfare interests, but did not adequately ground that certainty and relied on speculative bureaucratic costs. B's clipped opening was an execution cost, yet its later answers clearly resolved the central uncertainty and proportionality clashes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange exposed A's circularity: A said no safeguard assesses organoid welfare because no welfare is possible, while B argued that review is precisely the mechanism for testing that contested premise. B then weighed bounded scrutiny against the risk of entirely unreviewed suffering.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO wins on precautionary asymmetry and scalable-framework reframe. CON asserts organoids cannot have welfare but never identifies a safeguard that assesses harm to the organoid itself, conceding Q2 with circularity. PRO's answers that learning triggers review rather than proving sentience, plus low-cost false alarm vs high-cost missed suffering, survive both pressure rounds and neutralize delays via proportionality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's R2 pressure and R2 answers exposing that CON's "no existing safeguard because no harm" begs the question, combined with the light-touch vs categorical-exemption cost comparison in A4-A5.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): CON built the more durable case by cleanly separating "learning" from "capacity to suffer" and by insisting the motion demands the specific animal-experimentation apparatus, not generic precaution. The decisive exchange came in Pressure Questions: CON asked PRO to name a framework that treats rudimentary learning as a trigger and that actually covers C. elegans/Drosophila. PRO's answer retreated to vague, unverifiable claims ("nearly all US research institutions," an invented-sounding EU "any invertebrate with noxious stimulus response" rule) and explicitly conceded "no major framework uses rudimentary learning as a sole automatic trigger" — a concession CON correctly flagged as fatal to PRO's central analogy, since the motion itself proposes that exact trigger. CON also pressed that once housing, husbandry, anesthesia, and euthanasia are stripped from "tiered" animal review, what's left is just generic independent review wearing an animal label — PRO's claim that "statutory harm-benefit authority" is distinctively animal-based was answered adequately by noting such authority can simply be granted to a new body. PRO's case also suffered execution costs: two rebuttal speeches were clipped for exceeding word limits, cutting off argument development. CON's answers to pressure questions were direct, properly labeled, and honest about what it did and didn't concede, while avoiding the brittle fake-precision PRO leaned on.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Questions (Q1 challenging the invertebrate/learning-trigger analogy) followed by PRO's weak, overclaiming A1 answer, which CON then used in Rebuttal 2 and Closing as a "decisive retreat" that undercut PRO's central functional-equivalence argument.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by separating the need for precautionary oversight from the claim that animal-experimentation frameworks are the appropriate machinery. Side B repeatedly mischaracterized A as opposing oversight and relied on sweeping, poorly grounded claims that standard frameworks generally cover fruit flies and nematodes based on learning capacity. Its eventual admission that no major framework uses rudimentary learning as the automatic trigger substantially undercut its claimed precedent. A also showed that the transferable protections are generic review powers that an organoid-specific framework could possess, while the distinctively animal provisions do not translate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s pressure question forced Side B to admit that no major framework actually uses rudimentary learning as the sole trigger. A then turned that concession against the motion and exposed the claimed invertebrate precedent as inconsistent and insufficiently grounded.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): CON won the core clash: learning is not valenced experience, and animal review is species/vertebrate-triggered, not learning-triggered. Pressure Q1 forced PRO to concede in [A4.1] that no major framework uses learning as sole trigger, collapsing the precedent claim and loophole argument. B's "distinctive parts don't translate / translatable parts aren't distinctive" and enforcement point left PRO with generic review in an animal label, while CON offered targeted precaution without factual overreach. A also suffered clipping costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.5/B4.3 – showing the animal framework's distinctive welfare tools don't apply and PRO's admission that no framework uses learning as trigger, turning PRO's invertebrate analogy and loophole claim into a concession that decides the motion.
