### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): B won a clear but competitive debate. A sharply exposed consent's insufficiency and the difficulty of defining harvesting, but B established non-consensual mass profiling as an independent autonomy harm and explained why an upstream burden reduces opaque stockpiling. A's substantive safeguards could coexist with opt-in, so they did not negate the proposition. A's clipped opening and B's stronger framing also mattered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's decisive response was that opt-in need not replace bans on discrimination, insecurity, or coercion: it adds an upstream gate before opaque profiles are accumulated. This blunted A's doorway-versus-conduct framing, although A effectively noted that consent validation also requires auditing.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won the definitional and remedy clash. PRO (B) made a strong autonomy and burden-shifting case with the mobile-prompt example, but CON exposed that B's narrowing to cross-context/intimate profiling isn't in the motion, and B4's expanded test confirms overbreadth. The decisive move was A4's audit-equivalence: if regulators can verify specific, uncoerced consent for every profile, they can verify minimisation, purpose limits, recipients and security; plus a valid opt-in still does not cure discrimination or breach risk, while benign large-scale uses are presumptively condemned. That left PRO defending a gateway rather than harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.5: CON's answer to opacity – consent verification needs the same documentation/audit capacity as conduct rules, so the presumption adds paperwork not visibility – plus the symmetry defect that consented discrimination remains lawful under PRO's test.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side A built a more cohesive and practically grounded case, effectively framing the debate around the operational impossibility of downstream enforcement ("auditing shadows") and the necessity of an upstream gate to shift the evidentiary burden. While Side B made strong theoretical points about the inadequacy of consent to cure harms like discrimination and the overbreadth of the motion's text, B struggled to overcome A's practical critiques of conduct-based regulation in opaque, real-time auction ecosystems. Side A's rhetoric was sharper, and Side B suffered a minor execution cost from a clipped opening.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which effectively turned Side B's "consent doesn't cure harm" argument by emphasizing that an opt-in requirement establishes non-consensual mass surveillance as an independent harm and shifts the evidentiary burden, while exposing the contradiction in B's reliance on platforms to self-police under vague conduct rules.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B won by consistently distinguishing a consent requirement from a ban and explaining why diffuse, inferential harms make collection-first remedies inadequate. Its browser opt-out alternative still left nonconsensual exposure as the default. B’s clipped first rebuttal was a minor execution cost, and it never fully eliminated the practical incumbent advantage, keeping the debate competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange answered the proposed browser-level opt-out: deletion and downstream liability may supplement protection, but they neither establish prior authorization nor solve opaque, cumulative profiling. This directly preserved B’s central burden-shifting case against A’s main regulatory alternative.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO maintained a live burden-shifting frame — profiler must prove explicit opt-in — and neutralized CON's central monopoly/entrenchment route by showing a genuine opt-in standard invalidates blanket login bundling for giants too. PRO's pressure questions exposed that gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.3/B4.1 turning the walled-garden claim: logged-in account ≠ consent to unlimited surveillance, so presumptive unlawfulness hurts incumbents equally and revenue loss reflects withheld permission, not confiscated entitlement.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A built a stronger, more cohesive case by consistently framing the debate around the burden of proof and the definition of genuine consent. Side A's answers to the pressure questions were direct and philosophically sharp, whereas Side B's answers largely restated the friction problem without resolving the normative clash. Side A's closing crystallized the legal and ethical stakes memorably.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's monopoly and paywall arguments by asserting that contextual advertising's lower payout establishes "at most, that surveillance is lucrative—not that it is legitimate," and that a publisher's financial needs do not entitle it to build a dossier on a transient visitor.
