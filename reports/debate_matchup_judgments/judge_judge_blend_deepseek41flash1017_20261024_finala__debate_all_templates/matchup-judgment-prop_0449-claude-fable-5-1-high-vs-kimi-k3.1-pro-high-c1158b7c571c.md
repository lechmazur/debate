### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): B won narrowly by showing that A's alternative lacked a credible response to deliberate free-riding: revised plans, support, and lost incentives do not sufficiently deter new coal investment. B also answered the blackout dilemma coherently by allowing continued operation subject to graduated penalties while emphasizing that credible enforcement changes investment years before 2040. A powerfully argued that sanctions cannot themselves build replacement capacity and exposed B's reliance on finance and differentiation not guaranteed by the motion, keeping the debate close. However, A never fully explained how its preferred framework would secure timely compliance from unwilling governments, and its clipped second rebuttal imposed a modest execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure question forced A to specify what happens after a missed country-specific deadline. A's answer of a revised plan, technical support, and lost incentives allowed B to crystallize the comparison: one regime gives planners enforceable reasons not to build coal now, while the other remains vulnerable to delay.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully proving that a strict deadline with penalties addresses a lack of political will but fails to address a lack of capability. Side B effectively forced Side A to concede that unprepared countries would keep burning coal while paying penalties, which B persuasively argued would worsen their finances without building clean replacements.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 response to A's "graduated penalties" scenario, pointing out that taxing a country for noncompliance due to a lack of capacity simply worsens its finances without building the missing clean infrastructure.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO built a highly persuasive case around ex-ante investment certainty, successfully arguing that hard deadlines and penalties change the calculus for grid planners today. PRO decisively won the clash over alternatives by exposing CON's "country-specific deadlines" as toothless when pressed, culminating in CON's concession that missing a deadline merely results in a "revised plan." While CON made strong points about physical grid bottlenecks and the unfairness of ex-post punishment, PRO's framing of the penalty as a deterrent that prevents the bottleneck from being ignored in the first place was the cleaner and more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's exploitation of CON's "revised plan" answer in the closing, which perfectly crystallized why CON's alternative fails to deter new coal investments compared to the motion's hard deadline.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A won by making enforcement, not coal’s harms, the decisive issue. It showed that penalties credible enough to change behavior could drain transition capacity, provoke retaliation, deter participation, and fall most readily on weaker states while remaining uncertain against China. Side B persuasively argued that conditional finance lacks a backstop, but never established that its backstop would retire more coal than it obstructed. Side A’s closing crystallized this comparison especially well. Side B’s repeated clipping also imposed an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s concrete tariff answer was met by Side A’s strongest exchange: tariffs on carbon-intensive exports would reduce the same export earnings needed for replacement infrastructure, while enforcement would be weakest against the most powerful coal user. That directly undermined the claimed value of enforceable penalties.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically deconstructing Side A's enforcement mechanism, successfully arguing that binding penalties would either fail to be ratified by coal-dependent nations or actively destroy the capital they need to transition. Side B's responses to the pressure questions were highly effective in turning Side A's specific examples against them.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal, where they turned Side A's concrete example of a tariff on steel and aluminum by pointing out it drains the exact export earnings needed to import clean technology.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling the practical mechanics of Side B's proposed penalties. A effectively demonstrated that severe trade penalties (like export tariffs) would drain the very capital developing nations need for the transition, and that the regime would inevitably result in selective enforcement—punishing weaker nations while failing to constrain major emitters like China. Side B's normative argument for a "backstop" was strong, but they could not adequately defend the economic and geopolitical realities of their enforcement mechanism against A's sharp, concrete rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in Rebuttal 2 to Side B's proposed carbon tariff, pointing out that export earnings from steel and aluminum are exactly what developing nations use to import clean technology, thereby proving the penalty is self-defeating.
