### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued a genuinely difficult, abstract economic question with discipline and without fake precision, but CON (A) did the more effective work on the motion's actual burden: establishing that net white-collar job loss is "likely," not merely possible. A repeatedly forced PRO to distinguish plausibility from likelihood and policy-in-general from forecast-specific preparation, and PRO's final position (a "demanding threshold" for offset, "capped contingency funding") concedes much of that ground without closing it. A's strongest unanswered move was the symmetry charge: PRO discounted new-entrant hiring as "not all net additions" due to customer redistribution, but never consistently applied the same redistribution logic to incumbent layoffs counting as net losses—B's closing reply ("moving customers... is not itself replacement employment") asserts rather than resolves this asymmetry. PRO's case was coherent and its "both sides of the ledger" mechanism was a reasonable structural point, but it never escaped the core evidentiary gap CON kept exposing. A's case suffered a real execution cost from two clipped speeches (losing the ends of its opening and first rebuttal), which slightly tempers the argument and ground scores, but the surviving content still carried the decisive comparative points through to the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's repeated redistribution-symmetry challenge (A2.1–A2.5, sharpened in A4.4–A4.5): if PRO discounts new-firm hires because of customer redistribution, consistency requires discounting incumbent job losses on the same ground—a charge PRO never squarely dissolved.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): CON won by holding PRO to the motion’s “likely net” standard. PRO built a strong, clean mechanism (leaner teams, AI on both incumbents and entrants, nonlabor costs limiting pass-through) showing displacement and that offset is not automatic, but repeatedly stopped at “the hurdle is demanding” without an economy-wide reason the expansion/entry/quality channels fail together. CON’s inconsistent-accounting and policy points landed: redistribution caution must cut both ways, and staged adaptable capacity works under many forecasts, so the unproven net-loss claim does little work. Clipped B turns cost some polish, but the burden clash still favors B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.4–B4.6 / B5.2: PRO discounts entrant hires as redistribution while treating every consolidated role as net destruction, and “demanding offset” is not evidence net loss is likely.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON wins a close clash by turning PRO's mechanism into an incomplete comparison. PRO shows falling labor per unit and that new firms also use lean AI staffing, establishing that offset is demanding. CON credibly counters that this proves possibility not likelihood: zero-to-some markets can add jobs even with lean staffing, and PRO inconsistently discounts entrant hires for redistribution while counting every consolidated role as net loss. On policy, CON separates adaptable infrastructure, which is worthwhile under any forecast, from a forecast-specific preparedness margin that PRO never quantifies. Clipping is an execution cost for A, but A1/A2 answers and weighing are sharper.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1/A4.4 zero-baseline and inconsistent accounting reply – new AI-lean entrants add from zero vs old model and redistribution must be applied to both creation and destruction, which undercuts B's core economy-wide comparison.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON (A) ran the more disciplined argument: it repeatedly forced PRO to justify why substitution should count as the "baseline" while demand-expansion and decentralized creation counted only as "conditional," and PRO never supplied that ranking beyond assertion (e.g., "direct, repeatable substitution... is the stronger baseline"). CON's sharpest and most decisive move was decoupling the policy prescription from the likelihood claim: since the motion conjunctively requires both "AI is likely to eliminate more jobs" and "policy should prepare early," CON could concede early preparation (wage insurance, portable benefits, reporting) while still denying the forecast, which neutralizes PRO's asymmetric-risk/insurance framing ("the usefulness of insurance is not evidence that the insured outcome is likely"). PRO's rebuttals (concentrated accountability, lean AI-native startups, timing-gap urgency) were substantive and kept pressure on, but PRO never cleanly answered CON's repeated "neither side gets to count its preferred mechanism while discounting the other as merely conditional" critique, and PRO's two clipped speeches (opening and Rebuttal 2) cost it some completeness and polish. The pressure-question exchange was handled competently by both with proper A1/A2 formatting, but CON's follow-up question ("which specific policy requires accepting your net-loss forecast") exposed a real gap in PRO's case that PRO's answer did not fully close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's closing line that "the usefulness of insurance is not evidence that the insured outcome is likely" directly dismantled PRO's central asymmetric-risk argument, which had been the backbone of PRO's case since Rebuttal 1's "timing gap" framing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the decisive burden on “more … than,” forcing A to show economy-wide net loss rather than vivid displacement, and by cleanly separating early worker protections from the contested forecast. A’s direct-substitution baseline and junior-rung/pathway points were sharp and rhetorically sticky, but never completed a scale comparison; B’s repeated weighing that both mechanisms are conditional, that leaner firms can coexist with more firms, and that wage insurance/reporting remain useful even if employment grows, neutralized A’s live route. Clipping costs on A and B’s clearer crystallizing of “more than” vs insurance-as-proof sealed a competitive but real edge for CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B turning A’s concession that transition measures still help if creation prevails into a burden kill: useful insurance does not establish that net white-collar destruction is likely (B2.7/B4.7/B5.4).
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO kept the core clash focused: substitution is the direct purchase rationale with scalable deployment, while offsetting white-collar hiring requires additional customers, financing and demand that must repeatedly outrun productivity gains. CON showed offsets are possible and supported early mobility/benefits reforms, but conceded in A4.1 it cannot rank likelihoods from incentives alone, and its decentralized-creation and accountable-worker points were answered by concentration of responsibility and lean AI-native startups. With both sides favoring early preparation, PRO's asymmetry plus insurance framing — prepare now because aggregates lag and rebuilding ladders takes years — gave the cleaner path to the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B in R1/R2 on accountability and expansion hurdles: senior review concentrates rather than replicates jobs, lean startups and internal-admin savings do not automatically restore employment, and expansion faces extra conditions beyond cost-cutting, directly undercutting CON's demand-expansion mechanism after B's Q1 pressure.
