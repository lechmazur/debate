### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): PRO won the decisive clash: livability harms (noise, lunging, allergies) are behavioral and already addressed by the same enforcement tools under either rule, while bans mainly exclude compliant owners and fail against hidden pets. CON’s “proactive shield” and financial-gap lines never neutralized that comparison or the certain human cost of blanket exclusion. Clipping hurt polish on both sides, but A’s pressure questions and A1/A2 answers narrowed the dispute cleanly; B stayed more repetitive and left the dander/visitor and silent-cat traps under-answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s A1/A2 on unreachable owners and lost sleep (car-alarm / progressive lease enforcement; insurance was never the noise remedy) plus the allergy trap: bans cannot deliver the clean building CON promised once dander arrives via visitors, vents, and hidden pets.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won the central comparison. CON's case was that insurance cannot cover noise, allergies, fear. PRO answered that those are behavior-based harms handled by nuisance rules, leash rules, and pet-free floors under either policy, so they don't distinguish ban vs default-allow. PRO's pressure Qs exposed that a ban must also ban the silent cat to stop one barker and cannot deliver allergy-free buildings if dander rides on clothing/visitors. CON never answered that, while PRO honestly conceded insurance doesn't pay for sleep and showed bans leave hidden, uncovered pets with less leverage. That enforcement equivalence + registration advantage gave PRO the cleaner path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 enforcement-equivalence move: barking/lunging is already policed by warnings/fines/eviction under both rules, insurance only needs to cover damage, and a ban only reliably excludes careful owners who would have posted deposits, leaving hidden pets worse.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B built a much stronger and more logically sound case. Side A's central premise—that blanket bans act as a "proactive shield" against noise and allergies—was thoroughly dismantled by Side B, who correctly pointed out that bans do not physically prevent secret pets from causing disruptions, and that nuisance enforcement is the actual mechanism for resolving noise in both scenarios. Side B's analogies (car alarms, human noise like drummers) and sharp pressure questions effectively exposed the flaws in Side A's reliance on a reactive vs. proactive framing. Side A struggled to overcome the reality that a lease clause cannot stop a hidden dog from barking, leaving Side B with the decisive advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they decisively crushed Side A's "proactive shield" argument by pointing out that a ban is just a lease clause and the enforcement mechanism (warnings, fines, eviction) is exactly the same for a secret pet in a no-pet building as it is for a registered pet in a default-allow building.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): CON won the decisive clash: deposits/insurance only protect landlord property risk, which owners already can accept voluntarily, while the default still imposes uncompensated neighbor externalities (noise, dander, bites) and erodes pet-free stock. PRO repeatedly conceded the mechanism targets only landlord damage and that neighbor issues are “separate,” which undercut the warrant for changing the default. PRO’s “opt out with cause” and standardization/flexibility answers stayed internally unstable under pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s R2/closing on PRO’s admission that deposits address only landlord property, not neighbors—so the motion adds nothing for the party it protects while removing choice from everyone else.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by keeping focus on third-party harms the deposit cannot fix. Opening [A1.2] shifted the metric from landlord floors to neighbors' sleep, allergies, and safety, and PRO conceded it in R2: "Deposits address landlord property damage, not neighbor disturbances" [B4.2]. CON's pressure question on that tradeoff and on the fixed-vs-flexible standard contradiction [A3.1][A3.2] was answered weakly, while PRO's own Qs were handled directly. With a clipped opening, PRO never closed the gap: admit the mechanism only helps landlords, plus note market already supplies pet-friendly units, leaves no warrant for a mandatory default that erodes pet-free choice.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON in R1 [A2.1-A2.4] and R2 [A4.5-A4.6] exposing that PRO's defense abandoned its mechanism — noise to ordinances, bite to liability, dander to "cooking odors" — and then highlighting the internal contradiction between "same rules, same coverage" [B1.3] and "raise deposits/demand supplemental" [B2.2], forcing PRO to concede the deposit only protects one party.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A decisively won by exposing the fatal flaw in Side B's mechanism. Side B argued that deposits and insurance justify a default allowance of pets, but Side A effectively demonstrated that these financial tools only protect the landlord's property, leaving neighbors to bear the externalities of noise, allergies, and bites. Side A forced Side B to concede that neighbor harms are "separate" and handled by other means, completely undermining the proposition's premise that the specified financial guarantees make pets safe enough to mandate as a default. Side A also trapped Side B on the contradiction between "standardized" policies and the need for landlord discretion, delivering sharp, memorable rhetoric throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's answers to the pressure questions. Side A highlighted that a dog barking while the owner is away is fundamentally different from human noise, and exposed Side B's concession that deposits only protect the landlord, crystallizing the argument that the deposit pays for floors while the neighbors pay for the default.
