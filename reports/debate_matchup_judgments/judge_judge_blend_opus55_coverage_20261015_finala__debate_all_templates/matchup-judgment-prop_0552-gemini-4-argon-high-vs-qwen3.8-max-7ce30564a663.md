### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently and followed the format rules (including proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 structure), but PRO (B) built a more consistent causal mechanism—toxic arbitrage, where informal processors underbid certified ones wherever a poverty/regulatory gap exists—and sustained it across rounds, using it to explain both why a ban is needed and why "regional hubs" differ from "certified lower-income plants" (peer economies lack the undercutting informal sector). CON (A) raised a strong structural worry (bans push trade underground, stranding reusable goods, weakening visibility) but never fully recovered from PRO's sharpest strike: CON claimed exporters' margins are too thin to even test devices domestically, yet also claimed the same low-margin brokers could fund bonded overseas disposal, tracking, and certified treatment of hazardous residue. PRO flagged this contradiction cleanly in Rebuttal 2 and hammered it again in Closing ("cheap labor lowers the cost of unscrewing a laptop; it does not lower the capital cost of neutralizing lead and mercury"), and CON's answer (distinguishing labor-cost diagnostics from destination bonding costs) was underdeveloped and left unaddressed in CON's final closing. That unresolved contradiction, combined with PRO's tighter thematic throughline, gives PRO the more complete path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's identification of CON's cost contradiction—thin margins blocking home diagnostics but somehow funding bonded overseas disposal and tracking—first raised in Rebuttal 2 ([B4.4]) and reinforced in Closing ([B5.2]-[B5.3]), was the most decisive exchange; CON's attempted distinction in [A4.3] did not fully neutralize it and the point went unanswered in CON's final closing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO won by making the decisive clash easier to decide: the economic reason the trade exists. Across rebuttals and closing, A showed that CON’s thin-margin reuse story cannot simultaneously fund bonded tracking, safe residue disposal, and certified overseas plants; shipping mixed broken electronics is profitable mainly if toxic carcasses are dumped. That neutralized CON’s regulated-trade alternative without fake precision. CON pressed real costs—port gray areas, chilled reuse, smuggling counterfactuals—and kept a live enforcement route, but never fully answered the residue destination or the home-vs-foreign cost contradiction. A’s regional-hub vs lower-income plant distinction (comparable capacity, no informal outbidding) held better under pressure than CON’s optimism that audits alone stop arbitrage. Net: clearer comparative weighing and stronger answers to live burdens for A in a competitive debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2/closing contradiction: CON admits exporters cannot afford origin power-on testing yet claims the same low-margin shipments will fund importer bonds, serial tracking, and full safe disposal of unfixable residues abroad—collapsing CON’s accountability alternative.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO won the central enforcement/incentive clash. It kept waste vs working goods distinct, answered scale with regional hubs, and turned CON's economics: if margins are too thin to test a laptop at origin [B2.2][B4.3], they cannot fund bonds, tracking, and safe residue disposal abroad [B4.1]. CON never explained where unfixable residues go after parts-harvesting [A3.1] and could not distinguish why audits work for regional hubs but not for lower-income plants [B3.2] beyond assertion, leaving toxic arbitrage [A1.3][A4.3] live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2/A5 math contradiction: thin-margin shippers can't afford a plug-in test at home yet are claimed to afford importer bonds, serial tracking, and full hazardous-waste disposal abroad, exposing that profit depends on dumping residues to informal pits.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON (A) built and sustained a clean, repeated line of attack — "certify the facility, not the flag" — that directly targets the proposition's core design flaw: banning by national income rather than by facility safety. PRO (B) never fully resolved this. B's rebuttals shifted from "ban targets only unverifiable waste" to "leakage happens even at certified sites," and in closing effectively conceded that the real test is "proof of a safe destination," which undercuts the income-based criterion the motion itself specifies. A's pressure question (A3.2) exposed this tension cleanly, and A's rebuttal 2 turned B's leakage argument around (audited rich-country shipments vs. porous fragmented regional streams), a point B's closing did not directly answer. A also maintained a live, unanswered economic point about the cost of pre-export verification undermining B's "donor device" exception. B's moral framing (toxic harm, "if you consume it, you recycle it") was compelling early but the debate's decisive mechanism-level clash tilted toward A by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.5), which flipped B's leakage/traceability argument by claiming audited rich-country shipments are more compliance-tracked than fragmented regional flows, combined with A's pressure question (A3.2) exposing the certification-vs-income contradiction that B's closing never squarely resolved.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won a competitive debate by making the decisive policy comparison clearer: facility certification beats an income-based export ban. B repeatedly pressed the repair/salvage economics and feedstock problems PRO never fully escaped, and exposed PRO’s internal tension—trusting certified lower-income facilities for residual/regional waste while still defending a national-income red line. A kept a strong moral burden frame and a real leakage concern, but those stayed less decisive once B showed the ban also destroys reuse, risks shredding salvageable goods, and can starve the very clean capacity needed to formalize informal yards. Over the full exchange, B answered pressure more cleanly and left the easier vote: certify the facility, not the flag.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 turning A’s donor-device / residual-waste concession against PRO: if certified destinations can manage residual scrap safely, the income-based ban collapses into arbitrary geography rather than safety.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): CON won by turning PRO's concessions into a facility-not-flag test. PRO allows certified regional processing and verified refurbisher exports in A4.1, which concedes audited destination is the real safety criterion, making income-based ban arbitrary. CON showed that high-wage testing costs mean a ban shreds millions of repairable laptops and critical minerals (B2.1, B4.4), and that losing steady rich-country feedstock starves emerging formal recyclers while local burning continues (B4.3, B5.2). PRO's leakage-outside-the-fence answer in A4.3 never overcame CON's mass-balance and contract-incentive response.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B R2 A1+A2: donor-device residual waste shows if PRO trusts certified facilities to handle toxic leftovers from repair, certification collapses the case for an income ban; plus mass-balance audits and loss-of-contract incentive directly answers leakage.
