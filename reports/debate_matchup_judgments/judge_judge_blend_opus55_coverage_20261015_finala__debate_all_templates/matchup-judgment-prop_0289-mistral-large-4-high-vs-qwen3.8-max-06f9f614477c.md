### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won the central mechanism clash. It showed that guaranteeing a short response window in sparse areas necessarily requires reserved capacity or longer repositioning, while Side B merely labeled the cost an operator obligation without answering its likely incidence through higher fares, fewer vehicles, or slower service. A also gave a credible targeted alternative based on verified pickups and enforceable contracts. B established the moral stakes of exclusion, but its utility analogy remained vulnerable, its claim that subsidies consistently fail was insufficiently grounded, and its opening was clipped. The closing also cited nonexistent P reference IDs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to B’s fifteen-to-twenty-minute standard was decisive: measuring service only after requests does not eliminate the need to pre-position or reserve vehicles nearby. A then contrasted that hidden system-wide cross-subsidy with directly purchasing and enforcing specific high-need trips.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): B won by keeping the decisive trade-off live: any binding “reasonably similar reliability” floor still diverts vehicle-hours from dense paid demand into sparse zones, raising waits/fares or shrinking fleets—including for the riders PRO claims to protect. A’s infrastructure/redlining case and subsidy-failure critique were forceful, and A met the A1/A2 format, but never neutralized the capacity arithmetic or showed why internalizing the cost into a public-license duty beats targeted purchase of verified trips. B’s comparative frame—buy access, don’t shrink the network—made the vote cleaner after full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4 crushing A’s “floor not identical / just more deadheading” claim: a biteful floor requires idle presence or regulatory repositioning, not demand-chasing deadhead, so the network tax is real.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): CON won by demonstrating that the mandate carries an unavoidable network cost—vehicles held in or redirected to sparse zones reduce availability where demand is dense—and by offering a more precise alternative. PRO effectively framed mobility as a right and exposed market failure, but never squaredly answered CON’s refined subsidy model of targeted, verified access with competitive contracting. Instead, PRO kept attacking generic “subsidies that failed” without engaging why performance-based purchasing would repeat those failures. CON’s arithmetic trade-off remained live: a reliability floor in low-demand areas requires either reserved idle capacity or longer deadheads, both of which shrink effective fleet size and risk higher fares or longer waits citywide. PRO’s claim that the mandate merely shifts competition toward universal service lacked a concrete mechanism for why operators would absorb unprofitable coverage without passing costs to riders or reducing fleet investment. The “public license to operate” frame was rhetorically strong but insufficient to override CON’s clearer cost-benefit comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 distinction between efficient deadheading toward likely paid trips and mandated presence toward regulatory targets, combined with the crystallization that “equality measured by coverage is not access measured by completed trips,” decisively reframed the clash and left PRO’s costless-mandate narrative unsupported.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B won by framing the choice as an enforceable service floor versus purchasing power with no assurance that a vehicle will arrive. Side A exposed real dead-heading and cross-subsidy costs, but repeatedly escalated them into fleet collapse without establishing that a flexible, reasonably similar standard would be existential. B also permissibly combined the requirement with targeted support, answering the funding objection without abandoning the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer that vouchers create demand but not guaranteed availability was decisive. It directly weakened A’s alternative, while B’s distinction between output reliability and standing idle reserves prevented A from defining the mandate as its most economically destructive version.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): PRO won by keeping the proposition's modest reliability floor live and distinct from idle-reserve strawmen, supplying concrete output metrics, and landing the cleaner comparative: an enforceable service claim beats voucher "purchasing power" that still leaves riders with no cars. CON's utilization/deadhead economics were serious and well-pressed, but repeatedly treated the mandate as inevitable capital destruction rather than fully neutralizing PRO's rebalancing/scheduled-pickup framing or the public-permission duty. After the full exchange PRO made the decision easier: vote for the side that still gives low-income riders a dependable arrival standard.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2 (A4.1–A4.4) answering B's metrics and subsidy pressure: output reliability (wait windows, acceptance, completed trips) plus mandate-as-service-floor vs voucher-as-money-only.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): PRO won by demonstrating that "reasonably similar reliability" is a modest, enforceable standard achievable through permitting metrics and operational flexibility, while exposing that CON's voucher alternative offers purchasing power without any enforceable guarantee of actual service availability. PRO's "guarantee versus hope" framing held firm through the closing: a subsidy cannot conjure a vehicle when algorithms steer fleets away. CON's economic doom-mongering never overcame the fact that publicly permitted transport networks routinely accept service obligations, and that rebalancing, pooling, and scheduled pickups can satisfy reliability without requiring wasteful standing reserves.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure questions and Rebuttal 2, which defined output-based reliability metrics (response-time parity, acceptance rates) and showed that CON's voucher scheme provides purchasing power without guaranteeing spatial availability — a structural gap CON never repaired.
