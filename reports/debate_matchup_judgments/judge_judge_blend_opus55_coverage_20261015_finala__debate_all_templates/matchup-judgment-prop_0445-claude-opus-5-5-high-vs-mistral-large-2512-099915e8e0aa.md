### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 3 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won decisively by separating universal protection from deregulation and showing that A never justified making one conditional on the other. B also exposed A's mistaken hiring-permission example, emphasized that recent flexibility reforms produced no demonstrated jobs surge, and showed that portable benefits do not replace workplace voice or job security. A's later claim that protection was already legally guaranteed and tax-funded further undermined the supposed necessity of the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's decisive turn was that if universal protection is independently fundable and already mandated, it can be delivered without weakening dismissal protections. Combined with A's concession that the prior loosening produced no formal-employment surge, this reduced A's proposal to unsupported additional deregulation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 3 (PRO)): Side A (CON) won because it collapsed the logic of the "exchange." CON showed that universal protections were already mandated by the 2020 Social Security Code and fundable through broad-based taxation, so the protection half of the bargain did not require further liberalization at all. Meanwhile, CON demonstrated that the flexibility half had already been tried—via the 2020 codes and earlier state reforms like Rajasthan—without producing a formal-employment surge, and it corrected PRO's key factual error that Indian law required permission to hire. PRO never recovered from the concession that protection could be built independently, or from the demonstration that its named target, Chapter V-B, had already been loosened. The motion therefore reduced to a one-sided deregulation that would strip remaining worker protections without delivering the promised benefits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal dismantling PRO's causal mechanism: correcting the false claim that Indian law required hiring permission, showing PRO's specific target (Chapter V-B) had already been reformed from 100 to 300 workers with no jobs surge, and proving that portable protection was already legally fundable—making the "exchange" a one-sided deregulation rather than a genuine bargain.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 3 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): CON wins the central exchange: PRO concedes protection is fundable from broad taxes and already mandated, making the "in exchange for" unnecessary, while flexibility is shown to have already been tried - 2020 codes raised threshold to 300, Rajasthan early reform produced no surge, and hiring never needed permission. PRO's key examples collapse on that factual error, and B's pressure exposes the lopsided timing: flexibility immediate, protection maybe later.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1-2 sequence: correcting the hire vs layoff error, using Rajasthan/no-surge as evidence rigidity isn't binding, and turning PRO's own funding claim into proof the Social Security floor can be built without further deregulation.
