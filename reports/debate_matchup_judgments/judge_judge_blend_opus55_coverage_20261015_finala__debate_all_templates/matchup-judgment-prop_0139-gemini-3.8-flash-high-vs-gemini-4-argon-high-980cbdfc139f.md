### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but A built the tighter, more motion-responsive case. A repeatedly exploited the resolution's own text ("even if only a partial solution") to turn CON's "majority left unprotected" argument into a demand to ban any triage measure, a framing CON never fully escaped. A also developed a genuinely useful mechanistic distinction—boundary policing (a visible man at a car door) versus interior crush-load policing (a hidden hand in a packed crowd)—and used it consistently to answer both CON's enforcement-paradox attack and the pressure questions. CON's strongest material, the institutional-signaling/moral-hazard argument (designating a safe car brands mixed cars as free-for-all), was conceptually sharp and well-pressed in questions, but across rounds it was mostly reasserted rather than deepened, and A's answer (staff/bystander blame already exists in high-harassment systems; reporting mandates address staff conduct across all cars) blunted its force without CON fully re-engaging. CON's pressure questions were good, but A's answers (A1/A2) directly confronted them using the motion's permissive framing, which CON's closing never dislodged.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The crux exchange was B2.1's enforcement-paradox attack (if crowds are too jammed to police, how can you police the women-only boundary?) versus A4.4/A5.2's distinction between an eye-level, binary boundary check at the door versus invisible mid-crowd groping. A's distinction was the more decisive move because it survived B4.1's "visibility isn't enforcement" reply without being meaningfully refuted.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO won by locking the clash to the motion's "even if partial" rule and the crush-load physics: hidden groping between stations is hard to police, boundary visibility is easier, and denying optional refuge leaves every woman exposed while CON treats them as political leverage. CON landed real pressure on coverage limits, door-guard diversion, and risk-shifting signaling, but never neutralized that banning the tool fails to make mixed cars safer today; PRO's comparative weighing stayed cleaner and more decisive across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's visibility answer (Q1/A1 and B4.4-B4.5/closing): a male intruder is eye-level obvious while a waist-level grope in crush load is not, collapsing CON's claim that boundary enforcement equals interior policing.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by grounding the debate in the inescapable physical realities of transit policing. A's distinction between the invisibility of waist-level groping in a moving crush-load versus the high visibility of a man boarding a designated car decisively neutralized B's enforcement and resource arguments. Furthermore, A effectively weaponized the motion's "partial solution" clause, framing B's demand for universal coverage as a cruel all-or-nothing trap that uses women's safety as political leverage. B's normative arguments about signaling and moral hazard were rhetorically polished but ultimately outweighed by A's concrete, immediate safety mechanisms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantle Side B's claim that stationing guards at designated doors squanders resources. A points out that assaults happen inside the moving train between stations where platform guards cannot reach, making the door guard highly effective, while the interior of the women-only car is self-policing due to eye-level visibility.
