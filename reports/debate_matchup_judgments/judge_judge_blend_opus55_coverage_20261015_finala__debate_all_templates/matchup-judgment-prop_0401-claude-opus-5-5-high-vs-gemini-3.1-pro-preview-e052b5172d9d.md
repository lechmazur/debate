### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO forced the live clash onto whether “mend” can preserve anything uniquely golden without the sale of membership itself; CON’s passive-capital defense narrowed exactly into that target and never neutralized the EU externality or adverse-selection pressure. CON’s debt-free funding path remained live but was out-weighed once PRO showed ordinary markets already supply the capital without the card.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO R1/R2 collapse of reform: real-job/presence requirements become ordinary investor visas (conceding the motion); pure passive cheques keep the passport-as-product problem and seller-vetter externality.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by exposing the structural dilemma in CON's reform case: any genuine reform either removes the passive-residency-for-cash mechanism that defines golden visas, conceding the motion, or preserves it, leaving security, housing, and moral harms intact. PRO consistently maintained that EU-wide externalities of small-state sales cannot be solved by better paperwork alone, since the seller still profits while others bear the risk. CON's economic defense—that these programs provide unique non-debt capital—was effectively narrowed by PRO to the admission that residency itself is the product being sold, shifting the debate onto PRO's territory of principle and security risk. PRO's later speeches crystallized the comparative choice clearly, while CON's answers on vetting and small-state competitiveness, though plausible, never fully neutralized the core externality or reform paradox.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 was decisive in narrowing CON's case to its essence: the only unique function of golden visas is using residency as a sweetener for capital that would not otherwise come, proving that residency—not investment—is the actual product being sold.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's core defense of "reformed" passive investment. Side B effectively demonstrated that if an investor accepts zero financial return for a non-repayable donation, the residency itself is the commodity being sold, which inherently attracts those who need a new identity or jurisdiction the most. Side B also maintained a strong structural advantage on the security externality, pointing out that the selling state keeps the revenue while the rest of the Schengen area bears the risk—a point Side A's "EU vetting" answer failed to neutralize. Side A's economic arguments about debt-free capital were logically sound but ultimately conceded the exact mechanism Side B used to prove the program's inherent danger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they turned Side A's defense of "zero-interest, non-repayable donations" into proof that the passport is the primary product, thereby attracting the exact high-risk buyers Europe should avoid.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON won by making reform vs abolition the decisive comparison and holding it under pressure. PRO’s security, housing, and integrity harms were real, but CON repeatedly showed they are design and coordination failures addressable by redirects (Portugal), shared standards, and revocation—not proof that the channel itself must burn. CON’s live routes survived: the vetted checkpoint vs anonymous shell-company back door, housing harm living in property/tax rules rather than the visa, and the internal contradiction that states too greedy to vet cannot be trusted to abolish either. PRO kept the incentive/race-to-the-bottom and Schengen-externality points alive, but never squarely defeated the reform alternative or the checkpoint weighing. Clipped CON turns cost polish, not the core comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s closing contradiction plus checkpoint weighing: if governments are too profit-driven to vet, they will not choose abolition either; abolition removes the named, revocable residency check while the anonymous property path remains.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by showing that abolition does not solve the harms PRO identified while destroying the one checkpoint that forces wealthy migrants to identify themselves and submit to scrutiny. CON’s turn on feasibility was decisive: if the EU lacks jurisdiction to impose reform, it cannot impose abolition either, leaving national choice as the only path; and if governments are too profit-driven to vet applicants honestly, they are even less likely to voluntarily surrender all revenue. PRO’s commodification and security framing remained live but could not overcome the comparative demonstration that reform preserves capital, oversight, and revocation power, whereas abolition preserves none of them and leaves shell-company anonymity untouched.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s exposure of PRO’s internal contradiction on state incentives—arguing governments are too corrupt to vet yet trustworthy enough to abolish—combined with the checkpoint argument that abolition removes the named-registration requirement while leaving anonymous property purchases untouched.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A built a much stronger logical case, particularly by trapping Side B in a fatal contradiction in the closing: if national governments are too driven by profit to enforce vetting, they are equally too driven by profit to voluntarily abolish the programs. Side A also brilliantly reframed the security clash, arguing that golden visas actually force wealthy foreigners to identify themselves and undergo background checks, whereas abolition allows them to buy property anonymously through shell companies. Side B's arguments were solid but relied on a rigid view of regulatory capture that Side A successfully dismantled with the banking analogy and the jurisdiction trap. Despite being clipped at the very end of a few turns, Side A's core arguments were fully delivered and decisively won the key clashes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing argument exposing the contradiction in Side B's reliance on national governments to voluntarily abolish the very programs they are allegedly too greedy and corrupt to regulate.
