- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built a tighter, more sustained case around a genuine structural dilemma: either a "goal mode" still follows revealed behavior (making chosen control illusory, since engagement proxies return through the back door) or it ignores behavior and traps users in a stale self-description they must repeatedly renegotiate. B's answer—an editable "switch modes?" prompt—never escapes this; A's rebuttal 2 showed it collapses into "repeated negotiation most users ignore," and B's closing did not re-engage this specific point, leaving it live for A. A also landed a harder-hitting point that labeled "safe" modes (chronology, close relationships, learning) can still launder exactly the harms B claims to exclude, since follow networks and content choices determine exposure regardless of the ranking label; B's closing only partially conceded this ("chronology...can narrow a worldview") without neutralizing the broader "laundering" and "universal corrigibility" arguments. B's case was competent and adapted well in places (traffic-law analogy, distinguishing ends from means, contestability-through-named-modes), but its camera-mode analogy understated how contested proxies like "misinformation" or "diversity" are, and its opening speech being clipped cost it the chance to land its closing framing cleanly. Overall A maintained a clearer throughline and left B's central mechanism more exposed at the end of the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's recurring "control is illusory vs. users are trapped in a stale self-description" dilemma (first raised in A2.3, pressed again as Q1, and never fully resolved by B's "switch modes?" answer in B4.1) was the most decisive exchange, since it exposed the core mechanical weakness in PRO's model that was never fully closed out by the end of the debate.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralizes Side A's dilemma by comparing user control to choosing between portrait and panorama modes—meaningful choice over the outcome, even if the platform builds the underlying mechanics.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully trapping Side A in a core dilemma: if the platform tightly defines the presets to ensure safety, users haven't gained meaningful control over the goals; if users have genuine control, they can bypass universal safety and cross-cutting exposure interventions, destroying the platform's ability to universally correct harms. Side B's framing of "universal corrigibility" versus "fragmented purpose" was highly persuasive, and their rebuttals effectively dismantled Side A's "bounded menu" compromise. Side A's defense—that choosing an end is meaningful even if the platform builds the means—struggled to overcome Side B's concrete examples of how users could game those labels (e.g., a chronology feed of conspiracists) or how the platform would inevitably retain the underlying editorial power to make the presets safe.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they directly answered Side A's pressure questions by explaining that excluding an "outrage" button doesn't exclude outrage (since chronology or close relationships can serve the same function), and that the true mechanism of a single objective is universal corrigibility, exposing the shrinking core of Side A's "safe menu" model.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued sharply, but B controlled the central clash: it kept forcing A to admit that its "common objective" also relies on unauditable proxies, which undercut A's core claim that institutional single-goal ranking is uniquely accountable. B's traffic-law/speed-limit framing of a safety floor plus user-chosen purpose answered A's externality worry without conceding away user agency, and B's closing crystallized the asymmetry cleanly: under one hidden goal, failure gets relabeled as "your feed," whereas under chosen goals failure is visible and switchable. A's strongest point—those guardrails above the floor to cap amplification already require nearly all the consequential judgment, making the user's choice "marginal"—was real and not fully defused, but B's answer (the floor only constrains, the user still decides which reach serves which purpose) was adequate enough that A's rebuttal mostly repeated the same tension rather than landing a knockout. A also won the "observability" line distinguishing chronological/mute controls from abstract goals, but B neutralized this by noting A's own preferred objective is equally unobservable to users, just audited by the firm itself instead of the person affected. Pressure questions were well-targeted on both sides and answered with proper A1/A2 format, so no execution penalty either way. Overall B's progression better reconciled the floor/goal split and made the proposition's practical shape (presets + guardrails) feel more concrete and resolved than A's repeated "institutional judgment must do the real work" line, which conceded ground each time without fully closing the comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1/B2.4 forcing A to concede that its own "quality/relevance/integrity" objective needs equally unauditable proxies, paired with the traffic-law/floor-vs-goal reframe that A never fully dislodged despite repeated attempts in A4.4-A4.5.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully deconstructing the motion's concept of 'control'. By drawing a sharp distinction between verifiable inputs (chronological sorting) and unobservable intentions (learning goals), Side A proves that giving users choices over algorithmic proxies is merely 'consent theater' that fragments platform accountability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive, specifically the 'observability' argument in A4.2 and the dilemma in A4.4, which exposed how user goals either remain marginal under platform guardrails or create systemic risks.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully turning Side B's strongest argument—that abstract goals require imperfect, opaque proxies—back on Side B's own preferred alternative of a single "quality and relevance" objective. Side A effectively framed the core clash around who gets to author the user's purpose, making a compelling case that imperfect, switchable user control is vastly superior to submission to a single, platform-defined black box. While Side B landed sharp points regarding the contradictions inherent in "amplification budgets" and guardrails, Side B suffered from a clipped opening and ultimately struggled to persuasively defend why a platform-chosen standard is more accountable to the end user than a user-chosen one.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 [A2.1-A2.3], which brilliantly inverted Side B's argument that abstract goals like "learn" are unworkable by pointing out that Side B's preferred goals like "quality and relevance" are equally abstract and proxy-dependent, shifting the burden to who should choose the proxy's aim.
