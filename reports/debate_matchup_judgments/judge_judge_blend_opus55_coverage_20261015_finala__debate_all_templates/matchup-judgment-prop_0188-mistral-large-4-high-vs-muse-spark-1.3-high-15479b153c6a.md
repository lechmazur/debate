### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but A (CON) landed the more damaging and unanswered blow. A's pressure question exposed a real tension in B's framework: if journals retain discretion to reject for "rigor" or "importance," what stops that discretion from being used as disguised outcome-based rejection? B's answer asserted that "importance is fixed at the protocol stage" and editors "cannot" retroactively reject disappointing results, but in Rebuttal 2 A pointed out that quality failures like attrition and broken measures are necessarily assessed post-hoc, reintroducing exactly the bias B claimed to eliminate. B's closing restated the "locked before data exist" claim without engaging this specific vulnerability, leaving a live route to victory for A unaddressed. A also sustained vivid, consistent examples (sleep/mood, evictions/job-networks) across every round to argue that labeling genuine discoveries as merely "exploratory" demotes honest science, and B's answer—that researchers "keep funding and publication slot" but lose only the HARKing label—never fully neutralized A's point that hiring committees and guidelines discount exploratory findings in practice. A's equity argument (large labs can write "bulletproof preregistrations," small/qualitative/crisis researchers cannot) was also left comparatively underdeveloped by B's rebuttal, which asserted without much grounding that field researchers "already preregister" adaptively. Overall A made the clash easier to resolve and left B with an unresolved structural gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 response (A4.1) to B's answer on pressure Q1: by showing that post-hoc assessment of execution quality (attrition, broken measures) reintroduces the very outcome-bias discretion B claimed the motion eliminated, a point B's closing never directly answered.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A systematically dismantled the practical mechanics of Side B's proposal, proving that evaluating a study's worth before execution ignores the realities of fieldwork and data collection. Side A's argument that guaranteed publication removes quality control for mid-study attrition or broken measures went unanswered and served as a decisive voting issue.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Turn 8 (Side A's Rebuttal 2), specifically A4.1, which brilliantly exposed that attrition and broken measures occur after protocol acceptance, rendering the guarantee of publication a choice between enshrining noise or risking accusations of outcome bias.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively attacking the practical and sociological realities of the proposition's mechanism. While Side B defended an idealized version of preregistration and registered reports, Side A sharply pointed out that poor execution, attrition, and broken measures occur after protocol acceptance, trapping journals into publishing botched nulls or facing charges of outcome bias. Side A also successfully argued that labeling mid-study pivots as "exploratory" functionally demotes them in the real academic economy, exposing the PRO side's solution as sociologically naive. Side A's rhetoric ("filters applied after learning, not guarantees sold before it") crystallized the clash perfectly and secured the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1), which dismantled Side B's claim that journals could still reject poor methodology by pointing out that poor execution and massive attrition happen after the Stage 1 protocol acceptance, thereby breaking the post-hoc quality control filter.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): B built a tighter, more unified case around a single decisive frame—judging research before versus after the outcome is known—and defended it consistently through rebuttals, pressure questions, and closing. B's clarification that "regardless of outcome" means outcome-blind, not quality-blind, closely tracks the real-world "registered reports" model and effectively neutralized A's "quota" and "freeze" fears by showing amendments are timestamped, not banned, and that stage-one review still screens for power and importance. A raised genuine concerns (diversity of research designs, compliance costs, equity across institutions) but largely repeated these across rounds without adapting to B's strongest counter: that A's own preferred alternative, voluntary registered reports, only solves the file-drawer problem for journals that opt in, leaving the core incentive untouched elsewhere. B's pressure question on this point was never squarely answered, while B's own answers to A's pressure questions (A1/A2 in Turn 7) were clear and consistent with B's throughline. A's closing reframe ("quality vs. compliance") was a decent rhetorical move but arrived too late to undo the ground B had already secured.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question 2 ("If rigorous, well-powered nulls can still be rejected for being unexciting, how do voluntary sharing and disclosure stop file drawers?") combined with A's unresolved answer—falling back on voluntary registered reports without explaining how non-adopting journals' incentives change—was the most decisive exchange, since it exposed a structural gap in A's alternative that was never closed.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by cleanly resolving the core clash over publication quality. Side A repeatedly argued that the mandate would force journals to publish trivial or underpowered studies as a quota. Side B effectively neutralized this by consistently explaining the Stage 1 review mechanism (judging rigor and importance before results are known), which Side A's final speeches failed to accurately address, instead attacking a strawman where quality review is abolished entirely.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's A1 in Rebuttal 2 decisively clarified that journals still reject underpowered designs at Stage 1, neutralizing Side A's central fear of a junk-science quota.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing a clear, compelling principle ("judge the question before the answer") and correctly framing the publication mandate as the standard Registered Reports model (Stage 1 quality review, Stage 2 results-blind acceptance). Side A's case relied heavily on a strawman, repeatedly insisting that the motion required journals to blindly publish every completed filing regardless of quality, which contradicted both the standard definition of the policy and Side B's consistent clarifications. While Side A made valid points about the burden on exploratory and qualitative research, Side B's responses were sufficient, and Side A's failure to effectively challenge the core incentive shift proposed by Side B left Side B with the stronger path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1-B4.3), where they decisively dismantled Side A's pressure questions by clarifying that Stage 1 review preserves quality control and that registered nulls include power calculations to prevent misinterpretation, effectively collapsing Side A's "quota" and "junk science" fears.
