### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built a tighter, more battery-specific mechanism: capital intensity, permitting, refining, and upstream supply chains are bottlenecks that a repealable consumer credit cannot reliably finance, and leakage means demand subsidies without domestic capacity transfer money abroad. A's reversal of PRO's tariff argument (domestic capacity still serves home/allied markets; reliance on foreign supply is the actual vulnerability) and its parity point (demand-side tools also pick winners and often subsidize affluent/import buyers) were sharp, direct, and never fully neutralized. PRO (B) had a coherent framework (market pull, phase-out, avoiding trade war, avoiding permanent entitlements) and decent rhetoric, but its flagship Norway example was challenged twice as a small, wealthy-country outlier that doesn't demonstrate mid-sized-economy cell/cathode construction, and B never directly re-defended it after the first rebuttal. B's closing also simply re-asserted the tariff-fragmentation claim without engaging A's rebuttal that tariffs don't strand domestically-used capacity, which reads as an unaddressed drop rather than resolution. Both pressure rounds were sharp and well-targeted, but A's answers (reframing tariffs, parity on winner-picking) landed more cleanly than B's answers (the procurement/auction analogy for bankability was plausible but B's own example blurs the demand/supply distinction central to its case, and the 'scale pulls depth' claim remained largely asserted). Overall A gave the clearer, more battery-specific causal story and more decisively exploited unresolved tensions in B's case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's turn on the tariff-retaliation argument (A4.1): instead of conceding that tariffs strand subsidized capacity, A argues tariffs merely block exports while domestic/allied-market capacity remains valuable, flipping B's own Q1 pressure point into evidence for reducing import dependence. B's closing never circled back to rebut this reversal, leaving it live and decisive.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): B won by showing that demand can increase purchases without ensuring domestic factories, upstream inputs, knowledge, or resilience. A offered a coherent market-pull theory, but its claim that sufficient demand necessarily pulls the entire supply chain locally was asserted rather than demonstrated and required border or domestic-content measures as extra support. B also credibly distinguished temporary consumer incentives from conditioned subsidies that directly absorb construction risk and attach funding to rooted assets.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B most decisively answered A's FDI argument: local demand may attract assembly or imports while leaving cells, refining, equipment, and IP abroad. This undermined A's central claim that market scale automatically produces a deep domestic industrial base.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively proving that the specific characteristics of the clean tech industry (capital-intensive bottlenecks, entrenched foreign incumbents) mean demand-side policies alone primarily lead to imports rather than domestic capacity. Side A's responses to Side B's pressure on trade wars and stranded assets cleanly neutralized Side B's main offensive routes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in Rebuttal 2 (A4.1) that retaliatory tariffs do not make domestic capacity worthless, but rather validate the need to supply home markets and reduce import dependence, neatly flipping Side B's trade war argument.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): A built a tighter, more decisive case around a structural point B never fully answered: demand-side tools are inherently confined to finished-product markets, so they cannot reach the capital-intensive upstream bottlenecks (refining, cathode materials, precision equipment) that actually determine supply security. A also landed a sharp "collapse" move—showing that once B's demand-side tools are conditioned on domestic content or final assembly to avoid leakage, they functionally become producer subsidies, undercutting PRO's core distinction. B's best defense (the "payment trigger" distinction: pay on delivery vs. pay on construction) was a clever but narrow rejoinder that left the upstream-capacity gap unresolved; B's closing largely pivoted to "producer subsidies can't solve this either," which is a tu quoque, not an affirmative answer. Both sides executed the format well (clean Q1/Q2 and A1/A2), but A's security/resilience framing proved more robust under pressure and was never squarely neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's "collapse" argument (A4.3/A2.2) — that demand-side credits conditioned on domestic content or offtake are "producer subsidies by another name" — forced B into a technical distinction (payment trigger) that didn't fully resolve the upstream bottleneck problem, leaving A's resilience case the stronger live route.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by defending demand-side policy as broader than consumer rebates and showing how procurement, auctions, and offtake commitments create bankable orders while tying public spending to delivered outcomes. Side B established real concerns about upstream capacity, leakage, and crisis resilience, but repeatedly relied on guaranteed offtake while trying to exclude it from demand-side policy. It also never fully answered A’s comparative case about idle capacity, technological lock-in, and taxpayer risk.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest response was that B’s preferred offtake guarantees work precisely by creating committed buyers. Defining procurement as merely a targeted industrial tool did not defeat A’s distinction between paying for delivered output and subsidizing factory construction regardless of utilization.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A wins by grounding the debate in the physical and temporal realities of battery manufacturing. A effectively demonstrates that demand-side policies either leak to foreign incumbents due to construction time lags or require strict domestic conditioning that morphs them into producer policies. Furthermore, A's argument that downstream consumer subsidies cannot provide the massive upfront capital needed for upstream bottlenecks (like lithium refining) goes largely unmitigated by B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal (A4.2) sharply contrasts how producer subsidies can directly finance capital-intensive upstream refineries, while demand-side tools provide no upfront capital for these critical chokepoints.
