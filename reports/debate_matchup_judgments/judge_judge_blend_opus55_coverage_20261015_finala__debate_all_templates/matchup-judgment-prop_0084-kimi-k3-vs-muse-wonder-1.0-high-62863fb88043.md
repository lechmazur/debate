### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing that verification need not prevent every first offense to materially reduce repeat and scalable abuse. Its stable-identity mechanism made reports, linked complaints, and bans more consequential, while Side A's alternative credentials were also replaceable and did not answer why an additional identity anchor lacked value. Side A raised serious privacy and exclusion costs, but often assumed permanent document storage despite B's data-minimizing implementation and overstated how little platform exclusion helps against reported scammers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's answer to the badge dilemma was decisive: verification can provide narrow identity assurance while doing its main work behind the scenes, so its benefit does not depend on users treating the badge as proof of honesty. This preserved B's repeat-abuse case while weakening A's strongest fraud turn.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by establishing a devastatingly clean weighing mechanism: when Side A's alternative friction tools fail, status quo harms occur, but when Side B's mandatory ID fails, status quo harms occur plus a permanent honeypot of intimate data is exposed. Side A also successfully handled B's pressure questions, particularly by reframing the anonymity of abuse survivors around the risk of becoming findable in a database rather than the abuser's ability to re-register.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1 and A4.3, where Side A introduces the asymmetric failure modes of both policies and brilliantly pivots the abuse survivor argument to focus on the danger of centralized exposure.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by executing sharper, more decisive rebuttals that directly dismantled Side B's core mechanisms. Side A effectively exploited the text of the motion, pointing out that Side B's privacy safeguards (like tokenization and deletion) were design aspirations rather than requirements of the proposition as written. Side A also landed a devastating structural blow in Rebuttal 2 by highlighting the asymmetry of failure modes: when Side A's alternative tools fail, the result is the status quo, but when Side B's verification fails, the result is a massive breach linking government IDs to intimate data. Furthermore, Side A's "badge dilemma" (that verification either manufactures false trust for scammers or provides no user-facing benefit) was never fully resolved by Side B. Side A's closing speech perfectly crystallized this clash, arguing that the only thing guaranteed to "last" under the proposition is the permanent archive of vulnerable users' data, not the consequences for offenders.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, specifically the argument on asymmetric failure modes (A4.1) and the devastating critique of how verification revokes the survivor's choice to remain unfindable (A4.3), which squarely answered Side B's pressure questions while exposing the fatal flaws in Side B's privacy defenses.
