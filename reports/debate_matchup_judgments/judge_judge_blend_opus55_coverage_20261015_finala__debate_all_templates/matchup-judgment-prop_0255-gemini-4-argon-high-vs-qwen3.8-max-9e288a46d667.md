### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Both sides built real cases, but B's framing (deletion as withdrawal of consent, with clean carve-outs for public figures and lawful warrants) stayed anchored to the text and survived contact better. A's strongest weapon—fraud/cybersecurity carve-outs—forced useful pressure, but when pressed on the opt-out vs. retention dilemma, A's answer (retain "high-risk indicators" before any crime is known) effectively conceded that brokers must preemptively archive innocent citizens' deleted data "just in case," which is exactly the mass-surveillance outcome A had earlier denied making. B's closing correctly identified this as a self-indicting admission and narrowed the debate to that single unresolved contradiction, while A's closing reasserted its position without resolving the preemption problem. A also drifted toward defending a modified regulatory regime (consent, purpose limits, opt-outs) rather than squarely defending the status quo against the literal prohibition, which weakened its grip on the motion as written. B's case required fewer exceptions, stayed more consistent across rounds, and ended the debate with the cleaner, more decisive unanswered point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 "fatal contradiction" move (A4.1 vs A2.2/A2.5: opt-outs vs. mandatory retention) combined with the Pressure Questions forcing A into choosing between honoring deletion (which destroys the fraud-detection advantage) or ignoring it (which requires preemptive mass archiving of innocent citizens) was the turning point A never fully escaped.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side B won by showing that the proposition’s broad language also prohibits retaining and selling cross-platform fraud identifiers and identifiable threat signals. Side A persuasively established serious privacy harms but repeatedly narrowed the motion through anonymization, first-party records, and noncommercial consortiums without preserving the broker-based defensive uses B identified. B directly answered the opt-out pressure with purpose-limited exceptions, while A never fully resolved the repeat-fraud problem and incurred smaller execution costs from nonexistent reference labels.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 most decisively answered A’s claimed contradiction: ordinary resale could honor opt-outs while narrowly restricted fraud signals remain available to protect others. B then returned to the proposition’s text and showed that A’s preferred exceptions were not actually contained in its blanket ban.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO kept the motion on its text while CON conceded the core – ordinary marketing/background sale of deleted traces must stop [A4.1][A4.5]. CON's remaining path (fraud/threat intel) requires purpose-limited retention despite opt-out, which PRO's Pressure Qs exposed as preemptive commercial surveillance of innocents and a contradiction with CON's own consent/opt-out alternative. PRO's distinction between first-party logs/anonymized telemetry and commercial brokering of tied personal traces survived, leaving autonomy/physical safety as the cleaner live route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's R1 + Pressure Qs turning CON's "consent/opt-outs and preservation before warrant" into a dilemma – if you honor deletion scammers opt out, if you ignore it you mandate mass surveillance [B2.4][B3.1][B3.2] – which CON's R2 answered by admitting brokers may ignore opt-out for "high-risk" [A4.1].

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON built a sharper, more sustained case by holding PRO to the literal text of the motion. CON's opening established that a blanket ban on archiving/selling "deleted or non-public" traces would gut fraud detection, credit reporting, and investigative journalism, all of which rely on third-party brokers handling exactly this kind of data. PRO's rebuttals repeatedly tried to carve out "purpose-limited risk signals" and "regulated bureaus" as compatible with the ban, but CON correctly and repeatedly pointed out that the proposition as worded contains no such exceptions — PRO was effectively defending a narrower, better-designed policy than the one on the table, which is a structural weakness PRO never cleanly resolved. CON's pressure-question answers were direct and consistent (conceding sensitive ad-targeting should be banned via use-restrictions while maintaining the blanket archiving ban is overbroad), while PRO's defense of credit/fraud carve-outs kept reintroducing exactly the kind of brokered retention and sale the resolution purports to forbid. PRO's autonomy framing was compelling in isolation, but under cross-examination it could not reconcile "no sale without ongoing consent" with the practical need for cross-platform risk-signal sharing about non-consenting bad actors, leaving CON's internal-contradiction attack as the single most damaging and unanswered line of the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 / Closing move pointing out that PRO's own defense (regulated bureaus trading "purpose-limited risk signals") requires exceptions that do not exist in the resolution's actual text, which undercuts PRO's claim to be defending the motion as written.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B won by focusing on the proposition’s blanket scope. Side A persuasively established privacy and autonomy harms, but repeatedly relied on exceptions for regulated bureaus, lawful risk-sharing, and purpose-limited signals that the motion does not contain. Side B showed that these concessions undermine a categorical prohibition and support targeted use restrictions instead.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered the medical-search challenge, then showed that Side A’s proposed survival of regulated credit and fraud systems effectively rewrote the motion with purpose-based exceptions.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON won the scope clash. PRO's autonomy / loss-of-exit case was strong but collapsed when PRO admitted in R2/Closing that regulated bureaus could still retain and sell purpose-limited risk signals and that selling medical searches to advertisers should be banned via use-rules. Those admissions concede CON's central principle — regulate purpose and sensitivity, not impose a flat ban on all deleted/non-public traces — and contradict the proposition's text, which has no fraud, credit, or journalism exceptions. CON answered Q1/Q2 directly; PRO's answers required rewriting the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3-A4.5 exposing that PRO's defense relies on "purpose-limited" and "regulated bureau" exceptions that do not exist in the proposition, turning PRO's burden-placement into a concession to CON's targeted-regulation framework.
