### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): A built a tighter, more coherent case. The opening established a clear mechanism (opacity lets platforms skim surge while distorting tips and preventing market discipline), and Rebuttal 1 landed a real hit by exposing the apparent tension between B's "normalization" claim and "driver revolt/rider boycott" claim. B's attempt to resolve this by splitting timelines (instant driver rejection vs. slow rider normalization) was plausible but left an unaddressed seam: B's own numbers imply a 30% "normal" cut coexisting with a secretly skimmed 55% surge cut, which undercuts the normalization story A flagged in Rebuttal 2 and B never squarely answered. B's mechanism claims (e.g., "dispatch algorithms are calibrated to hold margin for 3 to 5 minutes") were asserted with unwarranted precision and no real backing, which counts against grounding discipline. B also fumbled the Pressure Questions round by attributing a claim to A that was actually B's own ([B1.6]), and A caught this cleanly in A4.1, scoring a clear credibility point. A's closing crystallized the clash well with the "hire employees instead of secretly cross-subsidizing contractors" framing, which B's closing did not directly rebut, instead repeating the "algorithm holds the line" assertion. Overall A answered more of the live pressure more precisely and left B's reliability argument as an asserted mechanism rather than a demonstrated one.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's exposure of the normalization/revolt tension in Rebuttal 1 ([A2.1]), combined with catching B's misattributed claim in the Pressure round ([A4.1]), did the most damage to B's throughline and was never fully repaired.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO maintained the cleaner comparative path: riders already accept the total price so disclosure doesn't shrink demand, and driver rejection of a 55% skim is solved by real-time bidding up rather than losing the $42 fare. CON's core tension - that transparency both normalizes a 30% take and enrages drivers/riders into boycotts - was never resolved, and its reliance on fixed 3-5 minute hold-the-margin algorithms conceded that platforms choose to burn rides to protect opacity. The independent-contractor burden and tipping distortion remained live for PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4 collapsing CON's normalization vs revolt claims and forcing the admission that algorithms would rather offer $30 of $42 than forfeit the booking.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B built a much stronger and more cohesive case, successfully exposing contradictions in Side A's arguments regarding normalization versus immediate market revolt. Side B effectively countered Side A's "stranded rider" mechanism by pointing out that platforms would simply recalibrate their dispatch algorithms to offer fair splits upfront under transparency, rather than intentionally burning lucrative bookings for a few minutes. Side B also dominated the pressure question exchange, easily swatting Side A's misdirected question while asking pointed questions that forced Side A into defensive admissions about the necessity of deception in their model.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side A's pressure question about rider boycotts by pointing out Side A's misreading of the transcript, and logically proved that transparency would force algorithms to adjust initial offers rather than intentionally burn rides.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly, but CON built a more layered causal case (cross-subsidy flattening, backseat arbitrage incentives, tipping suppression, and the "own-side info is sufficient" framing) and used its Pressure Questions round to expose a real tension in PRO's position: PRO simultaneously treated variable cuts as legitimate cross-subsidy and as suspicious evidence of unfairness. PRO's answers (A4.1–A4.6) asserted that context could resolve this but never explained why a footnote would stop the backlash-driven flattening dynamic or the tipping-suppression point CON raised, and never directly engaged CON's grocery-receipt analogy. PRO's case remained emotionally resonant ("manage users like children") but structurally thinner, mostly repeating the "secrets breed conflict" mantra rather than dismantling CON's specific mechanisms. CON's closing crystallized this unresolved tension effectively, making it the cleaner path to a decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Question (B3.1/B3.2) forcing PRO to reconcile "variable cuts are fine" with "50% vs 15% cuts for identical work is suspicious," and PRO's incomplete answer in A4.1–A4.2, which CON then exploited decisively in B4.4.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON won the core economic clash: per-ride splits are a misleading unit in a pooled network, and default display creates asymmetric outrage that forces flattening of variable takes and hands riders/drivers exact arbitrage math for uninsured cash deals. PRO's answers that context can fix it and that transparency calms resentment were asserted without mechanism and contradicted by its own framing of 50% vs 15% cuts as suspect.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2 A1/A2 - asymmetric outrage forces platforms to flatten and exact $22 spread enables off-app arbitrage, directly undermining B's claim that seeing the number reduces conflict.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully reframing the debate from a simple moral binary (transparency vs. secrecy) to a complex economic reality. Side A's argument that per-ride splits represent a "false unit of accounting" that ignores necessary cross-subsidization and risk pooling is highly persuasive. Furthermore, Side A's point that exact fare splits provide the "arbitrage math" needed to facilitate off-app cash deals effectively neutralizes Side B's claim that transparency reduces resentment. Side B's reliance on "just add a footnote" to explain complex pricing pools fails against Side A's behavioral argument about the visceral shock of headline numbers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal to the off-app cash deal argument. While Side B claimed drivers already guess the platform's cut, Side A sharply countered that guessing requires awkward interrogation, whereas broadcasting the exact spread hands both parties the precise arbitrage math to cut out the app, removing the friction that keeps them on the platform.
