- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins because it successfully reframed the debate as a comparative weighing problem and showed that Side B (PRO) never proved the alternative to English dominance would actually improve conditions for non-anglophone cultures. CON’s examples of high English proficiency coexisting with robust local-language institutions in parts of Europe and India gave concrete grounds for that rebuttal. By the closing, CON had the cleaner comparative story: removing the bridge does not equalize access; it strands ordinary researchers and workers while wealthy elites retain private routes to cross.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal (Turn 8 / A4), which argued that PRO’s pluricentric-hub alternative would not abolish gatekeeping but multiply it behind larger regional languages like Spanish, Mandarin, and Hindi, and would leave wealthy elites with private tutors and foreign degrees while stripping ordinary speakers of their only scalable global bridge. ### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by separating the usefulness of English as a shared bridge from the harms of English dominance. It showed that coordination benefits can survive through shared translation, multilingual indexing, and broader institutional recognition, while domain loss, epistemic filtering, and native-speaker advantage arise specifically from monopoly and prestige. Side B repeatedly defended the need for a lingua franca rather than proving that its dominance does more good than harm, and overstated multilingual coordination as mathematically impossible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both pressure questions directly, kept English as a bridge, and reframed translation as shared infrastructure rather than a private tax. This undercut Side B’s false choice between English dominance and total linguistic isolation.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A built the cleaner path by consistently separating a useful bridge from dominance itself (monopoly on prestige, indexing, and categories). That distinction survived B’s isolation scare and open-protocol reframes, and A’s irreversible-capacity vs reversible-translation weighing gave a clearer reason to affirm the motion as written. B kept proving the value of shared access while collapsing any non-monopoly alternative into silos, which left the native-speaker advantage and domain-loss claims under-answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.5): answers B’s pressure by keeping a bridge while rejecting monopoly, then collapses B’s case that benefits require the toll/filter.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by sharpening the decisive distinction between English as a useful bridge and English as a structural monopoly. Side A ably defended global coordination, but B demonstrated that the benefits CON praised—international collaboration, patent protection, market access—do not require *dominance* and could survive in a plurilingual system where English is one bridge among many. Meanwhile, the harms B identified—epistemic filtering, extraction of indigenous knowledge into English-legal categories, asymmetric costs borne by non-natives, and long-term demotion of mother tongues from scientific domains—are unique products of dominance itself. CON never squarely answered why non-natives must pay private editing and accent penalties while anglophones inherit the standard free, and CON’s "repatriation" examples inadvertently conceded that scientific prestige has already shifted to English. B’s closing crystallized the weighing: reversible shared translation costs versus irreversible loss of local knowledge-producing capacity. That comparison gave the cleaner reason to vote PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s dismantling of the "commons" and "open-source code" analogies in Rebuttal 1 and 2, particularly the argument that a metre has no native owners while English does, and the crystallization of the bridge-versus-dominance distinction. This reframed the debate so that CON was left defending a monopoly rather than mere interoperability.
