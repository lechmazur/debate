### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides opened strongly, but B's case proved more resilient under pressure. B's central distinction—curb space that serves rotating, flexible uses across a day versus exclusive lanes idle when demand is absent—was never cleanly answered by A, who kept recycling the "idle metal box" image without engaging B's reframe that a parked car at 2am is "completing a trip" for its owner. More decisively, A's answer to B's Q1 pressure question (conceding that reallocation "inherently means reallocating...on arterial and commercial corridors") effectively narrowed PRO's case toward the case-by-case, demand-based reform B had been advocating all along, undercutting the sweeping "most parking" scope the motion requires. B also successfully flipped A's equity argument by pointing out that car-dependent residents are often outer-neighborhood renters and shift workers with no transit alternative, a point A never substantively rebutted beyond asserting predicted mode-shift and off-street parking availability—exactly the kind of unverified prediction B flagged as insufficient. B's closing crystallized the dispute cleanly (completed trips vs. theoretical lane capacity), giving the clearer decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answer to Q1 (reframing an overnight parked car as mid-trip, serving future use, versus a bus lane that serves literally no one off-peak) combined with its exploitation of A's "arterial and commercial corridors" concession, which exposed that PRO's defense had quietly narrowed into the case-by-case reform B championed rather than the blanket reallocation the motion demands.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B won a competitive debate by making the decisive comparison clearer: completed door-to-door trips and multi-hour curb flexibility beat PRO’s theoretical lane throughput. B repeatedly damaged A’s equity and access claims with aides, tradespeople, and off-peak users who cannot mode-shift, while offering priced/time-limited parking plus targeted loading as a live alternative that does not abandon arterial improvements. A’s geometry and network-contiguity case stayed strong, but mode-shift/off-street answers to displacement pressure remained thinner than B’s “where do essential cars go” burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2): empty exclusive lanes forbid end-of-trip work a parked car is still completing, and short loading bays plus predicted mode shift do not replace ordinary parking for multi-hour care/trade visits.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A won by demonstrating that protecting most on-street parking imposes a hard geometric ceiling on urban mobility, while reserved transit and loading lanes move exponentially more people and goods in the same footprint. Side B highlighted real equity and flexibility concerns but never overcame the core inefficiency of dedicating scarce public space to static private vehicle storage, nor adequately justified why most curb space should remain parking rather than strategic movement infrastructure. Side A’s case that contiguous networks require a default priority shift, and that the status quo already fails the essential workers CON invoked, sustained its burden through the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 1 turn that on-street parking is already chronically monopolized by long-term resident storage, forcing the very essential workers Side B claims to protect to circle and double-park ([A2.3]–[A2.4]). This undercut CON’s equity defense and reframed reallocation as a solution for, rather than a threat to, tradespeople and aides.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): A built a tighter, more disciplined case: the "more, not most" distinction from the motion was established early and consistently used to defuse B's strongest recurring charge (that PRO wants to eliminate parking). A's throughput/equity/congestion mechanisms (one stored car vs. hundreds of transit riders, circling-for-parking causing congestion, non-drivers subsidizing drivers) were clear and consistently reinforced across rounds. B landed real punches—especially the "geometric reality" point that continuous bus/bike corridors functionally eliminate parking on commercial streets, and the follow-up claim that A's "side streets" answer validated B's displacement warning—but A had a substantive reply (route bikes/buses on arterials while preserving parking on parallel/side streets, a real urban-planning pattern) that B never fully dismantled, mostly reasserting "side streets lack elasticity" without a deeper mechanism. B's equity counter-examples (self-driving disabled residents, families with heavy purchases, night-shift workers) were strong but A's "brief stop vs. all-day storage" reframing partially absorbed them by redirecting attention to priority of use. Both sides executed the format well (clean Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 labeling), but A's closing more crisply crystallized the decisive principle (storage vs. access/first claim) than B's otherwise vivid but slightly more diffuse "city as destination" closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over spatial feasibility: B's pressure question ("how can cities install continuous bus/bike corridors without eliminating parking?") and follow-up charge that A's "side streets" answer was a fatal admission of displacement, versus A's counter that networks can route transit on arterials while preserving parking on parallel streets — this was the clash that most directly tested whether "more, not most" was coherent, and A's answer, while not airtight, held up better than B's rebuttal to it.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): A won by keeping the motion as written (more curb for movement rather than protecting most for storage) and converting B’s strongest examples—loading, paratransit, trades, heavy goods—into demands for brief high-value access rather than all-day warehousing. B repeatedly argued near-total eradication and displacement without fully neutralizing A’s corridor/side-street/network distinction or the throughput-vs-first-claim weighing. A’s pressure answers and closing made the decisive choice cleaner: use over storage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2): continuous corridors can drop parking while access stays nearby, and short loading/drop-off bays serve B’s vulnerable users better than eight-hour storage.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by establishing a durable "use versus storage" frame and showing that reserving more curb for movement serves businesses, transit riders, and vulnerable users better than protecting most of it for parked cars. A effectively turned B's own equity and business examples—deliveries, paratransit, and heavy-goods shoppers—into arguments for brief dedicated bays rather than all-day private storage. While B's displacement and necessity arguments created real pressure, A's corridor-specific management answer kept the geometric "eradication" claim from sticking, and A's closing crystallized the clash more decisively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over vulnerable-user curb needs, culminating in A's second rebuttal (A4.2): A answered B's pressure question by showing that tradespeople, paratransit, and shoppers require loading zones and short-term pickup spaces, not all-day parking, turning B's equity examples against CON.
