- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that uncertainty supports prominent conditional ranges rather than silence. Side A compellingly exposed model mismatch and anchoring risks, but overstated the choice as individualized precision or no numbers; labeled population ranges can still improve on qualitative warnings that reveal no magnitude.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest response was that unknown egg count does not make a conditional table useless: showing outcomes across different egg counts teaches why age, retrieval yield, and multiple cycles matter before purchase. This directly weakened Side A’s false-precision objection, though Side A effectively noted the tension between relying on labels and arguing that prominent numbers swamp qualifiers.

CON also provided a compelling alternative (stark qualitative warnings plus bans on deceptive language) that achieves PRO's educational goals without false precision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4 rebuttal, which uses PRO's premise that "prominence overwhelms qualifiers" to show why PRO's "population, not personal" disclaimer on the statistical table will fail to prevent consumers from misinterpreting the data.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B won by showing that the mandated disclosure would be unusably broad before testing and would create misleading false precision. B effectively turned A's own anchoring/prominence theory against the mandate: if big messages swamp qualifiers, a prominent "population, not personal" average anchors dangerously. B's alternative—prominent qualitative warnings plus bans on deceptive terms—addressed the harm without requiring ads to deliver prognosis-level math they cannot accurately own. A never fully resolved the epistemic gap between population models and individual patients, leaving B's comparative route cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's exposure that conditional age-and-egg tables would span an unusably wide range (6 to 20 eggs) before stimulation, and that mandating population averages would produce false precision—turning A's own logic about prominence overwhelming fine print against the mandate.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that age and egg count define the product being advertised, while consultations occur only after advertising has shaped expectations and timing. B answered the false-precision objection with ranges, explicit assumptions, standardized denominators, and uncertainty, whereas A’s deterrence scenario never established why withholding material averages until a seller-led consultation better protects autonomy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was its answer that informed refusal after seeing low average odds is autonomy exercised, not autonomy lost. This directly weakened A’s central deterrence claim and exposed that A’s alternative prioritized getting women into consultations over giving them material information before the sales process begins.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by cleanly winning the clash on autonomy and successfully framing Side B's position as paternalistic. Side A's responses in Rebuttal 2 provided a clear, workable mechanism that resolved Side B's practical objections, and decisively characterized B's reliance on consultations as "protecting bookings, not women."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.2) effectively neutralized Side B's strongest argument about deterrence by pointing out that opting out based on average odds is 'autonomy exercised,' exposing B's stance as forcing women into a sales environment.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by demonstrating that fertility ads currently mislead through selective denominators and framing, while CON's defense of clinic consultations essentially protected seller-controlled, late-stage disclosures. A directly answered the pressure questions by specifying national registry data, refresh cycles, and uncertainty disclaimers, and turned CON's individual-variation objection into a case for standardized baselines. A also successfully framed CON's deterrence argument—that prominent odds would scare women away—as paternalistic and protective of clinic bookings rather than patient autonomy. CON never substantiated that existing deception law actually catches the selective-truth harm A described, and B's closing rehashed the deterrence narrative without resolving this gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (Turn 7), where A answered CON's pressure questions with a concrete standardization proposal (national registry, refresh cycles, clinic supplements), collapsed the consultation argument by showing prominent disclosure would confirm doctor-delivered attrition rather than contradict it, and reframed deterrence as autonomy exercised rather than autonomy denied ("CON's alternative protects bookings, not women").
