### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that headquarters-based rules exempt foreign providers serving the European market, disadvantage EU firms, and merely shift verification burdens onto hospitals and banks. Side A raised legitimate concerns about diffuse impacts and foreign inspection, but overstated subpoena power as indispensable and never resolved how provider obligations could protect Europeans when major providers are headquartered abroad.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s closing exposed the key contradiction: Side A conceded that unverified foreign systems should not be deployed, so its claimed avoidance of exclusion disappears; headquarters jurisdiction only transfers responsibility from the provider controlling the model and data to the European customer.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO maintained the live route that a headquarters rule exempts the dominant US/China labs and creates domestic competitive disadvantage and flag-of-convenience / brain-drain incentives. CON's answer admitted provider mandates would not reach those foreign firms and shifted to deployer regulation, which PRO turned to collapse CON's own "digital blockade" objection - if hospitals cannot deploy unvetted code, verification is still required. CON's enforceability and reciprocity pressures were real but largely answered by market-access enforcement and high-risk narrowing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing/Rebuttal 2 turn that CON's A1 in B4 admits foreign providers are exempt and pushes burden to the Milan hospital, which simultaneously negates the geoblocking fear and exposes headquarters rule as shifting cost to the least-capable actor.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by decisively exposing the fatal flaw in Side B's headquarters-based rule: it completely exempts foreign tech giants (whose real seats are in the US/China) from EU provider mandates. Side A brilliantly dismantled Side B's fallback to "deployer liability" in the closing, pointing out that shifting the massive burden of auditing proprietary datasets from Silicon Valley giants to local European hospitals is both impractical and defeats the purpose of the regulation. Side A also effectively neutralized Side B's enforceability concerns by correctly framing market access and third-party conformity assessments as the standard, workable mechanism for international product safety, making Side B's "subpoena power" argument seem legally naive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing argument dismantling Side B's reliance on domestic deployer laws, showing that it merely shifts the impossible burden of auditing foundation models from foreign tech giants to local European hospitals, thereby conceding the failure of the headquarters rule to regulate providers.
