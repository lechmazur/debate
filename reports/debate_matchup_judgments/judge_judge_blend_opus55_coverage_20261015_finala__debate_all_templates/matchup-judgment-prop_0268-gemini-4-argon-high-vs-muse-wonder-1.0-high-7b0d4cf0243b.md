### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but A built the more complete path to victory. A's behavioral mechanism—that habitual shoppers rely on static cues and won't re-check unit prices without a salient change-flag—was never squarely defeated; B's reply that "habit will carry them past a flag too" cuts against both sides equally and doesn't explain why current unit-price display alone suffices. A also neutralized B's core "same-product/definitional chaos" objection by reframing the disclosure as a neutral weight/price fact rather than a claim about recipe continuity, and by turning B's own point (retailers already link old and new barcodes for "buy again" carts) into evidence that the infrastructure for disclosure already exists. B's strongest late move—that deterrence could simply be evaded via "relaunch as new," making the mandate unreliable either way—was a sharp dilemma, but it arrived late and was not fully pressed home against A's concrete 60–90 day regular-price rule. B's case was logically tight and cautious (avoiding invented precision), but it mostly played defense and offered no equally vivid account of why current unit pricing overcomes the habit problem A described. A's closing crystallized the clash more memorably and left B's "mismatch" framing only partially answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's reframing in Rebuttal 2 ("if manufacturers can tout formulation changes on the box, the disclosure is just neutral math about weight and price") directly defused B's definitional-chaos objection, while B's counter in Rebuttal 2 (the "relaunch as new" evasion point) was the sharpest unanswered threat to A's deterrence claim, though it came too late to fully reshape the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing why current unit prices alone do not alert habitual shoppers that a familiar deal changed. Its direct-replacement rule and retailer-database mechanism made the mandate reasonably workable, while Side A established genuine comparator ambiguity but overstated it as fatal. B also weighed deterrence effectively: shifting firms from stealth downsizing toward visible price increases advances transparency rather than defeating the policy. Some claims about costs and existing pricing law were too confident, keeping the debate competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B most decisively turned A's warning that manufacturers might use visible headline price increases instead. B showed that this is the transparency outcome the mandate seeks, while A's response that it penalizes one pricing format did not overcome the central distinction between visible and concealed changes.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A established a live route to victory by showing that shrinkflation exploits consumer autopilot and that retailers already track the necessary barcode and pricing data to flag changes cheaply. A neutralized B's administrative-burden objections with the "direct replacement" standard and analogies to existing retail pricing law, while B's definitional worries remained speculative edge cases that did not undermine the clear central scenario. A's Rebuttal 2 delivered the decisive turn by converting B's own deterrence argument into proof that the mandate would force brands back into transparent headline-price competition. B's alternative—making current unit prices clearer—was plausible but never overcame A's core claim that habitual shoppers need a change signal to break their autopilot and compare rivals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A in Rebuttal 2 turning Side B's deterrence concession—that manufacturers would simply raise headline prices instead—into proof that the mandate successfully restores price transparency and honest competition.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): The central clash was whether a shrink-triggered notice would distort comparison by flagging weight cuts while leaving headline price hikes "unmarked." B's strongest weapon was the asymmetry argument and the repeated claim that A's notice can't deliver a clean unit-price comparison because rival unit prices remain in "tiny print." A directly answered this in A4.1/A5.4 by noting the notice places the *current* unit price prominently next to the pack, so the shopper doesn't need memory of history to compare £1.11 vs £1.25 — they just need both numbers visible, and the mandate forces the shrinking product's current unit price to be salient while the rival's £1.25, even in small print, is still printed somewhere on shelf tags (standard unit pricing already exists per both sides' concession). B's closing repeats the "how?" rhetorical question without engaging that A's mechanism specifically foregrounds today's unit price alongside the notice, not just historical data. B never squarely rebuts A's "certainty over risk" framing (A4.5): invisible shrinkage is guaranteed to mislead, whereas a truthful notice might be misread only in the counterfactual B constructs (rival headline price hike also unmarked) — but that's a separate flaw in the existing unit-pricing regime, not an indictment unique to this proposal. B's skimpflation/evasion argument is clever and lands some damage, but A's response (A4.3) that reformulation carries its own costs and that "imperfect guard is not worse than no guard" is a reasonable answer that B doesn't fully dismantle in the final round — B just reasserts the loophole without showing why it's a decisive reason to reject the whole mandate rather than supplement it. On operational burden, A's answer (new barcode/listing as natural trigger point) was reasonably specific and B's rebuttal (apps picking from mixed stock) is a real friction point but minor compared to the core transparency argument. Overall A better answered the decisive pressure points and maintained a cleaner throughline (visible current price vs. invisible change), while B's repeated "asymmetry" framing, though sharp, didn't fully reckon with A's clarification that the notice doesn't suppress rival unit-price visibility, it only adds information to the shrinking product.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1, where A clarifies that the notice places the current unit price directly beside the pack for immediate comparison rather than relying on shopper memory, directly defusing B's central "shoppers can't compare" asymmetry argument — though B's closing (B5.2) pressed back hard on this without fully conceding it.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B narrowly won by sustaining the key distinction between a headline price that is visible at purchase and a quantity reduction concealed behind an unchanged price and familiar package. Side A sharply exposed the mandate's incomplete cross-brand comparison and possible stigma effect, but its claim that truthful notices would systematically steer shoppers toward worse value remained speculative. Its skimpflation objection also showed imperfect coverage rather than that exposing shrinkflation causes net harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal most decisively answered the asymmetry challenge: the notice supplies the missing quantity change without banning smaller packs, while current headline prices remain directly comparable. Its argument that partial protection against a measurable concealment is preferable to continued invisibility survived Side A's strong but less proven distortion claim.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing a structural asymmetry in Side A's proposal that Side A could not reconcile. Side A convincingly showed shrinkflation exploits inattention, but conceded throughout that rival unit prices remain in tiny, inconsistent print or hidden behind app taps. Side B demonstrated that this means the mandate would place a prominent warning and unit-price spotlight on the downsized product while leaving competing price hikes buried in fine print, potentially steering shoppers toward worse value rather than enabling genuine comparison. Side A's partial coverage beats blindness frame was weakened by Side B's showing that the coverage is not merely partial but distorted, and Side B effectively crystallized this contradiction in the closing. Side A never fully answered why singling out one pricing tactic for a history badge helps consumers when the broader unit-pricing system remains broken.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's identification of the fatal contradiction between Side A's admission that rival unit pricing is invisible or broken and Side A's claim that shoppers can use the new notice to verify the downsized product is still the best value (B4-B5), which undermined Side A's competitive-market and informed-choice justification.
