- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): A won a clear but competitive debate by keeping the written motion live: "too few" usable standard options, not only zero access. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): A opened strongly with vivid framing (the "tax" metaphor, captive-demand circularity) and pressed B hard on whether sold-out premium shows prove genuine preference. But B's case tightened as the debate progressed: B correctly noted that "several standard auditoriums during the day" does not establish spare evening capacity, forcing A to specify a concrete "fair-share" test only in Rebuttal 2—a speech that was formally clipped before the judge saw its end, a real execution cost. Critically, B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing turned A's own non-interchangeability principle (time-sensitive access matters, screenings aren't fungible) back against A's proposed fix of "rotating" prime slots across days: a family that can only attend Saturday loses exactly the kind of access A says ordinary moviegoers deserve, just relocated to a different victim. A's Closing never answered this specific point, leaving B's sharpest rebuttal live and unresolved at the end. B also offered a clearer, more honest limiting test (case-by-case balancing with a heavier burden at zero standard screenings) versus A's vaguer "floor," which strengthened B's answer to the Pressure Questions. On balance, B did the more effective work of neutralizing A's central mechanism without ever denying the real cost A identified, and ended the debate with the cleaner unrebutted argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 / Closing point that A's own rotation solution ("share prime time across days") merely relocates the exact harm A's opening decried—using A's own non-interchangeability-of-screening-times argument against A's proposed fix—landing unanswered in A's final speech.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): A won by making the motion's trigger do real work: when usable standard screenings are scarce, premium sell-outs cannot prove format demand, so dominance granted by removing the alternative is coercive rather than earned. B landed the best counter-pressure on displacement and non-interchangeable access for other films, and answered pressure cleanly, but never supplied a floor that could reliably stop zero prime standard options. A's fair-share floor kept premium optional while restoring a genuine comparison; B left affordability as a cost that could always lose. A's clipped R2 was a real execution cost, yet the remaining answers and closing still resolved the live clash more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R1 circularity turn: remove the 7 p.m. standard option, then you cannot treat the premium queue as free choice for the format—forcing B either to invent a test it would still permit schedules to evade, or to defend manufactured scarcity.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): A won by establishing that premium dominance manufactures its own demand: removing standard prime showings makes the premium queue a false signal of preference, especially for families and lower-income viewers who need usable evening times. B conceded the cost was real but never escaped the circularity or offered a concrete floor that would actually stop premium from monopolizing prime slots. B's displacement objection landed, but A showed the adjustment could come from the blockbuster's existing screen footprint, leaving B without a decisive answer to the core coercion dynamic. The clipped Rebuttal 2 cost A some momentum, but the closing crystallized the clash cleanly enough.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2's manufactured-demand rebuttal: the framing that "you cannot first remove the alternative and then cite the queue as a free choice" set the epistemic standard that B's uncertainty defense never overturned.
