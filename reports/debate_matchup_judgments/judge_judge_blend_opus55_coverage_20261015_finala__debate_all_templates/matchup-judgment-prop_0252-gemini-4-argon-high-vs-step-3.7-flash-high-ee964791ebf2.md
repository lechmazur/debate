### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): CON's strongest move was catching PRO in a genuine definitional contradiction: PRO's opening tied "genuinely educational" to academic credit and faculty oversight, but when pressed on excluding non-students, PRO pivoted to "certified skill-building outcomes" with no tuition requirement. CON correctly identified this as conceding the compliance-loophole problem—if certification is just self-documented paperwork, large corporations with HR departments trivially comply while small nonprofits and local outlets abandon internships rather than risk legal exposure. PRO never resolved this tension in later speeches, instead restating the "lightweight paperwork" claim without addressing how that paperwork simultaneously avoids a tuition paywall and remains a meaningful educational bar. CON also won the grounding battle: PRO's rebuttals leaned on invented specifics (a $12/hour stipend, a $2,000 grant, an unverifiable claim that "late 2010s" regulatory tightening converted unpaid journalism internships to paid roles), which CON effectively punctured by noting the real driver of newsroom job losses was collapsing ad revenue, not interns. CON's economic mechanism—that mandating wages makes hiring high-stakes and pushes employers toward already-credentialed candidates, while supervision costs for novices can exceed their output value—was consistently argued without comparable fake precision. PRO's equity framing (full-time unpaid roles favor the wealthy) remained a real point, and CON partially conceded it, but CON's reframe toward flexible part-time stepping stones as the actual working-class pathway, combined with the unresolved compliance/loophole critique, gave CON the cleaner, more damage-resistant case by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's [B2.1]-[B2.2] catching PRO's shift from "academic credit/faculty oversight" to "certified skill-building outcomes," further pressed in [B4.3] showing that PRO's own clarification concedes the big-corporation-loophole/small-org-elimination dynamic CON predicted.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): CON won the implementation clash. PRO opened defining educational as academic credit/faculty oversight, then pivoted to industry frameworks when pressured, creating the dilemma B exploited: strict definition = tuition paywall barring non-students; loose paperwork definition = big firms with HR keep unpaid interns while small non-profits drop them. PRO's claim that restrictions converted roles to paid jobs in late-2010s was contested and its $2k-grant funding answer was weak, leaving inequality concerns without a workable restrictive mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4 exposing the contradiction between A1.2 and A2.1 and building the regulatory dilemma - lightweight certification is a loophole for corporations but legal risk for cash-strapped charities, so heavy restriction either entrenches privilege or destroys grassroots stepping stones.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively exposing the fatal flaws in Side A's regulatory mechanism. When Side A shifted its definition of "genuinely educational" from university credit to "lightweight paperwork" to avoid a tuition paywall, Side B brilliantly trapped them, demonstrating that this merely creates a compliance loophole for large corporations with HR departments while crushing cash-strapped small nonprofits. Side B's economic reasoning regarding the marginal productivity of novices and the reality of nonprofit funding was far more grounded than Side A's shaky historical claims about 2010s newsroom regulations and easily accessible animal shelter grants. Side B controlled the clash, adapted perfectly to Side A's pivots, and offered a much cleaner, more practical reason to vote CON.
