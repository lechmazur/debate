### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B's architecture (opt-in porting, aggregate metrics rather than full reviews, "starting signal vs. ongoing operations") proved more resilient under pressure. A's strongest weapon was the "scarlet letter/unraveling" argument that declining to port signals hidden bad history, but when pressed, A's attempt to ground this in a concrete mechanism (platforms cross-checking phone numbers/licenses to detect switching veterans) was cleanly dismantled in B's closing: identity checks only confirm history with that same platform, not access to a competitor's private roster. A never recovered a purely game-theoretic version of the signaling argument that could survive B's newcomer-pooling defense (genuine first-timers and resetting veterans are observationally identical without voluntary disclosure). B also neutralized the free-rider and joint-product objections by distinguishing one-time verification from ongoing operational investment and by narrowing the transferred data to aggregate metrics rather than raw third-party reviews. A's closing restated its paradox but did not engage with these specific rebuttals, leaving B's case the more resolved and easier to adjudicate position.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's closing (B5.2-B5.3), showing that A's claimed mechanism for detecting "hiding" veterans (cross-checking IDs/phone numbers) only reveals history with the same platform and cannot access a competitor's private data, which undercut the coercion/scarlet-letter argument A relied on throughout.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): B won by showing that portability is optional, preserves the current zero-history floor, and gives reliable veterans a valuable route out of lock-in. A's adverse-inference argument was creative, but its decisive claim that platforms can identify workers who declined to transfer relied on unsupported and implausible cross-platform detection. B also persuasively distinguished portable reputation signals from the ongoing operations, vetting, and support competitors must still provide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's closing response to A's scarlet-letter mechanism was decisive: a receiving platform cannot infer prior work for a competitor merely from a license or phone number, so a non-porting veteran can resemble an ordinary newcomer. This undercut A's central claim that optional portability inevitably becomes a permanent blacklist.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A wins decisively by dismantling Side B's key empirical claims and logical contradictions. While Side B presented a rhetorically appealing "paradox" regarding opt-in coercion, Side A successfully contextualized it against the status quo, proving that the worst-case scenario under the motion is identical to the universal reality workers face today. The decisive moment was Side A's closing, which cleanly exposed the factual flaw in Side B's Rebuttal 2: checking a driver's license or phone number does not grant a platform access to a competitor's private employment roster, meaning a resetting veteran truly does blend in with genuine newcomers. Side A also effectively used pressure questions to highlight Side B's contradictory claims about whether ported data is "meaningless" or "powerful," forcing Side B into a weak defensive posture.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing dismantling of Side B's claim that platforms could use license and phone cross-checks to identify workers hiding bad reviews, logically proving that a receiving app has no mechanism to access a competitor's private roster.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): B landed a real hit early by exposing A's opening as overreaching the motion's "competing platforms" scope (fast food vs. home repair), forcing A to retreat to weaker within-market claims. But the decisive exchange came in the Pressure Questions round: A forced B to admit, via A1 ("Nothing stops them"), that platforms remain free to discount imported ratings entirely, which directly undercuts B's own earlier promises of restored "priority dispatch" and broken lock-in. B's fallback—"even partial credit is enough"—is a visible retreat from the case's original strength, and A's closing crystallized this concession cleanly alongside B's parallel retreat on fraud-filter verification (from "run every fraud filter" to "just dashboard numbers"). A's dilemma (either platforms trust unverifiable imported data, risking safety, or they ignore it and lock-in persists) was never fully resolved by B, who ended up defending a watered-down version of the original claim. B's "clean slate" rebuttal (you can still withhold data) had some force but didn't fully answer A's signaling-stigma point. Overall A pressed harder and more effectively, and B's concessions under pressure were the turning point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Questions (A3.1/A3.2) forcing B into "Nothing stops them" [B4.1] and the fraud-filter walk-back [B4.3], which A then used in Rebuttal 2 and Closing to expose the central contradiction in B's case.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by establishing a difficult efficacy-safety dilemma and sustaining it through the closing. If platforms may ignore sanitized rival data, the promised mobility gains are uncertain; if they use it to bypass probation or award priority, missing fraud and safety context creates risk. Side B plausibly defended partial informational value, but repeatedly asserted rather than demonstrated that partial credit would occur, while never adequately answering A's adverse-signaling argument about voluntary non-transfer. B's unsupported 30-40% and six-week figures also weakened its grounding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange paired B's concessions that platforms may discount imported records and that transfers exclude underlying complaints and telemetry. A used those concessions to argue that the tool either fails to deliver meaningful mobility or induces unsafe reliance, while B answered mainly by assuming partial credit would still be granted.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing a fatal contradiction in Side A's case: Side A promised the mandate would secure priority dispatch while simultaneously claiming platforms weren't forced to trust imported data. When Side A retreated to arguing that "partial credit" is better than nothing, Side B effectively demonstrated that liability-conscious platforms would give zero credit to stripped-down summary metrics lacking underlying safety logs. Side B also decisively won the "clean slate" clash by explaining market signaling, which Side A failed to rebut.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, which weaponized Side A's own concessions from the Pressure Questions round (admitting platforms could discount ratings entirely and that the tool excludes raw safety logs) to dismantle Side A's remaining claims of worker mobility.
