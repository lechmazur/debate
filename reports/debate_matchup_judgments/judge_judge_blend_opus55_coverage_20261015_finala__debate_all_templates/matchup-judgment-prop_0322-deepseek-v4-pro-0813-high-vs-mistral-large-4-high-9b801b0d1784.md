### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): A built a tighter, more coherent line: existing fraud/publicity law already covers unauthorized or deceptive clones, while authorized truthful endorsements cause no legally cognizable harm, making the mandate both redundant and overbroad/vague (comedy filters, accessibility tools, translation). A's sharpest moment was exposing the internal tension in B's case: B argued listeners cannot detect AI clones (justifying disclosure) yet also claimed a simple label would fix the problem — if the clone is undetectable, a label is either ignored or, if made unmissable, destroys the ad's persuasive value, intensifying rather than resolving the compelled-speech problem. B's rebuttal 2 tried to escape this by relocating enforcement to a 'pre-publication paper trail' (vendor metadata, sponsor agreements, audits), but A correctly noted this contradicts B's earlier framing of the rule as a costless 'short label,' revealing a heavier compliance burden than B had admitted, which is exactly the chilling-effect/overbreadth harm A had been pressing from the opening. B's closing reasserted a compelling moral frame ('the texture is the ad') but didn't fully repair the labeling-mechanism contradiction or the overbreadth/vagueness critique. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions format correctly with proper A1/A2 answers, but A's answers were more direct and damaging, while B's answers, though clever, ultimately conceded ground that strengthened A's redundancy and burden arguments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 catch of B's internal contradiction (undetectable-therefore-no-fraud-remedy vs. a-label-will-fix-it) in [A2.1], and A's follow-up in Rebuttal 2 showing B's 'short label' claim had quietly become a full compliance/paper-trail regime, was the most decisive exchange of the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that synthetic delivery is material precisely because host-read advertising derives value from the apparent personal act of speaking, not merely the abstract existence of a favorable opinion. A also established that an ex ante factual label addresses an undetectable substitution more effectively than ex post fraud remedies. Side B raised credible overbreadth and compliance concerns, but repeatedly exaggerated a narrow label into a major infrastructure burden and never persuasively distinguished AI disclosure from ordinary commercial disclosures.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A decisively answered B's claimed contradiction between an undetectable clone and effective disclosure: an audible or visually unavoidable notice supplies the very information the synthetic voice conceals. B's reply that such a notice might reduce persuasive power effectively reinforced A's claim that the concealed production method is material.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): PRO won the central deception-and-enforcement clash. CON relied on existing fraud/publicity law, but PRO's Q1 exposed that consent blocks those claims and undetectable clones leave listeners with no cue to complain. PRO answered CON's detection paradox with a plausible ex ante paper-trail model parallel to "sponsored" enforcement, while CON's answer that authorized clones cause no cognizable harm and that audible labels are merely compelled speech failed to neutralize the argument that host-read value is the personal delivery itself - the "texture is the ad."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 - shifting enforcement from listener detection to pre-publication records and reframing the label as medium-disclosure not sincerity-taint, which directly neutralized CON's contradiction and no-harm claims.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built a tighter, more disciplined case around the actual text of the motion: it has no carve-out for consented, host-approved AI use, so the rule sweeps in laryngitis-style self-cloning, audio repair, and other harmless cases where no deception exists. A's pressure question forced PRO to bite the bullet and admit disclosure is required even for fully approved clones, which PRO tried to justify by redefining "material fact" as the literal vocal cords rather than consent/approval. A exploited that concession decisively in Rebuttal 2 and the closing, pointing out the asymmetry with ghostwritten copy, hired voice actors, and scripted ad reads, none of which require disclosure despite being equally "not spontaneous." PRO's opening and first rebuttal were vivid and emotionally compelling (the insomnia-cure hypothetical, the FTC analogy, consent-under-duress scenario), but PRO never resolved the overbreadth problem once A isolated it, and the late "voice as commodity" reframe in Rebuttal 2/Closing arrived without enough support to overcome the comparison to accepted non-spontaneous endorsement practices. Both sides executed the format cleanly (clear Q1/Q2 and A1/A2), but A's answers and framing were more consistently targeted at the literal motion text, making the debate easier to decide in CON's favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.5), capitalizing on PRO's pressure-question concession that disclosure is required even for a host-approved, consented AI clone (the laryngitis case), and using it to show the rule targets the technology itself rather than any deception, while contrasting it with unlabeled ghostwriting and voice-actor practice.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing the gap between deceptive impersonation and the broader mandate actually proposed. Side A established that unauthorized cloning can counterfeit trust, but its concession that even a host-written, host-approved laryngitis clone requires labeling left it relying on an asserted consumer interest in physical vocal performance. Side B more effectively argued that authorization and endorsement responsibility are the material facts, while the mandate remains overbroad and difficult to delimit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's laryngitis example was decisive. It forced Side A to admit that disclosure applies despite full host approval, allowing Side B to reframe the law as regulating a production tool rather than targeting theft or deceptive endorsement.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): CON won the materiality and overbreadth clash. PRO conceded in B4 that even a host-written, host-approved clone used for laryngitis must be labeled, confirming A's claim the rule bans a tool, not deception. A showed sponsor disclosure + authorization already gives the material fact, existing fraud/right-of-publicity covers theft, and the AI continuum creates unworkable vagueness for small creators. PRO never explained why medium alone is material when the endorsement is authorized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's laryngitis Q in Pressure and B's A1 answer in Rebuttal 2 - B admitted Yes disclosure even with full consent, which A then exploited as proof the proposal targets delivery method, not consumer deception.
