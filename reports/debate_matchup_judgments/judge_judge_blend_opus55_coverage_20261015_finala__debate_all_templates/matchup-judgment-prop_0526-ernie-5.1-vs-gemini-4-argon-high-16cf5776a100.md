### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): The decisive clash was definitional: PRO insisted the proposition's "comparable long-term integration outcomes" clause folds in all fiscal/economic/societal effects, making any economic-selection advantage disappear by stipulation. CON offered a sharper, more defensible reading: integration (employment, language, civic participation) is distinct from occupational/skill composition, so two cohorts can have identical integration scores while only one satisfies acute sector-specific shortages (the recurring clerk vs. ICU nurse example). PRO never adequately closed this gap—asserting that "fiscal impact" is already covered by parity is a stretch that CON correctly flagged as a quiet redefinition of PRO's own earlier terms. CON also won the sharpest rhetorical exchange of the debate: when PRO used the developing-country nurse example to call credential-based exclusion "hereditary gatekeeping dressed as fairness," CON turned that exact language back on PRO, noting that family reunification—not economic selection—is the system that structurally requires a blood/marital tie regardless of capability, while economic selection's credentialing issues are a fixable implementation flaw rather than a structural feature. PRO's closing restated the parity-therefore-principle argument without resolving either the integration/skill-matching distinction or the gatekeeping reversal. Both sides executed the format cleanly (correct Q1/Q2 and A1/A2), but CON's answers were more directly responsive, while PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2 largely reasserted the premise rather than engaging the clerk/nurse distinction. CON's case was narrower, better defended under pressure, and left the cleaner reason to vote for it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's reversal of PRO's "hereditary gatekeeping dressed as fairness" language (B4.5/B5.3) against PRO's own family-reunification model, combined with the recurring clerk-vs-ICU-nurse distinction between integration and skill-matching, which PRO never fully neutralized.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B won by correctly distinguishing comparable integration from identical sector-specific contributions. Side A repeatedly expanded the premise to include fiscal effects and healthcare staffing without justification, while still claiming superior family benefits. Most damagingly, A condemned excluding a qualified nurse for lacking blood ties, which describes family priority rather than economic selection. B exposed that reversal clearly and carried it through the closing. Both sides used some invalid reference labels, but this did not overturn B’s decisive substantive advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal identified that A had reversed the policies: economic selection admits the qualified nurse on capability, while family priority is what favors an applicant with existing blood ties. A repeated rather than repaired this mistake in closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A won by exposing that PRO misapplied the parity condition and accidentally conceded the moral argument. CON demonstrated that comparable individual integration does not erase sector-specific shortages, caught PRO in a double standard on fiscal outcomes, and turned PRO’s nurse example to show family reunification—not economic selection—practices the hereditary gatekeeping PRO condemned.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.5–A4.6: CON’s identification that PRO, in attempting to indict economic selection with the developing-country nurse, had in fact described family reunification’s blood-tie requirement, collapsing PRO’s moral high ground.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): A set the terms of the clash early by catching B in a self-defeating move: B's opening leaned heavily on claims that family migrants have worse economic outcomes, slower integration, and higher per-capita costs — all of which directly contradict the motion's stipulation that long-term integration outcomes are comparable. A's Rebuttal 1 named this contradiction sharply ("fights the premise of this debate"), and B never fully recovered; B2.1 conceded the fiscal point "fades" under the premise but kept reviving dependent-cost arguments anyway, which A's pressure questions and Rebuttal 2 exploited again. B pivoted to a distributive-fairness/anti-privilege frame ("contribution not kinship"), which was its strongest surviving argument, but it never squarely answered A's "state's duty runs to its own residents, not to non-citizen strangers abroad" counter, nor A's clever scope distinction in A4.2 (the premise equalizes migrant trajectories, not spillover benefits to existing residents). A's closing crystallized this into a clean asymmetry: zero public cost difference versus severe, avoidable harm to insiders. B's closing restated the fairness frame well but did not dismantle A's duty-based rebuttal or the scope-distinction move, leaving A with the more resolved and more consistently defended case by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 exposing that B's opening attacks (worse earnings, enclaves, higher costs) contradict the stipulated "comparable outcomes" premise — a contradiction B never fully escaped despite later pivoting to a fairness-based argument.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by exploiting the stipulated comparability of long-term integration and identifying a persuasive asymmetry: if public outcomes are similar, protecting an existing resident's family life supplies a concrete tie-breaker. Side B's scarcity and merit framing remained relevant, especially for extended-family admissions, but it repeatedly revived fiscal, linguistic, and contribution differences after conceding that those concerns fade. Its dismissal of family separation as merely emotional never adequately outweighed the direct harm to current residents. Both sides also used some incorrect reference IDs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange was the dilemma over dependents: if economically selected migrants may bring spouses and children, Side B loses much of its claimed service-cost advantage; if they may not, its retention case weakens and the family-separation harm returns. Side B distinguished principal selection from kinship-based slots, but did not fully resolve that dilemma.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by exposing that Side A's fiscal and efficiency objections either contradicted the comparable-outcomes premise or collapsed into a dilemma: economic migrants either bring dependents too, erasing the per-capita cost advantage, or are barred, fracturing households and driving talent away. Once that empirical attack fell, Side B's framing of state duty to existing residents and the asymmetry of severe private harm versus zero public gain provided the cleaner tie-breaker. Side A's fairness argument stayed live but never overcame the burden that a state owes its own members the bedrock dignity of family unity when no broader cost exists.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal [B4.1-B4.3], which leveraged Side A's earlier concession that fiscal worries fade under comparable outcomes to dismantle the dependent-cost attack and pose the unanswerable retention dilemma.
