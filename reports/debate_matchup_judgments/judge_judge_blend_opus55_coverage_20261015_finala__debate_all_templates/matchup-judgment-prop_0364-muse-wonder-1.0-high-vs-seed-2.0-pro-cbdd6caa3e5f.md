### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that data used to calculate compensation functions like a pay stub, while limiting disclosure to affected creators and separating standardized measurement from standardized pay. Side A’s predicted industry-wide abandonment of performance pay remained largely asserted and sat uneasily beside its admission that ordinary creators lack leverage to negotiate audits themselves.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s closing exposed the key contradiction: Side A proposed individually negotiated audit rights while simultaneously arguing that most creators lack the leverage to obtain better terms. That made a baseline disclosure mandate the more credible remedy.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by land decisive, well-grounded rebuttals against Side A's three core harms. PRO's historical analogy to box office and Nielsen ratings showed that transparent reach data and performance pay coexisted for decades. Its "thermometer" distinction—standardizing a basket of metrics without dictating which metric determines pay—defanged CON's claim that any rule necessarily picks winners and losers. PRO also convincingly separated aggregate viewership (a pay stub) from operational secrets (a playbook), undercutting the competitive-leakage panic. CON's prediction of an industry-wide shift to flat buyouts was asserted rather than substantiated, and it could not answer why private, opaque platform definitions are more trustworthy than a public measurement standard. The closing crystallized the clash effectively: unverifiable upside is not upside.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's "thermometer" rebuttal at [B2.3] and [B4.2], which argued that standardizing a basket of viewing metrics does not force all creators to be paid for the same measure, decisively neutralized CON's claim that any single definition of reach would arbitrarily favor some content types over others.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B built a highly compelling case around the core principle that if compensation is tied to a metric, the payee must be able to verify it. Side B effectively neutralized Side A's competitive harm and standardization arguments by distinguishing between aggregate viewership and proprietary algorithms, and by proposing a flexible basket of metrics that preserves contractual freedom. Side B's decisive blow came in the closing, where it exposed a fatal contradiction in Side A's case: Side A argued that ordinary creators lack the leverage to demand higher upfront pay if bonuses disappear, yet simultaneously claimed they currently have the leverage to negotiate independent audit clauses. Side B's framing that "unverifiable upside is discretion dressed as reward" perfectly crystallized the debate and secured a clear victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing argument exposing Side A's contradiction regarding creator leverage—pointing out that creators who lack the power to negotiate better upfront fees also lack the power to negotiate robust audit clauses, rendering Side A's defense of the status quo incoherent.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won a competitive debate by offering automatic confidential verification that addressed pay accuracy while avoiding broader disclosure risks. Side B had the stronger moral slogan, but narrowed “standardized” into basic contract consistency and repeatedly treated A’s enhanced audit model as the weaker status quo despite A directly distinguishing them. B also failed to show why recipient-level standardized data would catch errors better than an auditor able to compare records across titles.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both pressure questions directly, distinguished automatic verification from patchy historical audit rights, and showed that B’s castmate example could not establish underpayment without knowing contract rates and role weightings.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON successfully distinguished the motion’s demand for standardized, shareable viewing data from the genuine goal of pay verification, showing that confidential audits achieve fairness without the collateral harms of metric distortion and competitive leaks. PRO’s attempt to define “standardized” narrowly as mere contract consistency was caught in a squeeze: if modest, it is redundant with auditing; if broad, it imposes an arbitrary common currency. CON’s rebuttal of the castmate-comparison example and its standing leak concern remained largely unaddressed, giving CON the cleaner path to victory despite PRO’s strong fairness intuition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3-A4.4: CON exposes the definitional squeeze on “standardized” and shows that cross-castmate pay comparison fails without contract-tier data, undermining the claim that standardized disclosure is necessary to catch systemic bias.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A built a much stronger, logically rigorous case by distinguishing between targeted, confidential pay verification and the creation of a standardized, shareable data currency. Side A effectively trapped Side B on the definition of "standardized," proving that Side B's redefinition either reduced to basic contract enforcement (which routine audits can handle) or required an imposed industry metric that distorts creative incentives. Side A's rebuttals were sharper, particularly in dismantling Side B's castmate comparison, and Side A's closing perfectly crystallized the core clash without relying on empty rhetoric.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, specifically A4.3 and A4.4, where they dismantled Side B's redefinition of "standardized" and logically destroyed the castmate comparison by pointing out that seeing a reach count doesn't reveal a castmate's negotiated rate, role weighting, or contract tier.
