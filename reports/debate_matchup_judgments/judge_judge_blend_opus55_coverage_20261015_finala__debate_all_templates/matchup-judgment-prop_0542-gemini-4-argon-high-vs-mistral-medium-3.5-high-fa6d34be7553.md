- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built a tighter, more internally consistent case: it conceded the probabilistic nature of all pretrial risk assessment and then showed that human judges make the same probabilistic bets as algorithms but without auditability, turning CON's "transparent, appealable human judgment" claim into a liability rather than an advantage. CON's core themes (algorithmic bias, due process, irreversible harm of wrongful detention) were substantive and repeated with some force, but CON never squarely answered A's recurring point that unaudited judicial intuition processes the same biased arrest records with less accountability, nor did CON engage the asymmetry argument (death vs. recoverable disruption) on its own terms, instead restating that wrongful detention is "equally irreversible" without addressing the scale/severity distinction A pressed. The debate progressed with A sharpening its framing each round while CON largely reasserted earlier points.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 move ("CON condemns tools for bias yet trusts unaudited human judgment") combined with the Pressure Questions exchange, where CON's evasive non-answer on an acceptable false-positive rate was then used by A in Rebuttal 2 and the closing to show CON cannot escape the same probabilistic logic it criticizes.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that every detention decision already predicts future risk, so the relevant comparison is auditable, validated assessment versus uncalibrated judicial intuition using much of the same information. Side A established real concerns about bias and false positives but never demonstrated why its qualitative imminent-danger standard avoids those problems, and its claim that human judgment is more transparent remained inadequately defended. Side B occasionally overstated the hypothetical violent outcomes as murders, but its comparative case remained substantially stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s pressure questions exposed Side A’s double standard: judges consult the same potentially biased records and also generate false positives. Side A answered by invoking appealability and a qualitative evidentiary standard, but did not explain how either makes prediction non-probabilistic or judicial assumptions genuinely auditable.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO won by establishing that pretrial detention decisions are inherently probabilistic and showing that validated tools are more auditable and consistent than unguided judicial intuition or cash bail.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7), where they directly answered CON's pressure questions, exposed CON's double standard of demanding flawless precision from algorithms while exempting uncalibrated human judgment, and vividly turned CON's own 100-defendant hypothetical against them by showing it required accepting twenty preventable homicides.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B built the more coherent and adaptive case. It exploited A's key concession (A2.1: it is a "statistical inevitability" that most flagged defendants won't reoffend violently) relentlessly, turning it into both a constitutional due-process argument (statistical group profiles can't satisfy Salerno's individualized "concrete threat" standard) and a safety-mechanism argument (mass false-positive detention is criminogenic via job/housing loss and coerced pleas). B's pressure questions were pointed and forced A into weak answers: A4.1 analogized to sentencing/parole, which B correctly noted applies to convicted people, not pretrial (innocent) defendants, and A4.2 never substantively engaged with the criminogenic-harm mechanism, instead repeating "temporary vs. permanent" framing. B explicitly and accurately called out this evasion in both Rebuttal 2 and Closing, leaving a key line of clash unanswered by A. A's case remained forceful and morally clear but largely static across rounds, relying on repeated assertions of harm asymmetry rather than engaging with B's base-rate/false-positive mechanics or the bias-laundering argument. B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly by naming the unanswered question, while A's closing mostly recapitulated its opening framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answers to A's pressure questions (A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2, B4.1–B4.2) distinguishing Salerno's individualized hearing standard from pretrial algorithms built on convicted-population data, combined with B's repeated, unanswered criminogenic-harm argument that A never substantively rebutted.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by turning Side B’s concession about majority false positives into a sustained challenge on due process, aggregate harm, and the claimed superiority over cash bail. Side B repeatedly asserted that irreversible violence outweighs temporary detention, but minimized lasting job, housing, and plea consequences and never established why broader detention’s aggregate harms were smaller. A somewhat overclaimed that destabilization necessarily creates more crime than detention prevents, yet B’s unsupported harm asymmetry and repeated conflation of broader detention with replacing cash bail left its central case weaker.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to B4.2 was decisive: it showed that calling detention temporary does not answer the lasting destabilization of numerous false positives, while B merely repeated that violent harm is graver without resolving the aggregate comparison.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by converting Side B's central concession—that most defendants in the highest-risk tier will not reoffend violently—into a decisive due-process problem and an empirical safety dilemma. A showed that a group profile wrong most of the time cannot be a constitutionally adequate individualized threat, that B's sentencing/parole analogy collapsed because pretrial defendants are legally innocent, and that B never engaged the criminogenic mechanism by which mass false-positive detention permanently destroys lives and manufactures future crime. A's closing cleanly crystallized these live burdens, while B's closing simply repeated the harm-asymmetry frame without answering A's pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal, which used Side B's concession on false-positive predominance to undermine both the constitutional concrete-threat standard and the safety calculus, while exposing the inapt sentencing/parole analogy and B's evasion of the criminogenic destabilization argument.
