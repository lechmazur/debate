### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B won by introducing and sustaining a cleaner definitional distinction: meritocracy is a claim about what decides at the point of selection (demonstrated ability vs. inherited title), not a claim that childhoods are equal. This let B absorb A's "cumulative advantage" evidence (SAT gaps, tutoring, stability) as upstream input inequality rather than proof that selection itself is rigged. B's pressure question ("what success could ever count as merit under your logic?") exposed that A's framework risks making "merit" unfalsifiable, and A's subsequent answers conceded "genuine merit exists" and that privilege is only "probabilistic" — concessions B correctly flagged in Rebuttal 2 and Closing as abandoning the sweeping version of the motion. A's second rebuttal was notably thin and did not recover from this trap, while B's closing crisply reiterated why probabilistic tilt falls short of the proposition's stronger claim. A's case remained forceful on mechanisms (legacy admissions, unpaid internships, hidden curriculum) but never resolved the "proves too much" charge or B's point that elites already openly discuss privilege, leaving B's reframing as the dominant lens for judging the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's "that standard dissolves merit entirely, then complains it cannot find it" response to A's laundering argument (Rebuttal 2), combined with the pressure question forcing A to concede merit is real and privilege only probabilistic — this exchange most decisively reframed the debate in B's favor.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully narrowing the definition of meritocracy to a selection mechanism based on demonstrated competence, rather than a promise of identical childhoods. Side A fails to overcome B's argument that mastering difficult skills represents genuine, selectable ability regardless of the wealthy inputs that helped cultivate them, and B effectively uses A's concessions to show the system functions as intended.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first rebuttal decisively neutered A's "laundering" charge by pointing out that if every formative advantage invalidates achievement, the standard dissolves merit entirely.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively controlling the definition of meritocracy. Side A argued that because wealth buys the inputs for skill development, selecting for those skills is merely class reproduction. Side B effectively countered that this framework makes merit impossible by default—even a poor student helped by a public library would lack "merit"—and that selecting for demonstrated competence is fundamentally different from aristocratic inheritance. Side B also neutralized Side A's evidence, noting that eliminating standardized tests shifts selection to more wealth-biased metrics, and that legacy preferences and unpaid internships are marginal. Side A's concession in Rebuttal 2 that privilege is merely "probabilistic" fatally undermined their claim of a deterministic inheritance system, allowing Side B to cleanly close the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 argument that eliminating standardized tests shifts selection to more wealth-biased metrics like essays, combined with the logical trap that defining all formative advantages as "reproduced privilege" makes merit impossible by definition.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built a coherent causal account (unequal pre-admission preparation converting into credentials, the "loaded dice" and "price of entry" framings) and explicitly disclaimed determinism from the start, which preempted B's main counter. B's central rebuttal — that first-gen/under-resourced students who get in perform at parity, therefore merit dominates — is vulnerable to an obvious selection-effect critique, and A raised that critique twice (A2.3, A4.2) without B ever substantively answering it; B simply repeated the parity claim in both Rebuttal 2 and Closing. B also leaned on an unsupported precise statistic ("nearly a third of Ivy League classes are first-gen") that reads as brittle fake precision, while A's reasoning stayed at the level of plausible mechanism and analogy without overclaiming. A's closing explicitly turned B's own evidence against it (survivors proving the filter is harsher, not that privilege is irrelevant), giving the clearer final reason to vote; B's closing mostly recapped without engaging that reframe.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's repeated selection-effect point (A2.3, restated and sharpened in A4.2/A4.3) — that parity among admitted under-resourced students is exactly what a harsher filter predicts, not evidence against class-based reproduction — which B never directly rebutted and which A used to reframe its closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won decisively by dismantling Side B's core logic. When B argued that disadvantaged students perform equally well once admitted, A brilliantly framed this as survivorship bias: a harsher filter means only the most exceptional disadvantaged students make it, which explains the parity. B never answered this, clinging to a deterministic strawman that A had repeatedly clarified.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 flawlessly turned Side B's evidence of equal performance into proof of a harsher, class-based filter (survivorship bias).
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A dominated the debate by systematically dismantling Side B's core logical flaw: conflating a systemic "primary driver" with strict determinism. Side A's "loaded dice" analogy and explanation of survivorship bias (the "harsher filter" for under-resourced students) decisively neutralized Side B's main evidence regarding post-admission parity. Side B relied on repetitive strawmen, failed to engage with the psychological "overstated" aspect of the prompt, and used questionable precision ("nearly a third") to support its claims, whereas Side A maintained strong epistemic discipline and analytical depth throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they explained that under-resourced high scorers matching privileged peers is exactly what a harsher filter predicts (survivorship bias), completely deflating Side B's main empirical argument.
