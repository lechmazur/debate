### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully turning Side B's regressivity argument against itself, framing the disease burden of diabetes as a much harsher, unavoidable regressive tax. Side A also laid a brilliant structural trap by pointing out that the motion mandates a tax high enough to 'meaningfully shift consumption,' meaning Side B's worst-case scenario of 'certain financial extraction with no health gains' inherently contradicts the premise of the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 double-bind: if the tax shifts consumption, families avoid the cost and gain health; if it doesn't, it's not the tax defended by the motion.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): PRO won the central clash on substitution and regressivity. A turned B's own Mexico example into proof price works, then closed the loophole: motion taxes all SSBs so juice-drink shift is still taxed and reformulation makes burden avoidable. B's elastic/inelastic split and dairy/alcohol escape left low-sugar water as the taxed alternative, conceding health gain. PRO's reframe – regressive in cents, progressive in health, disease as the more unavoidable tax – was never neutralized, and tobacco analogy answered the duress pressure. B kept moral regressivity but no design answer to reformulation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2/A4 turning substitution into concession and using motion definition to close juice loophole – showing if consumers substitute they prove price mechanism, and what remains to switch to is lower-sugar, plus reformulated drinks pay no tax at all.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A built a stronger analytical framework by anchoring tightly to the motion's requirement that the tax must be "high enough to meaningfully shift consumption." This allowed A to neutralize B's "tax fails to change behavior" arguments as outside the motion's scope. A also effectively reframed the regressivity debate by contrasting the avoidable financial cost of the tax with the unavoidable, highly regressive health costs of the status quo (diabetes, amputations). Side B's rhetorical flourishes ("taxing poverty to cure poverty") were memorable and emotionally resonant, but B struggled to resolve the tension between claiming consumers are too addicted to change behavior and claiming they will simply substitute to other sugary drinks. Furthermore, B's alternative (funding prevention through general revenue) failed to address A's core mechanism of using price signals to change the default choice on the shelf and drive industry reformulation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's "certain extraction vs. speculative health" dichotomy in Rebuttal 2. A pointed out that if the tax successfully shifts consumption (as the motion requires), the financial extraction from the poor is low because they switch to untaxed alternatives, while the health gain is real. If it fails to shift consumption, it is not the tax the motion defends. This logical trap effectively neutralized B's primary impact calculus.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): CON won by brilliantly weaponizing PRO's own example of the UK tax, framing it as a producer-side reformulation levy that achieved health benefits without burdening poor consumers, thereby proving the motion's regressive consumer tax unnecessary. PRO also made a critical unforced error in Pressure Q1 by inexplicably feeding CON their own argument about inelastic heavy drinkers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO's Q1, which correctly identified that PRO had just asked a question that proved CON's own point about heavy drinkers getting the bill without the cure.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): CON built the decisive live route: the high consumer price is not necessary to get the sugar out. B exposed PRO's contradiction between "most likely to switch" and "few dollars," showed the inelastic heaviest-risk users become poorer and no healthier, and turned PRO's best example - Britain's tiered producer levy - into proof that reformulation cuts sugar before shoppers pay more. PRO's collapse of producer vs consumer tax as the "same instrument" in closing left the motion's "even if" clause unjustified despite B's clipping cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2's contradiction trap (A2.1 vs A2.4) plus the Philadelphia car-vs-corner-store avoidance point, sharpened by B's Q1, forced PRO to concede in A4.1 that the tax must register in tight budgets while still calling it trivial - crystallizing CON's regressivity-without-necessity win.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively attacking the necessity of the regressive burden. By using the UK example to show that producer-targeted levies can reduce sugar without passing the cost to poor consumers, B proved that the "even if" condition of the motion is unjustified. Side A's attempt to collapse the distinction between producer and consumer taxes in the closing was effectively exposed by B as a fatal concession, making B's path to victory clear and logically superior.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing response to Side A's claim that producer and consumer taxes are "the same instrument," sharply pointing out that the incidence of the burden is the exact question the motion asks.
