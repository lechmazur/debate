### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by distinguishing the importance of financial knowledge from the effectiveness of a mandatory standalone course. A established immediacy and unequal family access, but largely asserted that exposure would prevent harmful behavior. B more fully compared alternatives, answered the resource contradiction, and showed concrete opportunity costs after A conceded that an elective must be displaced.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was the dilemma that a genuinely serious course necessarily consumes substantial curriculum and staffing, while a lighter applied-numeracy version can be embedded in existing classes. A never fully showed why the displaced credential, language, or career elective was less valuable, nor why course completion reliably creates durable protection.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B won by forcing the live comparison: PRO had to show a broad early pass-to-graduate course beats embedding plus targeted help at real decisions, not merely that knowledge beats ignorance. A’s equality and age-18 cliff stayed vivid, but B kept converting them into narrower checklist needs, exposure fade, and real elective/tradeoff costs, while answering capacity pressure as a focused existing-touchpoint add-on rather than a new semester. A never closed the displacement-vs-seriousness bind or the behavior-retention burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s bind that a course cannot be both costless applied math/reading and a serious multi-topic mandate—if serious it displaces; if applied math the separate gate is unnecessary—most damaged A’s route.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won by building a highly realistic, structurally sound case that exposed the practical flaws of a blunt graduation mandate. Side A effectively argued that a serious standalone course creates severe opportunity costs (displacing valuable career or college-prep electives) and becomes a punitive graduation hurdle in under-resourced schools. While Side B relied heavily on the "financial cliff at eighteen" and the moral imperative of equality, they struggled to overcome Side A's sharp distinction between the heavy resource burden of staffing a full semester course versus the lightweight reality of adding targeted checklists to existing FAFSA and counseling nights. Side A also brilliantly neutralized Side B's claim that "just-in-time help is an illusion because counselors don't go to car dealerships" by pointing out that a high school course taken at sixteen doesn't go to the dealership either. Side A's alternative of embedding practical numeracy into core classes and targeting help at the moment of decision was far more persuasive and grounded in educational reality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled Side B's pressure by clearly distinguishing the resource burden of a full semester course (staffing, grading, credit recovery) from targeted help (adding a checklist to existing application nights), and sharply noted that a high school course taken at sixteen doesn't accompany a student to a car dealership any more than a counselor does.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): A won by establishing that near-graduation financial decisions are universal, consequential, and not adequately covered by generic literacy or optional exposure. A directly answered knowledge decay by distinguishing durable principles from changing forms, and answered the diploma objection with supported competency requirements analogous to other required courses. B's embedded-modules alternative was credible, and the graduation-barrier attack created real pressure, but B repeatedly caricatured practical instruction as fleeting trivia and overstated the inevitability of diploma denial without showing that the course would be uniquely difficult or exclusionary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest response was that simulations are the vehicle for durable concepts: algebra can calculate interest, but it does not teach students where fees hide, how minimum payments obscure total cost, or which questions to ask. This materially weakened B's claim that foundational math and reading make a dedicated course redundant.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): A won by keeping the motion's comparison live: requirement vs elective/family exposure. Equity-plus-timing and the seatbelt weighing held up better than B's decay and foundational-skills case. B's strongest live route—the diploma-as-wrecking-ball turn—forced real pressure, but A answered it directly with the parity-to-existing-requirements + supports reply and showed embedding without protected time recreates uneven exposure. B never fully neutralized the guarantee problem; A crystallized the decisive clash more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's seatbelt-to-wrecking-ball diploma gate (B2.1) vs A's A1 reply treating finance like other required courses with recovery supports (A4.1), which kept equity from flipping against the mandate.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully defending the core mechanism of the proposition—a standalone graduation requirement—against Side A's attacks. Side B effectively neutralized the "knowledge decay" argument by framing senior year as just-in-time and distinguishing durable principles from changing specifics. Crucially, Side B dismantled Side A's primary offensive regarding the "diploma denial" equity harm by comparing it to existing foundational requirements like Algebra and highlighting standard remediation pathways. Side B also exposed Side A's alternative (embedding via state standards) as uneven "elective exposure with better branding." Side B's comparative framing on equity and guaranteed protection was cleaner, more realistic, and more persuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they directly answered the pressure questions by defending the graduation requirement through standard remediation practices and decisively refuted the "embedding" alternative by pointing out that without a standalone credit requirement, coverage relies on individual teacher bandwidth, failing to solve the equity gap.
