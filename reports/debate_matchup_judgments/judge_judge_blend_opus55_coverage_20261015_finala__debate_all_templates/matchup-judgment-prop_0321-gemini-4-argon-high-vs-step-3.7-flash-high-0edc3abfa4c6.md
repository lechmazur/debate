### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won the decisive clash by exploiting a fatal concession A made under pressure: in A4.1, A admitted that an individual's opt-in veto does not stop AI firms from training on stock libraries or consenting artists to produce the same genre-level competing output. B correctly identified this as making the policy "purely symbolic" for the exact harm A's case was built on (the watercolor illustrator example). B then compounded this by turning A's own "collective licensing like music streaming" analogy against them, noting music licensing is notorious for paying indie creators fractions of a cent — directly undermining A's compensation story. B also offered a coherent counter-mechanism (free open web datasets enabling small AI competition) that A never fully dismantled, while A's claim that opt-in would help small developers rested on an unproven licensing-pool mechanism that B showed mathematically favors incumbents. A's case had a reasonable moral framing (fairness, burden-shifting) but its practical mechanism collapsed under B's pressure, and critically, A's final two speeches (Rebuttal 2 and Closing) were cut off by the word-limit clip, meaning A never got to rebuild from that admission or land a clean closing argument. That execution failure, combined with B's sharper, more consistent rebuttals, gives B a clear edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's exploitation of A4.1 ("PRO concedes...AI firms will still train on stock libraries and consenting artists to generate the exact same competing watercolor art...an opt-in veto is a purely symbolic gesture") was the most decisive moment, as it neutralized A's central economic-harm narrative and went unanswered due to A's clipped closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that the proposed veto would neither prevent genre-level AI competition nor provide meaningful individual compensation, while likely raising entry barriers for smaller developers. Side B retained a plausible consent-and-fairness case, but its collective-licensing solution was speculative, its claim that A admitted scraping required billions in resources misdescribed A’s argument, and two clipped turns weakened execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it used B’s own answer to show that models trained on consenting or licensed substitutes could still produce the same competing art, then exposed the tension between affordable blanket licensing and meaningful payouts to millions of creators.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B won by demonstrating that Side A's proposed opt-in right would be largely symbolic. Side A conceded that an individual opt-out could not stop genre-level AI competition, since models would still train on stock libraries and consenting artists to produce identical competing outputs—leaving creators with the same economic harm and zero compensation. Side B then exposed the double bind: if creators opt in, collective licensing pools diluted across millions of contributors would yield only fractional pennies, while if they opt out, they receive nothing and suffer the same market displacement. Side B also effectively showed that opt-in would destroy the free open datasets that currently allow small AI startups to compete, entrenching the very monopolies Side A claimed to oppose. Side A compounded these substantive losses with execution costs, including clipped late speeches and a closing that mischaracterized Side B's position on data-scraping costs. Side B crystallized the decisive clash cleanly: the veto neither protects livelihoods nor delivers meaningful revenue, yet inflicts real collateral damage on the open web and AI competition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 8), where CON leveraged Side A's admission in A4.1—that an individual opt-out cannot stop genre-level competition—to show the veto is a "purely symbolic gesture" that changes nothing about the creator's economic survival, while simultaneously exposing how opt-in would still yield fractional-penny payouts and destroy the open datasets small developers rely upon.
