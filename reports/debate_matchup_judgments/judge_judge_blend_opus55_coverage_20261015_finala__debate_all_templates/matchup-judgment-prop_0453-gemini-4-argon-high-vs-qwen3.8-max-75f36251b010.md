### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and executed the Pressure Questions round properly with clear A1/A2 answers. The decisive clash was whether the policy rate is the right instrument for a largely supply-driven, credit-leaky problem. B's strongest claim—that only the base rate reaches shadow-banking and cash-buyer demand—was met by A with a specific, plausible alternative (investor-specific capital charges, debt-service coverage, valuation haircuts, portfolio-leverage caps) that B's closing dismissed as "hypothetical" without substantively re-engaging. A also kept effective pressure on B's fiscal-cushion dependency ("why impose the damage first?"), and while B answered this with automatic stabilizers in Rebuttal 2, A's closing reasserted the tension and B's closing did not fully resolve it. A's framing that the motion demands prioritizing housing over general price stability (not merely acting on housing at all) gave a cleaner, more consistent throughline than B's broader systemic-risk narrative, which relied on the contestable premise that macroprudential/regulatory tools cannot substitute for blanket rate hikes. The result is a close but real edge to A on tool-effectiveness and internal consistency.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 answer to the "cash buyers bypass mortgage caps" pressure (A4.2), offering investor-specific capital charges and leverage caps as a direct substitute for blanket rate hikes, which B's closing did not substantively refute beyond labeling it "hypothetical."
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side B won by keeping the motion-specific burden clear: housing matters, but that does not justify making it the priority when the policy rate is broad, recessionary, and unable to repair scarce supply. Side A established plausible demand and bubble channels, but repeatedly assumed the recession would be mild and the eventual crisis worse, without adequately proving either. B also offered a more proportionate combination of overall-inflation tightening and targeted financial controls. A’s repeated nonexistent C-reference IDs were an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s final rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions, conceded calibrated tightening when shelter spills into general inflation, and then distinguished that from prioritizing housing inflation at recession risk. Its point that unemployment and reduced construction can worsen housing hardship effectively undercut A’s claim that broad rate hikes are the uniquely necessary cure.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): A won by keeping the live clash on institutional reality: when politicians fail on supply, only the policy rate is under CB control and reaches leveraged plus yield-chasing capital, and temporary cushionable recession beats unanchored shelter costs plus a leveraged bust. B’s blunt-instrument and macroprudential path stayed coherent and landed real damage on construction pass-through and cash-buyer immunity, but never fully neutralized the paralysis/surrender problem A forced after the pressure exchange, so A left the cleaner decisive comparison for the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2 answers on demand/land-value channels and automatic fiscal stabilizers, then flipping cash buyers into yield-sensitive investors whom only base rates reprice.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A built a tight causal chain (rate hikes choke construction financing and freeze existing mortgages, worsening the physical shortage that drives housing inflation) and then landed a decisive trap: B's own fix for the post-recession period relies on macroprudential tools (LTV/DTI caps) to prevent reflation, which A pointed out could simply be used now instead of inducing a recession at all. A pressed this contradiction in Rebuttal 2 and reiterated it forcefully in closing, turning B's "cash buyers" warning back on itself. B's case—resting on credibility, expectations psychology, and the asymmetric reversibility of recession vs. housing inflation—was coherent and emotionally compelling, but never squarely resolved why macroprudential tools sufficient to prevent reignition after a recession could not do the same job without one; its late attempt to patch this (tools can be evaded by cash/nonbank credit) arrived only in the closing and was neutralized by A's final speech, which had the last word. B's pressure questions were answered competently by A (targeted tools act faster than lagged rate policy), while A's pressure questions exposed a genuine tension in B's recovery mechanism that was only partially addressed. Overall A offered the clearer, more resolved path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3), turning B's own concession about keeping macroprudential limits in place after a recession-driven rate cut into proof that those same tools could substitute for the recession entirely — a contradiction B never fully resolved even in closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing that recessionary tightening attacks housing demand while also choking construction, freezing existing inventory, and shifting would-be buyers into rentals. Side A never adequately proved that housing inflation is less reversible than unemployment or that broad rate hikes outperform targeted credit rules. B incurred a minor execution cost from nonexistent reference IDs, but its comparative case remained substantially stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange exposed A’s concession that loan-to-value and debt-to-income limits could prevent renewed bidding after later rate cuts. B persuasively asked why those tools could not be used immediately without first causing unemployment, then linked A’s “slower household formation” mechanism to the same crowding and delayed families A condemned.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won by making PRO’s instrument self-defeating: aggressive rate prioritization freezes construction and inventory, crowds rentals, then requires later easing that restarts bidding unless macroprudential caps already work—which undercuts the need to risk recession. A’s credibility and irreversibility framing remained coherent but never neutralized the supply-cycle critique or the point that “slower household formation” is the same crowded-homes harm A promised to prevent. B forced clearer mechanism comparison and answered pressure more damagingly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 (B4.3–B4.5): used A’s own post-recession LTV/DTI concession against A, then equated A’s rent-cooling via slower household formation with the social misery A’s opening opposed.
