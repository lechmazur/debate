- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): CON built a tighter, more internally consistent case: supply scarcity is the binding constraint, ownership doesn't equal wartime control, and enforceable mitigation addresses capture without sacrificing financing. PRO's case leaned on a string of suspiciously specific, unverifiable "examples" (a precise 2021 DRC cobalt venture with a 30% voting cap, a 2020 Australian consortium, a 2022 Nevada lithium seizure) introduced mid-debate rather than in the opening, which both strains the evidence rules and weakens grounding. PRO also had two rebuttal speeches clipped for exceeding word limits, an execution cost that left key arguments unfinished. CON, by contrast, kept its claims plausible and general, answered pressure in proper A1/A2 form, and closed with a clean, memorable framing ("security is mineral output a host state can command") that directly crystallized the central clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Questions round (Q1 on whether mitigation-rebuttable presumption is just relabeled case-by-case review, Q2 on whether the investigation needed to apply the 25% threshold defeats the speed/security case for presumption) forced PRO into an answer that effectively conceded the presumption either collapses into case-by-case review or becomes an absolute ban—this was the decisive exchange of the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): Side A systematically dismantled Side B's case by exposing a core contradiction: applying a presumptive block still requires the same lengthy investigation into ownership and financing that Side B claimed the block would avoid.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal brilliantly weaponized Side B's own DRC cobalt example, pointing out that if covert control operates through debt and offtake, an acquisition block fails to stop it, whereas a rigorous case-by-case review could condition those exact channels.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won decisively by exposing the logical and practical flaws in Side A's core mechanisms. Most notably, Side B dismantled Side A's claim that a presumptive block saves time by pointing out that regulators must still conduct the exact same lengthy investigation to determine if a firm meets the "closely tied" threshold before they can block it. Side B also brilliantly turned Side A's own example of covert evasion (the DRC cobalt venture) against it, noting that if state control is exercised via debt and offtake rather than equity, an acquisition block fails to stop it, whereas case-by-case mitigation can regulate all channels. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): A built a tight, consistent causal story—control over long-lived, irreplaceable critical-mineral assets is established quietly through financing, board seats, and offtake before any "concrete evidence" of weaponization exists, so by the time proof arrives it is strategically useless. A used this to justify a rebuttable presumption that shifts the (cheaper, reversible) cost of delay onto the party with superior information. B's core reply—that existing CFIUS-style review already captures the same signals and that mitigation/divestiture tools can handle a crisis—was answered directly by A: divestiture litigates ownership, it does not compel shipment of ore during an active confrontation, and hold-back licensing does not solve the supply problem either. B never squarely closed that gap. B also repeatedly exceeded word limits (opening, Rebuttal 1, Rebuttal 2 all clipped), losing the ends of key arguments and, in one clipped rebuttal, introducing an oddly precise, unverifiable anecdote (a stalled Midwest lithium plant with exact cost figures) that reads as brittle fake precision rather than grounded reasoning. Both sides answered pressure questions in correct A1/A2 format, but A's answers (functional-control test, disclosure-based unwinding of shells) were more concretely responsive than B's, which leaned on existing-tool assertions already contested. A's closing crystallized the timing asymmetry cleanly; B's closing largely restated earlier claims without resolving the divestiture-timing problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer to B's shell/proxy question (A4.2): conceding no guarantee is airtight but showing that hidden control requires disclosure, monitoring, and risk of forced unwinding—paired with the closing point that forced divestiture litigates ownership but "does not deliver lithium... while ships are stopped"—most sharply exposed the weak link in B's reliance on ex-post mitigation tools.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing the debate around the burden of proof and asymmetric risk (reversible delay vs. irreversible dependency). Side A made a strong technical case that existing case-by-case reviews use the exact same evidence to block deals without the economic fallout of a presumption, but Side B smartly weaponized this in Rebuttal 2, pointing out that if the triggers are the same, the severity of the threat justifies placing the evidentiary burden on the acquiring firm. Side A also suffered from repeated word-count clipping, which blunted its final impacts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Turn 7, where Side B points out that Side A's reliance on existing observable ties justifies the presumption, and that forced divestiture litigates ownership but fails to physically move minerals during a crisis.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A won by establishing a clear, decisive weighing mechanism: the asymmetry between reversible delay and irreversible dependency. Side A successfully argued that case-by-case reviews fail because they wait for concrete proof of weaponization, which arrives too late, and that a presumption simply shifts the burden of proof to the acquirer within the existing framework. Side A also delivered devastating rebuttals against Side B's reliance on ex-post divestiture, pointing out that litigating ownership during a crisis does not compel physical shipments. Side B's arguments about administrative burden and WTO rules were less compelling in the face of A's national security framing, and B suffered execution costs from clipped speeches that truncated its conclusions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's reliance on forced divestiture during a crisis by pointing out that "divestiture litigates ownership, it does not deliver lithium... while ships are stopped," effectively neutralizing Side B's main alternative safeguard.
