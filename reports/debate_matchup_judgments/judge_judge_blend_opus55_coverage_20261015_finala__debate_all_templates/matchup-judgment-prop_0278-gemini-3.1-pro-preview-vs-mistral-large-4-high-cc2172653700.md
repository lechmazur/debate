### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): PRO built a tighter, more consistent case by exploiting a logical asymmetry CON never fully dissolved: if Israel is too intransigent to cede a fraction of land for a border, it is even less plausible that it would voluntarily dissolve its sovereign state and merge security with a historically hostile population for a binational democracy. PRO pressed this contradiction in every round and closed on it cleanly ("if a nation refuses to move a border fence, they will not hand over the keys to the state"). CON's counter—framing one-state as mere "extension of existing democracy" via civil rights mobilization rather than "surrender"—was a clever reframe but remained abstract and never specified a mechanism as concrete as PRO's land-swap/settlement-geography answer to the cartographic-impossibility charge. CON's strongest original point (two-state as "alibi" legitimizing indefinite occupation) was interesting but under-pressed and not fully leveraged against PRO's "least-bad achievable" framing. Pressure-question exchanges were answered in correct A1/A2 format by both sides, but PRO's answers were marginally more concrete and responsive, while CON's answers leaned on assertion (self-determination without sovereignty) that PRO had already flagged as question-begging. Both sides argued skillfully and vividly, but PRO's throughline was clearer and less internally strained by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's recurring "5% vs 100%" contradiction (first raised in B2.1, sharpened in B4.3, and crystallized in the closing) was the most decisive exchange: CON's attempt to escape it by distinguishing "extension of democracy" from "political surrender" (A4.1-A4.2) never convincingly explained why dissolving Israel's core national identity via external pressure would be easier than negotiated land swaps already modeled in past diplomacy.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B won the central feasibility comparison. Side A vividly argued that settlements have hollowed out partition, but repeatedly treated the moral simplicity of equal rights as proof that a binational democracy is politically achievable. B showed that if Israeli resistance makes limited territorial concessions impossible, transforming Israel’s sovereignty, security structure, and national character is even less plausible. B’s exact settlement figures were unsupported and its diplomatic mechanism remained vague, but its comparative case better addressed “least-bad achievable.”
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive response was that A cannot cite Israeli intransigence to defeat two states while assuming civil-rights pressure can secure the much larger concession required by one state. A’s “extension, not surrender” framing sharpened the moral case but did not resolve the political-feasibility objection.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): B wins on achievable comparison. CON's core claim that settlements make two states cartographically impossible was undermined by B's land-swap mapping and then turned into a fatal contradiction: if Israel won't yield a fraction of land, it will not voluntarily surrender sovereignty for a binational state. B's Q1 exposed this and A's A1 answer rebranded surrender as extension without solving feasibility. B consistently weighed logistical vs structural barriers and self-determination, while A leaned on normative justice and de facto one-state framing that did not prove one-state more achievable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's contradiction attack - B2.1/B3.1/B4.3 - that CON requires Israel to yield 100% after claiming yielding 5% is impossible; capped by B5.2 'If a nation refuses to move a border fence, they will not hand over the keys.'

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built the tighter logical frame from the outset by attacking the word "achievable" itself, arguing a non-achievable outcome cannot be "least-bad achievable" regardless of its merits. CON's most decisive moment was the Rebuttal 1 scale critique of PRO's Gaza/Sinai analogy (8,000 vs 100,000+ settlers), which PRO never fully neutralized—PRO's later answers (A2 in Rebuttal 2) reasserted that both paths require "political will" but never directly re-engaged the magnitude problem. CON also effectively turned PRO's own subjugation argument into an indictment of the two-state framework as a "diplomatic shield" for ongoing occupation; PRO's "proves too much" rebuttal addressed this only generically rather than refuting the specific causal story. PRO's pressure questions were sharp and forced CON to specify an alternative, and CON's political-vs-physical distinction (with the South Africa comparison) was a reasonable but contestable answer. PRO's closing point—that integrating settlers requires equally unprecedented political will and leaves an "endless struggle over the character of one state"—was the strongest late counter-punch and went unanswered in CON's final closing, but it arrived too late in the exchange to overturn the accumulated weight of CON's scale and sovereignty-paradox arguments. Overall CON produced the clearer, more sustained path to victory, though PRO kept real pressure alive until the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 response to the Gaza/Sinai withdrawal analogy, exposing the roughly fifteen-fold scale difference in settler evacuation, was the most damaging and least-answered blow of the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the stronger comparative case. Side A compellingly showed that settlements, security demands, and shared infrastructure make partition extraordinarily difficult, but repeatedly inflated difficulty into physical impossibility and never adequately explained how a single state or confederation would overcome competing national projects, security fears, and likely resistance. Side B established that both paths require major political transformation, while partition at least preserves self-determination for both peoples.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's decisive response was that Side A applied political impossibility selectively: integrating the populations into an equal binational system would require at least as dramatic a change in Israeli politics as evacuation and land swaps, yet Side A never resolved the resulting conflict over sovereignty and national identity.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): PRO retained the comparative burden: even with settlement growth and low trust, partition is the only framework that offers both national self-determination and a defensible border. CON hit hard on scale of evacuation and constrained sovereignty, but never solved B's pressure - its rights-based single state avoids physical uprooting only by demanding an equally unprecedented political dissolution that abandons Jewish self-determination and risks the civil war it fears. PRO's sequence of land-swaps, phased guarantees, and historical withdrawals kept achievability live as political choice versus structural impossibility of alternatives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1 vs A2.1 clash on Gaza 8k vs deep West Bank 100k+ was decisive; A exposed false equivalence scaling problem, but B4.2 countered that CON's own alternative requires the same leap of political will while delivering less of both self-determination goals, neutralizing CON's unachievability edge.
