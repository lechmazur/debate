### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built a more rigorous comparative case: it didn't just defend dynamic pricing in the abstract but consistently forced the debate onto the counterfactual — what happens to access and theater survival under a flat price regime (higher everyday prices, fewer showings, closures). PRO (B) never squarely answered that counterfactual; its closing and second rebuttal kept reasserting the cultural-ritual/airline-couch framing without resolving why "market discipline" would cap dynamic surge but not flat-price hikes, a contradiction CON explicitly exposed in Rebuttal 2 ("Market discipline cannot be absolute against flat pricing and absent against dynamic pricing"). CON also exploited a bait-and-switch in PRO's case (defending predictable discounts while attacking only unpredictable surge) and PRO's attempted threshold answer in A1/A2 (published baseline vs algorithmic surge) didn't cleanly survive CON's point that dynamic prices are also shown in advance at booking. PRO's cultural framing was vivid and emotionally resonant, but by the closing it was mostly recap rather than engagement with CON's sharpest structural challenge, leaving CON's "no cinema at an accessible price at all" reframe as the cleaner, unresolved path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 point that "Market discipline cannot be absolute against flat pricing and absent against dynamic pricing" directly dismantled PRO's A2 pressure-question answer and was never adequately answered in PRO's closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A won by more persuasively framing cinema as a discretionary social ritual whose survival depends on low friction and democratic access, not revenue optimization. A cleanly answered CON's pressure questions—establishing a clear threshold for "aggressive" pricing and showing market discipline prevents flat-price gouging—while demonstrating that dynamic pricing transforms moviegoing from a spontaneous mass habit into a stratified, calculated luxury. CON's economic defense remained plausible but never fully overcame the core claim that algorithmic stratification of peak communal moments pushes marginal audiences toward frictionless streaming.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which distinguished captive airline demand from discretionary cinema demand, directly answering CON's pressure questions and showing that market discipline from streaming caps flat prices while algorithmic surge fatally erodes the spontaneous social impulse that sustains theaters.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively dismantling Side B's core distinction between traditional time-based discounts and aggressive dynamic pricing. Side A successfully argued that dynamic pricing can be transparent and published in advance, neutralizing Side B's claim that it inherently creates unpredictable friction. Furthermore, Side A presented a stronger economic reality check: a flat price high enough to sustain theaters would price out the exact casual viewers Side B claimed to protect. Side A's reframing of a "mass habit" as broad participation over a film's run, rather than simultaneous attendance on opening night, decisively resolved the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they exposed the contradiction in Side B's claim that market discipline prevents flat prices from reaching peak levels, while simultaneously arguing that dynamic pricing allows theaters to extract those peak levels. Side A also cleanly answered the pressure questions by clarifying that "flexing" means shifting from Saturday night to Saturday afternoon, not abandoning the social experience entirely.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) kept the debate anchored to the motion's actual target—surge pricing on standard seats, not legitimate off-peak discounts—and exploited a real logical gap in CON's case: if a screening sells out at a stable standard price, the theater already captures full seat revenue, so the "surplus needed for survival" argument conflates extra profit with necessity. CON's rebuttals (A4.1, A4.2, A5.1-3) kept reasserting the subsidization/solvency narrative without directly closing that gap, and largely repeated earlier framing rather than adapting. PRO's pressure questions forced this issue cleanly, and its answers to CON's pressure questions (distinguishing repeatable, substitutable films from scarce live events) were sharper and more responsive than CON's answers, which mostly restated prior claims. PRO's closing crystallized the clash (yield vs. participation) more precisely than CON's closing, which largely recapped its opening thesis.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B4.1 ("a full opening-weekend house at the normal price already monetizes every seat... Surge adds extraction from the same crowd, not new attendance") directly undercut CON's central survival-necessity argument and was never squarely answered.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A successfully narrowed the motion to aggressive surge pricing above a standard price, repeatedly exposing that B defended ordinary off-peak discounts and revenue management generally rather than the specific practice at issue. A cleanly distinguished repeatable, substitutable cinema from scarce live events, undermining B's sports and concerts analogy, and showed that a full opening-weekend house at a stable standard price already monetizes peak demand through seats plus concessions without extracting more per seat. B never overcame the burden of proving that surging standard seats creates new habitual moviegoers rather than simply yielding more from existing enthusiasts while fragmenting simultaneous cultural participation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal (A4), where it answered B's pressure questions by distinguishing movies from sports and concerts on repeatability and streaming substitutes, then crystallized the decisive clash as extraction versus participation.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins decisively by controlling the definition of the motion. PRO successfully narrowed "aggressive dynamic pricing" to mean surge pricing above a standard baseline, explicitly conceding predictable off-peak discounts. Side A (CON) failed to adapt to this narrowing, spending most of the debate defending the utility of off-peak discounts rather than justifying the upside surge penalty. Furthermore, PRO delivered exceptional answers to CON's pressure questions, particularly the sharp distinction between scarce live events (sports/concerts) and high-frequency, repeatable movies with streaming substitutes. PRO's framing of cinema as a mass habit reliant on "easy yeses" and cultural simultaneity was highly persuasive and largely unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2, specifically the answers to the pressure questions (A1 and A2). PRO's explanation that a full house at a standard price already monetizes the event via tickets and concessions, and that movies are repeatable high-frequency events unlike scarce live sports, completely neutralized CON's economic and comparative arguments.
