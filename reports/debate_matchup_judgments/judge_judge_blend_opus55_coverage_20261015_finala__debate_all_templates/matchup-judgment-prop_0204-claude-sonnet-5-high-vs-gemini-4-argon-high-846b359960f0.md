### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won by showing that pre-closing mitigation can localize foreign capital and technology without conceding control, while a default block risks preserving the rival’s existing overseas chokepoint. A’s strongest risk-allocation case was weakened by repeatedly portraying B’s safeguards as post-investment remedies even though B specified pre-closing conditions. A’s clipped opening and B’s erroneous closing reference IDs were execution costs, but not decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 most decisively answered A’s shell-company theory: a presumption limited to firms identified as rival-tied cannot expose a covert shell before ordinary intelligence screening does. B also effectively challenged A’s assumption that proprietary technology would be transferred without meaningful equity and clarified that mitigation occurs before closing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won a close, high-quality clash by making the baseline and commercial comparisons easier to decide. After A’s strong asymmetry/irreversibility frame and layered-control attack on pure geography, B repeatedly showed that rivals already hold midstream abroad, that blocked capital prevents onshore capacity rather than protecting it, and that tech transfer without equity plus a presumption that still depends on first identifying “close ties” do not solve the problems A claimed they solve. A’s weighing (cost of delayed capital vs captured chokepoint) and pressure on stroke-of-a-pen timing were sharp, but B’s answers on ex-ante capacity never built, enforceable pre-close mitigation under ordinary review, and capital flight to third-country jurisdictions left A’s decisive routes damaged. Opening clip and slightly cleaner late crystallization for A did not outweigh B’s better live route resolution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3–B4.4: tech-without-equity is commercially unrealistic, and shells never trigger the presumption until ties are already found—neutralizing A’s main fixes.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won the weighing clash. PRO's core asymmetry/irreversibility case was undercut by two live CON answers: (1) host-state export controls override private offtake without seizure, preserving built capacity, while a presumptive block prevents capacity from being built at all; (2) the presumption only applies after a buyer is identified as closely tied, so it does not solve the shell-structure problem it claims to fix, whereas case-by-case screening already supplies the needed leverage for mitigation. CON also made the dependency-comparison decisive: chokepoint already sits inside rival borders [B1.1], so blocking onshore plants with foreign tech/equity locks dependency abroad and leaves ore in the ground.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.4 + A4.1/A2.1 – CON shows shell posing as allied never triggers the presumption unless intelligence already finds the tie, neutralizing PRO's burden-shift fix, and distinguishes ex-ante blocking (kills concrete) from ex-post export licensing (keeps built plant under host control).

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that conditioned minority investment can expand total mineral output while reserving supply for allied processing, whereas A's zero-off-take standard would exclude most commercially useful rival-linked capital. A compellingly established the security risk and exposed tension between rival off-take and diversification, but never fully answered B's comparative claim that capped off-take can leave allies better supplied than an unfunded project. B's offshore-jurisdiction scenario was somewhat contingent, and its clipped opening was an execution cost, but A relied too heavily on the unproven assumption that allied capital would reliably replace excluded bidders.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer that case-by-case review can cap rival off-take and mandate allied set-asides was decisive. It directly answered A's strongest contradiction by explaining how rival capital could still produce net diversification, while turning A's zero-off-take exception into a near-prohibition on realistic deals.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): A won a clear but competitive debate by locking the decisive clash: B conceded both rival refining dominance and that rival-linked capital mainly buys off-take that feeds that monopoly, then never cleanly showed why approving those deals diversifies supply rather than entrenches it. A’s burden-shift frame fit the irreversibility of physical ore better than B’s mitigation/jurisdiction path, especially once the motion is read as written about acquisitions rather than all third-country financing. B’s later jurisdictional-blind-spot and conditioned-off-take replies were sharp and original, but they arrived partly as repair after A’s pressure exposed the internal tension, and they leaned on optimistic assumptions that paper conditions and retained visibility would hold under crisis.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Q1 on off-take feeding the refining monopoly, crystallized in A4.3: approving rival investment for locked ore does not diversify supply away from the choke point B itself named.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): CON's jurisdictional evasion and refining-chokepoint mechanism survived. PRO's physical-diversion point is strong but does not answer why pushing ownership beyond review improves security.
