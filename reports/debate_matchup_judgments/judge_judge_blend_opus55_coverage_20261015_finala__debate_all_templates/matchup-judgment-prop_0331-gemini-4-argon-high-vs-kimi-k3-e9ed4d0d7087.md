### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and engaged the actual motion, but CON built the more durable structural case: the three-element gambling test was shown to be overinclusive (Pokémon packs, capsule-toy machines, digital card apps), and CON repeatedly forced PRO to concede that its own preferred remedy—banning randomized paid sales to minors—is itself achievable through ordinary consumer/age-restriction law (the alcohol/tobacco analogy), not uniquely through gambling classification. PRO's strongest material (behavioral engineering, dopamine loops, brain vulnerability) established that regulation is needed but never cleanly established why the "gambling" label specifically was necessary rather than just rhetorically convenient. The pressure-question exchange was pivotal: CON's Q2 forced PRO to bite the bullet that digital card-pack apps would count as gambling too, which CON then used in Rebuttal 2 to show PRO's own definition swallows ordinary products, while CON's answer to PRO's Q1 (distinguishing enforceable consumer bans from unworkable licensing regimes) held up reasonably well. CON also got the stronger final word, neutralizing PRO's closing "ban vs. caps contradiction" by pointing out that categorical bans paired with adult-facing regulation (alcohol/tobacco) are ordinary age law, not evidence that gambling classification is required. PRO's case remained forceful and well-framed but ultimately rested on asserting that only the gambling label has "teeth," a claim CON's "bluff" critique left exposed through the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's use of PRO's own Pressure Question answer (A4.2, conceding digital card-pack apps count as gambling) as a concession proving the three-element test overreaches, combined with CON's closing alcohol/tobacco analogy dismantling PRO's "ban vs. caps" contradiction claim.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): CON maintained two live routes that PRO never neutralized: (1) the three-element gambling test overreaches to capsule machines and, by PRO's own concession, to all digital card-pack apps, collapsing category to technology not gambling; (2) the effective protection both sides want - no paid randomization for minors - is achievable as a consumer-law sales ban with a compliance path, while gambling classification adds only unworkable licensing and a record of exit. PRO's velocity/friction distinction was answered as degree vs category, and its Rebuttal 2 conceded the remedy is disabling sales, which is A’s framework.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's capsule-toy / gumball machine rebuttal in A2.2/A4.4 - that paid random prize machines sell to 8-year-olds and meet PRO's three elements yet are not gambling, forcing PRO in B4.2 to bite the bullet that all digital packs become gambling.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B won by decisively severing the link between the desired outcome (protecting minors) and the proposed mechanism (the gambling label). B effectively demonstrated that consumer law already possesses the authority to categorically ban harmful products for minors without needing to misclassify them as gambling. B also successfully pressed A on the overbreadth of the "consideration, chance, prize" test, forcing A to concede that digital trading card apps would also be classified as gambling. While A argued that the stigma of the gambling label was necessary to force developers to remove the mechanic, B convincingly countered that a direct consumer-law ban achieves the exact same prohibition without the collateral damage of casino licensing regimes and publisher exits. B's framing that "protection that binds beats a label that bluffs" cleanly resolved the core clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled A's "unplug the machine" analogy by pointing out that consumer law routinely enforces categorical bans for minors without the gambling label, and that the definition of gambling must actually fit the product before the regulatory "unplugging" power is invoked.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-defended cases, but A's framework proved more resilient under pressure. A's core move — showing that every remedy B proposed (odds disclosure, spending caps, manipulative-design bans, parental consent gates) is itself drawn from the gambling-regulation toolkit — was never squarely answered beyond asserting a label distinction. A also exploited B's concession that cash-out marketplaces justify targeted intervention, turning the "binary" cash-out line into an admitted matter of degree, and neutralized B's strongest practical objection (invasive ID verification) by showing B's own "family-approval flow" alternative rests on the same unverifiable premise (someone at the controller clicking "approve"). B's best uncapitalized point was the "retreat" charge (A1.3's spending-limits language vs A2.6's total-ban framing) and the strong proportionality/criminalization framing in closing ("should a $10 purchase be a crime?"), both of which added real pressure but didn't fully topple A's structural argument. A's Rebuttal 2 being clipped is a real execution cost, but the loss was minor in substance, so it only slightly tempers an otherwise clearer, better-sequenced case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2/Closing move showing that B's "default-off parental consent" mechanism suffers the identical verification problem B raised against age-gating ("a server that cannot see who holds the controller cannot see who taps 'approve' either") was the most damaging exchange, since it defused B's central practical objection without B fully recovering an asymmetry argument.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): CON maintained a cleaner legal boundary: gambling requires chance to win cashable value, not just variable digital items. PRO's attempt to drop cash-out led to the blind-bag reductio — every trading card pack would be gambling — and its friction/control distinctions were degree arguments. CON's enforcement contrast was decisive: consumer law only needs to verify adult payment authorization via push notification, while a gambling ban must verify who holds the controller, forcing ID/biometric walls. PRO's proposed family-approval flow is parental consent, which PRO earlier said must be illegal, conceding CON's alternative. Clipped R2 also cost PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.5 distinguishing cardholder authorization (parental consent) from controller-holder verification (gambling ban) and showing B4.3's family-approval flow concedes CON while still failing to stop a parent handing the controller without invasive checks.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully absorbing Side B's strongest enforcement objection and turning it into a normative trap. While Side B effectively caught Side A in an early contradiction regarding "spending limits" versus "total bans" for minors, Side A smoothly pivoted to the total ban framework and argued that parental consent to gambling is inherently invalid. Side A's "plumbing" argument in the late rounds effectively neutralized Side B's claim that consumer law verification is less invasive than gambling age-gates, demonstrating that both rely on the same flawed assumption of parental policing. Side A's closing crystallization of the clash was decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled Side B's "cardholder vs. player" verification distinction by showing that if a server cannot identify the player for a gambling ban, it equally cannot identify who is tapping "approve" on a parental consent prompt, thus collapsing Side B's enforcement alternative into the exact same practical reality while lacking the normative force of a gambling prohibition.
