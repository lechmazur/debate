### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B identified and sustained the decisive structural weakness in A's plan: the motion explicitly accepts reduced smallholder income and export growth, yet A's entire mechanism for achieving cleaner outcomes (funding zero-burn machinery, forcing conglomerates to "invest downward" into smallholders) depends on capital that the very regulations foreclose. B's pressure questions forced this contradiction into the open, and A's answer—that avoided healthcare costs would supply liquid state subsidies—was exposed as "fiscal fiction," a charge A never adequately rebutted in Rebuttal 2 or Closing. B also landed a clean self-contradiction catch: A claimed conglomerates exploit smallholders as a legal shield, then claimed the same conglomerates would generously fund those same smallholders once regulated, which B correctly flagged as inconsistent. A's irreversibility framing and peatland/haze arguments remained rhetorically strong and kept the environmental stakes vivid, but without a credible transition-financing mechanism, A's own prescriptions risked being self-defeating (leakage to soy/rapeseed abroad, smallholders pushed into shadow-economy burning), a critique B pressed consistently from Opening through Closing. A never neutralized this live route, making B's case the cleaner and more resolved path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1–B4.3) calling A's healthcare-savings-as-subsidy claim "fiscal fiction" and exposing the contradiction between "conglomerates exploit smallholders" and "conglomerates will fund smallholders," which A's Closing never directly repaired.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON built the stronger path to victory by dismantling PRO's mechanism for transition. PRO argued aggressive regulation would force intensification on existing land, but CON exposed that cutting smallholder income and export growth eliminates the very capital needed for replanting and zero-burn machinery. CON's "fiscal fiction" rebuttal—showing that avoided healthcare costs do not materialize as liquid subsidies—and the "compliance liability" frame—showing that conglomerates would sever untitled smallholders rather than bankroll them—damaged PRO's solvency. PRO's closing reversibility frame was powerful, but it depended on regulation actually halting deforestation, and CON persuasively showed that paper boundaries without economic capacity would push desperate farmers into unmonitored shadow clearing while exporting demand to less efficient crops abroad.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 exposure of PRO's "magical economics" and contradiction between corporate exploitation and corporate benevolence: by noting that untitled smallholders become "toxic compliance liabilities" under aggressive traceability rules, CON severed PRO's claim that capping expansion would force conglomerates to invest downward into smallholder networks.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): CON won the intensification clash that became central. PRO needs the pivot to degraded land [A2.3] but explicitly accepts cutting the income/export that funds it. CON exposed this contradiction in B2.1/B3.1 and showed why PRO's bridges fail: health savings aren't liquid fiscal cash [B4.1] and conglomerates sever untitled smallholders as compliance liabilities rather than subsidize them [B4.2], pushing them into shadow burning and exporting demand to land-hungry soy. PRO's irreversibility weighing [A5.2] remains powerful on peat carbon but doesn't neutralize the perverse-outcome mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4 - CON's double hit that PRO cannot finance intensification while cutting income, and that aggressive traceability rules cause corporations to drop untitled smallholders, turning them into shadow encroachers, rather than trickling wealth down.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and executed the Pressure Questions format cleanly, but A's framing proved more resilient under pressure. A's irreversibility-vs-reversibility frame directly engaged the motion's explicit trade-off, and A's distinction between export "growth" (which the motion sacrifices) and existing baseline export revenue (which funds transition support) undercut B's recurring claim that suppressing growth starves enforcement budgets—B never squarely answered this distinction. A's pressure question exposing the apparent contradiction in B's case (farmers supposedly too poor to replant but able to access formal credit/machinery for expansion, while bureaucracies supposedly too weak to fund grants but able to police complex peatland management) forced B into a narrower, somewhat ad hoc reply (collateral logic, cooperative self-regulation) that partially defused but did not fully erase the tension. B's strongest and most original line—that aggressive regulation pushes smallholders into an untraceable black market via middleman laundering—was a genuinely sharp rebuttal to A's "mill chokepoint" solution, and A's closing essentially conceded this as a "real friction" rather than rebutting it, which is a real point for B. But A recovered by re-centering the cost-benefit calculus on irreversibility (floods and haze destroying existing smallholders' livelihoods too) and arguing enforcement leakage is a fixable friction while ecological collapse is not. On balance, A's rebuttals were slightly more incisive and better tied back to the stated motion, giving it a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over A's pressure question (A3.1/A3.2) about B's apparent contradiction—formal credit/enforcement capacity assumed for expansion but denied for replanting/grants—and B's A1/A2 answer distinguishing collateral-backed expansion loans from income-freezing replanting loans was the most decisive clash, though B's black-market "middleman laundering" rebuttal to A's mill-chokepoint claim (B4.3), left largely unanswered in A's closing, was the single most damaging unrebutted point in the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO's physical mechanism—drained peat inevitably becomes combustible regardless of permits, and frontier expansion triggers haze and floods that destroy the very existing smallholder plots CON claims to protect—proved harder to answer than CON's black-market counter. While CON landed real pressure with the middleman laundering and rural poverty arguments, CON never squarely addressed the self-undermining ecological Ponzi scheme at the heart of its own model: expansion cannibalizes existing farms. PRO sharpened the clash across rounds, cleanly answered pressure questions, and crystallized the irreversibility argument in the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO in Rebuttal 2 exposing CON's contradiction—farmers are simultaneously too destitute to manage replanting yet capable of securing formal capital for frontier expansion, while states are simultaneously competent to manage peatland drainage but incompetent to distribute replanting grants—and pairing it with the hydrological impossibility of "safe" peatland expansion.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO wins the central weighing: irreversible peat drainage, haze and flood costs wipe out marginal export gains, while tighter rules are reversible. CON's black-market/fire claim is damaged by PRO's mill-chokepoint logic and internal contradiction between capable legal expansion and destitute replanters. CON's strong collateral-gap answer doesn't overcome the hydrological impossibility that oil palm requires drained peat, which PRO crystallized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.4-A4.5 exposing CON's double standard on capital/bureaucracy and framing peat water management as hydrological impossibility — it undercut the CON route that regulated expansion can be environmentally safe.
