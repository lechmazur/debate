### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): A won a competitive debate by controlling the live clash on the stacking blind spot both sides conceded. A showed that CON’s registry-plus-caps package only works if it carries real ability-to-repay, disclosure, statements, dispute rights, and reporting—i.e., core lending duties that make BNPL much more card-like than a checkout feature. B’s best weapon was the “much more” scale attack and access/migration costs, but B never cleanly separated a pure traffic-light ping from the enforceable duties A listed, and the “declined users just move to worse credit” claim stayed speculative and partly self-contradictory. Function-over-form therefore stayed the cleaner decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s force that a stacking registry without affordability/statements merely documents the collision (A2.3–A2.4, then A4/A5) against B’s lightweight-registry alternative.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by showing that BNPL's functional reality—advancing money, expecting repayment, and imposing consequences for default—demands core lending duties rather than checkout-style treatment. CON's central constructive proposal, a lightweight registry to prevent stacking, collapses under PRO's pressure: a registry without affordability standards, consolidated statements, and enforceable dispute rights merely documents rather than prevents harm. PRO effectively parried CON's migration argument by noting that credit cards use the same affordability checks, so declined BNPL borrowers would not automatically qualify for costlier revolving credit. While CON landed real blows on proportionality and pressed the "much more" wording, they never fully resolved why their own stacking limit would not require the very credit-card infrastructure they dismissed as disproportionate. PRO's cleaner functional framing and more decisive rebuttals on the registry-migration nexus leave a clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's demonstration that CON's registry-only solution is "surveillance without protection" because flagging stacked plans does nothing to prevent an unaffordable fourth plan without the affordability checks, standardized statements, and reporting duties that define credit-card regulation—effectively showing CON's moderate position collapses into PRO's framework once enforcement is required.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by decisively dismantling Side B's proposed alternative. While B argued for a "lightweight registry" to prevent stacking without the heavy infrastructure of credit card regulation, A brilliantly pointed out that a registry merely flagging open plans without an affordability check just "documents a collision" rather than preventing it. A successfully argued that the core lending duties of credit cards (affordability checks, dispute rights, consolidated reporting) are functionally necessary to protect consumers from fragmented debt, rendering B's semantic arguments about the phrase "much more" less impactful.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's "lightweight registry" proposal, specifically the argument that a system which merely flags existing plans without assessing ability to repay is just "documentation of a collision already happening," which exposed the practical inadequacy of B's proportionate regulation.
