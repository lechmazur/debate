### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but CON built the more durable economic case. PRO's opening relied partly on an unsupported assertion ("rates track market power, not default rates") and its strongest rhetorical move—framing credit-card debt as "the same trap with better branding"—was directly neutralized when CON pointed out the card retains timing, dispute rights, and credit-building advantages over payday loans, a distinction PRO never fully answered. The decisive exchange was the "two incompatible stories" pressure PRO raised (B2.6 vs B2.3): PRO claimed CON couldn't simultaneously say underwriting is individualized/surgical and that lenders need above-cap pricing to survive. CON's answer (B4.3/B4.5) was economically coherent and went unrebutted in substance: judging a borrower's risk precisely does not reduce the expected loss that must still be priced in, so a smaller approved limit does not make an unsecured high-risk loan profitable at a lower rate. PRO's closing restated moral framing ("pricing desperation") rather than engaging this logic, and was also literally clipped before finishing, a real execution cost. CON's closing cleanly crystallized the core admission—caps don't lower the price for the riskiest borrowers, they remove the option entirely—and tied it back to the unresolved short-term-vs-chronic-use distinction. That combination of stronger grounding, a sharper rebuttal on the key contradiction, and a cleaner, uninterrupted closing gives CON the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's answer to PRO's "contradiction" charge (B4.3/B4.5): individualized underwriting can judge risk precisely while still needing an above-cap price, since a smaller approved limit does not reduce the probability or proportional cost of default—this went effectively unanswered in PRO's final speech.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that the tradeoff explicitly accepted in the motion is worthwhile: the high-rate status quo is structured to extract wealth from trapped borrowers, while a cap preserves individualized underwriting and safer products like modest-limit or secured cards. Side B turned Side A's central argument by demonstrating that the individualized underwriting Side A preferred is fully compatible with a price ceiling, collapsing the distinction between the two regimes and leaving Side A defending only the unchecked high price. Side A's liquidity and substitution arguments were blunted by Side B's "on-ramp" framing and the concession that alternatives should also be regulated, while Side A never fully recovered from the compatibility turn.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing turn on underwriting compatibility: Side B showed that if lenders can surgically approve a risky borrower with a modest limit, they can do so under a cap, using secured cards or credit-union-style products. This exposed an alleged contradiction in Side A's case and reframed the choice as individualized underwriting with extraction versus individualized underwriting with a ceiling, making the cap the clearly preferable option.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A won by effectively embracing the motion's explicit concession and reframing the "lost access" as the removal of a debt trap. Side A's "on-ramp" argument successfully neutralized Side B's substitution harm, and Side A's closing crystallized the clash brilliantly by arguing that individualized underwriting paired with uncapped rates is just targeted extraction. Side B argued well but struggled to overcome Side A's comparative weighing of the harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's "individualized underwriting" argument, pointing out that if lenders can surgically underwrite a modest limit, doing so at an uncapped rate simply turns that precision into a tool for targeted extraction rather than protection.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B landed the decisive late blow: it exploited the motion's built-in concession ("cuts access") by arguing PRO must show the conceded harm is outweighed, then directly attacked PRO's central reversible/irreversible framing by noting that permanent exclusion from the credit system ("never builds the file") is itself irreversible. PRO's closing never engaged this specific counter, instead repeating its earlier assertion that denial is merely reversible — a live, unrebutted point that undercut PRO's core architecture. B also pressed the substitution-to-payday-lending and secured-card-requires-savings-you-don't-have lines effectively, which PRO answered only generically. PRO's case remained clean and well-argued (strong behavioral-economics framing, no fake statistics), and its rebuttals on fee migration and subsidization were solid, but it did not close the loop on B's sharpest rebuttal. B's case suffers modest costs: two clipped speeches (though substance was largely delivered before cutoff) and one ungrounded "48%" statistic. On balance, B's tighter exploitation of the motion's own concession and its unanswered rebuttal on irreversible exclusion give it a narrow but clear edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal-2 point that a borrower denied a starter card "never builds the file at all" and is "locked out... every bit as irreversible as a damaged score" directly dismantled PRO's reversible/irreversible asymmetry — the backbone of PRO's closing — and PRO never answered it.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing that PRO’s central asymmetry—claiming capped exclusion is “reversible” while high-APR debt is “irreversible”—fails because being locked out of the credit system for years is equally irreversible. CON also pressed an unanswered cost question and sustained the substitution concern, while PRO’s clipped rebuttals and closing never recovered from the mirrored irreversibility argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal and closing turned PRO’s reversible/irreversible framework by demonstrating that credit exclusion is itself irreversible, neutralizing PRO’s core comparative claim without a response.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing the core trade-off not merely as "access vs. no access," but as "active financial destruction vs. delayed credit building." A's rebuttal on the asymmetry of harms—where a smaller limit is a reversible delay but a 30% debt trap causes irreversible solvency loss—was the decisive clash. A also brilliantly reframed B's "fee migration" argument as an improvement in market honesty and upfront transparency. While B's substitution argument (payday loans) and the "irreversibility of total exclusion" were strong and highly readable, B suffered from clipped speeches in both rebuttals, losing crucial concluding thoughts. Furthermore, A's philosophical willingness to bite the bullet on excluding unsustainable credit was more cohesive than B's defense of subprime break-even pricing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's reframing of "fee migration" from a hidden harm to an improvement in upfront honesty, alongside the clear delineation of the asymmetry between reversible delayed credit-building and irreversible debt-trap solvency loss.
