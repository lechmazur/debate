### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B narrowly won by preserving a coherent regulated version of P2P trading: shared grid, backup, and universal-service costs remain non-bypassable, while peers can still compete over the residual retail supply margin. Side A powerfully exposed cost-shifting risks and showed that P2P is not uniquely necessary for load shifting or renter access, but it did not establish that the residual benefit must be zero or that these risks require prohibition rather than conditions. B therefore had the stronger case for allowing, rather than mandating or subsidizing, the market.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s A1 response was decisive: it directly answered A’s “full charges or cost shifting” fork by identifying a contestable retail supply margin that can remain after shared-system costs are recovered. A effectively challenged the size of that margin, but did not eliminate it.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by establishing a decisive dilemma: if P2P network charges recover all fixed and backup costs, the remaining margin is too thin to deliver the massive savings Side B promises; if the savings are large, it is because system costs are being shifted. Side A systematically dismantled Side B's attempts to escape this fork.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal and closing sharply exposed the contradiction in Side B claiming all systemic risks are fully paid while a massive contestable profit margin still remains to fund a double-win for peers.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): CON won by establishing a durable analytical dilemma that PRO never fully resolved: either peer-to-peer trading survives full network, balancing, backup, and universal-service cost recovery only as a thin administrative margin too small to materially shorten solar payback, or it generates a meaningful discount only by shifting pooled obligations to non-participants. PRO asserted that a large contestable retailer margin remains after full cost recovery, but CON demonstrated that this margin is largely composed of the very system costs PRO agreed should be recovered through non-bypassable fees. CON also successfully undermined PROs equity case by showing that the regulations needed to prevent adverse selection in P2P would reduce it to a costlier version of the community solar and time-varying export rates CON already supported.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CONs closing and second rebuttal crystallized the tension in PROs retail-margin theory: PRO could not coherently claim that peak supply, risk, backup, and universal service were already covered by fixed fees while simultaneously insisting that a thick monopoly retailer margin remained to split between peers, a contradiction that collapsed PROs claim that P2P would cut payback by years without cost-shifting.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by exploiting the proposition's permissive burden: if traders pay cost-reflective network, balancing, and backup fees, Side A never established why a voluntary exchange with even modest remaining energy value should be prohibited. Side A presented a strong cost-shifting dilemma and credible regulated alternatives, but redundancy, platform expense, and the possibility of imperfect fees did not prove that properly regulated trading should be banned.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's answer to the fee dilemma was decisive: full grid fees can cover shared infrastructure while leaving the separately priced energy component available for trade. Its follow-up that users can choose a tariff instead if platform costs erase the gain turned Side A's duplication argument into an insufficient case for prohibition.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B narrowly wins by successfully dismantling Side A's core dilemma. Side A argued P2P either causes cost-shifting or becomes a useless billing layer once grid fees are paid. Side B precisely isolated the "energy value" from the grid services, proving a legitimate margin remains even with full fees, and successfully framed Side A's stance as an unjustified ban on voluntary trade just to protect a monopoly's administered tariffs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing correctly identifying that Side A's argument ("a regulator could return that margin by tariff instead") justifies letting platforms compete, not banning them.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): CON won by establishing a durable dilemma: peer-to-peer trading either underfunds grid fixed costs and shifts burdens onto non-solar households, or pays for them so fully that only a trivial margin remains, reducing P2P to an expensive billing layer. PRO never fully recovered. CON showed that P2P platforms remain price-takers dependent on the utility for balancing and backup, undermining the claim that peer competition disciplines monopolies. The transaction-cost and complexity arguments undercut PRO's democratization narrative, and CON demonstrated the same value could be captured more cleanly through dynamic export tariffs and scalable community solar. PRO's voluntarism frame lost force once CON proved the mechanism was structurally redundant and carried real downside risk of regressive cost-shifting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 [B4.1-B4.3], which collapsed PRO's "competition disciplines monopoly" narrative by showing P2P platforms must buy firm backup and balancing from the utility at regulated terms, and reframed the choice as either regressive cost-shifting (light fees) or a thin software margin (accurate fees).
