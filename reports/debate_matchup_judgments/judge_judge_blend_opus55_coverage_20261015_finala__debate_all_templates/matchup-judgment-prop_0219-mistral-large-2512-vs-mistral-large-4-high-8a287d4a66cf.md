### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 3 (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): PRO built a vivid, confident case but leaned almost entirely on suspiciously exact, uncited statistics (Colorado's "5.2%," "1.3%," a "30% fewer mismatched hires" tech-company anecdote, "25% more negotiate") presented with unwarranted precision — exactly the brittle fake-precision the rules flag for discount — and was twice clipped for exceeding word limits, an execution cost. Crucially, the motion covers both gender and racial pay gaps, yet PRO's evidence was almost entirely gender-specific; CON's pressure question (B3.1) exposed this gap directly, and PRO's answer ("racial pay gaps follow the same mechanism") was asserted, not argued. CON maintained the comparative framing ("certain efficiency cost vs. marginal, temporary gender-only benefit") throughout, cleanly resolved PRO's "contradiction" charge (insufficient AND restrictive are not mutually exclusive), and introduced a plausible, non-fabricated mechanism (compliance-driven midpoint anchoring/ceiling effect) that PRO never fully neutralized. CON's closing crystallized the unmet burden most clearly; PRO's closing largely re-asserted the same Colorado stat without new resolution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B3.1 pressure question — forcing PRO to admit its sole evidence (Colorado gender new-hire data) could not support the motion's racial-gap claim — combined with PRO's thin, assertion-only answer in A4.1, was the most decisive exchange of the debate.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 3 (PRO)): A won by keeping the proposition’s comparative burden live and unresolved against B. B’s core route depended on brittle Colorado-style specifics and “halving maximum discrimination” arithmetic that never established racial-gap effects or net superiority over efficiency losses. A’s later speeches narrowed the clash; B largely recycled the same contested metric.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1 resolving B’s alleged contradiction: ranges can be both too weak to stop within-band discrimination and still rigid enough to cap exceptional candidates.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 3 (CON)): Both sides built coherent causal stories, but A maintained a more consistent throughline (secrecy as the discriminatory mechanism, transparency as the corrective, efficiency losses as self-correcting) and was more effective at converting B's own attacks into support for its case—most notably turning the "smokescreen/wide range" objection into evidence that even wide ranges create contestable public benchmarks. B's strongest point, that posted ranges are legally tied to the job rather than the individual and can be defended as "market rate," genuinely dented A's legal-exposure mechanism, but A's Rebuttal 2 partially recovered by shifting to cross-employer comparison as the audit mechanism, leaving the clash muddied rather than clearly won by B. B also suffered real execution costs: Rebuttal 1 was clipped for exceeding the word limit, and its late defense that employers could "outsource or automate" nursing roles to preserve leverage was a weak, poorly-fitted example given the running nurse hypothetical, slightly undercutting its credibility. Pressure questions were well-targeted on both sides and answered in proper A1/A2 format, but A's answers did more to shore up its central comparison (equity gains structural, efficiency losses temporary) while B's answers, though substantive, didn't fully dislodge that framing. Closings mostly crystallized existing clash without major new ground, and A's closing stated the decision rule more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The sharpest exchange was A's Q1 pressure question about posted ranges becoming "documentary evidence" of discrimination versus B's A1 answer that ranges are tied to the job rather than the individual and can be defended via "market rate" justifications—this real legal nuance was the closest B came to breaking A's core mechanism, though A's follow-up (cross-employer benchmarking) kept the point contestable rather than resolved.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 3 (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): B built the clearer decisive path: secrecy as the operative mechanism of pay gaps, ranges converting private discretion into auditable/testable claims, and equity gains as structural while efficiency frictions self-correct via candidate refusal and competitor pressure. A raised real adaptation and distortion worries, but never resolved the internal tension—if employers still pay favored workers above a low posted floor that is documentary risk; if they do not, the same-work gap shrinks—and the clipped Rebuttal 1 plus more recap-heavy closing left B controlling the comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure Q1 and A1 reply: a low posted floor while continuing higher pay to white male nurses becomes documentary evidence and an audit trail, not merely a rebrandable “market condition.”
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 3 (CON)): PRO won the weighing comparison. A framed secrecy as the mechanism for gaps and turned CON's own "wide range smokescreen" into a benchmark that creates legal/market accountability. A answered B's pressure about rebranding bias with audit-trail logic; B's answers to A's questions about documentary evidence and self-correcting competition were weaker and relied on role-redefinition speculation. CON also suffered a clipped R1 and never resolved the efficiency claim - A showed ranges improve self-selection vs. opacity-driven turnover.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R1/R2 turn on wide ranges and auditability: $60k-$150k still reveals a ceiling and forces justification, converting the CON smokescreen objection into evidence for PRO, plus A1 answer that market-conditions claims must survive competitor comparison and regulatory scrutiny.
