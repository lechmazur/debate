### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): A won by controlling the motion’s key comparator—“other discretionary industrial loads”—and forcing a cleaner comparative: interruptible loads already take discounts to curtail, while firm-paying AI loads underwrite capacity. B repeatedly substituted continuous-process hazards (foundries, cold storage) and never fully neutralized the contract/funding and diesel-backfire paths. A’s answers to pressure stayed direct and tied outcomes to public-grid financing; B’s closing still rested on the phantom continuous-process frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s sustained rebuttal that B’s physical-ruin examples are firm continuous loads, not the discretionary interruptible loads already compensated by rate discounts, collapsing B’s equity claim into free-riding.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A wins the definitional clash. The motion is about AI vs other discretionary loads, not AI vs furnaces/cold storage. A shows true discretionary loads (crushing, pumping, electrolysis, crypto) already have physical buffering and are paid year-round via interruptible discounts to be the first to curtail. B never rehabilitates its physical-ruin comparison and reduces to calling firm contracts "raw capital power," without answering who funds grid upgrades. A also damages B's environmental claim by showing mandatory curtailment incentivizes emergency diesel or private gas, which B concedes still "fulfills" the mandate despite toxic local emissions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1/2 chain that interruptible customers already contracted and compensated to pause, so cutting firm-paying AI first rewards free-riding and destroys the financing model for public transmission/storage - directly neutralizing B's furnace/spoilage asymmetry as non-discretionary.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B decisively won by exposing Side A's fundamental category error: comparing AI data centers to continuous-process hazards (foundries, chemical plants) rather than the "discretionary industrial loads" explicitly specified in the motion. Side B effectively argued that true discretionary loads (crypto, pumping, rock crushing) already accept interruptible contracts for discounted rates, while AI pays firm premiums that finance grid upgrades. Side B also cleanly resolved Side A's pressure question regarding diesel backups by explaining the either/or reality of regulatory exemptions for grid emergencies and the resulting capital flight. Side A's rhetoric was polished, but its core case relied on a strawman that Side B dismantled and punished throughout the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where it systematically dismantled Side A's comparison to continuous-process hazards, clarified the definition of discretionary loads, and explained the contractual and regulatory realities of interruptible tariffs and emergency diesel exemptions.

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO won by keeping the decisive clash on real-world harm asymmetry and finite-grid queue-jumping clearer than CON’s market-purity frame. Checkpointing vs irreversible physical damage, plus the deep-pockets problem when capacity is exhausted, survived as the cleaner reason to prefer mandatory AI-training curtailment. CON’s strongest moves (voluntary contracts, seconds-vs-minutes inconsistency, battery incentives) were answered directly and left looking like moral hazard or overstated technical fragility rather than a decisive veto. Progression stayed on the live tradeoff instead of widening, so PRO’s path was easier to vote for after full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO Rebuttal 2 (B4.1–B4.3) resolving the warm-idle/speed contradiction while flipping CON’s battery claim into an own-goal on hardware damage.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built the decisive comparative harm story — checkpointed batch software vs physical industrial processes — and sustained it. PRO also neutralized the thermal-transient attack by defining ramp-down over minutes as rapid versus hours for smelters and noting compute drives most load, while exposing CON's contradiction of batteries existing yet curtailment being catastrophic.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 and Rebuttal 1: turning A's seconds-vs-minutes charge into an advantage (minutes IS emergency relief vs thermal plants) and showing compute load drop vs warm idle, plus the battery-vs-fragility contradiction that collapses A's hardware-damage claim.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A built a much stronger and more intuitive case by distinguishing between fault-tolerant batch software and physically fragile industrial processes. A effectively neutralized B's market-pricing arguments by highlighting how hyperscalers' deep pockets would simply outbid local manufacturers for firm power, coercing them into interruptible tariffs. A's rebuttals were sharper, exposing contradictions in B's claims about hardware fragility and battery usage, while B's reliance on free-market optimization failed to address the real-world physical and economic harms of prioritizing AI training over physical industry.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's "thermal shock" and "battery" arguments in Rebuttal 2, pointing out the contradiction that if hyperscalers use batteries to avoid peak prices, they can easily use them to avoid grid curtailment without suffering hardware damage.
