### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but B built a tighter, more mechanistically grounded case. B's opening distinction between catastrophic/unpredictable risk (insurable) and routine/predictable spending (better handled by targeted subsidy) directly undercut A's core "prevention saves money" claim, and B sustained this through the debate. Crucially, B corrected A's mischaracterization of the preserved private tier as mere cosmetic surgery, showing it actually lets the same GPs sell faster supplementary appointments—an "exit" mechanism that hollows out the public floor while the affluent keep their public entitlement. A never squarely neutralized this point in Rebuttal 2 or Closing, instead reasserting "political durability" without engaging the exit dynamic. B also answered A's strongest pressure question (income cliffs deterring care) with a concrete, plausible mechanism—automatic enrollment and risk-weighted tapering subsidies—that took the sting out of A's best argument. A's counter-question about private markets draining a "massive" non-poor private sector was answered by B clarifying its model isn't full privatization but a sliding scale, which A did not fully rebut. A's mandatory-public-hours and statutory-board mechanisms were also plausibly challenged as not solving supply constraints. Both closings were rhetorically strong, but B's crystallized the decisive clash (promise vs. actual access) more precisely.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.5's correction that the preserved "supplementary" private tier is the same doctors selling faster appointments, not sealed-off cosmetic surgery, which A never fully recovered from in later rounds.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing that a formal universal entitlement does not ensure timely access when clinicians and appointments are scarce, especially while supplementary private care permits affluent patients to buy priority. Side A established the value of prevention but repeatedly overstated its cost savings and relied on ad hoc boards, mandatory hours, and political solidarity without adequately resolving supply constraints. B also presented a credible automatic, tapered, risk-weighted alternative that preserved early intervention without subsidizing routine care for everyone.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s proposed safeguards was decisive: mandatory public hours do not create clinicians, cost-effectiveness boards do not cap total demand, and affluent exit weakens rather than guarantees solidarity. This directly undercut A’s answer to the queue and private-tier problem.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A built a more compelling case by framing universal primary care as a necessary clinical and fiscal firewall against catastrophic emergencies, while effectively attacking Side B's means-tested alternative as politically fragile and administratively burdensome. Side A's argument that Side B's model would create a massive private primary care market that starves safety-net clinics of doctors was a decisive turn that Side B did not fully neutralize.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 turn arguing that leaving primary care entirely private for the non-poor majority (Side B's model) would drain doctors from safety-net clinics far more ruthlessly than a regulated universal floor with supplementary private options.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): The decisive clash was whether "elective and supplementary services" (the only carve-out in the motion) could be stretched to include life-saving specialist treatment, as CON's entire pipeline argument required. PRO repeatedly and precisely pointed out (A2.1, A4.1) that the motion only excludes genuinely elective extras, not essential follow-up care, and that nothing bars governments from also funding necessary treatment while still leaving cosmetic/amenity services private. CON never squarely engaged this textual distinction, instead repeating that "a diagnosis is not a cure" and treating any public funding of secondary care as dissolving the motion into "limitless budgets" — an assertion rather than a rebuttal of PRO's floor/ceiling framing. PRO also directly answered CON's targeting-vs-universal pressure question in its closing by reiterating the stigma/cliff-edge costs of means-testing, a point CON's targeted-subsidy proposal (B4.1) never addressed. CON's workforce-drain argument remained a live, under-rebutted concern, but it was secondary to the central framing battle, which PRO won by keeping the debate anchored to the motion's actual wording and consistently reframing CON's strongest line (the "hollow pipeline") as an attack on a ceiling the motion never promised.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4.1 ("CON keeps converting optional extras into life-saving care to invent a trade-off") decisively exposed that CON's central pipeline objection depended on conflating "elective/supplementary" with "essential treatment," a conflation CON never resolved in later speeches.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by defending the proposition as written and establishing that universal primary care prevents deterioration, reduces downstream demand, and avoids the delay and cliff edges caused by means-testing. Side B offered a memorable pipeline critique, but repeatedly treated essential specialist care, drugs, and surgery as necessarily private even though the motion only allows some elective and supplementary services to remain private. Its claims of inevitable workforce depletion and zero-sum displacement were also asserted more strongly than demonstrated. Side A directly answered both funding and staffing pressure while showing that Side B's targeted alternative leaves the crucial front door dependent on income.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's decisive response was that Side B had turned a guaranteed floor into a ceiling: permitting private markets for some optional services neither requires privatizing life-saving follow-through nor implies that government does nothing beyond primary care. This substantially weakened Side B's diagnosis-without-treatment framing.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by decisively exposing Side B's misinterpretation of the motion. Side B built its case on the assumption that the motion forces the privatization of life-saving secondary care, but Side A correctly clarified that "elective and supplementary" does not include essential specialist treatment. Side A's "floor, not a ceiling" framework and "rationing by deterioration" argument provided a much stronger and more textually faithful path to victory, while Side B's zero-sum assumptions collapsed under pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which sharply dismantled Side B's core premise by clarifying that essential survival care is not "elective," exposing Side B's reliance on a strawman interpretation of the motion.
