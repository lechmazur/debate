### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON built a cleaner, more sustained throughline: conditioning permits on venue-submitted plans certifies paperwork, not outcomes, and structurally favors large operators who can afford consultants. The decisive moment was CON's pressure question about a city-run bus failing despite a compliant plan: PRO's answer conceded the venue keeps its license as long as it "executed its own obligations," even though patrons remain stranded and streets remain dirty. CON exploited this concession sharply in both Rebuttal 2 and Closing, showing that PRO's own prevention framework collapses into the same "paper success, real-world failure" problem it accuses CON's enforcement model of having. PRO's tiering answer (A1) asserted that scaled requirements are "standard administrative practice, not discretionary corruption," but never squarely answered CON's objectivity critique that a decibel reading is measurable while an official's judgment of a "convincing dispersal narrative" is not — leaving the veto/discretion line of attack live through the end. PRO's moral-hazard and externality-internalization framing was coherent and intuitively appealing, but it was largely repeated across rounds rather than sharpened against CON's strongest counter, and PRO never fully resolved the dilemma that tiering is either toothless for small venues or prohibitively burdensome in a way that favors big chains. CON's alternative (district-funded transit/sanitation plus strict ex-ante decibel/staggering rules and real enforcement) was positioned as equally preventive but without the paperwork gate, and PRO never dismantled that comparison. Both sides executed the Q&A format properly, but CON's question did more real damage than PRO's, and CON's answers to PRO's questions held up better under scrutiny.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Question about a cancelled municipal bus, and PRO's concession in A2 that the venue keeps its license by having "executed its own obligations" regardless of whether patrons actually got home safely — a concession CON converted into its central closing argument that the policy certifies paper compliance rather than real dispersal.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B won by distinguishing real, district-scale prevention from venue-level plan approval. B offered a clearer preventive alternative using funded shared services and enforceable operating conditions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s tiering was decisive: if small venues satisfy the transit condition through schedules and rideshare points, the requirement is largely theatrical; if they must create actual capacity, it becomes disproportionate and favors larger operators. A never fully escaped that dilemma.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): CON successfully exposed a decisive dilemma in PRO's framework: venue-specific transit and sanitation plans either constitute paperwork theater (when light enough for small venues to satisfy) or corporate favoritism (when heavy enough to require real capacity). ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but PRO (B) adapted better across rounds. B's opening externality framing ("who pays for the peak the venue creates") stayed consistent and sharpened into a clean weighing principle by closing. Critically, B answered the pressure questions in the required A1:/A2: format and used the exchange to narrow "stronger plans" to proportionate, scalable measures (staggered closing, staffed/lit pickup, shared district costs) rather than the expensive private-infrastructure version CON kept attacking. CON (A) landed some strong isolated hits — the sewer-fee analogy critique and the "corporate moat" concern — but in Rebuttal 2 and Closing it largely re-asserted the moat/chaos claims without directly engaging B's narrowed, low-cost version of compliance, making its final speeches feel repetitive rather than progressive. A's answer to B's Q1 ("Yes, grant the license anyway, patrons manage via taxis") effectively conceded the externalization B was attacking, and B exploited this well in closing by reframing it as proof that unconditional hours just export costs to residents. A's closing never directly countered that "who pays" framing, settling for recap of earlier points instead of fresh weighing. That asymmetry in late-round adaptation and direct pressure-answering gives B a clear, if not overwhelming, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answer to A's Q1 ("A1: It does not need the agency... if no safe dispersal is possible, denial is not favoritism—it is the point") was the pivotal exchange: it honestly engaged the hardest question against the policy and reframed the apparent trap as the policy working as intended, and A's closing leaned on this concession rhetorically but never supplied a mechanism defeating the scaled, proportional compliance B had just described.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by framing later hours as a privilege that creates marginal costs and by showing that proportional, shared plans need not require every venue to operate public infrastructure. Side B raised meaningful affordability and concentration concerns, but repeatedly assumed an inflexible, prohibitively expensive mandate despite A’s scalable-plan clarification. B’s willingness to grant later licenses even without stronger dispersal or mitigation planning reinforced A’s externalities case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A most effectively turned B’s staggered-dispersal argument: varied closing times help only if patrons can depart safely, so staggering supports conditional extensions with transport and crowd-management plans rather than unconditional licenses. B never convincingly established that proportionate district-level plans would necessarily exclude independent venues.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won because it established a principled cost-internalization framework for late-night externalities and successfully neutralized CON's strongest objection. When CON argued the policy would build a corporate moat by requiring transit, sanitation, and noise plans that only mega-clubs could afford, PRO introduced a decisive proportionality response: small venues meet the requirement at small scale through district frameworks, shared shuttles, and scaled sound controls. CON largely repeated its "corporate moat" claim without fully engaging the substance of this rebuttal. Additionally, CON's answers to PRO's pressure questions conceded that it would reject conditional licensing in principle and grant late hours even without dispersal plans, leaving its alternative exposed as simply exporting costs to residents and public services. PRO crystallized this in the closing, making the comparative choice clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was PRO's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7), where it directly answered CON's pressure questions by introducing proportionality and district-framework mechanisms. By showing that transit plans could be met through pooled district shuttles rather than venue-run subway lines, and that non-residential venues face lighter noise standards, PRO collapsed CON's central "impossible for independents" narrative and kept its own case alive.
