### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built the more damaging structural attack: in Rebuttal 1 it forced PRO into a dilemma—either the proposed substitutes (synthetic data, subsets, federated evaluation) satisfy "sufficient for independent reproduction," in which case the rule collapses into CON's tiered-transparency position, or they don't, in which case the rule refuses valuable, ethically-constrained research (hospital, climate, genomics). PRO's answer to the pressure questions was honest and consistent—it bit the bullet, admitting such papers would indeed be refused unless redesigned into narrower claims—but this concession validated exactly the practical cost CON had been warning about since the opening. CON's final rebuttal and closing capitalized on this cleanly, reframing the stakes as "credential versus science" and arguing that stripping the real-world result leaves only an untestable, unprioritizable "traveling method." PRO's case remained coherent and well-grounded (the chemistry/reagent analogy, the falsifiability argument, the credential framing), but it never fully neutralized the core practical harm CON kept pressing: that an absolute refusal rule, as literally worded, forecloses legitimate research the motion's own defenders admit cannot comply. Both sides executed the Q&A format properly with clear A1/A2 labeling, so no execution penalty applies either way, but CON's dilemma-and-concession sequence was the more decisive piece of clash in the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 (A2.1-A2.3), which exposed that PRO's compromises (synthetic data, subsets, federated evaluation) either concede tiered transparency or still require refusing valuable research—followed by PRO's Rebuttal 2 explicitly biting the bullet on refusal, which CON then used to argue the rule guarantees real scientific loss.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): PRO kept the sharper live route: publication is a credential, and unreproducible computational results are assertions, not inspectable claims. That preserves the proposition as written and still lets methods travel via code/protocol. CON made the cost vivid (lost real-world findings; resource bias; performative dumps), but never neutralized the credential/falsifiability core; later replication of a method is not reproduction of the reported result, and PRO correctly treated that as a different, weaker publication package. Across the full exchange PRO answered pressure more directly and made the decisive comparison easier to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1–A4.3 answering B’s pressure: synthetic/subset/federated options do not meet the motion; refuse unreproducible hospital/supercomputer results rather than certify them, while still allowing redesigned transferable methods.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins by defending the motion with strict definitional discipline and successfully distinguishing reproduction of a specific reported result from later replication of a method. This bright-line credential argument—that journals should not certify unverifiable empirical results—survived CON’s pragmatic objections more cleanly than CON’s tiered-transparency alternative survived the textual requirement for independent reproduction. CON’s most powerful lines about silencing vital research were blunted when CON itself conceded that synthetic data and subsets do not reproduce the original result, and B exploited that concession to show that CON was effectively arguing for a different standard than the motion.

This honesty under pressure sharpened the decisive clash over whether a journal’s credential should attach to results that cannot be independently reproduced.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases, but CON (Side A) ultimately won the decisive exchange. PRO's strongest move—redefining "sufficient for reproduction" as tolerance-based and redefining "provision" to include controlled-access/synthetic data—was exploited by CON, who showed this concession turns the rule into an unverified "deposit receipt": PRO explicitly conceded journals "need not rerun every paper" and that a deposited package "does not guarantee truth," meaning the promised stress-testing is voluntary and may never occur. CON then used this to frame the decisive asymmetry in the closing: the rule's harm (certain, immediate rejection of sound but legally/ethically constrained research, e.g., privacy-governed clinical data) is guaranteed, while its benefit (actual independent reproduction) is merely possible and often never realized. PRO's closing reasserted its core thesis about "possibility of correction" distinguishing science from testimony but did not squarely rebut this asymmetry or fully resolve the tension created by its own concession. CON's pressure questions (on self-selected tolerances and denied access) also forced PRO to bite real bullets—admitting that genuine denial means "publication waits or is refused"—which directly validates CON's collateral-damage argument. PRO's answers to CON's questions were competent but did not neutralize the live asymmetry that CON crystallized in the final round, making CON's path to victory cleaner and more resolved by the end of the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.5) and Closing (A5.1), which seized on PRO's concession that journals "need not rerun every paper" and that a deposited package "does not guarantee truth," reframing PRO's rule as an unverified "deposit receipt" that guarantees certain costs (rejection of privacy-bound sound research) while only promising uncertain, voluntary future verification—an asymmetry PRO's closing never directly rebutted.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): PRO kept the decisive clash on what journals certify: checkable computation versus persuasive story. Clarifying sufficiency as rerunnable pattern-within-tolerance, not bit-identity, neutralized CON’s impossibility claim; controlled-access/independent-repository provision answered the data veto without abandoning the rule. CON’s strongest weighing—the certain-publication veto versus unguaranteed later runs, and the “deposit receipt” charge—landed cleanly, but did not show why permanent inaccessibility should still earn journal authority, nor escape the circularity PRO’s pressure exposed (you cannot demonstrate withholding undermines a claim if you never see the materials). PRO’s comparative path stayed closer to the motion as written and made the vote easier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2 A1/A2: disclosure enables outsiders to stress-test seeds/tolerances/hardware, and real provision requires independent access bodies—not author veto or mere named routes—collapsing CON’s box-ticking and privacy-impossibility routes at once.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): CON wins by turning PRO's central concession—that journals need not actually rerun code to enforce the rule—into a decisive liability. CON showed that without pre-publication verification, "sufficient for independent reproduction" degrades into an unverified deposit receipt: it immediately vetoes sound, privacy-bound, and low-resource work while offering only a speculative, often unrealized possibility of later checking. PRO's principled distinction between science and testimony never overcame this practical asymmetry, and CON's feasibility-sensitive alternative remained the cleaner fit for what journals can realistically enforce.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's "deposit receipt" turn in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which leveraged PRO's admission that journals would not rerun papers to show the rule guarantees immediate suppression without assured verification.
