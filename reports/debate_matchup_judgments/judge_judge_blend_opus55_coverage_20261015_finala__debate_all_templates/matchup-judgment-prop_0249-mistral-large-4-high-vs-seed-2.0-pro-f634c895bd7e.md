### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built a tighter, more mechanistic case: it identified concrete loopholes in the mandate (fines get priced in and negotiated down, as regulatory history generally shows; budget devices exit the legal market entirely; and most damagingly, manufacturers can keep "support" technically open while simply never publishing a patch, which the mandate does not prohibit). This last point, raised in Rebuttal 2, directly undercuts PRO's central causal story and was never squarely answered in PRO's closing. PRO (Side B) had a strong opening frame (market failure, externalized risk, seatbelt/food-safety analogies) and scored a real point in Pressure Questions by forcing CON to admit customs currently lacks any legal standard to act on insecure devices — but CON's answer that legal authority without enforcement capacity changes little was a reasonable parry, and PRO's own fixes ("set fines higher," "pair with market-access bans," "$35 plug") were asserted rather than mechanistically defended against CON's specific economic and loophole objections. CON's closing crystallized the decisive tradeoff (partial leverage over legal vendors vs. zero leverage once everything flees to grey markets) more sharply than PRO's recap-heavy closing resolved it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point that manufacturers can leave formal "support" technically open while simply never shipping a patch — directly neutralizing PRO's claim that a mandated timeline converts discretionary support into enforceable duty — was the most damaging exchange, and PRO never closed this loophole in the remaining speeches.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that legally required updates change security from discretionary support into an enforceable duty. Side A's affordability and grey-market concerns were relevant, but rested on unsupported absolute predictions and several brittle figures. Its central distinction between maintaining nominal support and actually patching also misread a proposition requiring devices to receive security updates. Side B sometimes stretched beyond the motion by invoking market-access bans and extra border infrastructure, but its core case survived without those additions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's most decisive response was that active engineering support is not equivalent to a legal obligation to issue security updates. This directly undercut Side A's Mirai argument: manufacturers choosing not to patch despite having engineers demonstrates the incentive problem that enforceable duties address, rather than proving a mandate irrelevant.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side B wins by demonstrating a structural mismatch between the mandate and the actual problem, plus perverse market effects. B successfully turned A's Mirai example by showing those devices were actively supported yet unp patched, proving the flaw is patch incentive, not abandonment timeline. B then showed how manufacturers could evade the mandate by leaving support technically open while never publishing patches, and argued budget devices would flee to untraceable grey markets rather than comply. A's responses on fine calibration and border enforcement were plausible but never fully neutralized B's core comparative tradeoff: the proposition would eliminate traceable legal vendors and push vulnerable hardware into channels where no enforcement can reach it, likely worsening the botnet risk rather than reducing it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 demolition of the Mirai example and the "technically open support without patches" evasion mechanism, which showed the mandate targets abandonment rather than the actual failure to patch, and that the timeline requirement is circumventable without fixing the underlying incentive problem.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases without fabricated sourcing, but CON (A) adapted more effectively across rounds. CON's key reframe—that the mandate does not eliminate the externality B relies on, but merely relocates it onto poor consumers via higher upfront prices—directly neutralized PRO's central "who pays" framing rather than just restating it. CON also introduced a tort-liability alternative to the externality problem in Rebuttal 1 that PRO never engaged with in Rebuttal 2 or closing, leaving a live, unanswered route for CON. PRO's rebuttals were solid (tiered mandates, recall/bond analogies) but PRO's second rebuttal was explicitly cut off ("[clipped]"), an execution flaw, and PRO's bond-mechanism claims about "standard" practice in airbags/chemical disposal edged toward unsupported precision. CON's distinction between reactive recall liability and this proposal's perpetual proactive obligation was a sharp, underexplored point that PRO's analogy never fully overcame. The debate stayed close and both sides argued skillfully, but CON's rebuttals did more actual damage to PRO's core framing, and PRO left more pressure unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.1 reframe—arguing the mandate doesn't erase the externality PRO describes but merely shifts its cost onto low-income buyers—was the most decisive single exchange, since PRO's closing never offered a substantive answer beyond reasserting that hidden costs were "larger," without engaging the shift-vs-elimination framing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side B won by centering the third-party harms of insecure devices and explaining why consumer choice, reviews, and tort liability cannot adequately address diffuse botnet risks. Side A raised credible affordability and market-consolidation concerns, but repeatedly mischaracterized a finite minimum support period as perpetual or indefinite maintenance and never established that cheaper devices outweigh costs imposed on non-buyers. B's claims about bonds costing pennies were insufficiently grounded, but the core externality case remained stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's most decisive response was that accepting risk is not purely a consumer choice when an abandoned device can attack unrelated people and public services. This undercut Side A's affordability framing, while B's allowance for tiered finite periods exposed A's repeated perpetual-maintenance characterization as overstated.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by keeping its externality framework live while materially weakening Side B's objections. A showed that abandoned IoT devices impose hidden costs on everyone via botnets and compromised networks, and it directly answered B's core burdens: the hidden-cost argument undermined B's regressive-tax frame, and the per-unit bond mechanism solved the live orphan-device problem. B's claims that the market is already correcting were unsupported and effectively dismantled by A, and its hardware-limitation argument was turned into a safety threshold. B's remaining barrier-to-entry frame was not enough to outweigh the demonstrated third-party harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7): A directly answered both pressure questions with the bond mechanism for orphaned devices and the transparent-pricing reframe (hidden costs vs. upfront costs), which decisively blunted B's regressive-tax and startup-failure objections.
