- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the motion as written (easier pause/split/reconfigure vs lock-in/opacity) and forcing a cleaner comparison: fixed costs and pooling justify cost-reflective premiums and genuine annual commitments, not opaque rigidity. B conceded flexibility may not be cheaper and cross-subsidies may shrink, then leaned on budget optics; that did not defeat A’s standard that households should see the trade and choose. Overall, A made the decisive clash easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 redistribution turn: selection may shrink discounts, but that is partly financed by intermittent viewers’ unwanted months—so CON counting the recipients’ savings while ignoring the payers’ bills fails the fairness comparison.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A won by exposing the normative asymmetry in CON's pooling argument: rigid bundles rely on intermittent viewers subsidizing year-round users through unwanted payments. CON's adverse-selection mechanism was plausible but never overcame PRO's fairness turn or explained why voluntary commitment discounts would collapse rather than simply redistribute costs. A's rebuttals were sharper, narrowing the debate to whose costs count, and B's closing restated harms without answering that burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal (A4), where it reframed adverse selection as a redistribution problem and argued the "cheaper annual rate is partly financed by another household's unwanted spending," directly blunting CON's core economic objection.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully reframing Side A's strongest economic argument (the adverse selection and death spiral of the bundle's cross-subsidy) as a defense of an unfair baseline. Side B's distinction between binding annual commitments and cost-reflective flexible rates neutralized the "gaming" objection, and its closing crystallized the clash over whose costs should count, making the choice clear for a third-party reader.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing to Side A's "selection effect" argument, where Side B pointed out that Side A was treating intermittent viewers' unwanted payments as the baseline to protect, effectively flipping Side A's defense of the "pooled bargain" into a defense of trapped consumers.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): A won a close, high-quality exchange by keeping the decisive clash on who does the arithmetic: monthly defaults should be pausable/splittable/reconfigurable while genuine commitment is sold as an open, separate discount. B’s best live route—repricing risk and that unused flexibility can still raise the standard price—landed real pressure, especially after Qs, but never fully neutralized A’s answers that trapped quiet-month revenue is not an earned discount and that coexistence already exists for monthly vs annual plans. A also kept splitting and component exit live while B narrowed them toward mere portability. Net: cleaner burden framing and better resolution of the pressure exchange for the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2: if active-month prices erase pause value households stay subscribed, and commitment should earn its own discount rather than be financed by locked monthly users.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A won by keeping the debate anchored to the proposition’s core concern—monthly bundles should not mimic cable lock-in—while demonstrating that flexibility and stable annual commitments can coexist. A reframed CON’s economic warnings as quiet-month subsidies extracted through friction rather than honest discounts earned by choice, and crystallized the clash around who gets to do the arithmetic: the household or the provider. CON’s repricing argument remained speculative and never overcame A’s concrete examples of modern household disruption and the feasibility of transparent component pricing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal, where it answered B’s pressure questions by distinguishing commitment chosen openly from revenue extracted through forced continuation, and showed that a pausable default does not prevent households from staying subscribed if repricing makes pausing uneconomical.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully reframing the core economic clash. While Side A argued persuasively that baking flexibility into the default monthly bundle would raise its baseline price and harm stable households, Side B decisively countered that if the current baseline price relies on trapping users in dead months, it is an extracted subsidy rather than a genuine discount. Side B's argument that true commitment discounts should be explicitly chosen rather than enforced via friction was more normatively compelling and effectively neutralized Side A's defense of the status quo. Side B also maintained sharper rhetoric and better progression throughout the rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side A's pricing argument by distinguishing between a "subsidy extracted because exit is cumbersome" and a "discount earned by choosing commitment," effectively invalidating Side A's defense of the locked-in baseline price.
