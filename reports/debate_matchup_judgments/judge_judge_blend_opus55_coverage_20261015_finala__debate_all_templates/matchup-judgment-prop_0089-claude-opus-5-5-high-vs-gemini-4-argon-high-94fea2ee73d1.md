### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B won by separating open eligibility from genuinely universal capacity. Boston supplied Side A's best lasting-outcomes evidence, but its seat lottery did not establish that every child could be served at comparable quality or cost. Side B then tied that gap to the motion's cost-benefit burden: gains concentrated among high-risk children do not automatically justify full-price subsidies for families already purchasing preschool. Side A effectively challenged Tennessee and Quebec as direct comparisons, but never supplied a convincing marginal-benefit calculation for universality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange was B4.2-B4.5: it rejected Side A's demand that CON prove worsened adult outcomes, restored PRO's burden to show lasting gains exceeding universal costs, and explained why targeted-program returns cannot simply be multiplied across affluent children with lower marginal benefit.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): CON built the clearer path to victory on the motion’s cost-benefit terms. PRO admitted that no statewide universal program has proven lasting adult gains, and conceded that middle-class children gain less while requiring full public-school-year spending to avoid thin classrooms. CON leveraged those admissions to show that universalizing pre-K inflates the taxpayer bill with massive deadweight loss: most subsidies go to affluent children who already attend private preschool, so the average cost per unit of benefit becomes economically indefensible. Boston’s lottery evidence, while strong for open enrollment, was limited by capacity constraints and did not refute the scale problem. CON successfully neutralized PRO’s political-economy and childcare arguments and crystallized the debate around the implausible cost-efficiency of universal provision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal and closing exposing that PRO’s cost framework collapsed under pressure: PRO shifted from “a few thousand dollars” to “full public-school year cost” and conceded middle-class children “gain less,” which CON weaponized to show that universal scale makes the cost-per-unit-of-benefit economically indefensible.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by effectively neutralizing Side A's core evidence and controlling the comparative clash. Side B successfully argued that Tennessee and Quebec were not universal 4-year-old pre-K programs, turning Tennessee's failure back on Side A's targeted/sliding-scale alternative. Side B also defended the Boston study convincingly by clarifying that universal eligibility with a lottery for excess demand is the standard and fairest test of a universal program's design. While Side A made a strong cost-benefit argument regarding affluent deadweight loss and scaling costs in the later rounds, Side B's political economy defense (universality protects funding and quality) and holistic weighing of the long-term gains provided a cleaner, more resilient path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's persistent and effective turn of the Tennessee evidence, pointing out in Rebuttal 1 and the Closing that Tennessee was a targeted program for low-income children—exactly the model Side A was advocating for—thereby making Side A's alternative the only model in the debate proven to fail.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by separating evidence that a high-quality local program can work from proof that universality itself reliably creates lasting gains worth its added cost. Side A offered an appealing integration and political-coalition mechanism, but did not establish that universal enrollment actually produces integration or that Boston and Oklahoma prove the universal model at scale. B also supplied a credible sliding-scale alternative and directly exposed A's overstatement of B's concession. B's clipped first rebuttal and initial Oklahoma error narrowed the margin but were later handled openly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response that Boston's rationed lottery demonstrates the potential of a carefully staffed program, not that universality causes quality, decisively weakened A's main empirical example. The neighborhood-enrollment answer further undercut A's peer-integration mechanism, leaving the extra cost of subsidizing affluent families insufficiently justified.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): CON successfully demonstrated that scalable evidence for universal pre-K shows fade-out or reversal (Tennessee, Quebec, Head Start), while PRO's positive evidence came from non-scalable showcase programs (Boston) or showed fading gains (Oklahoma). CON's argument that neighborhood enrollment undermines PRO's peer-integration mechanism, and that PRO's case ultimately reduced to "works when quality is high" rather than proving universal scale produces results, created a cleaner path to victory. PRO's pattern of relabeling negative evidence and asserting political buy-in as a justification for subsidizing affluent families failed to meet the motion's burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal exposing that universal pre-K enrolls by neighborhood, so socioeconomic segregation persists despite formal universality, directly dismantled PRO's peer-effects mechanism, while CON's crystallization that PRO's defense had become "pre-K works when quality is high"—a different motion—sharpened the decisive clash.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's empirical examples and causal mechanisms. A brilliantly exposed the contradiction in B's defense of Boston (which used a lottery, meaning seats were rationed, undermining B's claim that it proves universal scale works) and introduced a devastating, unanswered logical rebuttal regarding neighborhood assignment negating B's peer-effects mechanism. B relied heavily on moving the goalposts—dismissing negative results as "daycare" or "targeted"—which A effectively called out and penalized. A's argumentative discipline and sharp traps made the decisive clash much clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it used Side B's own definition of universality (no lotteries because no one is rejected) to disqualify Boston's lottery-based study, and introduced the neighborhood assignment argument to neutralize the peer-effects mechanism.
