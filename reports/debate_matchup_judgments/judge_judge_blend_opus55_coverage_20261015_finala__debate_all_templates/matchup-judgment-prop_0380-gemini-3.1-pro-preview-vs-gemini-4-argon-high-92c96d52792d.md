### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B built a tighter, more coherent framework ("ballot as sovereign power" vs. mere self-expression) and sustained it through every round. The decisive exchange was the jury-duty pincer: B repeatedly pressed that A cannot simultaneously claim sixteen-year-olds have full adult-level "cold cognition" while conceding they're unfit for jury service, and A's answer (jury duty is "specialized," voting is just "values") was thin given that jurors are ordinary laypeople too. B's PQ answers were also stronger substantively—distinguishing taxation from suffrage with the non-citizen/child-worker analogy directly undercut A's "taxation without representation" pillar, and the "mock elections already deliver civic education without lowering the voting age" point neutralized A's habit-formation/classroom-integration argument. A's closing reasserted its themes but never resolved the dependency paradox B crystallized: the very "stability" and parental/school custody A cites as making 16 ideal is what B uses to show these voters lack independent political standing. B's closing line tying this together was the cleanest crystallization of the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to A's Q1 (taxation doesn't purchase a ballot, citing child workers and non-citizen taxpayers who pay taxes without voting) directly dismantled A's core "taxation without representation" argument, and B's recurring jury-duty contradiction pressure was never adequately resolved by A.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON won by establishing a clearer, more consistent framework around the nature of the franchise as sovereign authority rather than mere self-expression. PRO opened with a strong case emphasizing habit formation, cold cognition, and stakeholder fairness, but under pressure retreated to weaker claims—most notably that voting is simply an expression of lived experience and values, while jury duty is specialized. CON pounced on this inconsistency, showing that if sixteen-year-olds lack the maturity to apply a single law as a juror, they should not elect the lawmakers who write the entire legal code. CONs mock-election counter effectively neutralized PROs civic-education argument without widening the debate, and CONs closing crystallized a devastating paradox: the very features PRO cited to make sixteen optimal—structural dependency, shelter from adult chaos, and high school guidance—are precisely why those teenagers should not wield coercive power over fellow citizens. PRO never resolved the tension between legal minority status and sovereign authority, leaving CONs path to victory live after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CONs dependency paradox and the mock-election counter. CON demonstrated that PROs argument for stability and high school integration actually undermined the claim to sovereign authority, while showing that all civic-education benefits could be achieved through mock elections without the risks of classroom coercion or partisan capture.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's core analogies and reframing Side A's main practical advantage (stability) into a fatal normative flaw (structural dependency). Side B's framing of voting as coercive sovereign power rather than mere self-expression forced Side A onto the defensive. Furthermore, Side B cleanly neutralized Side A's taxation argument by pointing out that non-citizens and younger teens pay taxes without the franchise, and Side B's closing paradox perfectly crystallized why an environment ideal for learning is disqualifying for wielding sovereign authority.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they flipped Side A's jury analogy (noting jurors are laypeople deciding a single factual dispute, while voters impose laws on millions) and neutralized the taxation argument by pointing out that 14-year-old workers and non-citizens pay taxes without voting.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly around a clear central clash: does legal dependency disqualify sixteen-year-olds from voting, or does bearing adult-like obligations (taxes, labor, court liability) entitle them to representation? PRO's strongest move was consistently distinguishing contract-signing capacity (protection from individual exploitation) from voting (collective judgment with no personal exploitation risk), and this distinction was never fully dismantled by CON, who kept asserting the "double standard" without directly engaging why voting's risk profile differs from contract risk. PRO's pressure questions landed well, particularly forcing CON to admit the eighteen-year-old's "right to lease" is often theoretical rather than actual, which CON's answer conceded implicitly ("regardless of where the young adult temporarily sleeps") without fully resolving the force of the point. CON's own pressure questions were sharp (especially Q2 on the fourteen-year-old slippery slope), but PRO's answer—that sixteen is a real statutory threshold for labor/schooling exit while fourteen is not—was a serviceable, non-arbitrary line that CON did not meaningfully reopen in closing. CON's closing repeated its core "legal autonomy as bedrock" framing without engaging the unanswered logistics-of-first-vote/habit-formation argument or the specific contract-vs-ballot distinction, making PRO's final synthesis feel like it crystallized the debate more effectively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2.3/A2.4 distinction between contract risk (personal, impulsive, exploitable) and voting (slow, deliberative, collective) directly undercut CON's central "legal dependency" framework, and CON never fully answered why this distinction fails.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B successfully defended the representation and habit-formation advantages of lowering the voting age, while parrying Side A's legal-autonomy objections with the deliberative-versus-logistical distinction and the observation that many eighteen-year-olds remain in identical practical circumstances. Side A's double-standard argument on contracts was sharp but overstated PRO's concession and never fully neutralized the policy-distortion and turnout empirics that PRO kept live through the final rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's distinction between deliberative capacity and logistical friction in B4.1, which cleanly answered Side A's pressure question and preserved the habit-formation argument.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by successfully dismantling Side B's core arguments regarding maturity and independence. A's distinction between the logistical friction of voting at eighteen and the cognitive deliberation required to choose candidates cleanly answered B's best pressure question. Furthermore, A's practical observation that an eighteen-year-old high school senior living at home faces the same household dynamics as a sixteen-year-old effectively neutralized B's reliance on the theoretical right to sign a lease as a marker of true independence. Side B relied too heavily on the contract law argument, failing to adequately address A's distinction between protecting minors from personal financial exploitation and granting them collective political representation. Side A's case was more adaptable, logically rigorous, and rhetorically sharp.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's pressure question, distinguishing the cognitive ease of deliberative voting from the logistical friction of registering during life transitions, and the subsequent attack on the theoretical right to sign a lease for eighteen-year-olds still living at home.
