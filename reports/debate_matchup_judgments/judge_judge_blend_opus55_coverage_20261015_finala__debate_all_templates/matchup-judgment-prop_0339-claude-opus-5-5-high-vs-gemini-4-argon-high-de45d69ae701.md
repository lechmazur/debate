### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won by keeping the decisive clashes live and unresolved against PRO: the motion regulates the wrong layer (app stores cannot hide installed icons or auto-re-lock without becoming an OS mandate PRO had to invent), the mandated switch creates false security while the browser remains the real vector, and vague “high-risk social” labeling produces over-blocking that tired parents cannot reverse. PRO’s design defenses and opt-in framing repeatedly collapsed into rewriting the proposition or conceding that usable tools already exist at the OS level; clipped rebuttals further limited PRO’s recovery. The kitchen-tablet comparison therefore favors no mandate over a placebo floor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s layer and false-security dismantling (A2.1–A2.5 then A4.1–A4.6): store ≠ home-screen/OS lock, and a government “simple switch” plus open browser is a deadbolt on an upstairs window while the front door stays open.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): CON won by showing the mandate regulates the wrong technical layer and creates a dangerous false sense of security. PRO established a sympathetic problem—shared family devices and buried settings—but never fully recovered from CON's demonstration that app stores already ban hardcore pornography, that legal casino apps require adult verification, and that the real harms children face flow through the unregulated web browser. CON successfully turned PRO's "exhausted parent" frame against the proposal: a busy parent flips the government-mandated switch, assumes the device is childproof, and hands it over while the browser remains wide open. CON also exposed the vagueness of "high-risk social apps" and the practical impossibility of whitelisting benign apps that are hidden from search and view. PRO's defense required treating an app-store mandate as an OS-level security feature, a stretch CON cleanly identified, and two clipped speeches added an execution cost for PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (Turn 8 / B4), which argued that PRO could only defend the mandate by rewriting it to regulate operating systems rather than app stores, and that the actual one-tap dangers children face are in the web browser the mandate completely ignores. This exchange neutralized PRO's harm scenario and crystallized the technical and comparative flaw in the model.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B wins by effectively dismantling Side A's proposed mechanism. B correctly identifies that an app store mandate cannot control home-screen icons or app-launch permissions without actually being an operating-system mandate, a distinction A blurs by relying on OS-level features like auto-locking PINs. Furthermore, B decisively undercuts A's core emotional example by pointing out that legal casino apps already require ID and banking verification, making the real danger the web browser. A's mandate leaves the browser wide open while creating a dangerous placebo effect for exhausted parents, a comparative harm B successfully weighs to secure the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it exposes that Side A's defense of the "auto-locking PIN" relies entirely on operating-system security features rather than app store functionality, and definitively neutralizes the "nine-year-old downloading a casino app" threat by noting that legal gambling apps require adult identity verification.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): CON won the decisive clash on the motion’s novel piece: government-defined “high-risk social apps.” PRO’s shared-device and profit-friction case worked well for gambling/porn, but under pressure the social category either overreached (encrypted chat, then Roblox/Reddit/Minecraft via design audits) or recreated the blunt lock parents abandon—while adding a lasting state content lever. CON kept the comparison cleaner: redundant where categories already exist, costly and leaky where new.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s A2/A4 sequence: PRO’s “objective” stranger-discovery fix either misses in-app hazards or hides major platforms unless regulators force default design changes, collapsing the “simple existing taxonomy / not censorship” claim into a state-audited risk class.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by demonstrating that the mandate solves a concrete shared-device problem that existing blunt age locks cannot address without locking parents out of everyday utilities. Side B's central objection—that the "high-risk social" category creates an unacceptable state lever—was blunted by Side A's refinement to objective functional criteria (apps featuring open stranger discovery or public lobbies) and the fact that the filter is entirely optional. Side B's dilemma that the category is either too narrow or too broad was answered when Side A showed that capturing multiplayer games with public lobbies is a feature, not a bug, because it protects children without blocking adult news, browser, or podcast apps. Side A's Pressure Question also exposed that Side B was defending the status quo's 17+ locks despite their identical flaw of blocking support forums. Side B's "state overreach" framing remained more abstract and speculative than Side A's grounded, comparative analysis of parental control architectures.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 turn that caught Side B objecting the filter misses stranger contact inside games while simultaneously complaining that an objective "open stranger discovery" standard captures those same games, showing Side B's objections were contradictory indictments of the status quo.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing the mandate as merely exposing existing app store metadata via a user-facing toggle, which neutralized Side B's censorship concerns. A's comparative argument—that parents disable current 17+ locks because they block adult utilities like browsers, not because they hide games like Roblox—decisively answered B's dilemma about the toggle being too broad.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where A trapped B in a contradiction regarding games with open stranger lobbies (B complained the filter missed them, then complained it caught them) and effectively defended the scope of the toggle.
