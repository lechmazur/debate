### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by defending bounded localization of critical bottlenecks rather than the autarkic, walled-off duplication Side A repeatedly attacked. B directly explained why contracts cannot diversify supply until subsidized physical capacity exists, why several large regional hubs can retain scale, and how tapering support can limit the price premium. A offered a credible cheaper alternative through coordinated trusted hubs, but that alternative conceded much of B's industrial-policy mechanism and never established that the proposition requires twelve subscale national factories or trade walls. A's claims that premiums inevitably become permanent and are currently killing projects were asserted more strongly than grounded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange began with B asking which alternative suppliers currently exist without someone subsidizing capacity. A conceded that none exist, then proposed internationally coordinated subsidized hubs. B persuasively argued that this conceded the need for industrial policy and physical diversification, while its large-hub model answered A's scale objection.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins because CON's central alternative strategy—diversifying through foreign contracts—collapsed under pressure when CON conceded that no alternative suppliers exist at scale today for critical nodes like wafers, cathodes, and magnets. While CON recovered with a clever reframe toward coordinated international open hubs, that alternative remained underdeveloped and vulnerable to PRO's closing rebuttal that such hubs still constitute industrial policy abroad and leave countries dependent on foreign access in a crisis. PRO bounded the cost premium honestly (20–30% on components during scaling), effectively weaponized CON's admission in closing, and maintained that fragmentation into large regional hubs preserves enough scale to learn. CON's live arguments—that localization kills current deployment and fragments learning curves—stayed plausible but could not overcome the proposition's explicit acceptance of tradeoffs when the primary counter-path lacked physical grounding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure questions in Turn 5 asked which alternative suppliers exist today outside the dominant producer. CON's Rebuttal 2 answer conceded "There are none right now" (A4.1) and pivoted to an untested international-coordination model. This concession destroyed CON's opening claim that countries could simply contract with diverse foreign suppliers, shifting the burden to an underdeveloped alternative and enabling PRO to close on the point that contracts without physical capacity are "promises waiting for a crisis."
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won by successfully trapping Side B on the physical reality of the current supply chain monopoly. When pressed in the Pressure Questions round on which alternative suppliers exist, Side B admitted there are none, which fatally undermined their core alternative of simply "diversifying through contracts." Side A effectively argued that localization of critical nodes is the only way to create the physical capacity needed for true diversification. Side A also provided realistic, bounded answers to the pressure questions regarding cost premiums and economies of scale (pointing to large regional hubs like the US, EU, and India), whereas Side B's pivot to "internationally coordinated shared hubs" felt idealistic and less responsive to the prompt's focus on national industrial policy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they capitalized on Side B's admission that no alternative suppliers currently exist, proving that Side B's "just diversify" alternative is physically impossible without the very industrial policy the proposition advocates.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by showing that domestic factories remain dependent on imported minerals and equipment, take years to build, and raise near-term deployment costs, while diversified capacity plus domestically held stockpiles better addresses immediate shocks. Side B never adequately answered this input-dependency problem and relied heavily on unsupported certainty about future shortages, export bans, and bounded price costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange turned Side B’s hoarding argument against localization: if allies withhold inputs during crises, a domestic production line can also stop, whereas finished components stockpiled at home are already under national control.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON wins by exposing a fatal gap in PRO's resilience claim: domestic assembly plants still rely on imported lithium, graphite, wafers and machine tools, so localisation does not eliminate supply risk—it merely adds cost and delay. CON showed that diversified contracts plus domestic stockpiles provide usable redundancy faster and cheaper, meeting PRO's own test of controlling supply before a crisis. PRO never squarely answered why a factory that must import inputs is more reliable than a physical stockpile under domestic control, and repeatedly slid between the modest goal of ending 80-percent dominance and the costly proposition of national localisation. CON's sharper weighing—usable resilience now versus expensive vulnerability later—carries the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's turn demonstrating that localisation fails PRO's own security test because subsidised factories remain dependent on foreign critical inputs, whereas stockpiles are physically under domestic control and deployable in weeks. This exchange recurs in A2.6, A4.4 and A5.2 and is never squarely answered by PRO.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively dismantling Side A's core argument about resilience. Side A argued that domestic production is necessary because allies will hoard goods during a crisis. Side B effectively countered that a domestic factory still relies on imported raw materials (lithium, graphite, etc.), which will also be hoarded, rendering the domestic factory useless in a crunch. Side B's alternative—stockpiling finished components and diversifying contracts—provides immediate, physically controlled resilience without the massive delays and costs of building domestic factories. Side B's distinction between a "payment guarantee" and a "postal code" was rhetorically sharp and logically fatal to Side A's case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it answered the pressure question by explaining that a domestic factory is not a stockpile and remains vulnerable to raw material hoarding, whereas physical stockpiles of finished components provide immediate resilience.
