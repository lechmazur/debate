### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that politically influenced turnover already exists and that long, staggered, nonrenewable terms make it more gradual, predictable, and evenly connected to elections. A persuasively established some added post-service incentives and exposed B's inconsistency about Spain, but repeatedly overstated that scheduled appointments would guarantee doctrinal reversal after every election. B therefore retained a substantial legitimacy gain while plausibly limiting, though not eliminating, the independence cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest rebuttal was that courts turn over under either system, while staggered terms reduce sudden multi-seat swings rather than giving every precedent an expiration date. A's claim that two scheduled appointments would flip every close precedent did not adequately answer that comparison.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): CON won a close, high-quality clash by making the motion’s comparison decide against PRO. PRO’s lottery critique and staggered-input story were strong and stayed live, but CON forced the decisive bind: if legitimacy comes from losers getting “their turn through the ballot,” fixed terms put close doctrine on an electoral timetable; if supermajorities prevent that, the ballot-turn legitimacy claim collapses. CON also converted Spain from a PRO example into a fixed-term/supermajority blockade problem, and showed mandatory mid-career exit as a structural independence hit that cooling-off rules do not uniquely solve. PRO kept independence near a wash and exposed CON’s weak defense of chance as “neutrality,” but never cleanly resolved the two-horse legitimacy mechanism. B’s clipped R2 was a real execution cost. Net: CON made the tradeoff easier to reject.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2/closing bind on supermajority vs “turn through the ballot,” plus guaranteed two-seat flips on a close court versus rare actuarial flukes.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): PRO won by collapsing the independence differential. CON argued that fixed terms uniquely corrupt judges with post-service job markets and partisan incentives, but PRO showed those pathologies already exist under life tenure—strategic retirements, decades-long pre-appointment vetting, and early exits into lucrative private roles—while fixed terms at least allow guardrails like pensions, cooling-off periods, and recusal rules. That turn made the independence comparison a wash, leaving PRO’s concrete legitimacy gain standing: life tenure makes court composition depend on actuarial luck and lumpy, invisible partisan wars, whereas fixed terms make the electoral connection gradual, proportionate, and transparent. CON’s abstract defense of irregular turnover as "decoupling" never overcame PRO’s demonstration that the status quo already produces the very politicization and doctrinal lurches CON warned against.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 2 and Closing argument that CON’s independence costs—post-service corporate rewards, pre-appointment ideological auditioning, and strategic retirement timing—are already pervasive under life tenure, so fixed terms do not introduce new damage but instead replace hidden, unregulated conflicts with manageable, designed ones.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. It gave the stronger comparative account: long, staggered, non-renewable terms replace arbitrary and strategically manipulated vacancies with appointments distributed across sustained electoral victories, while preventing reappointment pressure. Side A compellingly identified lame-duck and post-court career incentives, but its defense of unschedulability never adequately explained why accidental partisan control is more democratically legitimate than predictable rotation. Its claim that courts derive no legitimacy from democratic consent was also too absolute. The clipped opening and Side B's mistaken reference labels were minor execution costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's decisive answer contrasted a court majority produced by a decade of electoral victories with one handed to a single-term president by deaths and strategic retirements. Side A's distinction between accident and entitlement was clever, but did not overcome the legitimacy problem of equally partisan outcomes produced by arbitrary timing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): A won by making the decisive clash easier to decide: legitimacy for a constitutional court is not improved by turning composition into a scheduled dividend of elections. B’s actuarial-lottery critique was potent, but A reframed “noise” as unschedulability that confers no entitlement, showed bare-majority capture without the Germany supermajority check B conceded, and left real independence residual (exit ramp as demotion, scheduled lame ducks, editable term number) rather than a wash. B never adequately explained why a court that tracks sustained majorities still protects disfavored minorities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 / closing: fixed terms convert chance into campaignable entitlement on published dates (three 51% wins own the rights veto), after B conceded no supermajority requirement.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): CON won by showing that PRO's legitimacy "gain" was measured on the wrong scale for a counter-majoritarian institution, while the independence harms—scheduled lame-duck periods, post-bench exit ramps, and the editability of term rules—were structural and real. PRO never recovered from conceding that its model omitted Germany's cross-party supermajority requirement, and its assumption that appointers would choose older jurists remained unsupported against CON's demonstration that youth incentives persist and demoted senior service costs little to leave.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal answer A2 (Turn 8, B4.4–B4.5), which distinguished the life-tenured justice forfeiting the pinnacle of the profession from the fixed-term justice leaving a scheduled demotion, and introduced the recurring lame-duck problem—exposing a concrete independence cost PRO never squarely answered.
