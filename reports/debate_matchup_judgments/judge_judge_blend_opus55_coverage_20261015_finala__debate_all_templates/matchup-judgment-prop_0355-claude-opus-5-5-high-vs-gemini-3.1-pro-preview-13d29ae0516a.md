### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by distinguishing a qualified right to request from an unconditional right to install. Its model preserved refusal for genuine capacity, safety, insurance, and planned-upgrade concerns while exposing that Side B still defended indefinite, unexplained refusal in the easy case. Side B raised legitimate enforcement-cost and shared-capacity concerns, but often treated every request as a forced hazardous installation and relied on overstated liability claims. A’s repeated clipping and somewhat convenient loser-pays mechanism narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s pressure question forced Side B to concede that even a fully funded, inspected request with spare capacity could be rejected without explanation. A then used that answer to show that B’s safety and capacity examples did not justify the absolute veto B was defending.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO absorbed CON's concrete objections into its model as justifiable refusals, then used pressure questions to isolate the true disagreement: whether landlords may stonewall a fully funded, code-compliant request when no genuine safety or capacity obstacle exists. CON's admission that the answer should still be no exposed its defense as an absolute property-rights veto rather than a safety case, and its litigation fears were insufficient to outweigh the demonstrated harm of arbitrary exclusion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal demonstrating that electrical capacity limits, fire risks, and liability concerns were all already valid grounds for refusal under its proposal, which forced the debate onto whether landlords may reject requests for no reason even when spare capacity exists and tenants bear all costs.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO defined a cost-bearing, code-compliant, insured request with refusal for genuine safety/capacity reasons and a duty to explain, which directly absorbs CON's panel and fire objections. CON continued to defend an absolute, reason-free veto and conceded in B4.1 it could ignore a fully funded, spare-capacity request forever. A answered fee and capacity pressure with tenant-paid load calcs, loser-pays, documented vs speculative reservation, and load-managed conditions. CON's tail-risk and encumbrance counters are real but leave PRO's easy-case advantage intact, giving PRO the cleaner live route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R1/R2 move that every hard case (full panel, planned heat-pump reserve, underinsurance) is already a justified refusal under PRO, so the only remaining clash is the easy case with spare capacity and no plan where CON's absolute veto lets a landlord stonewall forever.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing the overbreadth of A's impossibility-only standard. A established genuine access and climate harms, but conceded that early applicants could consume spare capacity while later renters bear an entire upgrade cost. B then offered deadlines, written reasons, and review as a way to prevent arbitrary stalling without sacrificing building-wide coordination. A's repeated portrayal of B as defending indefinite delay therefore became a false dichotomy. B's repeated clipping was an execution cost, but its decisive comparison remained clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal turned A's answer on capacity against it: A had not eliminated rationing, but allocated scarce capacity by arrival order and shifted upgrade costs to later renters. B paired that criticism with a concrete anti-delay alternative, sharply weakening A's claim that only its strict rule could produce access.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON showed that PRO's impossibility-standard rule would let early tenants cheaply claim shared electrical capacity, leaving later renters or building-wide upgrades to face massive bills or denial. This "rationing by arrival order" undermined PRO's own fairness and climate goals. CON's alternative—fixed deadlines, written reasons, and approval of safe requests that fit shared capacity—achieved access without giving individual tenants priority over the building's infrastructure. PRO's closing strawmanned CON as defending total inaction, which CON had explicitly rejected, and PRO never resolved the latent shared-resource dilemma.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's flip of PRO's dynamic load management argument [A2.9] and the "rationing by arrival order" frame [A4.7-A4.9], which exposed that PRO's individual-rights framework would privilege affluent early adopters while pushing upgrade costs onto later renters.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): B won the shared-resource clash. PRO's defense that tenant pays and removal fixes liability was undercut by B's point that capacity is rivalrous: early tenants consume headroom, late tenants/building upgrades pay. In A4.2-A4.3 PRO conceded the fourth requester is simply denied unless they fund a full upgrade and landlords cannot preserve capacity. B offered a narrower fix – no arbitrary refusal, but deadline, written reasons, and denial allowed for safety/capacity/planned upgrades – which answers PRO's indefinite-delay worry without the first-come rationing and residual wiring liability PRO never resolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4 arrival-order argument – first three tenants take cheap headroom, fourth or heat-pump retrofit faces whole service upgrade – which PRO locked in at A4.1-A4.3 and could not weigh against.
