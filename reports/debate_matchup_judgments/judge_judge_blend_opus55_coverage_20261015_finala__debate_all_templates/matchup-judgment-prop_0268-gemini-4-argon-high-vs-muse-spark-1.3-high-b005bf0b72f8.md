### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): A built a cleaner causal story (static unit prices don't help habitual rebuyers who never recheck per-gram math) and backed it with a feasibility claim (retailers already link old/new barcodes for reviews and reorder history). The decisive exchange came when A caught a real contradiction in B's opening (B claimed consumers prefer smaller $4 packs, which undercuts B's own warning that disclosure would force price hikes) and later weaponized B's own concession about barcode-linking into an "asymmetry" argument: if the database link is reliable enough to preserve sales continuity, it's reliable enough to trigger a factual notice. A also answered both of B's pressure questions concretely (a time-limited flag tied to the regular base price, mirroring existing "Save $1" tag logic), while B's rebuttal to that resolution mostly reasserted "liability trap" without explaining why the already-functioning base/promo distinction couldn't carry over. B's core objection — that a size notice is a "public accusation of stealth pricing" rather than a neutral fact — was directly and effectively punctured by A's closing reframe ("mathematical fact, not courtroom verdict"), and while B got the last word and repeated its regulatory-risk framing, it never substantively rebutted that specific reframe beyond repetition. B's consumer-preference and administrability arguments stayed coherent throughout but were increasingly on the back foot after the contradiction and asymmetry points landed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 catch that B's "shoppers prefer smaller $4 packs" claim undercuts B's own warning that transparency would force price hikes, combined with A's later leveraging of B's barcode-linking concession into an asymmetry argument that B never fully neutralized.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by establishing that shrinkflation exploits habitual shoppers who rely on visual and price anchors, and by showing that retailers already possess the technical infrastructure to flag size drops through existing database links used for reviews and reorder history. Side B's counterarguments about liability and unit pricing were plausible but never fully overcame the repeat-buyer information gap or the double standard of accepting commercial continuity while rejecting transparent disclosure. Side A's pressure questions and closing crystallized this asymmetry cleanly, leaving a clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 turn that "if consumers truly preferred smaller packages at the same price, brands would advertise the reduction proudly, not hide it" ([A2.1]), which undermined Side B's budget-family defense and exposed that transparency only damages strategies built on deception.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B built a much stronger case by effectively trapping Side A on the technical feasibility of the mandate. B's argument that retailers already link new, smaller barcodes to old product pages to preserve reviews and "reorder" history decisively countered A's claim that identifying the "same" product is an unworkable liability trap. B also effectively neutralized A's argument that budget shoppers prefer shrinkflation by pointing out that brands hide the size reduction rather than advertising it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where B exposed the double standard in Side A's argument: retailers rely on the exact same database link to carry over reviews and reorder history for commercial benefit, but claim it is too "sloppy" to trigger a regulatory shrinkflation warning.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but B built a single sustained mechanism — the Brand A (quiet price hike, unflagged) vs. Brand B (quiet shrink, flagged) comparison — and rode it through every round, turning it into a genuine "selective disclosure distorts competition" case. Crucially, B exploited A's own concession in Rebuttal 2 ("even if some just avoid the flag, that is still an informed reaction, not distortion") to argue that A had admitted the flag causes shoppers to irrationally avoid a product that remains the better deal, which B repeatedly and effectively labeled a debate-ending concession. A's defense — that price hikes are self-evidently visible today while shrinkage requires hidden memory — is a reasonable distinction, but A never fully closed the gap B kept reopening about why only one form of quiet margin-taking gets singled out, and A's answer to the behavioral "stop sign" critique was thinner than its answer to the compliance-cost critique. B's compliance-cost and "manufacturers will shift to skimpflation" arguments were less decisively won (A's barcode-tracking and ingredient-disclosure replies were plausible), but the core distortion/concession exchange was the most consequential clash of the debate and B controlled it more effectively through Pressure Questions and both rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 response turning A's "even if some just avoid the flag, that is still an informed reaction, not distortion" into a labeled concession, then using it in Pressure Questions and the closing to argue the mandate steers budget shoppers toward costlier unflagged rivals — a point A never fully neutralized.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): A successfully defended the proposition by establishing that shrinkflation is uniquely deceptive: a current headline price is immediately visible, whereas a package-size cut hides in identical packaging that demands historical memory no rushed shopper has. B's Brand A/Brand B asymmetry argument overstated the parity between price hikes and size cuts, and A blunted it by distinguishing present salience from concealed change. A also cleanly absorbed B's pressure and turned the skimpflation worry into an argument for more transparency, not less. Across the exchange A sharpened the clash around stealth versus sunlight, giving the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's opening rebuttal (A2.1–A2.2) dismantling the Brand A/Brand B comparison by showing that a headline price hike is already salient today while a gram shave is designed not to be, which neutralized CON's core comparative objection and preserved PRO's central claim about asymmetry.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively exposing the logical flaws in Side B's core asymmetry argument. Side B claimed that direct price hikes are obvious while shrinkflation is hidden, but Side A effectively demonstrated that neither is obvious to a shopper who doesn't memorize historical baselines, as shelf tags only show the current price. Furthermore, Side A brilliantly exploited Side B's concession that shoppers might just "avoid the flag" without checking the unit-price math. Side A proved that the mandate would actively harm budget shoppers by steering them toward costlier, unflagged rivals, turning Side B's own "stop sign" analogy against them. Side A's consistent focus on the distortion of current value over historical changes made for a cleaner, more compelling path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's claim that a direct price hike "screams from the shelf tag," followed by the ruthless exploitation of Side B's concession that reflexively avoiding a shrinkflation flag without checking the math is an "informed reaction." Side A correctly identified this as state-engineered distortion that harms the very budget shoppers the proposition aims to protect.
