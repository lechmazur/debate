### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but B built the more internally consistent case and landed the most damaging unanswered rebuttal: that A's defense against incumbent entrenchment relied on a small-donor matching scheme that is not actually part of the motion (a ban plus lower caps). B pressed this repeatedly ("a reform that needs rescuing from its own predictable winners has not equalised citizenship"), and A never offered a fix contained within the literal proposition, instead continuing to reach for "layered reform" outside its scope. B also exposed tension in A's own framework when A tried to carve out voluntary nonprofits/family businesses from the corporate ban in Rebuttal 2, which sat awkwardly with A's earlier blanket claim that corporate speech is never any individual's speech. B's framing that banning pooled, disclosable donations while leaving celebrities, media owners, incumbents, and self-funders untouched actually concentrates power in the least accountable actors was never squarely neutralized, and B's closing line contrasting "equal votes" with "equalised volume" crystallized that clash memorably. A's opening was also clipped for length, a real execution cost. A had a strong consent-based critique of corporate treasury money and good use of the broadcast-regulation analogy, but by the end B had the cleaner, more complete path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's repeated point (Rebuttal 1, Pressure Q2, Rebuttal 2) that A's small-donor matching-funds answer to the incumbent-entrenchment problem is not actually contained in the motion, leaving A's case without an in-scope remedy for the challenger-disadvantage objection.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A won by showing that the proposed ban selectively disables disclosed, pooled support while leaving incumbency, celebrity, media ownership, self-funding, and outside spending intact. Side B made a persuasive equality and non-consent case, but never adequately established that influence equals corruption or answered the displacement problem. Its defenses also depended on narrowing the blanket corporate ban and adding small-donor matching funds outside the motion. The clipped opening was a further execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A decisively answered Side B’s equality framing by arguing that caps magnify the relative advantages of celebrities, media owners, self-funders, and incumbents. Side B’s response relied on matching funds and an exemption-like distinction for voluntary nonprofits, allowing Side A to show that the motion itself lacked those safeguards and was being rewritten under pressure.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully exposing the practical flaws in Side A's proposal, demonstrating that banning pooled resources disproportionately harms challengers while empowering incumbents, celebrities, and self-funders whose advantages cannot be capped. Side A attempted to rescue its case by introducing small-donor matching funds, which Side B correctly identified as outside the motion. Side B's argument that pooled donations serve as the primary counterweight to un-cappable advantages like fame and personal wealth was decisive and effectively neutralized Side A's democratic equality claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's out-of-motion small-donor matching fix, and its sharp inversion of Side A's celebrity and media owner concern by arguing that pooled donations are the exact counterweight needed against those un-cappable advantages.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-defended cases, but the decisive clash—whether bans/caps actually reduce capture or merely drive it into unregulated channels (dark money, bundlers, outside spending)—was pressed more effectively by CON. CON grounded this in the familiar, plausible historical pattern of campaign-finance reform producing circumvention, and repeatedly turned PRO's own concessions (that outside spending persists, that migration occurs) into a self-defeat argument. PRO's best answer—that costlier, less-coordinated influence is still meaningful progress—is a reasonable conceptual rebuttal but never matched CON's concrete mechanism with an equally concrete counter-mechanism, and PRO's closing left the "state power wielded asymmetrically against outsiders" point largely unanswered across rounds. PRO did out-perform CON in the pressure-question exchange, answering both of CON's questions directly while CON's A1 answer to PRO's "what counts as capture" question was more of a deflection than a real threshold. That execution edge, plus PRO's clean union/membership distinction, keeps this close, but CON's sharper, more consistently reinforced central argument and more memorable closing framing ("permission slip for the dark, with a press release attached") gave it the slightly stronger path to victory overall.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's repeated use of PRO's own concessions in [A2.5]/[A4.1] ("less efficient" outside spending still buys influence) to argue the ban fails on its own terms, culminating in the closing line that bans "clean the ledger while capture goes invisible," was the most damaging and clarifying exchange of the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that direct, candidate-controlled donations create a distinctive dependence that disclosure merely reveals after the fact. Side A’s migration argument was the strongest objection, but it repeatedly treated some displaced influence as proof that influence remains equally potent. B plausibly explained why uncoordinated outside spending is costlier, weaker, and less likely to create personal donor leverage. A also relied on broad, insufficiently grounded claims that past reforms uniformly produced dark money, and its opening was clipped. B’s erroneous reference labels were an execution flaw, but the intended arguments remained clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1 most decisively answered A’s leakage case: conceding migration did not concede policy failure, because removing candidate control and direct patron identification reduces the efficiency and dependence-producing force of each dollar. A never established that redirected spending preserves equal or greater influence.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A won by successfully distinguishing between the corrosive dependence created by direct, candidate-controlled donations and the less direct influence of outside spending. When Side B argued that bans merely push money into dark, uncoordinated channels, Side A effectively countered that this migration actually weakens the direct candidate-donor gratitude loop, and that we should not legalize the most corrupting channel simply because a lesser-regulated one exists. Side A's crystallization in the closing ("Disclosure names the owner of the leash. It does not cut it.") provided a decisive and memorable weighing mechanism that Side B could not overcome.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal to the "migration" argument, using the analogy that we do not legalize direct bribery just because favors might migrate to unrecorded dinners, effectively neutralized Side B's strongest point and shifted the burden back to the nature of the influence itself.
