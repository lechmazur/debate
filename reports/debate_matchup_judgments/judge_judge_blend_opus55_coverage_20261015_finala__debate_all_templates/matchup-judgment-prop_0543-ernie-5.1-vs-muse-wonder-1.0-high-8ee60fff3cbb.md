- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): CON wins by exposing a structural dilemma in PRO's case: privacy-by-design defaults must either be sticky enough to measurably change behavior—in which case they burden lawful speech and require invasive age verification—or loose enough that teens and predators easily opt out, undermining harm reduction. CON sharpened this trade-off across rounds, showing the mandate would likely rely on behavioral surveillance and content classification that excludes vulnerable minors. PRO never fully resolved the tension or demonstrated a feasible design that satisfies both promises in the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's too-weak/too-strong trap (B2.1-B2.2) and the turn that PRO's workaround relies on behavioral surveillance and content classification, creating the very privacy invasion and soft censorship it claims to avoid (B4.1-B4.4).

Side B effectively argued that if privacy defaults are easy to opt out of, they fail to measurably reduce harm; if they are sticky (e.g., requiring parental consent or age checks), they act as censorship and disproportionately harm the vulnerable, parentless teens Side A sought to protect. Side A never successfully resolved this dilemma, and their reliance on "behavioral patterns" for age verification inadvertently conceded Side B's surveillance concerns. Side B's closing crystallized this structural trap perfectly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1, which introduced the devastating "toggle dilemma" (defaults must be either too weak to measure or strong enough to censor), fundamentally shifting the debate and trapping Side A's core mechanism.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides executed at an exceptionally high level. Side B won by successfully defending the "measurable harm reduction" prong textually and providing a decisive comparative weighing on the censorship prong: framing the choice as irreversible exposure versus delayed reach. Side B absorbed Side A's strong "stickiness" critique by arguing that the cost of inertia should fall on reach rather than safety.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing reframed Side A's "stickiness" attack by contrasting irreversible exposure with opt-in reach, effectively neutralizing Side A's censorship claim.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A built the stronger path to victory by showing that the proposition only requires a measurable reduction in harm—not the elimination of dominant harms—and by identifying concrete, countable reductions in stranger contact, location exposure, data profiling, and viral spread that even B conceded. A also neutralized B's censorship argument by demonstrating that privacy defaults involve no content removal, no viewpoint discrimination, and no speech prohibition, only a reversible delay in public reach. A's closing crystallized the tradeoff decisively: exposure cannot be undone, but reach can always be expanded. B's attempt to equate privacy architecture with significant censorship was rhetorically inventive but remained intuitively strained and never overcame A's distinction between audience-setting and state suppression of ideas.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal of the censorship charge (Turns 3 and 7), where A reframed privacy defaults as a "delay, not deletion" of speech, exposed that B's definition would absurdly classify every private message or follower approval as censorship, and leveraged the irreversibility of exposure against the reversibility of reach.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by successfully defending a plain-text reading of "measurably reduce harm" (showing that preventing stranger contact, data harvesting, and viral spread counts as measurable reduction, even if it doesn't cure all peer abuse) and by dismantling Side B's overly broad definition of censorship. Side A's closing on the "cost of inertia" was highly effective and memorable. Side B struggled to prove that limiting public discoverability and algorithmic amplification equates to actual censorship, relying on a stretched definition that Side A easily neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal to the censorship claim, pointing out that if reduced automatic amplification and public indexing equals censorship, then every private group chat and follower approval would also be censorship, effectively collapsing Side B's "architectural censorship" argument.
