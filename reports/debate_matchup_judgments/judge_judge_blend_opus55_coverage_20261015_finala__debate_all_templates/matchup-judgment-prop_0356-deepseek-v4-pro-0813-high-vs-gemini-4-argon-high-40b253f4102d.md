- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by exposing a central tension in Side B's (PRO) position: PRO's standard of "genuinely fair terms" requiring no penalty for refusal effectively bans actuarially sound behavior-based pricing because already-safe drivers cannot receive discounts without the unmonitored pool paying more—a mathematical necessity PRO dismissed as a "selection effect." It exposed PRO's contradiction between allowing telematics for the "already safe" young driver while forbidding discounts that remove her subsidy from the unmonitored pool, and it crystallized the reframing that PRO's rule forces cautious drivers to bankroll reckless ones rather than protecting them.

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by showing that existing law still permits the specific coercive practices the motion targets—buried data-brokering consent in adhesion contracts, trade-secret scoring weights, and disclosed punitive non-participation surcharges—while ex post enforcement cannot restore privacy or enable ordinary drivers to challenge black-box algorithms. Side A (CON) never overcame the structural coercion created by state-mandated insurance combined with a degrading untracked pool, and its claim that current regulation is sufficient collapsed under PRO’s concrete examples of legal gaps. CON’s vagueness objection was partially answered by PRO’s rate-filing mechanisms, leaving PRO with the cleaner comparative path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 2 answer A1 ([B4.1]), identifying that current law still permits fine-print data-brokering consent, trade-secret scoring weights, and disclosed punitive non-participation surcharges, decisively undermined CON’s core defense that existing regulation already prevents the harms the motion addresses.

B answered that and the vagueness charge with a concrete operationalization (bar treating non-participation as adverse factor, cap spread for clean records) and showed irreversibility of data sales and industry-wide pooling makes ex post insufficient. CON's market-correction story never explained why a spotless refuser should subsidize reckless refusers in a mandated market.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1-B2.5 and pressure Q1 – exposing A1.1 vs A1.2 contradiction on competition rescuing refusers and A1.5 vs A1.6 contradiction on vagueness, reframing baseline inflation as industry-wide privacy tax rather than selection.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's reliance on ex post enforcement by pointing out that privacy breaches (like selling location data to brokers) cannot be undone after the fact, rendering Side A's "existing law" argument functionally insufficient for the specific harms of continuous surveillance.
