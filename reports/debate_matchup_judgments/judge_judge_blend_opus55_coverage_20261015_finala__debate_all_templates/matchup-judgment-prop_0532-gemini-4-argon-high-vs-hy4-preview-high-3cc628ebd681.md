### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): The debate turned on whether pricing can actually relieve curb overflow or merely reallocates an unchanged shortage. CON's recurring "a price is an allocation mechanism, not a construction crew" line was sharp and forced PRO to keep answering the core objection. PRO's strongest answer—that dynamic fees shift marginal, time-flexible demand (non-urgent parcels, cruising ride-hail) off-peak while leaving urgent trips largely unaffected because the fee is small relative to trip value—is the standard real-world logic behind congestion/curb pricing, and CON never fully engaged with this elasticity mechanism beyond repeating that "fourteen vehicles still need to stop somewhere." CON did land one genuine hit: PRO's retreat to a simple "time-of-day rate" to dodge the compliance/API objection sits awkwardly with PRO's earlier claim that London's pricing failed specifically because it was static and not demand-responsive. PRO's closing gestures at distinguishing "rates that adjust by demand" from a flat cordon charge but doesn't fully dissolve the tension. On balance, PRO's mechanism-based case (fleet-level aggregation, differential elasticity between urgent/flexible trips, rerouting ride-hail off arterials) answered more of CON's structural challenge than CON answered PRO's elasticity story, giving PRO a modest but real edge after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.1/A4.3 exposing the static-vs-dynamic contradiction in PRO's "just a time-of-day rate" was the sharpest single rebuttal, but PRO's B4.1-B4.2 answer distinguishing fleet-wide modest fees from per-trip negligible cost for urgent deliveries was the more decisive substantive response to CON's core "price can't create space" objection.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): B won by showing that the relevant problem is not merely physical capacity but peak demand allocation. A repeatedly argued that pricing cannot create space, but B persuasively explained that it can shift marginal deliveries, consolidate fleet activity, and move ride-hail stops away from transit arteries. A never showed how time limits alone prevent overflow when legal zones remain full. A’s static-versus-dynamic dilemma was rhetorically sharp but overstated: a curb-specific rate varying by time or demand is materially different from London’s flat cordon charge and need not require every small operator to integrate dispatch software.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was its answer to A’s claim that fees must be either ineffective or punitive: a modest charge can be negligible for an urgent, valuable delivery yet scale across thousands of flexible platform stops, changing fleet-wide routing and timing. That directly answered A’s elasticity challenge and made pricing’s comparative benefit clearer than enforcement alone.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO kept a live scarcity logic: at peak the curb is finite so zero price rations by waste and circling, while even modest fleet-scaled fees shift flexible parcels and ride-hail off-peak or to side streets. CON never solved overflow — more loading zones are still finite, and enforcement only punishes after legal spots fill with low-value trips. PRO also answered the tax dilemma with marginal vs essential value and defused compliance burden via existing meters.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R1/R2 distinction between fixed unload time and elastic fleet timing/consolidation, plus the A1 answer that a few dollars scales across thousands of parcel stops but is trivial for a high-value produce truck, which neutralized A's inelasticity and tax-on-essentials trap.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON's concrete logistical objections (sequential freight loading, plate-cameras reading vehicles not cargo manifests, ride-hail circling to dodge a ticking curb meter) survived the exchange largely intact. PRO's attempts to patch these objections—grace periods for pickups, pre-published price "bands," address-based discounts—functionally converted dynamic pricing into the same time-limit-plus-fine model CON proposed, which CON exploited directly ("that leaves dynamic pricing caught in a fatal contradiction... stripped away what makes it dynamic"). PRO's clever closing reframe (CON's own system is just a crude two-tier price) is a nice rhetorical turn but doesn't resolve the unanswered verification problem (how cameras distinguish small-business discounts from corporate freight on the same truck) or the inelastic-demand critique for ride-hail and freight. CON's equality-vs-ability-to-pay framing in the final speech gave a clean, intuitive reason to prefer rule-based rationing, which PRO never fully neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A2.1 catching the contradiction between B2.1 (enforcement can't cover every block) and B2.4 (cameras can enforce curb sessions) was the pivotal exchange, forcing PRO into increasingly rule-like, time-limit-style patches that CON then used in closing to argue PRO's "dynamic" pricing added no real marginal benefit over CON's camera-enforced time limits.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate. It showed that the camera enforcement Side B needed for pricing could instead enforce uniform loading limits, while rigid delivery schedules make some charges unavoidable pass-through costs. A also persuasively explained how charging for waiting can encourage ride-hail circling. B established real scarcity and some elastic behavior, but never proved dynamic prices outperform A's enforced limits and expanded loading zones.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange exposed the enforcement asymmetry: B dismissed ordinary limits as unenforceable but relied on cameras to meter priced sessions. A then showed that B's grace period resembles a time limit and does not solve legal circling while passengers are delayed.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won the enforcement symmetry clash. If cameras can meter dynamic curb sessions, they can enforce simple time-limited loading zones, undermining B's necessity claim. A also pressed two live costs B never resolved: sequential pallet loading makes multi-stop freight inelastic to minute-by-minute prices, and ticking curb meters incentivize circling in the unpriced travel lane. B's retreat to grace periods and pre-published time bands was framed by A as conceding that the workable model is a time limit, not dynamic pricing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1-A2.2 and A4.1 exposing that B's camera-enforced pricing requires the same enforcement capacity as A's preferred time limits, and that B's grace-period-plus-penalty is a time limit not dynamic pricing.
