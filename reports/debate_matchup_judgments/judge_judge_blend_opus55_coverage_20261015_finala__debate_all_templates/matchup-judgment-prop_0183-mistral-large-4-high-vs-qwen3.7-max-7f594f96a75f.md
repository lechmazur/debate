### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side B won by exposing the mismatch between Side A's narrow preservation examples and the proposition's broad exemption. Side A never justified letting nonprofits distribute active, in-print works, and its proposed retention of actual damages did not answer B's point that enforcement costs would make those rights practically useless. B offered a clearer alternative of secure preservation, controlled access, and targeted safe harbors. A's clipped opening and closing were additional execution costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was its response to A's claim that removing statutory damages and takedowns merely recalibrates copyright. B explained that independent creators often cannot afford litigation or prove sufficient actual damages, so the remaining rights would be functionally unenforceable while nonprofits distributed their works globally.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): CON won by making the proposition's overbreadth the decisive clash: a blanket exemption is not targeted orphan/abandoned-work rescue but legally sanctions free global distribution of active commercial works, practically voiding independent creators' rights once statutory damages and takedowns vanish. PRO's digital-amnesia framing was vivid and repeated well, but never cleanly limited scope, relied on a false preservation=unrestricted-distribution identity, and paid execution costs from clipped opening/closing. CON's answers on dark archives vs public entombment and Trojan-horse use of orphans were sharper and easier to vote on.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.2 / A4.3-A4.4: orphan tragedy cannot justify unrestricted distribution of locatable in-print works; stripping statutory damages for mass-user nonprofits renders exclusive rights practically unenforceable for independents.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): CON successfully exposed the proposition's fatal overbreadth, framing the blanket exemption as a Trojan horse for mass distribution of active commercial works rather than a narrow preservation fix. CON's argument that removing statutory damages and takedown mechanisms renders copyright practically unenforceable for independent creators—because federal litigation costs dwarf the lost royalties of individual authors—was devastating and went largely unanswered. PRO's attempt to justify preserving works before they become legally "orphaned" created a moving target where virtually any work could be deemed abandoned, but PRO never provided a principled reason why the exemption must cover in-print books by living authors instead of being limited to genuinely lost or unlocatable works. While PRO effectively established the reality of digital decay, CON showed that controlled dark archives, on-site access, and targeted safe harbors already permit preservation without cannibalizing creator livelihoods. CON's path was clearer and narrower, protecting digital heritage without gutting the economic rights of the people who produce it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's argument in Rebuttal 2 that stripping statutory damages and takedown tools makes copyright enforcement economically impossible for independent creators—because the cost of federal litigation far exceeds the market harm of a freely distributed book—rendering their exclusive rights functionally void.
