### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): PRO built a sympathetic, well-illustrated case that tax-driven abandonment can permanently erase commissioned work, and CON's pressure-question answer conceded exactly that. But CON converted that concession into the decisive frame: a fixed availability window either (a) is redundant, since titles with audiences already migrate or get re-licensed without a mandate (Westworld), or (b) is punitive, forcing studios to keep paying residuals, rights renewals, and compliance costs on unwatched titles, and even then only delays rather than prevents the eventual write-off. PRO's strongest rebuttal (distinguishing true tax-abandonment erasure from routine rotation) never answered CON's sharpest follow-up: PRO's own three-to-five-year window still ends in the same write-off, and PRO's claim that "the financial motivation evaporates" after the window was asserted without support and was directly challenged as unproven. CON's Q1 in Pressure Questions ("have you prevented erasure or merely delayed it?") exposed a structural gap in PRO's remedy that was never closed, and CON's closing crystallized the unanswered redundant-or-punitive dichotomy cleanly. PRO's closing restated moral stakes but did not resolve this core efficacy problem, leaving CON with the clearer, more damaging path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Question Q1 ("have you prevented erasure or merely delayed it?") combined with PRO's A1 concession that the same write-off can occur after the window — this exchange exposed that PRO's remedy does not actually solve the harm it is built around, and CON exploited it through to the closing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B won a competitive debate by making the policy choice clearer after full exchange. A landed a real hit on permanent tax-abandonment erasure and forced B’s honest concession, plus a useful reframe that “publicly available” can include second-life licensing. B still controlled the decisive comparison: the mandate is redundant when titles have buyers (Westworld-style migration already happens) and punitive when they do not (studio must keep paying residuals/rights/compliance). B also showed A’s 3–5 year window mostly delays rather than prevents vaulting, so A’s core harm was incompletely solved while chilling effects and cost-shifting remained live. B’s answers and closings narrowed that trap better than A resolved it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 / closing trap: A’s own answers made the rule redundant with demand and costly without it, while only postponing write-offs.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by exposing a structural dilemma in PRO's proposal: the mandate is redundant when a title can find a second-life licensee, yet punitive and cost-prohibitive when no buyer exists, forcing continued residual, rights, and compliance payments on unviable works. While PRO successfully showed that tax-abandonment write-offs cause real erasure and harm creators, CON demonstrated that the proposed window merely delays rather than prevents that erasure, and the attached commercial-hosting penalty would chill the very risk-taking that generates original productions. CON's cleaner comparative weighing—preservation through non-commercial archiving rather than forced commercial exploitation—provides a more coherent route to victory after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal and closing crystallized the "toothless or punitive" trap and the "delay not prevent" objection, directly undercutting PRO's claim that a modest availability window durably solves the erasure harm.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A's central move—narrowing the motion to ban only total erasure while conceding that licensing, transfer, discounting, and write-downs all remain lawful—proved durable under pressure. This let A absorb B's strongest economic points (residuals, music clearances, accounting write-downs) without conceding the core claim, and it turned B's own "reallocation to ad-supported services" example into evidence that availability can persist. B's Pressure Questions exposed a real seam (does the rule ban takedown-for-licensing windows?), but A's answer, while not perfectly addressing transitional gaps, held the line that relocation satisfies the minimum, and B's closing never fully reconciled its "digital mausoleum" framing with A's concession-heavy rule. B's answers to A's pressure questions (fixed guild residual floors, mandatory-retirement tax rules) were clever patches but read as somewhat invented precision rather than secure background knowledge, slightly undercutting grounding. Overall A kept the clash narrower and cleaner, made the stakes (erasure vs. flexibility) easier to adjudicate, and closed more decisively on the actual text of the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 move at [A2.1], turning B's "titles get transferred to ad-supported platforms" opening example into proof that availability can be preserved, forced B to spend the rest of the debate defending a "flexibility" argument that mostly didn't contradict A's actual proposed rule.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A won by making the decisive clash clearer: sole-gatekeeper erasure is unique because no lawful copies escape, and the rule still permits licensing, transfer, write-downs, and a priced finite window. B’s strongest live routes—carrying costs and greenlight chill—were narrowed rather than abandoned, so A’s “only total disappearance is banned” weighing survived intact while B’s resolution-text trap and franchise-retreat prediction did not neutralize the public-finality claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s transfer/licensing answer (A2.1 then A4.1/A4.3): third-party exclusivity still counts as public availability, so B was left defending only total vaulting rather than ordinary commercial exits.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing a cleaner, narrower interpretation of the proposition—banning total erasure while preserving legitimate relocation, licensing, and accounting exits—and showing that Side A's predicted chilling effect depended on costs and constraints that B's framework did not actually impose. B's rebuttal turned A's own examples against A, maintained consistent framing across rounds, and crystallized the stakes around gatekeeper finality, while A's late tax-specificity and nonexistent reference IDs incurred execution costs without fully neutralizing B's live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first rebuttal (B2.1), where PRO showed that CON's own example of relicensing to ad-supported services actually satisfied the minimum-availability requirement, collapsing the distinction between relocation and erasure and forcing CON into narrower, less persuasive technical objections.
