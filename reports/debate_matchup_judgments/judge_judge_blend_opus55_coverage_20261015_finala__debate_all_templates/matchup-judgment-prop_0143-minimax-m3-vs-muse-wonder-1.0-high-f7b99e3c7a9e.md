### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly within format, but B built the more resilient structural case. A's strongest move was forcing B to concede that any workable regime needs someone with standing to enforce use-limits, and B handled this reasonably by distinguishing categorical prohibitions (enforced like speed limits) from a transferable consent license. The decisive exchange came when B pressed that under A's estate-consent model, a single signature from an estranged heir or executor could legally authorize exactly the stranger-facing commercial exploitation the debate agrees is harmful, while B's possession-plus-use-limits model bans that outcome outright regardless of who signs. A never cleanly closed this loophole in rebuttal 2 or closing, instead asserting "same authority, different rhetoric" without showing the two regimes produce the same outcomes. A also quietly narrowed the motion's scope (adding "and licensing outward" as a precondition) to shield private use, which is a reasonable but not textually obvious move that B's closing implicitly exploited by reframing the clash as "a signature versus a boundary." That reframe was sharper and more responsive to the live tension than A's closing recap, giving B the cleaner final comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal 2 (B4.3) showing that under A's consent model one estate signature can authorize commercial exploitation over a spouse's objection, paired with B's closing "signature vs. boundary" framing, which A's closing did not directly neutralize.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won by landing a decisive solvency turn: a prior-consent regime creates a transferable, licensable right that allows a distant estate or executor to commercialize a dead person’s intimate voice and sell it to strangers over a spouse’s objection, thereby enabling the very exploitation the motion claims to prevent. Side B sustained a coherent alternative—outright bans on secondary simulation, onward sale, and stranger access, plus design mandates like disclosure and easy deletion—and successfully resisted Side A’s attempt to collapse that framework into a hidden consent regime. Side A’s closing argument that use-limits still require a rights-holder was answered by Side B’s public-law analogy (speed limits do not need a car owner’s permission), and Side B’s pressure questions exposed that a consent signature does not prevent grief-trapping harm, adding only paperwork. Side A never fully explained why estate consent is superior to direct prohibition for stopping commercial abuse, leaving B’s comparative framework cleaner and more protective.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 turn that prior consent creates a tradable veto which an estate can exercise to license a dead parent’s voice to strangers, permitting exploitation when the “right” person signs, while simultaneously blocking a grieving spouse from privately commissioning a bot from voicemails left personally to her. This exposed the consent gate as both over- and under-inclusive, and Side A never fully recovered from the framing that the motion’s mechanism enables the very harm it seeks to prevent.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A won by effectively exposing the practical and legal flaws in Side B's estate-consent model. Side A's argument that a consent requirement is both over-inclusive (blocking private, intimate use by a spouse due to estate bureaucracy or estranged heirs) and under-inclusive (allowing commercial exploitation if an executor signs off) was highly persuasive. Side A's "signature vs. boundary" framing in the closing crystallized the clash perfectly. Furthermore, Side A's answers to the pressure questions successfully defended their "possession plus use limits" alternative, cleanly distinguishing between data custody and title without collapsing their model into a hidden consent regime.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled Side B's claim that use limits inherently require an estate rights-holder by distinguishing between custody and title, and using the speed limit analogy to show how behavioral boundaries can be enforced by regulators and recipients without inventing an "owner of memory."

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): A built a cleaner, more resilient case around two load-bearing moves: (1) distinguishing authorized remembrance (reading real letters, recordings, retelling) from unauthorized generation of new intimate speech, and (2) framing prior consent as the only mechanism that acts before an irreversible psychological event — the moment a bereaved person bonds with fabricated words. B's strongest hits (estate-as-proxy problem, toggle-as-corporate-capture, "consent via strangers with paperwork") landed real damage and were never fully erased, but A's responses (probate ordering with spousal priority, default-to-no, deceptive toggles failing the requirement) were adequate holding actions rather than full refutations — enough to keep A's structure standing. B's counterexamples meant to show the line is unworkable (funeral readings, recipe recordings) were cleanly defeated by A's existing-words-vs-invented-words distinction, which blunted B's "theater" charge. B's ex-post toolkit (fraud, defamation, right-of-publicity, cooling-off) is a coherent alternative in principle, but B never squarely answered A's sharpest pressure question — who sues, and for what, when the griever voluntarily bought and cherished fabricated words — leaving the irreversibility argument live and decisive in the closing. B's case also suffered two format-noted clippings (opening and Rebuttal 2), cutting off arguments mid-thought at exactly the moments meant to crystallize the rebuttal, which is a real execution cost under the judging frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 distinction between reading a deceased person's actual words (letters, recipes) versus generating unauthorized new intimate speech directly dismantled B's funeral-reading/recipe-recording counterexamples from Rebuttal 1, while B's unanswered pressure point about estate/toggle consent being reconstructed by strangers rather than the deceased remained the most damaging but ultimately non-fatal counter-punch.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A won by defending a clean distinction between passive remembrance and open-ended generative impersonation, and by showing that once a bot invents intimate speech in a loved one’s voice the harm is irreversible no matter what post-hoc fraud or consumer-protection suit follows. Side B’s after-the-fact toolkit never adequately answered A’s pressure on who would sue when the grieving buyer had already bonded with and cherished the fabricated words, and B’s attempt to blur AI chatbots with funeral readings or voice memos failed to collapse A’s workable line. Execution costs from two clipped speeches compounded B’s difficulty in closing out these gaps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Pressure Questions and Rebuttal 2 answers were decisive: Q2 exposed a standing gap in CON’s remedy framework—who sues when the buyer is the grieving survivor who treasures the bot—and A’s A1/A2 answers preserved the preferability of an estate gate defaulting to no simulation over possession-is-authority, while clarifying that affirmative independently-regulated consent, not a seller-drafted toggle, is what PRO requires.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B built a stronger, more cohesive case by clearly distinguishing between reproducing past words and generating new intimate speech, and by leveraging the "timing" argument in the closing to show why after-the-fact remedies are insufficient for irreversible emotional harms. Side A made strong points about the flaws of estate law and the bureaucratic burden on the undocumented, but suffered from clipped speeches and failed to decisively overcome Side B's distinction between prior authorization and post-hoc litigation. Side B's handling of the pressure questions was also cleaner and more direct.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it cleanly answered the pressure questions by explaining that probate is not a "filing race" and that consent toggles must meet independent validity standards (analogous to organ donation), effectively neutralizing Side A's attacks on the mechanics of the consent regime.
