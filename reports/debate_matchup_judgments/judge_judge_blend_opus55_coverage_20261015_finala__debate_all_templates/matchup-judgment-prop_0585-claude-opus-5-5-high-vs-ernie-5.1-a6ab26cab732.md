### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): B won by better explaining the word “primarily”: transmission and integrated markets address routine balancing, unlock renewable generation, and reduce the amount of storage required, while targeted storage can cover residual tail risk. A vividly showed that wires cannot create energy during continent-wide shortages, but never adequately established that this tail risk justifies making storage the primary investment despite its scale and cost. A also leaned on grid-battery deployment examples without firmly grounding their applicability to long-duration storage, and repeated clipping imposed an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer that A’s dark-week argument supports targeted storage rather than primary storage was decisive. A proved storage has a necessary role in extreme shortages, but B preserved the broader comparison by showing that transmission serves far more hours and acts as a platform for decarbonization.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by identifying and controlling the decisive margin of the debate: the rare but catastrophic multi-day, multi-region shortfalls where reliability and decarbonization are actually constrained. Side A argued persuasively that transmission is a platform enabling geographic diversity and lower-cost bulk power movement, but Side B turned this effectively by showing that the ordinary variability Side A claimed for transmission is already managed by existing infrastructure, gas, and short-duration batteries. The hours that actually break the grid are tail events like Uri or dunkelflaute, and during those events transmission routes little or no surplus because neighbors are short simultaneously. Side A was unable to escape the force of this example and their closing claim that transmission would retire gas peakers during a dark week was directly reversed by Side B's image of a line carrying nothing across a continent of stalled wind and dark skies. Side B also won the implementation timeline clash by showing that storage hardware can be deployed in years on existing sites, while transmission dollars under current rules largely buy delay. Side A's reliance on unspecified permitting reform was speculative compared to Side B's concrete causal story. Across reliability and decarbonization, Side B made the cleaner case that the binding constraint is multi-day firming and that primary investment should go to the technology that solves it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal turning Side A's "95 percent of hours" framework against it: arguing that those ordinary hours are already served by existing lines, gas, and short batteries, so spending primarily on transmission buys redundant comfort, while the residual tail hours that actually black out the grid are where storage is indispensable and transmission is impotent (B4.6-B4.7).
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won the decisive clash on reliability in the tail. By pressing the Uri/Elliott/dunkelflaute scenario, B showed transmission "routes whatever surplus exists" but routes nothing when neighbors are short, forcing A to concede "targeted storage for tail risk" and to retreat to "some surplus" and a 30-year payoff. That concession hands both halves of the motion to storage: the hours that actually black out the grid and keep gas on the books. A kept a live platform-multiplier argument for decarbonization, but B's permitting-timeline pressure and reuse-of-interconnection response were answered more directly than A's, leaving B's insurance framing clearer and more damaging despite clipped turns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1-B2.3 turning A's "targeted storage for tail risk" [A2.4] / "routes whatever surplus exists" into a concession that reliability is decided in the tail where transmission cannot create surplus, making A assign the reliability half of the motion to storage.
