### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but B's attack on the estate-as-gatekeeper mechanism proved more durable through the exchange. B repeatedly showed that "or their estate" hands authority precisely in the majority case (sudden, silent death) to whatever legal officer happens to hold the title — who may be the estranged relative, separated spouse, or abuser A itself worried about — while the loving partner without legal standing is frozen out during probate. A's answer to the pressure question on this point (divorce revokes executor status, "could have left a directive") only patched a narrow slice of the problem and was directly punctured by B's reply that this ignores unmarried partners and separated-but-undivorced spouses. B also successfully turned A's own obsessive-ex example back on it, showing the estate gate does nothing against an ex who is the executor, and offered a competing, fairly workable distinguishing principle (explicit refusal binds; silence does not default to a probate lock) that survived to the close. A's case remained solid but never fully closed the "wrong gatekeeper" gap that B kept pressing, which is why B's path to victory stayed cleaner at the end.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B's pressure question forced a decisive concession: A had claimed courts could remove a "faithless executor" as a safeguard against hostile gatekeepers, but under direct pressure admitted no such remedy exists for a lawful veto. This exposed that A's "estate consent as fallback" is not really honoring the dead's silence but simply handing one heir an unreviewable monopoly over others' grief — exactly B's core charge. B capitalized on this cleanly in Rebuttal 2 and the closing, anchoring the abstract dispute in the concrete, sympathetic hypothetical of a mother blocked by a hostile spouse-executor. A's reversibility argument in closing ("a cloned cadence cannot be recalled") was a genuine and valuable late save, but it arrived only after the dignity-of-the-dead framing had already been undercut, and it shifted the case toward risk-management rather than fully repairing the consent narrative. B's case was more concrete, sustained the autonomy/cruelty framing consistently, and answered A's strongest distinction (replay vs. generated speech) with a reasonable counter-analogy (playwrights, biographers) that was never fully neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question Q1 ("on what basis could a court ever remove an executor for exercising the exact veto power your rule grants them?") and A's forced A1 concession ("None... that veto is not a breach") — this exchange exposed that the estate-consent fallback is an unreviewable monopoly rather than a genuine proxy for the dead's wishes, and B exploited it decisively in the following rounds.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): B narrowly won by maintaining the crucial distinction between preserving real messages and manufacturing new speech that presents itself as the deceased. A powerfully exposed the harshness and arbitrariness of an estate veto, including B's retreat on court removal, but its claim that private simulation harms nobody largely assumed away the deceased's dignity and control interests. B gave the cleaner comparison: denial leaves mourners their authentic artifacts, while unauthorized cloning eliminates any enforceable refusal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response to the grieving-mother example was decisive: the mother retains the son's actual voicemails, while consent gates only fabricated new replies in his cadence. That distinction materially weakened A's emotional framing, even though A successfully showed that estate authorization is an imperfect proxy.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing that estate consent fails to protect the deceased's autonomy—since an executor can still authorize simulation without the deceased's prior directive—while creating an unreviewable veto that inflicts cruelty on non-heir mourners. Side A's "permission by silence" frame was powerful, but B showed that the proposed gate merely monopolizes grief rather than honoring the dead, and that private, non-commercial use of lawfully held memories harms no third party. B's weighing of immediate trauma to the living against the abstract protection of silence was the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal revealing that an executor's signature does not turn silence into agreement, coupled with the observation that the executor themselves could still authorize the simulation, transformed Side A's estate-consent fallback from a shield for the dead into a state-enforced monopoly over private mourning—a concession Side A never fully recovered from.
