### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B won narrowly by making the immediate liquidity burden and overbreadth of a heavy production tax more concrete than A's long-run mitigation. A compellingly established real externalities and showed that B's alternatives could also raise prices, but did not fully show how the tax avoids necessities produced within fast-fashion supply chains or how subsidized local mills become affordable quickly. B's narrower producer-side alternatives and industrial-policy critique preserved a credible reason to reject this specific mechanism. A's repeated clipped turns were also a modest execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's segmented-demand answer was decisive: optional trend churn can be elastic while low-income demand for cheap basics is inelastic, defeating A's claim that B needed one contradictory demand curve. A's response that basics are not trend churn was strong but did not establish that a production tax can cleanly separate them within the same supply chains.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A won by successfully maneuvering Side B into neutralizing its own primary offense. When Side B offered alternative mechanisms to make producers pay in Rebuttal 2, Side A's closing cleanly pointed out that those mechanisms would also raise consumer prices, making the regressivity argument a wash and leaving Side A's revenue benefits as the deciding factor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing speech perfectly capitalized on Side B's alternative policies (EPR, import standards) by noting that those costs would still reach the same low-income shopper at the register, effectively neutralizing Side B's regressivity impacts.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won a competitive debate by making the proposition easier to reject on its own design. PRO’s externality and “honest prices” framing was strong and vivid, and the closing flip—that CON’s EPR/standards alternatives also raise prices while funding nothing—was a real punch. But CON’s cash-constraint rebuttal permanently damaged cost-per-wear as an equity defense, and the R2 elasticity split was decisive: a production tax on the cheapest garments hits near-inelastic basics for low-income buyers while only weakly targeting elastic trend churn. PRO never cleanly solved overbreadth, leakage/enforcement limits, or domestic-mill wage competitiveness, so the certain regressive cost outweighed the packaged subsidy story.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2 elasticity split (elastic micro-collections vs inelastic basics) showing the production tax is over-broad and cannot deliver PRO's intended cut without taxing necessary poor-household purchases.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing that the policy guarantees higher necessity costs while leaving the affordable substitute speculative and underfunded. Side A never reconciled a heavy, behavior-changing tax with modest consumer effects, and its own late numerical examples exposed large price increases and a shrinking revenue base. B also offered more targeted, rebated alternatives and clearly distinguished improving overseas work from eliminating it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal turned A’s own examples against it: the proposed shirt and coat prices represented substantial increases, the durability claim failed for growing children, and the implied subsidy required far more revenue per coat than A’s tax example generated. A’s subsequent “design challenge” response effectively conceded the central feasibility problem.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively dismantling Side A's core mechanism using Side A's own numbers. By showing that the tax revenue required to subsidize a single local coat would necessitate the sale of multiple fast-fashion coats, Side B proved that the funding model inherently contradicts the goal of shrinking fast-fashion volume. Side A's admission in the closing that this was a 'design challenge' sealed the loss.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was outstanding, particularly the mathematical takedown of Side A's pricing example (showing it takes ten taxed coats to fund one subsidized coat) and the practical point that a durable coat doesn't help a child who outgrows it in one season.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): CON won the decisive clash on incidence, regressivity, and funding coherence. PRO’s market-failure case and supply-side claim were real, but the heavy-yet-modest tax story never escaped the pass-through/absorb trap, the multi-use revenue pot shrank by design, and PRO’s own price examples (shirt $5→$7; coat math) showed the poor paying first while the affordable substitute remained a design problem. CON’s targeted toolkit (EPR, rebated carbon, labour standards) plus household rebates beat protectionist subsidy once compatibility was conceded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R1/R2 incidence and arithmetic sequence: either pass-through hits the poor or absorption kills behaviour change; then using B’s $ figures to show tax revenue cannot fund the needed subsidy at scale.
