### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Both sides opened with coherent, well-grounded cases—B on equity/accuracy/accountability, A on consent, state conflict-of-interest, and the value of plural custodians. The decisive turn came in Rebuttal 1, where A exposed that B's own "tiered access" safeguards concede that fully open public access is unsafe, collapsing the distinction between a public portal and the private/tribal systems B criticizes. A pressed this into a clean dilemma in Rebuttal 2 ("make access universal and consent breaks; make it consensual and universality breaks") and reinforced it with the exit-option argument (families can leave a private/tribal custodian but cannot leave a state master index). B's answers to the pressure questions were direct and well-formatted, but never resolved this specific bind—B largely reasserted that equity should be a "design mandate" without rebutting the exit-option asymmetry or the reclassification/bulk-scraping risk A raised. A's closing crystallized the clash sharply ("who holds the keys") and turned B's authorship argument back on itself, while B's closing mostly restated earlier equity claims rather than directly engaging that reframe. A's rebuttals did more real work neutralizing B's live route to victory, giving A the clearer, more adaptive path through the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 move showing that B's tiered-access concession undercuts the motion's core promise of universal public access, later sharpened into the "universal vs. consensual" dilemma that B never fully answered.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A won by effectively weaponizing Side B's own mitigation strategy. When Side B argued that a public portal could use "tiered access" and respect consent, Side A brilliantly trapped them in a dilemma: if access is fully restricted, it fails the mandate of universal public access; if it is open, it violates consent and exposes vulnerable people. Side A also successfully flipped the "state authorship" argument, arguing that the state's historical role as perpetrator makes it the worst possible monopoly gatekeeper for truth and recovery.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 effectively crystallized the trap: "PRO now promises vetoes, advisory boards, and embargoes... make access truly universal and consent breaks; make it truly consensual and universality... breaks."
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B wins because it exposed a structural tension in Side A's case: publicly funded digitization with meaningful consent controls replicates the restricted access of plural custodianship while concentrating power in the same state that created the harm. B's rebuttals progressively sharpened this dilemma, culminating in the closing distinction that petitioning the perpetrator-state for a veto is permission, not sovereignty. A's equity advantage was neutralized by its own concessions on refusal, and its reliance on democratic oversight never overcame B's argument that a state monopoly hardens official truth and crowds out rival custodians.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal (B4), which argued that tiered access inside a state portal either fails to replace private recovery or violates consent, trapping PRO on both horns of a dilemma it never escaped.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): PRO built the more durable structural case by preempting CON's "government monopoly" strawman early (public funding can pay private/community executors while mandating open access), which neutralized much of CON's efficiency and politicization attack. The pivotal moment was PRO's pressure question forcing CON to concede that no private firm will rescue commercially unviable records (slave ledgers, sealed adoption files, untranslated displacement lists); CON's fallback—grants to nonprofits and tribal archives—is itself public funding, just without PRO's guarantee of open access, a concession PRO exploited sharply in closing to show CON's position had collapsed into a weaker version of the same mechanism. CON's remaining arguments (state-as-perpetrator holding the archive keys, opportunity cost favoring direct reparations) were vivid but not fully resolved against PRO's counter that non-digitization leaves the same discretionary power with less transparency, and that documentary proof is typically a precondition for claims rather than a competitor to them. CON's answer to Q2 was especially thin, asserting that legal aid and land trusts "already function" without engaging the question of what claimants do when the only evidence is undigitized. PRO's closing crystallized the clash more cleanly than CON's, which largely restated its opening thesis with a strong metaphor but no new rebuttal traction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure question ("which private firm will preserve and open unviable records?") and CON's answer conceding the point in Rebuttal 2, which PRO then used in closing to show CON's own fallback model was just public funding without the open-access guarantee—collapsing CON's central distinction from the proposition.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by decisively trapping Side A on the question of commercial viability. By forcing Side A to concede that private markets will not index unprofitable records, Side B successfully subsumed Side A's alternative (grants to nonprofits and tribal archives) as proof that public funding is indeed required. Side B also cleanly neutralized the reparations trade-off argument by demonstrating that these records are the necessary prerequisites for material claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing brilliantly flipped Side A's "grants to nonprofits" alternative, pointing out that this is exactly what "publicly funding access" means, contrasting it perfectly against leaving it to private markets.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): PRO wins because CON effectively conceded that private markets will not preserve the commercially unviable records—slave-sale ledgers, sealed adoption indexes, untranslated displacement lists—that are the core of the motion. PRO then demonstrated that CON's fallback (grant funding to nonprofits and tribal archives) still relies on public money while surrendering the results to subscription walls, institutional gatekeeping, or neglect. PRO's answers on contracting enforcement and digitize-on-request triage resolved CON's best objections on delay and accountability, leaving CON's politicization and opportunity-cost arguments rhetorically vivid but unable to overcome the central incoherence: CON defended efficient access to records the market already serves, while offering no sustainable path to the records it admitted the market would abandon.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7), where A answered CON's pressure questions with staged public contracting and claimant triage, then pivoted to show that CON's grant-funding alternative is simply public expenditure without the proposition's guarantee of open access—exposing that CON's model subsidizes rescue work only to let it remain gated.
