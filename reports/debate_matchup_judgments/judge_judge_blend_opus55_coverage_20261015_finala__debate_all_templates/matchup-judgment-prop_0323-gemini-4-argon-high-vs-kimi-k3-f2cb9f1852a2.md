B's rebuttals on shame psychology (public vs private knowledge) and misinformation virality were solid and kept the debate close, but did not fully neutralize A's structural critique, leaving A with the cleaner, more consistently executed path to victory.

That ambiguity let it claim autonomy benefits while evading B’s costs to users who value visible context. A’s nonexistent closing references were also a minor execution flaw.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its answer that hiding tallies does not stop engagement from driving distribution; it instead removes visible counter-signals such as quantified backlash and traction. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Both sides argued with discipline and clarity, but A's pressure question (A3.1) exposed a structural flaw B never fully closed: if counts default to hidden on both the poster's and the viewer's side, then "opt-in" display is nearly useless for triage, since ordinary correctors/whistleblowers won't proactively flip settings while only clout-chasers will, destroying the shared baseline B relies on for consensus. B's answer (B4.1) tried to resolve this by saying the switch works both ends, but A's final rebuttal (A4.1-A4.4) correctly pointed out this creates a contradiction: if defaults are powerful enough to meaningfully shield anxious users from comparison, they're also powerful enough to blind the public from ever seeing the "opt-in" debunker's numbers, undermining B's triage/discovery concessions. B's closing (B5.3) tried to spin this stickiness as cutting in its favor but didn't actually resolve the asymmetry A identified — it asserted the conclusion rather than re-engaging the mechanism. A also landed a clean point that surviving algorithmic optimization on hidden engagement data undercuts B's framing that hiding displayed numbers meaningfully stops a "popularity contest," since the contest simply moves underground into an opaque ranking system. B's rebuttal that "two distortions don't cancel out" was a fair response but didn't fully defuse the charge that B's proposal left the deeper driver of the harm (algorithmic sorting) untouched while only removing the one legible signal that let outside observers audit manipulation. On balance, A's rebuttals in the back half did more specific, structural damage to B's core "asymmetry" framing than B did to A's triage/discovery case, giving A the cleaner path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure question Q1 (A3.1) about whether the default hides counts for viewers or posters, and the follow-up in A4.1/A4.4 showing that B's two-sided default creates a self-defeating tradeoff between shielding the anxious and preserving public triage.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that public metrics have shared-baseline and network value that does not simply survive opt-in. Side A established plausible reductions in shame and herding, but its claim that readers could recover every benefit by flipping a switch ignored its own requirement that posters also choose to display their metrics. B further showed that private analytics and selective status displays preserve some comparison anxiety while hidden defaults remove context from most casual readers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A4.1 was decisive: if both viewers and posters default to hidden, a reader opting in still cannot see endorsements from ordinary posters who remain hidden. This directly defeated A’s repeated claim that all informational benefits survive opt-in and showed how selective display could privilege clout-seekers rather than preserve meaningful consensus.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side A wins by decisively exposing the mechanical and philosophical contradictions in Side B's proposal. Side A effectively demonstrates that if counts are off by default for viewers, the "opt-in" mechanism fails to preserve civic consensus or information triage, as casual readers will not see the metrics unless they also change their settings. Furthermore, Side A lands a strong philosophical blow by pointing out that hiding the metrics does not stop the platform's algorithms from optimizing around them in the dark, thereby failing the spirit of the proposition while stripping the public square of transparency. Side B's rhetoric was polished, but Side A's logical dismantling of the policy's practical implementation was superior.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they exploit Side B's clarification about the toggle mechanics to prove that default stickiness would blind the public to consensus signals while failing to protect teenagers from status-signaling peers.
