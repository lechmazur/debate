### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): A (CON) built a more internally consistent case and more effectively exploited tensions in B's position. A's sharpest move was catching B's property analogy flip-flopping between "divisible like a bookshelf" (opening) and "indivisible like a car/house, sellable by the estate" (rebuttal 1) — a contradiction A never let go of, and which pushed B into conceding that estates might sell libraries to third parties, a move that exceeds the motion's own "pass to next of kin" framing and opens a resale-market vulnerability A exploited well. A's cost argument (licenses priced for a single lifespan, transfer to a young heir effectively doubling the service horizon) was a plausible mechanism that B's rebuttal ("same seat, no new copy") only partially answered, and A's closing "actuarial fallacy" counter-rebuttal, while not perfectly airtight, landed harder than B's circular "if the decedent had lived fifty more years" reply. A also pressed a privacy point (the title itself, not just activity logs, can be sensitive) that B never actually answered, repeating only "no activity logs" without engaging the substance. B's strongest asset — the "windfall" framing and the narrow scope carve-out — was clever but undercut by B's own concession that platforms could simply abandon one-time purchases for subscriptions, which A repeatedly turned into evidence that the guarantee is self-defeating for future consumers. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions format correctly, but A's answers in Rebuttal 2 did more real work refuting live points than B's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 (A2.1–A2.3) catching B's shift from "digital libraries are like a splittable bookshelf" to "they're indivisible like a car that the estate can sell" was the decisive exchange — it exposed a structural inconsistency in B's property analogy that B never fully repaired, and it seeded the later resale-market and multi-heir objections A kept leveraging through the rest of the debate.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) built the cleaner path to victory by showing the guarantee collapses under its own terms: B’s explicit exemption for rentals/subscriptions creates a ready escape hatch that kills permanent purchases for the living, while multi-heir indivisibility and true multi-decade service-cost extension cannot be solved without either secondary markets or estate equalization that often fails. B’s property/windfall framing and “same seat” substitution stayed morally sharp but never neutralized the actuarial and incentive clash after full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2/Closing dismantling of B’s “same DRM seat / if decedent had lived longer” substitution plus the “protect existing libraries even if permanent model dies” concession, exposing the multi-generational free ride and living-consumer tradeoff.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B wins because A never resolved the central economic trade-off: guaranteeing inheritance for non-transferable digital licenses gives platforms and creators a powerful incentive to abandon permanent one-time purchases in favor of subscriptions and rentals, which A explicitly exempted. A conceded that sellers could abandon the purchase model and retreated to protecting only existing libraries, while B showed that this prospectively destroys the very ownership option the motion tries to preserve. A's property analogies and seat-transfer rebuttal were forceful, but B's pressure on indivisibility, actuarial pricing, and market response created a cleaner live path to victory that survived the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal (Turn 8) was decisive, exposing A's concession that the mandate would likely kill permanent purchases going forward, and weaving together the actuarial-cost, resale-market, and privacy problems into a coherent weighing argument that crystallized the debate.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but PRO (B) landed the more durable blows. B caught a real internal tension in CON's position (A1.8's "share credentials" fallback versus A2.1's denial of endorsing credential-sharing), and CON's attempted clarification in A4.3 only partially resolved it. B also neutralized CON's "lost downstream sale" market-harm argument by analogizing to physical book inheritance (B2.4, B4.4), and CON never directly re-engaged that analogy in Rebuttal 2 or Closing, leaving it as a live, unanswered route for PRO. On privacy, CON's sharpest point—that calling transfer "confiscation" begs the question since no transferable right currently exists (A4.2)—was a clever reframe, but B's "full retail price creates an ownership expectation regardless of fine print" response (B5.2) directly engaged rather than ignored it, keeping that exchange roughly even. CON's administrative-burden argument (foreign probate, heir disputes, indefinite support) was plausible but met by B's probate/bank-account analogy, and neither side definitively won that sub-point. On balance, B's rebuttals were more consistently responsive to CON's specific claims, exposed a real contradiction, and left fewer of CON's attacks fully live by the end, while CON dropped the market-harm/physical-analogy clash in its final two speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1's exposure of the contradiction between A1.1 ("non-transferable is a sacred bargain") and A1.8 ("families can share credentials"), combined with B2.4/B4.4's physical-book analogy neutralizing CON's downstream-sale objection—both left only partially answered by CON through Rebuttal 2 and Closing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the motion's normative question live: full-price "Buy" libraries should not vanish into platform windfalls at death. Its ownership-advertising and no-new-copy framing held up better than Side A's pure license rewrite claim, and B neutralized cost/privacy objections via probate practice, zero-net-user hosting, and the physical-inheritance parallel. Side A was coherent on continuing service liability and catalog-as-profile privacy, but kept undercutting itself with credential-sharing/voluntary-tools tension and never made digital confiscation feel less arbitrary than physical transfer. B made the decisive clash easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1–B2.4: credential-sharing both violates the contract A defends and consumes the same server/sale "harm," while physical books already deny publishers a repeat sale—collapsing A's necessity and copyright routes.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A won by framing digital purchases as assets consumers reasonably believe they own, then defusing B’s cost and copyright objections with the one-for-one heir replacement logic and the data-wiping transfer mechanism. B’s contractual privacy arguments were mitigated by A’s opt-out and physical-analogy responses, and A’s sharper rhetoric crystallized the clash more effectively across the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s counter that an heir simply replaces the decedent as a single active user, so hosting and security costs do not increase, blunted B’s claim that inheritance creates crushing ongoing infrastructure burdens.
