### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued with discipline and avoided fake precision, producing a genuinely close contest. CON (A) built a strong humanitarian frame ("certain hunger today for speculative abundance tomorrow") and landed a real hit with the "two places at once" critique of PRO's spillover mechanism, plus a clean distinction between "trimming" (gear bans, seasonal closures) and "amputating" (30% closure). But PRO (B) more effectively resolved the central enforcement clash: when CON proposed port-based license revocation as its enforcement mechanism for bottom-trawling bans, PRO turned this directly against CON by showing the identical port/satellite mechanism works just as well to enforce no-take boundaries, since presence-in-zone is a binary, easily verified violation while quota/catch compliance is harder to police. CON's answer to this symmetry (that port fines don't solve "paper parks" because they need flag-state cooperation and clean courts) applies equally to CON's own alternative, and CON never fully owned that parity. PRO also successfully flipped the cod example, arguing it indicts quota-based management rather than reserves, and PRO's "portfolio" answer (bulk offshore, strategic inshore fraction) at least partially defused the "two places at once" trap even though CON kept re-asserting the binary version in closing. Both followed the Q&A format correctly, but PRO's answers did slightly more actual work resolving the live tension, giving it the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's move in Rebuttal 2 converting CON's own port-license enforcement mechanism (offered as proof poor states can enforce gear/trawling bans) into proof that the same states can enforce no-take zone boundaries, which CON never cleanly distinguished from its own model's reliance on the identical port chokepoint mechanism.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing that a uniform 30% no-take requirement is poorly matched to ecological and economic variation. Its mobile-species, displaced-effort, enforcement, and immediate-livelihood objections remained live, while its targeted gear, licensing, and seasonal alternatives plausibly imposed less hardship. Side A made a strong sustainability case but did not prove that permanent reserves are uniquely necessary or that vulnerable communities could reliably survive the transition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s cut-versus-closure response was decisive: targeted gear rules and brief seasonal limits need not create the same deprivation as permanently removing productive grounds. This directly weakened Side A’s claim that both approaches impose equivalent transition costs, while the offshore-versus-inshore placement dilemma further challenged the promised spillover.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by demonstrating that Side B's alternative management regime imposes comparable transition costs and enforcement burdens while delivering a weaker biological payoff. A successfully pressured B on enforcement: if poor states can manage complex quotas, gear rules, and port inspections across the entire EEZ, they can enforce a clear spatial boundary. A also showed that B's seasonal closures and gear restrictions still expose fish to extraction upon reopening, whereas permanent no-take sanctuaries allow large breeders to survive and restock surrounding waters year after year. B landed powerful rhetorical blows about the cruelty of evicting dependent communities today for speculative spillover tomorrow, but never fully answered A's core comparative point that B's own rules reduce immediate harvests without guaranteeing stock recovery. The "which 30%" tension remained a live objection, yet A's portfolio placement offered a sufficiently plausible spatial model to keep their path to victory intact after the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's enforcement and comparative-biology pressure. In Turn 7 [A4.3-A4.5], A argued that B's landing-beach enforcement leaves industrial fleets free offshore, while port and satellite measures can police reserve boundaries; B's counter in Turn 8 [B4.1] inadvertently admitted that states can police industrial fleets at chokepoints, which A crystallized in closing [A5.2] to undermine B's "paper park" critique. Combined with A's point that B's gear and seasonal rules catch fish the moment seasons reopen—never letting old breeders survive—this left B looking like costlier pain for less biological gain.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases with strong rhetoric and no fabricated evidence, but CON (Side A) landed the more decisive tactical blow. A's pressure questions forced PRO into a dilemma over where the 30% reserve actually sits—inshore (crushing villagers) or offshore (unenforceable by villages, contradicting PRO's enforcement-simplicity argument). PRO's answer ("both") effectively conceded that the workable version of its plan is small inshore nursery cores plus offshore zones patrolled by state tech—structurally indistinguishable from CON's own co-management model. CON exploited this concession sharply in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, arguing PRO never justified why the rigid 30% threshold specifically (rather than smaller, targeted protections) is necessary, which is the actual burden of the motion. PRO's responses on "exit pressure" and "bridging aid" also understated the scale/duration problem CON raised (multi-year closures vs. a survivable "lean season"), leaving that live point only partially answered. PRO's constructive case (spillover mechanics, EEZ size argument, enforcement simplicity) remained intact in isolation, but after the exchange CON's reframing made the specific 30% mandate look unjustified and practically collapsible into CON's alternative, giving CON the cleaner path to the resolution as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1/Rebuttal 2 exposing the inshore-vs-offshore contradiction in PRO's 30% placement, and PRO's "both" answer in A1 that effectively conceded a hybrid model resembling CON's co-management proposal.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won by showing that A never adequately resolved the transition and enforcement burdens for subsistence communities. A’s offshore-plus-tiny-inshore design reduced local harm, but also weakened the case that a rigid 30% mandate was necessary rather than targeted co-management. B’s incentive-based enforcement alternative and critique of effort displacement remained live, while A relied heavily on uncertain bridging aid and the flawed assumption that protecting 30% of area makes a third of fish untouchable. B’s nonexistent P-reference labels were an execution cost, though the quoted claims were still identifiable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange exposed A’s “exit pressure” as hunger and bankruptcy for the least mobile fishers, then argued that A’s answer—tiny community-supported nursery closures plus offshore enforcement—largely conceded B’s targeted model without justifying the arbitrary 30% requirement.

Side A's attempt to split the difference—small inshore nursery pockets plus vast offshore zones—was deftly absorbed by Side B as an admission that targeted co-management, not a sweeping lockout, is the real solution. Side B also landed the sharper human-cost pressure, turning Side A's own phrases about "exit pressure" and "painful" crowding into evidence of callousness, and showing that criminalizing subsistence fishing destroys the compliance any reserve needs. Side A's biological spillover case remained plausible but never fully resolved the immediate survival contradiction or the geographic dilemma.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal, which reframed Side A's "both" answer on reserve location as a concession that the 30% mandate collapses into tiny inshore community zones (Side B's own model) plus irrelevant offshore industrial space, while also noting that if satellite enforcement exists, it can be used for trawler bans without the arbitrary percentage.
