### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued with sophistication, but CON built the more durable throughline: that pet damage insurance/deposits solve the landlord's property-risk problem while leaving the diffuse, uninsurable harms to neighbors (lost sleep, allergens, fear) completely unaddressed. CON's pressure questions (who compensates the neighbor, and the risk-pricing dilemma) exposed a gap PRO never fully closed — PRO's answer ("prevention plus swift removal") was repeatedly shown by CON to still require harm-then-documentation before relief, reversing who bears the burden. CON's original point that a default-allow regime scales total harm upward (many open pets vs. a self-selected few concealed ones) directly rebutted PRO's "secret pets are worse" claim, and PRO's closing never engaged that scale argument. PRO's analogies to human noise/parties were clever but CON turned them effectively, noting that buildings commonly do ban smoking outright precisely because spillover is hard to police, undercutting PRO's claim that bans are inherently arbitrary. CON's closing crystallized the clash cleanly around "who must prove harm before being allowed to avoid it," giving a sharper decision rule than PRO's final reassertion of its core framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.5 (scale argument: ten open dogs vs. one hidden cat) combined with B4.4's observation that PRO's own framework leaves neighbors with only "logs, complaints, and patience" while landlords get first-dollar insurance — this exchange most decisively exposed the unresolved asymmetry in PRO's case.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by exposing the mismatch between property insurance and harms to neighbors. Side B offered credible benefits from openness, conduct rules, and compensation, but leaned too heavily on the contestable claim that bans merely drive pets underground. Side A showed that bright-line bans deter many animals and avoid forcing neighbors to document noise, allergens, or fear after exposure. Its smoking analogy and burden-of-proof framing also weakened Side B's comparison to ordinary apartment noise.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal was decisive: it answered the human-noise analogy by distinguishing preventable, continuous pet spillovers and then highlighted Side B's concession that affected neighbors receive no compensation, only complaint-driven enforcement after harm.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully identifying a critical gap in Side A's policy: insurance compensates the landlord for property damage, but does nothing to protect neighbors from shared-living externalities like noise and allergies. Side B also effectively dismantled Side A's comparisons to human noise by likening pets to smoking and drum kits—optional nuisances that buildings frequently and legitimately ban.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal cleanly neutralized the "baby/human noise" analogy by pointing out that babies are people with housing rights, whereas pets are chosen nuisances similar to indoor smoking, which buildings routinely ban.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and engaged directly, but B built the more resolution-relevant critique: the motion's chosen mechanism (damage insurance/deposits) targets financial harm, while B consistently showed the real-world objections to pets in shared buildings are non-monetary (noise, allergens, fear, safety) and occur during the "warn-then-remove" window that insurance cannot touch. B's answer to Pressure Q2 was risky but honest and consistent, turning what looked like a "gotcha" (would you still ban with full insurance?) into reinforcement of its core thesis that this was never primarily a money problem. A's strongest material — the secret-pet argument and the cooking/baby/stereo analogies — scored real points, but A's own answer in A4.2 (landlords can't reject a routine insured pet just to stay pet-free) handed B a decisive rebuttal: default-yes effectively eliminates meaningful tenant sorting into pet-free buildings, which B exploited cleanly in both Rebuttal 2 and Closing. A never fully neutralized the "harm occurs during the enforcement lag" problem, which remained a live, unanswered route for B through the final speech.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to A's Pressure Q2 ("Yes, I would still allow a blanket ban even if a policy paid flooring, subfloor, and vacancy in full... because the motion's price tag never covered the real cost") combined with B's R4.6 use of A's own A4.2 concession ("every building becomes a pet building by default... tenants seeking pet-free living have nowhere to sort") formed the most decisive exchange, reframing the whole debate around unfinanceable shared-space harms and lost sorting options.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): A won by showing that insurance addresses property damage but not the inherent shared-space costs of noise, allergens, fear, and safety, while post-incident enforcement necessarily exposes neighbors first. B offered strong registration and overbreadth arguments, but never adequately answered A's key frequency comparison: occasional hidden or exempt animals do not create the same aggregate burden as making pets routinely legal in every building.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's sharpest response was that B's short-fuse removal process still spends neighbors' sleep and safety while violations are documented. The contrast between policing one prohibited pet and managing many lawful pets also directly weakened B's claim that bans leave residents exposed to essentially the same risks.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by consistently showing a mismatch between the motion's proposed solution (financial insurance) and the actual problems pets cause in dense housing (uninsurable noise, allergies, and fear). Side B successfully argued that a 'short fuse' for behavioral removal still requires neighbors to endure the harm first, decisively answering Side A's strongest points.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 response to A's "short fuse" argument: pointing out that a fuse still burns the neighbor first, and that warning-then-removal spends the neighbor's sleep to protect the pet owner's second chance.
