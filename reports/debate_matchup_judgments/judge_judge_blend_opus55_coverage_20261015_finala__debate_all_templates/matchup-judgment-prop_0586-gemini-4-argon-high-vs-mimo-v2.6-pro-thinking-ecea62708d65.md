### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): CON built the tighter, more falsifiable case. PRO's core claim—that sectoral calibration inherently produces fewer job losses at an equal wage level—was repeatedly asserted ("inherently produces fewer total job losses") but never evidenced, and CON's pressure question (Q1 in B3.1) exposed this directly; PRO's answer (A4.1) cited that boards set rates "above the general minimum" while sparing other sectors, which doesn't actually compare an equal-sized raise's job effects as asked. CON also successfully defused PRO's "contradiction" charge (A2.1) by clarifying its point was never that boards can't be implemented but that US wage boards function as industry-scoped minimum wages inheriting the same tradeoffs—a distinction PRO never cleanly rebutted. PRO's claim that CON "conceded" the case by allowing legislatures to write sector-differentiated schedules (A4.3) was a stretch that CON cleanly rejected (B4.1). CON leveraged the existing general consensus on modest minimum-wage employment effects as a benchmark PRO had to beat and never did. PRO's mechanism argument about differing margins across industries remained intuitively plausible but was undercut by its own examples (franchise/brand-wide floors), which CON repeatedly noted look just like scoped minimum wages rather than firm-level precision. CON's closing crystallized the burden clearly; PRO's closing mostly restated its opening framing without closing the evidentiary gap. CON's case lost some polish due to two word-limit clippings, which is a real execution cost, but the substantive argument and rebuttal quality still favor CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1, where CON clarified that citing actual US wage boards (NY/CA fast food) was not a contradiction but proof that US sectoral boards function as industry-scoped minimum wages—directly neutralizing PRO's "contradiction" charge from A2.1 and reframing the entire debate around whether scoping (not board mechanism) actually reduces job loss, a burden PRO never met.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): A won by keeping the proposition’s actual comparison live: large across-the-board hikes versus industry-calibrated floors in an uneven economy. A’s trap frame (high flat rate crushes fragile sectors, or low flat rate forgoes affordable gains) stayed clearer than B’s “same instrument in a narrower box” critique, and A converted B’s differentiated-schedules / florist-exemption language into a near-concession that uniform ATB needs sectoral tailoring. B scored real damage on coverage, classification gaming, and A’s thin empirical proof of fewer job losses, and twice lost ground to clipping; still, A answered pressure more directly, used B’s US board examples against the “no infrastructure” claim, and made the decisive employment-tradeoff logic easier to vote on.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s A4.3/A3.1 turn of B2.2: once B defends sector-differentiated exemptions to save low-margin employers, A locks the clash as ATB rigidity versus sectoral calibration.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO's core logical path survived: a large rate applied to a profitable sector must produce ≤ total job loss than the same rate applied economy-wide, while delivering big raises where margins allow. CON showed boards function as sector-scoped minima and raised the coverage/fragmented-tail problem, but conceded the tailoring principle by invoking sector-differentiated legislative schedules and never rebutted the subset-logic on employment losses, relying instead on an aggregate modest-effects record that doesn't address the large-hike comparison in the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Rebuttal 2 A1/B4.1-B4.3 – PRO turned CON's own "exempt a rural florist" defense into a concession that uniform hikes need tailoring, then deployed the inherently-fewer-losses subset logic that CON never quantified against.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent causal stories, but B identified and sustained a genuine structural contradiction in A's case: A needed broad sector coverage to stop cross-sector undercutting, yet also needed fine-grained tiering by margin to avoid job losses, and these two requirements undercut each other (a grocery deli in a "lower grocer's tier" next to a restaurant in a "higher tier" still creates the very undercutting A's mechanism was supposed to eliminate). A's attempt to resolve this in A4.1 by separating "coverage" from "rate-setting" dials never actually answered why differential tiers within shared coverage wouldn't still trigger substitution, and B pressed this cleanly in B4.3-B4.4 and again in closing. B also turned A's own concession ("push metro fast food and regional warehousing hard" to match aggregate pay lift) into a weighing point that directly damaged A's core "fewer employment losses" burden. On execution, A's Rebuttal 1, Rebuttal 2, and Closing were all clipped for exceeding word limits, cutting off the ends of key arguments (including the final sentence of the closing), which is a real cost per the judging criteria, while B's speeches were fully delivered, properly formatted (including clean A1/A2 answers to pressure questions), and directly responsive. B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly without introducing new material, while A's closing was literally cut off mid-argument. These factors combine to give B a clear, non-trivial edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's exchange in B2.1-B2.2 and B4.3-B4.4, exposing that A's "broad coverage plus fine tiering" model is self-contradictory (broad coverage stops substitution only if tiers are uniform, but fine tiering by margin recreates the substitution problem), was the most decisive clash point and was never fully resolved by A.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won by keeping a live contradiction at the center: A needed broad coverage to block substitution and fine capacity-based tiers to avoid uniform shocks, and never resolved how a grocery deli next door can be both covered and lower-tiered without consumer flight and job composition losses. B also forced the pay-lift/employment tradeoff—gentle thin-margin schedules undercut the equal-raise claim; hard sector spikes recreate concentrated losses—and answered pressure more cleanly. A had a coherent multi-dial mechanism story and scored on statutory one-number limits, but repeated clipping plus unresolved cross-street substitution left B with the clearer comparative path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1–B2.2 (and follow-through in B4.3–B4.4): A cannot both sweep delis into broad food-service coverage and spare them as thin-margin grocers.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): CON won the core comparative clash. PRO's differentiation advantage was answered by CON's geographic tiers for statutory floors, which block substitution because distance matters, while sectoral tiering does not because a restaurant and a deli across the street are direct substitutes. PRO's attempt to solve this with broad coverage plus internal tiers left the price gap and the conceded shift to lower-tier work intact, undermining both the pay-raise and fewer-losses prongs. CON also sustained the capture argument and forced PRO into admitting extreme spikes elsewhere to match aggregate pay.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 / Pressure Q1 exposing B2.1 vs B2.3 contradiction: if boards are broad to stop cross-street substitution they recreate uniform shock; if narrow to protect thin margins they trigger substitution and, per B5.4, shift demand into lower-tier work.
