### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): PRO built a coherent, scoped case (target screening at high-risk clusters, refund buyers, claw back from fraudulent sellers) and used the pressure round to expose a genuine tension in CON's position: CON claimed garments are too idiosyncratic for platforms to authenticate (B1.1) while simultaneously saying buyers can self-verify and third-party authenticators exist (B1.5, B1.6). CON's attempted fix—distinguishing a buyer's "sanity check" from true certification—actually conceded that sophisticated fakes fool buyers too, which PRO then leveraged to dismantle CON's "least-cost avoider" claim in Rebuttal 2. CON's structural points (lack of serial numbers, cost of scaling authentication, risk of a "curated boutique") were solid and never fully erased, but CON's closing mostly restated the boutique/cost fear without directly re-engaging PRO's narrowed "target where counterfeits cluster" proposal, leaving that mitigation unanswered. CON also never substantively rebutted PRO's "market for lemons" adverse-selection argument. PRO's closing crystallized the clash cleanly ("platforms cannot charge commissions for trust and then plead helplessness"), while CON's closing repeated earlier framing without resolving the live contradiction. This makes PRO's path to the resolution clearer and more complete by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Questions (A3.1/A3.2) forcing CON to reconcile "garments can't be authenticated" with "buyers/third parties can authenticate them," and CON's partial concession in the following rebuttal that buyers only perform a "sanity check" against sophisticated fakes, which PRO then used to collapse CON's least-cost-avoider claim.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by defending the motion’s incremental scope: targeted screening of higher-risk goods, broader guarantees, and platform recourse against sellers. Side A’s cost and delisting concerns were plausible, but it repeatedly inflated “more items and more risk” into authentication of every cheap garment and an unconditional platform-wide guarantee. That binary did not answer B’s tiered model or explain why the least-informed party should remain the main risk bearer. Both sides incurred minor costs for nonexistent C-reference IDs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange exposed the tension between A calling garment authentication nearly impossible while naming buyers as least-cost avoiders and directing them to third-party authenticators. A distinguished basic inspection from certification, but that concession reinforced B’s point that buyers cannot reliably detect sophisticated counterfeits and have less leverage than platforms.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) prevailed by maintaining a clearer live route to victory after the full exchange. It successfully framed platforms as the least-cost avoiders with superior data and screening capabilities, exposed a damaging tension in CON's case between claiming authentication is impossible and endorsing third-party authenticators for buyers, and turned CON's own pressure question about seller recourse into an argument for platform liability. While CON raised plausible concerns about authentication costs and market curation, its market-destruction thesis was blunted by PRO's tiered screening distinction, and its attempt to cast platforms as fraud "victims" alongside buyers backfired rhetorically and substantively. PRO's incentive critique—that platforms currently pocket commissions while buyers absorb losses—remained live and comparatively heavier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 exposure of the contradiction between CON's claim that clothing cannot be forensically authenticated and its simultaneous reliance on third-party authentication services for individual buyers, which undermined CON's "impossible standard" argument and forced CON into a weaker cost-based retreat that never fully recovered.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but CON built a more durable mechanistic case. Its core economic point—that authentication fees scale as a flat logistics cost while platform revenue scales as a percentage, so authentication cannot simply be pushed down to low-value everyday clothing—was introduced early and never dislodged. CON also sharpened the moral-hazard argument (swap-and-return fraud absent physical custody) and, crucially, caught PRO in an internal contradiction: PRO's opening insisted sophisticated counterfeits can't be caught by photos, yet PRO's later dispute-resolution mechanism relied on "photo forensics." PRO's answers to the pressure questions (tiering fees, batching shipments, AI pre-screening) were vague and were effectively turned against it as tacit concessions that authentication requires either higher fees or infeasible logistics for individual sellers. PRO's closing restated principle but never directly repaired the contradiction or answered the serial-number/physical-custody problem for ordinary garments, while CON's closing cleanly quoted PRO's own admission that physical pre-shipment verification is "the only mechanism" against fraud and used it to show uninspected peer-to-peer sales cannot be secured without destroying affordability. CON's progression across rounds was tighter and more responsive to live pressure, giving it the clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.4, which caught PRO relying on "photo forensics" and "AI pre-screening" to resolve disputes after PRO's own opening had asserted that photos cannot catch sophisticated counterfeiters—an unresolved internal contradiction that PRO's closing did not repair.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by grounding the comparison in flat authentication costs, percentage-based commissions, and buyer-side return fraud. Side B correctly emphasized platform capacity and the modest wording of “more,” but never identified a workable expansion that would reliably establish authenticity and custody without higher fees or unresolved swap fraud. Its answers largely shifted among physical hubs, AI, photo forensics, and risk absorption without resolving those tradeoffs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it contrasted Side B’s opening claim that photos cannot protect buyers with its later reliance on AI and photo forensics, then showed that neither photos nor escrow can determine who swapped an item absent physical custody. This left Side B choosing between costly hubs and liability amid irresolvable claims.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins by exposing PRO's fatal oscillation between physical authentication (which PRO admitted was the only anti-fraud mechanism) and cheaper photo-based solutions (which PRO's opening rejected). This contradiction left PRO unable to explain how platforms could absorb more risk on everyday peer-to-peer items without either destroying unit economics through mandatory warehouse hubs or inviting rampant buyer fraud through blind refunds. CON's scope correction—that most resale involves cheap everyday clothes, not luxury bags—held up, and their unit-economics argument (flat inspection costs vs. percentage commissions) was never squarely answered. PRO's intuitive "risk laundering" frame was rhetorically potent but collapsed under structural scrutiny when CON showed it would price ordinary secondhand shoppers out of the market.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's collapse of PRO's distinction between pre-shipment authentication and post-sale dispute resolution: after PRO insisted physical custody chains were necessary to stop swap-and-return fraud, CON showed that retreating to "photo forensics" and AI directly contradicted PRO's opening claim that photos were insufficient against sophisticated counterfeiters, exposing that PRO had no coherent mechanism to protect ordinary peer-to-peer transactions.
