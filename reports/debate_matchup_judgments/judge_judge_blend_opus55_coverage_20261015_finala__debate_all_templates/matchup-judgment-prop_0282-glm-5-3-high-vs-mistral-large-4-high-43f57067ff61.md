### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by exposing two unresolved burdens. Side A treated a structural incentive as proof that exploitation is routine, but never established prevalence after B directly pressed that distinction. More importantly, A's narrowed consent regime remained vague about who decides newsworthiness and when, while its reliance on courts and oversight weakened its claim that new rules uniquely prevent irreversible publication. B offered the cleaner comparison: strengthen targeted privacy remedies rather than create a genre-focused standard that risks chilling legitimate investigations. B's advocacy of better norms conceded some need for reform, but A did not capitalize on that tension enough to overcome its proof and implementation gaps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: it answered the permanence challenge by showing that A's court-enforced regime would also largely operate after publication, then compared both approaches through deterrence and the copyright analogy. It also connected A's shifting consent language to reluctant sources and investigative chilling, while A never supplied a precise limiting rule.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won by showing that the harms PRO identified are already covered by existing torts and that PRO's proposed regime is either post-hoc (not genuinely preventative) or, if treated as ex-ante consent, an overbroad prior restraint that would chill legitimate investigative work like wrongful-conviction reporting. CON's alternative—strengthening existing privacy remedies with fee-shifting—was more surgically tailored and constitutionally cautious. PRO never adequately answered why a vague, genre-targeted consent rule was superior to empowered enforcement of existing law, and never overcame the *Thin Blue Line* / non-consenting-source challenge to its standard. The closing crystallized this cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal symmetrical strike (A4.1–A4.3): PRO's complaint about permanent digitized harm applies equally to their own post-hoc penalties, so the real comparison is between precise existing remedies (with fee-shifting) and a vaguer new regime that still cannot un-publish content.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won the central weighing: B's permanence argument (harm is forever, so need new rules) was turned by A showing B's own consent/penalty regime is also post-hoc unless it is prior restraint censorship. A also damaged B's "no legal mechanism" claim by naming existing privacy torts for the exact private-records example, and framed a cleaner tradeoff — strengthen fee-shifting/ existing torts vs. a vague "private, non-newsworthy" genre ban that would veto reluctant witnesses essential to exoneration work. B redefined "routine" as incentive rather than prevalence and never solved the prior-restraint dilemma.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1/A4.2 - A shows PRO's rules are also post-hoc and cannot un-publish, so permanence does not favor new constraints; copyright analogy makes fee-shifting as deterrence coherent, collapsing B's core justification.
