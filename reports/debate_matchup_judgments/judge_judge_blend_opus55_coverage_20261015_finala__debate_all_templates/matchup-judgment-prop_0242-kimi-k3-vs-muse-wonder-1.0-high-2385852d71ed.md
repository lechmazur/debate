### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and executed the Q&A format cleanly with explicit A1/A2 answers. B's strongest contribution was the conceptual distinction between "fragmentation" and "polarization," arguing that scattered micro-feeds cannot coordinate two hostile blocs without a synchronizing national narrative supplied by cable, plus the audience-reach point that high-turnout, decisive voters skew cable-heavy. But A directly neutralized the audience point by showing Facebook/YouTube reach exactly that cohort, and answered the synchronization challenge with a genuinely new mechanism (engagement-ranking convergence on shared villains/labels via trending and cross-posting, i.e., "synchronized by destination, not schedule"), plus the purity-spiral argument explaining how fragmented routes still harden a common in-group/out-group identity. A also exploited the agenda-setting reversal (cable now chases viral content) to undercut B's clean "factory vs. pipe" framing, and B's reply ("packaging, not authorship") was more assertion than rebuttal. B's cross-national polarization claim in rebuttal 1 was also a bit of unverifiable precision that slightly weakens its grounding score. On balance A's feedback-loop/participatory-politics mechanism was more directly defended against the sharpest pressure B raised, giving A the cleaner, more resilient path to the motion as worded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer to B's synchronization question in Rebuttal 2 ("personalization synchronizes by destination, not schedule... trending lists, hashtags, recommendation chains... coordinate millions without requiring the same broadcast") most directly neutralized B's core "fragmentation isn't polarization" objection, and B's reply never fully rebuilt that ground.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by explaining why social media transforms rather than merely distributes polarization: personalization, peer enforcement, algorithmic feedback, and online discipline of political elites deepen hostility across democratic life. Side B offered strong chronology and audience arguments, but repeatedly asserted rather than established that cable must author every shared partisan script. It also relied on a late, undeveloped cross-national claim. A better answered the fragmentation objection and showed that varied informational routes can still converge on hardened partisan identities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest response was that feeds fragment the route while consolidating the identity. This directly answered Side B’s claim that personalization creates only chaos, explaining how micro-community purity competition can intensify two common hostile blocs without synchronized nightly broadcasting.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): CON won by keeping the decisive comparison live: polarization of democratic politics means large, coordinated hostile blocs that move voters, donors, and primaries—not personalized chaos. CON’s chronology-plus-audience-plus-synchronization package (oldest/most polarized are cable-heavy; scripts need a one-to-many supplier; SM mostly pipes/amplifies) survived PRO’s mechanism story. PRO was strong on engagement loops, purity spirals, and online primary threats, and answered pressure cleanly, but never isolated an independent content/authorship story that outweighed cable’s factory role for the electorate that decides. CON’s factory-vs-pipe / fire-vs-accelerant weighing was clearer and harder to escape.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s fragmentation≠polarization turn (A2.5→A4.3–A5.2): personalized routes still need a nationally legible shared enemy/script, which CON held as cable-supplied; PRO’s destination-not-schedule answer was clever but never fully broke the supplier problem.
