### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): A built a coherent, well-limited framework (work-only scope, no off-hours capture, human review, no-retaliation, equivalent alternatives) and defended the home-privacy rationale consistently. But B's rebuttal sequence did real damage: it caught that A's "opt-in" had quietly become role-based acceptance at hiring rather than an individual's ongoing choice within an existing job, which is what the motion's "opt-in consent" most naturally implies. B pressed this with the pressure questions, and A's answer (A4.1) confirmed rather than dispelled the shift, letting B credibly claim in Rebuttal 2 and Closing that PRO had retreated to something close to CON's own "neutral role-based rules" position. B also landed a sharper counter-example (slow, piecemeal data exfiltration via chat) that exposed a real limit of A's event-based flagging story, and its "unenforceable bias against refusers" point was never fully neutralized by A's no-retaliation answer, which only raises costs rather than closing the loophole. A's closing restated its vault/home framing clearly but did not directly re-establish that its model still satisfies individual opt-in as commonly understood, leaving B's reframing as the last clear, structuring claim standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1–B2.2, sharpened by B3.1 and answered unsatisfyingly in A4.1, where CON caught PRO quietly converting "individual opt-in" into "role-based mandatory terms accepted at hiring," then used that admission in Rebuttal 2 and Closing to argue PRO had essentially conceded the motion's core requirement.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by cleanly defending the practicality of strict limits and forcing Side A to walk back its opposition to them. Side A's late strategy relied entirely on accusing Side B of abandoning the motion's 'opt-in' requirement, but Side B consistently defended a valid interpretation of opt-in as an upfront choice between equivalent roles.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized Side A's "slow copying" example by showing how strict, targeted limits would still flag data anomalies without requiring continuous webcams, which forced Side A to concede in its next speech that those guardrails were reasonable.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): B won by keeping a coherent path for the motion: individual consent at a real decision point (disclosed monitored role with a genuine equivalent alternative and no retaliation) plus workable strict limits that separate targeted system/security pattern monitoring from bedroom webcam, emotion scoring, and blanket keylogging. A landed sharp pressure on two-tier stigma and the soft bias no-retaliation cannot police, and the bait-and-switch attack was the clearest hit of the debate, but A overclaimed total concession, underweighted B’s security/narrow-tool answers, and never showed why remote work must collapse once limits are role-justified rather than continuous home surveillance. On the full exchange, B answered the live clashes more cleanly and left the easier reason to affirm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s reply to the accountant/slow-exfiltration line (esp. B2.4–B2.5 and B4.3–B4.4): event/pattern limits inside sensitive systems catch the real risk without requiring continuous video, attention scoring, or personal keystroke capture of all remote workers.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B built a tighter logical structure and exploited a genuine contradiction in A's case: A called employer-imposed "take it or leave it" choices "extortion" when framed as pre-hire bargaining, but then defended essentially the same structure (reassignment/termination for refusing monitoring) as a "fair contract" once pressed on mid-employment scenarios. B's R1 move ("Notice what has just happened to the proposition") precisely caught A collapsing "opt-in" into mere disclosed terms, and B's closing drove this home by quoting A's own language back ("Ask what opt-in actually buys a worker"). A never resolved this tension, instead repeating the moral framing (rent, leverage, decency) without closing the logical gap. B's case on inflexibility of "strict limits" (new clients, breach investigations, regulatory shifts) was also well-illustrated and largely unanswered except by A conceding ad hoc exceptions ("temporary targeted monitoring... immediate notice"), which B then turned into a further contradiction (notice could tip off an investigated insider). A's rhetoric was vivid and emotionally forceful, but B's rebuttals did more actual argumentative work exposing incoherence, giving B the cleaner path to victory despite a clipped final rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 point [B2.1]-[B2.3], catching A's shift from a genuine opt-in veto to mere pre-hire disclosure, then showing the resulting dilemma (either consent is cosmetic or it creates unworkable security holes) — later reinforced when B quotes A's own "extortion" language against A's "fair contract" defense of reassignment in Rebuttal 2 and Closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by exposing a fatal contradiction in Side B's case: if an employer can legally fire or refuse to hire a worker who declines monitoring, "opt-in consent" is no different from the standard employment notice Side A defended. Side A effectively proved the proposition's unique burdens were either an empty ritual or a dangerous security blind spot.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which brilliantly weaponized Side B's admission that workers who refuse monitoring can be reassigned or fired, pointing out this was the exact "extortion" Side B originally condemned.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): A won by forcing a live dilemma B never escaped: separate opt-in either reduces to ordinary disclosed take-it-or-leave-it employment terms, or else creates security holes and unworkable rigid caps. B’s strongest privacy rhetoric (home surveillance, surprise monitoring) largely argued notice against secrecy, which A already conceded; once B admitted refusers can be refused hire/reassigned/terminated, A showed the promised “consent” is mostly ceremony plus costly rigidity. A’s later clip was a real but secondary execution cost that did not undo the comparative collapse.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s bank/funds-handler dilemma and follow-through after B’s reassignment answer: if refusal costs the role, B’s earlier ‘extortion’ charge applies to B’s own regime; if it does not, security is incomplete.
