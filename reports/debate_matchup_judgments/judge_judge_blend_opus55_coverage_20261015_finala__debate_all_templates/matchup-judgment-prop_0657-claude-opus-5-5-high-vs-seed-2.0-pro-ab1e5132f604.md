### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by exposing PRO's unresolved dilemma: the concrete items B proposed sharing were either indicators or vague reliability assurances, not sensitive methods. If genuinely useful methods are disclosed, A showed plausible irreversible risks from adversary adaptation, leaks, and democratic backsliding. B relied heavily on unsupported and internally inconsistent claims that every warning was discarded, despite initially saying no warnings occurred. A's two clipped turns were an execution cost, but the decisive reasoning remained visible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response to B's pressure was decisive: metadata fingerprints are indicators, while saying a verified channel produced them is merely another trust-us assurance. This undermined B's claim that limited method disclosure was both necessary for credibility and safe from compromise.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON wins by collapsing PRO's central distinction. PRO could never define a stable category of "limited method sharing" that was neither (1) an indicator/output—which democracies already share and CON supports—nor (2) a source compromise that burns collection capabilities and leaks to adversaries. PRO's best concrete examples ("metadata fingerprints" and "verified collection channel") were shown by CON to be either plain indicators or unverifiable trust claims that fail PRO's own test for why warnings are discarded. CON also turned PRO's cyber-analogy against them, showing those systems succeed precisely by guarding methods. PRO's repeated empirical claims about discarded warnings lacked any checkable grounding, and their procedural answer to authoritarian drift ("revocation") was undermined by CON's unrebutted point that shared knowledge cannot be revoked. PRO's rhetoric was polished, but their path to victory depended on a third category of sharing that they never coherently named, let alone defended.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's collapse of PRO's "limited method sharing" into a dilemma: either the shared material is an indicator (already available without the motion) or it is an unverifiable credibility assertion like "this came from a verified channel" (which fails PRO's own complaint that context-free warnings are discarded). This exchange, running from A2 through A5, gutted PRO's causal mechanism and left their weighing resting on a false premise.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON wins the central definitional clash. PRO never establishes a third category of "limited method sharing" that is both a method and safe: its examples (metadata fingerprints, verified channel claim) are indicators or "trust us" assertions that CON's model already provides. PRO's core empirical claim that indicator-only warnings were universally discarded is repeatedly asserted without checkable support, while CON's irreversibility, leak-surface, and illiberal-inheritance risks go largely unanswered. Pressure Qs from A expose the dilemma; answers in B4 fail it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R1/R2 dilemma – showing B2.4's three fingerprints are indicators not methods, and B4.1's reliability claim fails B's own trust test – collapses PRO's necessity claim and leaves the motion's costly clause with no distinct benefit.
