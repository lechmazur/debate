### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): The central clash was whether opt-in consent is a meaningful safeguard or a structural hole. A's strongest line—that making consent a "prerequisite for the role" simply relabels employment coercion as consent, and that one refuser breaks shared audit trails affecting coworkers/customers who never consented—was raised early (A2.1-A2.2) and never squarely answered. B's rebuttal (B4.1) just reasserted that role-based consent is "transparent and proportional" without addressing the take-it-or-leave-it critique or the third-party harm problem. A also pressed that B's own concessions (allowing "narrow, event-driven security" for payment approvals) undercut B's blanket-veto model, forcing B toward A's framework of purpose-bound, event-tied monitoring rather than a clean opt-in standard. B's strongest point—that continuous home video is a uniquely severe intrusion exceeding any security need, and that zero-trust architecture plus logs suffice—was met by A with the plausible counter that logs show credential use but not conduct, and that video/behavioral correlation helps establish who did what. This exchange was closer, but A's framing (safeguards need not be perfect to add deterrent value) was reasonably argued and not fully rebutted by B beyond repeating the "surveillance theater" framing. On pressure questions, A's Q1 (does refusal costing the job make consent illusory?) exposed a real tension B never resolved cleanly; B's Q1 to A about phones out of frame was answered adequately by A (safeguards need not be perfect). Overall A built a tighter, more responsive case that adapted to B's concessions and exploited the central contradiction in opt-in-as-job-condition, while B's closing largely restated its opening framing without resolving the third-party/coercion rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1's "opt-in is either theatre or a security hole" line, pressed again in A5.2, exploited B's concession that refusal can cost the assignment—exposing that B's opt-in model collapses into the same take-it-or-leave-it coercion B criticized in standard contracts, a point B never cleanly answered.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won by exposing the central contradiction in opt-in consent: if monitoring is mandatory for a role, consent is largely nominal; if refusal is costless, security gaps remain. A also distinguished opposition to an employee veto from opposition to privacy safeguards, weakening B's repeated portrayal of the alternative as unrestricted home surveillance. B made a compelling privacy case but never fully answered A's third-party security and audit-trail concerns, and occasionally mischaracterized A's position while citing nonexistent reference labels.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1 and A4.3 were decisive: Side A showed that B's role-based opt-in either recreates the employment coercion B condemns or leaves the riskiest conduct unmonitored. B's targeted-monitoring exception consequently conceded much of A's practical case without resolving the consent dilemma.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively dismantling Side A's core mechanism of "opt-in consent." Side B effectively demonstrates that if opt-in is truly voluntary, it creates a massive security loophole (the "fraudster veto"), but if it is a prerequisite for a specific role, it collapses into the exact same "take-it-or-leave-it" employment coercion that Side A condemns. Side A fails to adequately resolve this contradiction in its later speeches. Furthermore, Side B successfully reframes the debate around third-party harm (customers and coworkers affected by breaches) and clarifies that opposing rigid, universal caps and an individual veto does not mean opposing all privacy safeguards. Side B's advocacy for proportionate, purpose-bound rules (blurring, work-hour limits) absorbs Side A's valid privacy concerns while maintaining necessary security, making Side B's path to victory much cleaner and more practical.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2 dismantling the "opt-in" mechanism by showing it either creates a security hole (if truly optional) or collapses into the same "take-it-or-leave-it" employment coercion Side A condemns (if required for the role).

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): B built a durable organizing distinction—security of data/access points versus surveillance of the person/home—and applied it consistently to defuse A's insider-threat, identity-verification, and "paid for time" arguments with concrete, less invasive alternatives (managed devices, re-authentication, access logs, queue metrics). B's opening also operationalized "opt-in plus strict limits" with specific criteria, giving the motion real content to defend. A landed real pressure, especially the "fatal contradiction" gotcha (refusing monitoring forces on-site relocation, so consent isn't truly voluntary) and the smartphone-photographing/unauthorized-third-party scenario, both of which exposed soft spots B never fully closed in its final speeches. But A's closing leaned on repeating the contradiction rhetorically rather than showing why B's "on-site alternative preserves employment, not just privilege" response fails under the motion as written, while B's core proportionality framework survived the exchange largely intact. The result is a clear but competitive win for B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The pressure-question exchange on A1 is decisive: A's question about forced on-site reassignment for refusing monitoring was the sharpest moment of the debate, and while B's "A1: No... opt-in governs the tool, not entitlement to the assignment" answer was substantively defensible, B never fully drove home why that isn't coercive, letting A repeat the "fatal contradiction" charge unchallenged into the closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by consistently separating targeted security controls from continuous surveillance and showing that identity, access, and availability can be verified through less intrusive measures. Side A raised a serious residual risk involving unauthorized viewers and screen photography, but overstated continuous AI monitoring as uniquely necessary and never adequately weighed its privacy, error, and data-collection costs. The on-site alternative did not destroy opt-in consent: refusal could preserve employment without authorizing home surveillance, even if it did not guarantee the same remote assignment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s A1/A2 response was decisive: it directly answered the security questions with point-of-risk authentication, managed devices, access controls, and audits, then explained why none inherently requires capturing every keystroke or continuously filming the home. Side A’s consent-contradiction attack was sharp but treated loss of a particular remote arrangement as equivalent to forced surveillance.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a decisive distinction between securing company data and surveilling the worker's home. Side A effectively neutralizes Side B's security concerns by offering targeted, less intrusive alternatives while exposing the disproportionate privacy harms of continuous AI monitoring. Side B's primary offensive thrust—that allowing employers to revoke remote status for refusing monitoring destroys the opt-in premise—is a semantic trap that Side A successfully navigates by clarifying that consent applies to the surveillance itself, not an unconditional right to work remotely. Side A's rhetorical framing is superior, crystallizing the stakes around bodily privacy and proportional management.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantle Side B's semantic trap regarding remote work revocation by clarifying that an on-site alternative respects the privacy line, and sharply distinguish between locking the company vault and demanding surrender of the home.
