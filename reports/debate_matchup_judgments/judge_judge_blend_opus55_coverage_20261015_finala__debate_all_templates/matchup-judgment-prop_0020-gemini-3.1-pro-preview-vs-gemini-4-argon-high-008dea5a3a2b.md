### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B adapted more effectively across rounds. B's opening preemptively neutralized A's devaluation and Denmark arguments (pegs show the value of stability, not an "emergency exit"; competitive devaluation inside a borderless Single Market is beggar-thy-neighbor). Crucially, B turned A's own flagship example against it: the one country that actually left the EU, the UK, never adopted the euro, which directly undercuts A's claim that the currency is what fractures solidarity. A's rebuttal to this ("hostage situation") was rhetorically vivid but conceded the underlying mechanism rather than refuting it, and A never squarely addressed B's point about 1990s ERM-style speculative attacks on adjustable pegs or why major industrial economies devaluing against each other wouldn't trigger the trade retaliation B described. B also successfully reframed the debate around the motion's "today" language, using the pandemic/energy-shock response and post-crisis institutional buildup (banking union, recovery funds) as live, current evidence, while A's case leaned heavily on repeating the straitjacket/hostage metaphor without adding new refutation in later rounds. Both sides answered pressure questions with proper A1/A2 formatting, but B's answers did more direct work dismantling A's framing, while A's answers largely restated prior claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's turn of the UK/Brexit example (the only country to leave kept its own currency) against A's Denmark/Sweden argument, combined with A's "hostage situation" reply that conceded the binding mechanism rather than refuting its logic.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won by better upholding the motion's temporal focus on "today": it showed the euro forced durable institutional muscle (joint recovery funds, banking union) that shielded members from recent global shocks, and it turned Brexit into evidence that monetary independence enables fragmentation rather than cohesion. Side B remained anchored in the 2010-2012 debt crisis without adequately explaining why Eurozone members voluntarily built fiscal solidarity or addressing A's claim that Southern economies now outgrow Northern ones.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over whether monetary independence prevents or enables fragmentation, where A turned B's Brexit point into proof that easy exits undermine unity, and B's potent "hostage" rejoinder failed to explain why trapped states would voluntarily create joint recovery funds.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): CON won the present-day comparison. PRO's core lived in the 2010-12 debt crisis – loss of devaluation, one-size monetary policy, creditor/debtor resentment – but CON showed pre-euro volatility threatened the deeply integrated Single Market, adjustable pegs are speculative targets, and recent shocks prove the case: ECB firepower and joint funds shielded Southern Europe. The UK-only-leaver point decisively undercut PRO's populism claim, and PRO never answered CON's update that Southern economies are now outgrowing Germany. PRO's hostage metaphor was vivid but CON turned it into a strength – easy exit enables fragmentation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.5/B4.1 - UK left without euro while Eurozone populists dropped anti-euro platforms because voters value stability. This directly neutralized PRO's strongest political-fracture argument and turned PRO's admission that UK could leave because it kept its currency into evidence that the euro binds the EU together.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A built a more concrete, historically grounded case: the repeated use of real-world floating-currency examples (Poland, Sweden, and especially the UK) during the 2008 crisis directly undercut B's central claim that major-economy devaluation would have shattered the Single Market through retaliatory protectionism. A's pressure questions exploited this gap precisely, and B's answer (dismissing the UK as "an isolated financial hub, not a continental manufacturing competitor") was an assertion rather than a mechanism, leaving the counterexample largely unrebutted. A also turned B's own explanation of Spain's crisis (housing bubble fueled by cheap foreign capital) back against B by tying it to the euro's single interest rate, strengthening its causal story rather than conceding ground. B's case on reserve-currency geopolitical leverage and the risks of competitive devaluation was coherent and reasonably argued, but remained more theoretical and was answered adequately by A's points on market-size-driven leverage and continued sanctions compliance despite the euro. Across rounds, A progressively sharpened its comparison and closed with a vivid, clarifying frame ("breaking a runner's legs and praising the cast"), while B's closing largely reasserted prior claims without resolving the unanswered UK/Poland/Sweden pattern.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 use of Poland, Sweden, and the UK as real floating-currency examples during the 2008 crisis, directly refuting B's claim that major-economy devaluation would have destroyed the Single Market — a point B never fully neutralized despite later attempts to distinguish the UK as a "financial hub."
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by grounding its case in concrete historical counterexamples that directly undermined Side A's central counterfactual threat. Poland, Sweden, and the UK depreciated during the 2008 crisis without triggering Single Market collapse or protectionist spirals, making Side A's warning that floating currencies would destroy the union speculative and empirically unsupported. Side B's pressure questions exposed this gap effectively, forcing Side A into a weak distinction that the UK was merely a "financial hub." Side B also landed the sharper historical point about Spain entering 2008 with budget surpluses yet suffering a devastating bond panic precisely because the euro removed its central bank backstop—a point Side A never fully neutralized. Side A's reserve-currency and dollar-sovereignty arguments were plausible but remained abstract compared to Side B's realized harms, and Side B neatly deflated them by noting European trade settled without dollar dominance before 1999. Across the exchange, Side B progressively narrowed the clash to live empirical comparisons, while Side A repeatedly relied on hypothetical doom scenarios that the historical record contradicted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's "competitive devaluation would destroy the Single Market" thesis by pointing to the UK, Poland, and Sweden in 2008, paired with the devastating pressure question asking why Britain's sharp depreciation did not trigger the retaliatory barriers Side A claimed were inevitable. This exchange exposed Side A's core argument as counterfactual speculation and forced it into unpersuasive special pleading.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A (PRO) won the central clashes. Its empirical rebuttal that Poland, Sweden, and the UK devalued sharply in 2008 without triggering treaty retaliation or collapse directly undercut CON's core claim that competitive devaluation would destroy the Single Market. Its Spain example - low public debt yet bond panic due to lack of lender-of-last-resort - exposed a contradiction in CON's "unchecked borrowing" story, which CON never squarely answered. A also neutralized the reserve-currency/sovereignty argument with pre-1999 settlement and secondary-sanctions point, and linked Eurozone unemployment to Brexit pressures. B relied on hypothetical core-wide devaluation causing non-tariff retaliation and inflation warnings without answering the live counterexamples.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's use of non-euro members in 2008 plus Spain's pre-crisis surpluses to prove the Euro created, not prevented, sovereign panic and that the Single Market survived devaluations - A2/A4 answering B's Qs.
