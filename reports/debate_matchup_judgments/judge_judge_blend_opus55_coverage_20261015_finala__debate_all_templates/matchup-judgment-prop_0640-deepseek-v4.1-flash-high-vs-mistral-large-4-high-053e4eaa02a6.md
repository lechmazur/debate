### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by distinguishing ordinary conduct regulation from concentrated residual control over renewal, sale, and access. Side B offered strong category-mismatch and enforceability objections, but overstated how fully zoning, contract law, and tenant rules constrain owner exit, and treated limited ownership as though it prohibited institutional investment altogether.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange was its answer to the conduct-regulation alternative: zoning may constrain conversion, but it does not generally guarantee lease renewal or prevent portfolio-driven sale and concentration. Side B's legal response addressed some land uses but did not fully neutralize that residual-control distinction.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO won a competitive debate by making residual control the decisive clash: leases, zoning, and practice rules govern the relationship while it lasts, but title still decides who can sell, terminate, or switch without the farmer’s consent. CON’s liquidity, absentee-family, and enforceability points landed, yet never fully neutralized that structural gap or showed conduct tools as complete substitutes rather than complements. PRO also answered threshold/definition pressure cleanly enough, while CON’s A1 overclaimed how land-use law binds owner exit. The cleaner path to victory is PRO’s ownership-as-option frame plus complement-not-substitute weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO Q1 on what still binds an owner who sells/terminates/switches without farmer consent, carried through residual-control weighing in Rebuttal 2 and Closing.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully distinguishing between conduct regulation (which governs active farming practices) and residual control (the owner's ultimate right to sell, terminate leases, or exit). Side A's framing of "title as the option on the land's future" effectively neutralized Side B's primary defense that zoning and lease rules could substitute for an ownership cap. Side A also handled the evasion and definitional pressure questions cleanly, whereas Side B's reliance on zoning to protect tenant farmers from lease non-renewal was a logical gap that Side A expertly exploited.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they crystallized the "residual control" and "exit problem" clash, exposing that Side B's conduct regulations and zoning laws do not protect a specific farmer when an institutional owner simply chooses not to renew a lease or sells the land to another absentee buyer.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing that the proposed cap regulates title rather than the contractual control and land-use practices that generate the alleged harms. Side A established a plausible concern about financialization but never adequately answered how the cap prevents control through debt, leases, minority stakes, or management contracts. Its defense of wealthy local consolidation also exposed the weakness of treating local ownership as equivalent to stewardship. Side B offered the cleaner comparison: direct tenancy, conservation, access, and land-use rules bind conduct regardless of ownership while avoiding succession and buyer-pool harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response that “local” is not synonymous with “steward” was decisive. It turned Side A’s acceptance of wealthy neighboring consolidators against the proposition and reinforced that owner identity is a weaker proxy for resilience than enforceable rules governing actual land use and control.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): A (CON) won by keeping the decisive clash on mechanism: a title cap does not reliably change who controls farming, can push influence into less transparent contracts, and risks harming succession while leaving use-harms intact. B had a vivid financialization story and strong structural rhetoric, but never closed the gap between deed ownership and actual control/resilience. A’s alternatives-vs-label comparison stayed live and easier to vote on after full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s sustained attack on “control follows title” (esp. A2/A4), turning B’s concession that the cap need not stop leases/debt/contracts into proof the motion can leave (or obscure) the real harm.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively dismantling Side A's core mechanism. Side A argued that capping title would stop institutional control, but Side B persuasively demonstrated that in modern agriculture, control travels through debt, contracts, and leases, meaning a title cap is easily evaded and pushes control into less accountable private agreements. Side B also effectively highlighted the collateral damage to rural communities (harming retiring farmers' pensions and young farmers' collateral) and argued convincingly that direct land-use and tenancy regulations are a more robust solution than targeting a specific class of owners. Side A's responses relied on the assumption that "control follows title" and that local buyers are inherently better stewards, which Side B successfully challenged.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal to Side A's "control follows title" claim, pointing out that operational control routinely travels through debt covenants, offtake contracts, and management agreements, and that a local family can hold the deed while a corporate integrator makes every real decision. This effectively neutralized Side A's primary defense of the cap's mechanism.
