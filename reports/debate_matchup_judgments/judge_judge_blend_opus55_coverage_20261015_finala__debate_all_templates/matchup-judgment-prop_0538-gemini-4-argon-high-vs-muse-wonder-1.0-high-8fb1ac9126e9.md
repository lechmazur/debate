### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases, but A controlled the decisive clash on sequencing more effectively. A's pressure questions exposed a genuine tension in B's position (deprioritize raw exports vs. "demand processing investment"/"force" rents into industry), and B's answer that this is merely "incremental bargaining" rather than a priority shift never fully dissolved the tension—B's own language ("force," "demand") sounds like exactly the prioritization A calls for. A also landed an unanswered structural point (tariff escalation blocking African manufactured exports to rich markets), which B never directly rebutted, weakening B's "earn from the world first" model. B's strongest counter—that East Asia/Morocco industrialized by selling to rich markets, not regional neighbors—was a sharp distinction that A's rebuttal only partially addressed, but it wasn't enough to offset A's cleaner closing narrative ("sixty years of waiting never arrives") and the unresolved contradiction B had to spend much of Rebuttal 2 defusing. The debate was close and both sides argued with discipline and no fake precision, but A ended with the more coherent, harder-to-dismiss burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Questions (A3.2) forcing B to reconcile "processing destroys value" (B2.1) with "demand processing investment" (B2.5) was the most decisive exchange; B's answer in A2 (B4.3) clarified but did not fully erase the appearance of conceding A's core sequencing claim.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side A won the sequencing and feasibility clash. It supported manufacturing while distinguishing competitive, incremental processing from prematurely privileging higher-cost regional production. Side B persuasively identified commodity dependence as a trap, but never adequately resolved the hard-currency, production-cost, purchasing-power, and consumer-price pressures. Its assertion that regional integration would itself overcome these constraints was weaker than A's earn-and-invest alternative, and its closing also used nonexistent reference IDs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal decisively answered the alleged contradiction: using resource rents to build competitive industry is compatible with rejecting premature processing mandates or regional-market priority. It then showed why tariffs and local-production conditions do not by themselves solve power, credit, logistics, governance, or total-cost disadvantages.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): A won by making priority mean scarce-resource sequencing and keeping the live clash on hard currency, effective demand, and premature-processing losses. B’s 60-year trap and import-substitution reframe were strong, but never fully solved how regional low-income demand and higher unit costs replace the earner during transition; A’s answers and closings kept that burden decisive without denying industrialization as the goal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 distinction that incremental bargaining for competitive processing is not the same as demoting raw exports for sheltered regional sales, which strands both revenue and factories.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides built a coherent, well-structured case, and the debate stayed tightly focused on the real crux: can Africa afford to redirect investment toward regional manufacturing without losing the hard-currency base extraction provides. B's strongest and most consistent weapon was the balance-of-payments/currency-mismatch argument, repeated effectively across rounds. But A directly answered it in A4.1-A4.2 by proposing a dual-track model (existing exports keep earning dollars; only *new* investment shifts toward import-substituting production that reduces the recurring import bill for fuel, fertilizer, food, and medicine), and reframed the net foreign-exchange calculus (earnings minus leakage) in a way B never fully dismantled—B kept reasserting "extraction needs continuous capital" without engaging why diverting only *incremental* investment collapses existing output, which is the load-bearing claim. A's closing crystallized this cleanly: current dependence is fragile to price shocks and buyer switching, and 'leverage' from scarcity is brittle, whereas B's closing mostly repeated the BoP point without fresh engagement on the switching/stranded-leverage argument A raised in Q2/A2. The Pressure Questions exchange was roughly even, but A's answers (A4.1-A4.3) were slightly more responsive and concrete (sector sequencing: fertilizer, cement, cloth) than B's somewhat abstract answer about tying licenses to infrastructure, which doesn't resolve how currency-constrained states enforce that leverage during a price downturn — the very scenario A's Q2 highlighted. Overall A's argument gives a clearer, more resolved path through the clash; B's case, while strong and consistent, repeats its core mechanism without ever truly closing the loop A opened about crash-vulnerability and buyer substitution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A4.1/A4.2 response to B's BoP pressure question, proposing that existing exports continue funding import bills while new investment targets import-substitution to shrink the recurring currency drain, was the most decisive exchange — it directly answered B's strongest and most repeated attack line, and B's closing never fully neutralized it, instead repeating the same mismatch claim without addressing the dual-track structure.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A created serious pressure around hard currency, imported industrial inputs, and premature white elephants, but repeatedly treated prioritization as abandoning extraction. Side B directly clarified that existing exports continue while marginal investment shifts toward basic processing, import substitution, and diversified regional demand. That preserved a plausible financing bridge while better answering the proposition's central problem: enduring vulnerability to commodity prices and distant buyers. Side B's clipped first rebuttal was an execution cost, but not enough to erase its stronger overall comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: it answered the currency-mismatch challenge by contrasting largely upfront machinery costs with recurring imports of essentials, then proposed beginning with simpler sectors using existing local inputs rather than immediately attempting complex battery production. This materially weakened Side A's portrayal of the proposal as an abrupt, all-or-nothing industrial gamble.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): B won a competitive debate by owning the proposition as written: prioritization means re-aiming new roads, credit, and power at processing and regional essentials without shutting existing exports. A’s hard-currency/sequencing case and dual-use-infrastructure answers were sharp and consistent, but B’s trap, recurring-import leak, and “bridge never ends” weighing better showed why raw-export-first dependence keeps regenerating itself and fails under crash or buyer substitution. B also answered pressure more cleanly on mechanism and durable leverage; A’s continuous-upgrade attack landed but did not erase B’s resilience framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s R2 “if every new dollar must first upgrade extraction, the bridge never ends,” flipping A’s sequencing contradiction while answering the FX and white-elephant pressures with recurring-vs-upfront costs and simple-start benchmarks.
