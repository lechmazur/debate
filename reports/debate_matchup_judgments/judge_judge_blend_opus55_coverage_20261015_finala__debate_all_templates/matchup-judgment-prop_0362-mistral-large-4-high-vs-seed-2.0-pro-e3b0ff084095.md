  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1/A4.3 – No ambush, transparent posted prices you can lock weeks early; an empty seat does not host culture, turning B's budget-anxiety/volatility route into a choice argument.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side B wins by decisively dismantling Side A's core mechanism. While Side A offered highly polished rhetoric and a strong conceptual frame around "segregation by schedule," Side B's logical rebuttals and grounded reality checks made for the more convincing path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where they dismantled the "ambush/auction" framing by clarifying that dynamic pricing for movies involves posted, transparent prices rather than real-time hidden bidding, effectively collapsing Side A's volatility argument.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): PRO (B) built the cleaner path on the motion as written: aggressive last-minute surge on identical standard seats erodes the casual default habit more than it preserves venues. CON's solvency/cross-subsidy case was potent but stayed largely theoretical; PRO repeatedly forced the practice gap (no matinee cuts, only spontaneity penalties) and kept the habit-break mechanism decisive after the exchange. CON answered survival well but never fully neutralized the live "it guts the monthly regulars without the promised access gains" route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO Rebuttal 1 + pressure Q1/A1 sequence exposing the bait-and-switch: CON defended progressive discounts while the motion targets surge penalties that did not actually lower off-peak standard seats.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): PRO (B) kept the debate focused on the motion's aggressive same-seat surge and made its decisive test whether surge funds cheaper access. CON (A) relied on theoretical yield-management solvency but conceded under pressure that no chain actually cut matinee prices, reframing it as preventing hikes. B answered the control-group challenge with a targeted mechanism (flat opening-weekend vs midweek collapse) and distinguished IMAX premiums from same-chair variance, exposing CON's airline analogy. CON never showed closures slowed or total attendance rose, leaving B's mass-habit erosion as the only live empirical route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2 / Pressure Q1: bait-and-switch identification that surge and discount are presented as inseparable but chains added surge without cutting matinees, which CON never refuted with evidence and had to reframe in A4.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully dismantling Side B's core economic defense. Side B argued that aggressive surge pricing cross-subsidizes cheap off-peak tickets, preserving access and theater solvency. Side A effectively pointed out that in reality, chains implemented surge pricing without lowering matinee prices, rendering the cross-subsidy purely theoretical. Furthermore, Side A's distinction between "planning the outing" and "planning the ticket purchase" brilliantly defended the spontaneity of the mass cultural habit against Side B's claim that moviegoing is always a heavily planned event. Side A kept the focus tightly on the specific harms of aggressive dynamic pricing, while Side B struggled to defend the real-world outcomes of the policy and relied on an unfalsifiable counterfactual about prevented price hikes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they answered pressure questions by distinguishing between planning an outing versus planning a ticket purchase, and empirically dismantled the cross-subsidy argument by pointing out that general streaming declines hit all time slots evenly, whereas the midweek attendance drop was specific to the pricing change.
