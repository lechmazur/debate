### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases without fake sourcing, but B's rebuttals did more real damage. B's key pressure question (Q1) forced A to admit it had no concrete evidence of harm from capped, non-invasive doses in screened adults, exposing that A's strongest examples (the sleep-deprived teenager, the hacked/shared headset) were already excluded by B's proportional framework of age gates, screening, and tamper-resistant design. A never closed that evidentiary gap, instead reasserting precaution ("persistence is the risk") without engaging why proportional regulation couldn't manage it. B also neutralized the "black market" and "hacking" arguments by noting medical channels suffer the same diversion and tampering problems A relies on as a contrast. A's framing (brain as agency, 'remote control for minds') was vivid and rhetorically strong, and its closing crystallized the stakes memorably, but it rested on analogy and precaution rather than rebutting B's narrowed, evidence-demanding critique. B's second rebuttal was clipped for length, which is a real execution cost, but the substance delivered before the cut still answered the live clash cleanly and in the required A1/A2 format.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Questions Q1 ("What specific evidence shows capped, non-invasive consumer doses cause lasting harmful rewiring...") combined with Rebuttal 2's point that A's own examples (teenager, hacked/shared device) were already excluded by B's proportional model, leaving A's core harm claim for screened adults unsupported.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully distinguishing direct neural stimulation from Side B's analogies (caffeine, learning) by explaining that stimulation bypasses the body's sensory and homeostatic brakes. Side A also effectively proved that consumer safeguards like click-through prompts are insufficient for screening hidden neurological risks, a point Side B struggled to overcome since they conceded vulnerable users should be excluded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's argument in A4.1 that learning and traditional stimulants are filtered through the senses and homeostatic brakes, whereas BCI stimulation directly alters excitability, devastating B's core analogies.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by forcing the live burden: PRO never showed that capped, non-invasive, screened adult use produces lasting harmful rewiring rather than temporary shifts, so medical monopoly overreaches relative to proportional caps, age/screen lockouts, and direct bans on teen marketing/coercion. B’s uniqueness and “edits the chooser” framing was vivid and carried opening force, but repeatedly rested on vulnerable/shared/hacked cases A already excluded, while A better handled black-market and diversion parity. A’s clipped R2 was a real execution cost yet left enough A1/A2 and comparative spine to decide the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R1–R2 sequence: plasticity/learning ≠ injury, caffeine-style proportionality, then showing B’s teen/hidden-risk example is already screened out under CON’s consumer rules rather than justifying clinic-only for every adult.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly without fake sourcing, but A's central thesis — that a write-capable device compromises the very faculty supposed to judge its own safety — survived the debate largely intact. B's best weapons were the coffee/alcohol/contact-sports analogies and the equity argument (medical-only creates a concierge tier plus DIY underground), and these landed real damage, especially the sharp catch that A's "regulators can set power/placement/duration rules" line sounded like a concession to tiered consumer regulation. But A never let the precision/efficacy dilemma go (if diffuse, why sell it; if potent, why trust a questionnaire), and its rebuttal that session logs and check-ins cannot interpret delayed mania or sleep collapse as the user's own experience was never squarely answered — B's "logs plus liability" response is an assertion more than a refutation. A also answered the pressure question on where the medical line sits with a genuinely distinguishing anatomical criterion (cortex-targeting vs. peripheral nerve), which held up better under scrutiny than B's power-ceiling proposal did against A's heterogeneity point. A's closing, while it didn't explicitly rebut B's "conceded the model" gotcha, still crystallized the decisive asymmetry argument more memorably and left B's equity case as a real but secondary consideration rather than a refutation of the core safety logic.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's dilemma in Rebuttal 2/Closing — "either stimulation is too diffuse to matter, or it meaningfully shifts mood/sleep and is therefore unpredictable exposure a session log cannot interpret" — was the most damaging unresolved thrust, since B's answer (logs, check-ins, liability) asserted detection capacity without showing how a self-reporting system catches harms the user attributes to stress.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A established a highly cohesive framework centered on the unique danger of a product that alters the user's judgment and is optimized for engagement. Side A effectively dismantled Side B's analogies to coffee and painkillers by highlighting the reprogrammable nature of BCIs. Side B made strong pragmatic points about DIY markets, but relied on a slight mischaracterization of Side A's regulatory model in the final rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal, which cleanly separated BCIs from Side B's analogies by pointing out that coffee metabolizes and fades, whereas BCIs deliver targeted, software-controlled current optimized for engagement.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): B won by keeping the decisive asymmetry live: a write-capable stimulator can alter the chooser who must police it, so consumer engagement incentives, delayed undetectable side effects, and heterogeneity cannot be handled by labels and power caps the way coffee or TENS can. A’s proportional-regulation and DIY/inequality path was sharp and forced B to define a clean medical-consumer line, but never fully neutralized the efficacy paradox or the follow-up/stop-button problem. B’s answers after pressure and closing crystallized the choice more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Q1/Q2 pressure (efficacy paradox + who detects late mania) and the R2 placebo-or-peril dilemma, which boxed A’s “diffuse like caffeine + logs” reply.
