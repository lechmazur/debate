### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): A built a disciplined, consistent distinction between "marginal expansions" of end-of-life care and meaningful treatments like hospice and palliative care, and crucially grounded that distinction in the motion's own text ("marginal expansions," not existing care). B's entire case rested on the claim that any reallocation would inevitably strip hospice and palliative funding, but this reading strains against the motion's explicit wording, and B never squarely rebutted A's repeated point that the motion itself supplies the guardrail. When pressed on the threshold question (what level of benefit justifies funding marginal end-of-life care over prevention), B essentially refused to engage, asserting that "no fixed number... can capture the unique value," which dodges the forced-choice logic of finite budgets rather than resolving it. A directly answered both pressure questions (A1/A2) with concrete proposals (targeted outreach, mobile screening) and a clear accessibility argument, while B's answers leaned on unverified assertions ("every real-world instance," "disproportionately used by wealthy, insured patients") that function as brittle, unsupported precision. A also skillfully co-opted B's equity claim about who uses high-cost curative care, turning it into support for prioritizing accessible prevention. Overall A progressed the debate by sharpening and defending a clear decision rule, while B largely repeated its abandonment framing without closing the central threshold gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 answer to the threshold question (A4.4), showing that B's refusal to specify any threshold doesn't avoid rationing but simply defaults resources to patients already in tertiary care — a point B never adequately answered in its final closing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the motion’s live comparison sharp: finite next-pound trade-offs favor prevention once hospice/meaningful benefit are carved out from low-value terminal expansions. A forced and then exploited the unanswered threshold and opportunity-cost problem, answered B’s access/uptake pressure cleanly with outreach rather than passive programs, and neutralized the double-punishment equity story by showing tertiary expansions least reach the marginalized. B retained moral force on individual duty and implementation risk, but repeatedly collapsed hospice into the target and refused any workable weighing rule, so its route stayed more rhetorical than decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s wedding distinction (A2.1) plus A4 A1/A2 answers: meaningful functional time and hospice stay funded; refusal of a benefit threshold silently defaults scarce funds to tertiary patients already inside the system.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing a clear, defensible definition of the motion's target ("marginal expansions of high-cost end-of-life care") and successfully excluding meaningful palliative and hospice care from it. Side A repeatedly attacked the assumption that hospice would be cut, which Side B effectively neutralized by pointing to the text of the motion and explaining that comfort care is not a "marginal high-cost expansion." Furthermore, Side B successfully turned Side A's equity argument by noting that marginalized populations also lack access to high-cost tertiary ICU expansions, making targeted prevention the more equitable choice. Side A's refusal to provide any threshold for resource allocation in Rebuttal 2 was a critical evasion that Side B capitalized on in the closing to show that rejecting prioritization simply defaults resources to those already in tertiary beds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where it dismantled Side A's "wedding" example by distinguishing between meaningful functional life and burdensome prolonged ICU stays, and turned the equity argument by showing that high-cost tertiary expansions are also largely inaccessible to the marginalized.
