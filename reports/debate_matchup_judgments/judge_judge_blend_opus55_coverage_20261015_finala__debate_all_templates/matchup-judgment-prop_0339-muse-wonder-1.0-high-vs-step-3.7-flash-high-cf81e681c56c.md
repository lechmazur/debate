### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by exposing two unresolved design flaws: no demonstrated existing classification for “high-risk social” apps, and device-wide control concentrated in the primary owner. Side B repeatedly asserted that established frameworks precisely classify harmful apps while exempting support apps, but never substantiated or even named that category after direct pressure. Its answer that the household head controls the passcode strengthened A’s coercion argument. A then crystallized the dilemma effectively: profile-specific controls largely duplicate existing tools, while device-wide controls broaden gatekeeper power. B established genuine harms and a plausible usability benefit, but its implementation claims were too absolute and insufficiently grounded. A’s clipped opening was a minor execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both pressure questions directly, then used Side B’s own “primary registered owner” answer against it and highlighted B’s continued failure to identify the supposedly pre-existing “high-risk social” category.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won by keeping the live clash on undefined “high-risk social,” the simplicity/security trade-off, and shared-device coercion; A never named a real preexisting category and its “primary registered owner” answer crystallized B’s gatekeeping objection. B answered pressure cleanly and showed the mandate either duplicates existing controls or expands them into adult/device-wide blocks, while A’s usability-gap claim stayed abstract.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Rebuttal 2/closing turning A’s “primary registered owner / household head” answer into proof the toggle creates device-wide rule by the purchaser, not mere child protection.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively exposing the mechanical and social flaws in Side A's proposal. When pressed during the Pressure Questions round on who would control the toggle, Side A conceded that the "primary registered owner" or "household head" would hold the administrator passcode. Side B brilliantly weaponized this admission in their second rebuttal and closing to demonstrate that on a genuinely shared family device, this mandate concentrates gatekeeping power in one person, enabling coercion against other adults, grandparents, or vulnerable teens. Side B also effectively dismantled Side A's reliance on "pre-existing" app store categories, correctly pointing out that "high-risk social" is not a clean, objective classification and that legal penalties would cause risk-averse stores to over-block valuable support and health apps. Side A's responses were repetitive, relied on unsupported assertions about existing frameworks, and failed to solve the core trade-offs Side B highlighted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they took Side A's direct answer to the pressure question (that the "primary registered owner" holds the passcode) and showed how this explicitly creates the coercion and gatekeeping problem on a shared device, completely neutralizing Side A's defense.
