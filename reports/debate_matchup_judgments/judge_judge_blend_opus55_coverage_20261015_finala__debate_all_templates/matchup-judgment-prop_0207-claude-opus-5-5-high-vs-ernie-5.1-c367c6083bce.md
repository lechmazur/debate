### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by showing that even a broad flat transfer is more progressive than consumption-linked fuel support, then pairing that advantage with a clear sequencing rule: transfers arrive before prices rise. Side B’s concession that cash shifts substantially more support toward poor households weakened its defense of subsidies, while its closing largely reverted to assuming compensation must lag despite A’s explicit safeguard. B’s institutional-capacity concern remained meaningful, but its late exact thresholds and country statistics were unsupported, and the threshold ultimately functioned more as an implementation checklist than a reason to reject phased reform.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to the bureaucracy objection was decisive: if precise targeting is difficult, use a near-universal flat payment that still redistributes away from heavy fuel consumers; if targeting is feasible, narrow it further. Side B conceded the progressive shift and never established why an equal-budget transfer must cost more, leaving A’s dilemma intact.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A (PRO) won by showing that universal fuel subsidies are structurally regressive and that the transition risks Side B (CON) raised are manageable through sequencing, while the subsidy's harms are permanent. CON conceded that flat cash is more progressive [B4.1], leaving only transition risk in dispute. PRO turned CON's Egypt example into evidence for sequencing [A2.6, A4.1], absorbed CON's capacity threshold as an implementation checklist [A5.2], and deflated the "invisible poor" argument by showing they lack vehicles and thus receive almost nothing from cheap petrol [A5.4]. CON's institutional critique remained plausible but relied on overconfident specificity [B4.2] and could not recover from the concession that even a flat transfer redirects money from heavy consumers to the poor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 reframing of the exclusion-error critique: arguing that the universal fuel subsidy is itself "the largest exclusion error in the budget, built in by design" [A2.3]. This turned CON's strongest institutional argument into a point for PRO by demonstrating that the subsidy systematically excludes the poor in favor of those who consume the most fuel.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by establishing that universal fuel subsidies are inherently regressive and that even imperfect or near-universal cash transfers are more progressive because they do not scale with consumption. Side B effectively neutralized Side A's transition risks by proposing a strict sequencing rule (cash lands before prices rise) and brilliantly reframed Side A's capacity threshold in the closing as an implementation checklist rather than a reason to reject the motion. Side A's reliance on state capacity flaws was logically outmaneuvered by Side B's comparative weighing of the status quo's hidden exclusion errors.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's "flip the benchmark" in Rebuttal 1, arguing that universal fuel subsidies already commit massive exclusion errors against the poor (who consume little fuel), effectively dismantling Side A's claim that cash transfers are uniquely flawed for missing some recipients.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): B won by showing that universal price support does reach poor households indirectly through transport, food distribution, and cooking fuels, while targeted transfers can exclude informal workers and the near-poor. A never resolved that exclusion risk and conceded transfers would not automatically adjust during an oil shock, then contradicted that concession in closing. B also offered a clearer sequencing alternative: cut affluent leakage first and retain essential stabilization until cash delivery is proven.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B decisively answered A’s repeated claim that subsidies exclude the poor “by definition”: households receive lower kerosene, bus, and food-transport costs without needing to appear on a registry. B then used A’s admission that cash would not adjust immediately to undermine A’s claim of instant shock protection.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): B won by exposing the asymmetric risks of PRO's transition. CON's pressure questions revealed that PRO could not name an acceptable number of excluded families or an automatic shock-protection mechanism, and A's closing mischaracterized CON's differentiated fuel-floor alternative as a blanket SUV subsidy. CON successfully turned PRO's own 'capacity-building' language into a sequencing argument, showing the motion removes the universal floor before the registry is proven. B's rebuttals consistently crystallized the choice as reversible waste versus irreversible hunger, a frame A never neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal and closing turned PRO's concessions on capacity-building and shock-response lag into an asymmetric-risk frame: if CON is wrong, money leaks a little longer; if PRO is wrong, families go hungry through the next shock. This sharpened the decisive clash and was never fully answered.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A built a vastly superior case by exposing the practical flaws in Side B's cash transfer mechanism, particularly regarding state capacity, the indirect benefits of fuel subsidies on food and transport, and the legislative lag in adjusting cash transfers during sudden oil shocks. Side A's reframe on sequencing—removing leakage on premium fuels before removing the price floor on essentials—neutralized Side B's fiscal arguments while protecting the most vulnerable. Side B struggled to defend the economically inaccurate claim that universal subsidies miss the poor "by definition" and conceded the shock-response lag, leaving Side A with the decisive comparative burden: reversible fiscal waste versus irreversible exclusion and hunger. Side A's opening was slightly clipped, but its subsequent speeches were dominant.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's claim that universal subsidies exclude the poor "by definition" by pointing out the automatic, list-free protection of diesel and kerosene, and forced Side B to concede that the debate was ultimately about sequencing.
