### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that explicit consent and compensation can be negotiated up front for known uses while still establishing a universal baseline for performers without union or star leverage. Side A repeatedly treated the motion as requiring fresh, per-use approval, but that rigidity was not in the proposition and B directly offered production-wide riders. A's reliance on contracts and collective bargaining never adequately protected non-union actors or justified broad boilerplate rights, while its claims about hiring costs and synthetic replacement remained speculative.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange exposed the gap in A's bargaining solution: praising union-negotiated consent protections does not explain why non-union performers should lack the same baseline. B then answered the practical objection by allowing specific advance consent and a single dubbing rider, substantially weakening A's hostage and market-by-market scenarios.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): B won a competitive debate by keeping the live clash on biometric fabrication vs ordinary editing and on baseline protection for non-union/lesser-known performers. A’s two clipped turns and occasional circularity on scope were execution costs; B adapted more cleanly across rounds and left the clearer reason to affirm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4/B5 turn on A’s editing-within-hired-work claim: if AI synthesis is mere editing already paid for, a one-day hire can yield unpaid synthetic scenes; A’s “outside scope” answer never repaired the contradiction.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing a structural flaw in PRO's case: whenever PRO made the proposed rule practical—allowing upfront consent for specific uses, or single riders for international distribution—it effectively recreated the bargaining system CON defended. PRO's strongest moral appeals (biometric extraction, non-union vulnerability) were blunted by CON's scope-of-work distinction and the demonstration that the Hollywood strikes achieved the desired protections through collective bargaining, not rigid statute. CON's progression from rebuttal to closing consistently narrowed the debate to this core comparison, while PRO's closing failed to answer why the motion was necessary beyond what flexible contracting already delivers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's first rebuttal ([A2.1]–[A2.10]) was the most decisive exchange, particularly the argument that PRO's own example of the Hollywood strikes proved collective bargaining could deliver explicit AI consent and compensation, making a rigid legal mandate either redundant or harmful.
