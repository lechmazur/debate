### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that climate impact and industrial impact operate on different denominators: the affected vehicles can be marginal to global emissions yet decisive for Europe's auto ecosystem. A also answered the local-production objection by distinguishing partial dependency with European jobs, assets, and legal leverage from complete import dependence. B's solar case remained meaningful, but its claim that these two scales were contradictory was repeatedly asserted rather than established. A's repeated clipping was an execution cost that narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1 most decisively answered B's central contradiction charge: global climate effects can be relatively small while local industrial effects are existential. B repeated the same objection in B4.4 without addressing that denominator distinction.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by undermining the central promise of PRO’s case. PRO argued that treating Chinese EVs and clean-tech as an industrial threat would preserve European industry and avoid dependency, but CON showed that PRO’s own preferred policies—tariffs inducing relocation and local-content rules—still leave Europe reliant on Chinese capital, battery chemistry, and supply chains. Once CON collapsed the claim that the threat frame achieves true industrial independence, the debate turned on which form of managed dependency Europe should accept. CON successfully argued that the irreversible factor is the carbon budget and decarbonization speed, not manufacturing location, using solar as a concrete example where losing factories produced cheaper, faster clean-energy deployment. PRO’s clipped turns and inability to resolve the tension of calling EU demand marginal for climate while existential for industry further weakened its path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal (Turn 8), which demonstrated that PRO’s “relocation to Hungary” model still leaves Europe dependent on Chinese capital and chemistry—”dependency with a local postcode”—and exposed that PRO cannot treat the same EU vehicle market as both a rounding error for global climate and an existentially decisive industrial threat.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): PRO won the central irreversibility clash. CON's core "lose a factory and you can rebuild one" was exposed by A3 Q1; B4 A1 conceded no rebuilt clean-tech industry and pivoted to "was never the test." PRO's denominator distinction — tariff marginal globally for emissions but decisive locally for auto market — answered B's contradiction charge and kept its path live. B's strong "local postcode" dependency point was met with a plausible leverage distinction. Despite clipping costs, PRO gave the cleaner reason to treat Chinese clean-tech primarily as threat.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A3 Q1 demanding one rebuilt industry after Chinese overcapacity, and B4 A1 admitting none was rebuilt and that rebuilding "was never the test" — conceding the irreversibility premise PRO then crystallized in closing as "Europe won by losing the factory works only while Beijing chooses to keep supplying."

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): B won by distinguishing installed clean-tech capital from interruptible energy dependence, then showing that conditional investment can preserve leverage without making threat the primary frame. A never resolved the cumulative-carbon objection and relied on an uncertain future industrial revival to justify certain higher prices and delayed adoption. B also exposed that A's proposed joint ventures, localization, and market-access conditions largely resembled B's opportunity-first strategy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's final comparison was decisive: the solar precedent suggested protection imposed downstream costs without restoring manufacturing, while A explicitly accepted dearer EVs and slower adoption. B paired that with the point that later fleet turnover cannot erase carbon emitted during the delay.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built the stronger path to victory by grounding the debate in the concrete solar precedent: years of duties and price floors failed to revive European manufacturing while slowing deployment and costing emissions. PRO (Side B) never overcame this empirical counter; claiming the tariffs were simply "too weak" only invited CON's response that stronger protection would mean larger costs for the same failed result. CON also landed the decisive rebuttal that both sides accept conditional enforcement, but the frames differ in goals. The opportunity frame brings production onto European soil, where immobile sunk capital and EU law create leverage, while a threat frame aimed at shrinking Chinese presence leaves fewer assets to police. PRO's cumulative-industrial-collapse counter remained speculative next to CON's certain, cumulative climate costs. CON's closing crystallized the weighing cleanly: guaranteed higher prices and permanent carbon emissions versus an industrial revival that protection already failed to deliver once.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response in Rebuttal 2 and Closing that "enforcement needs something to enforce against"—showing that a threat frame aimed at keeping Chinese capacity out would leave Europe with fewer plants, fewer subsidy tranches, and weaker leverage, while an opportunity frame puts factories under EU law where conditional market access becomes a live weapon. This dissolved PRO's attempt to claim CON was smuggling in the threat posture, and it sharpened the core distinction between the two frames.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): B won the comparative weighing. PRO warned of dependency and industrial hollowing, but CON showed the threat frame was already tested in solar - duties and price floors ran for years and manufacturing collapsed while they were in force, not after. CON then forced the trade-off: emissions are cumulative and irreversible, while PRO conceded "prices and a few years of slower adoption are the cost" for a revival that protection has not delivered. CON's opportunity frame co-opted PRO's own China model - immobile capital under EU law gives enforceable leverage only if you invite the plants in.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1-B2.5 plus B4.1-B4.2: solar duties as failed threat-frame experiment and carbon irreversibility vs recoverable delay, combined with factory-as-hostage enforcement, directly neutralized A's core dependency and leverage claims.
