### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON built the debate around a genuinely sharp dilemma introduced in Rebuttal 1: either "low-cost mobile" counts as the universal standard (in which case the right is largely symbolic and mostly already achievable without enforceability) or it doesn't (in which case the obligation forces the costliest buildout first). PRO never escaped this trap — in Rebuttal 2 it explicitly said the floor must bar "throttled, data-capped" plans, which confirmed CON's dilemma rather than resolving it, and PRO's admission that exact speed/latency/price metrics should be left to future regulators handed CON the closing line that no household can state what it could actually sue for today. CON also neutralized PRO's strongest factual point (the expired Affordable Connectivity Program) by noting that any funding mechanism, enforceable or not, depends on continued legislative support, so enforceability adds litigation risk without solving the funding problem. PRO's case remained a reasonable equity narrative backed by historical analogy and some unsourced-sounding statistics (23 million, 14 million), but it never cleanly answered the core structural objection, and its Rebuttal 2 was flagged as clipped for exceeding the word limit, an execution cost that blunted its ability to crystallize a response. CON's closing was tighter and more directly resolved the central clash than PRO's, which mostly restated earlier equity framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 "dilemma" move (A2.6: if cheap mobile counts, the right is symbolic; if not, the costly buildout returns) and its reuse in Rebuttal 2/Closing after PRO's Rebuttal 2 admissions in B4.1 and B4.3 effectively confirmed rather than escaped the trap.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by consistently distinguishing the importance of internet from the wisdom of a universal, individually enforceable right. It exposed unresolved tradeoffs over the meaning, cost, and funding of “high-quality” service while offering more targeted alternatives. Side B plausibly answered that regulators can update standards, but never fully resolved marginal deployment costs, political control of funding, or why enforceability itself supplies resources. Its repeated exact claims about millions disconnected or lacking access were also insufficiently grounded, and its second rebuttal was clipped.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange was its answer to Side B’s permanence argument: a universal-service statute can also be underfunded, amended, or repealed, while courts cannot build infrastructure or appropriate money. That directly weakened Side B’s central comparison between supposedly fragile subsidies and supposedly permanent enforceable service.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully setting up and executing a dilemma regarding the standard for "high-quality" internet. When Side A clarified that existing low-cost mobile networks must not be data-capped or throttled to meet the mandate, Side B effectively used this to prove that the policy would indeed force the expensive, rigid infrastructure upgrades B warned about. Side B also successfully neutralized A's strongest point about the ACP's defunding by pointing out that universal service mandates are equally vulnerable to being underfunded by legislatures.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal decisively capitalized on Side A's pressure question answers, proving that A's proposed standard either lacks stable legal content or requires massive, expensive upgrades.
