### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A offered the sophisticated objection that gross retention is not profit and that trip-level optics could undermine useful cross-subsidies. But B showed that careful labeling and optional context address misinterpretation more proportionately than withholding the figure, while A never firmly established that disclosure would necessarily recouple prices or shrink guarantees. B also identified concrete choices the information enables and framed the accountability benefit more decisively. A’s clipped first rebuttal was a minor execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was its answer to A’s gross-retention objection: disclose the real residual, label it as before taxes and network costs, and permit further itemization. This turned A’s concern from an argument against disclosure into an argument for better explanation, while A’s predicted cross-subsidy harm remained plausible but speculative.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully proving that a per-trip receipt fundamentally misrepresents network-level portfolio costs. Side B's argument that the mandate forces "reputational recoupling"—harming drivers by replacing stable hourly earnings with volatile percentage-chasing—was more compelling than Side A's defense that platforms could simply add explanatory labels.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized Side A's "choice" argument by showing that teaching a driver to reject a good absolute payout merely because of a stranger's high fare leads to chasing percentages over actual earnings.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A built a much stronger and more sophisticated economic case, introducing the concept of "reputational recoupling" to explain how per-trip transparency destroys portfolio cross-subsidies and increases volatility. Side B relied on generic transparency arguments and failed to effectively counter A's explanation of why a legitimate gross margin would be misread as pure profit. A's rebuttals were sharper, particularly in exposing how B's proposed driver "choices" would actually harm drivers by forcing them to chase percentages rather than absolute earnings.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's claim that drivers would benefit from rejecting trips based on the rider's fare, pointing out that chasing percentages rather than absolute earnings per hour would actually harm drivers and force platforms to eliminate the cross-subsidies that stabilize pay.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a competitive debate by distinguishing gross transactional retention from net profit and showing why the former still informs decisions about surge, tipping, switching platforms, and continued participation. Side A raised a sophisticated shared-cost and cross-subsidy objection, but often treated possible misunderstanding as a reason for enforced ignorance and never firmly established that disclosure would unravel service. Its alternative regulations also did not negate the modest disclosure requirement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B most effectively answered the claim that “$25 is $25.” While the immediate payment remains unchanged, knowing what the rider paid reveals whether higher fares reach drivers and informs broader choices about staying, switching, tipping, and accepting future offers. This undercut Side A’s attempt to portray the information as simultaneously useless and dangerously influential.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully framing a per-trip fare split as a misleading accounting artifact rather than a true reflection of margin. Side B expertly neutralized Side A's pressure questions by clearly distinguishing between aggregate margin analysis (for regulators) and per-trip raw gaps (for users), and effectively weaponized Side A's own "$25 is $25" concession to prove the disclosure would breed irrational resentment rather than improve economic decision-making.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive, cleanly answering Side A's "incompatible stories" pressure by explaining how aggregate data turns a raw gap into a true margin, and how a disclosure can be simultaneously useless for economic choices but powerful for resentment.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively exposing the economic and behavioral flaws in Side B's per-trip transparency mandate. Side A successfully argued that a single-trip split is misleading without aggregate context (using the effective "thermometer vs. climate" analogy) and trapped Side B in a dilemma where the disclosure is either behaviorally pointless or actively harmful by unraveling necessary cross-subsidies. Side B's concession that platforms would have to reprice trips using "pooled revenue" handed Side A the winning impact regarding harm to vulnerable riders and thin neighborhoods.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it weaponized Side B's own answer to the pressure question ("openly from pooled revenue") to prove that the mandate would actively unravel cross-subsidies for short trips to low-income neighborhoods, turning Side B's defense into a fatal concession.
