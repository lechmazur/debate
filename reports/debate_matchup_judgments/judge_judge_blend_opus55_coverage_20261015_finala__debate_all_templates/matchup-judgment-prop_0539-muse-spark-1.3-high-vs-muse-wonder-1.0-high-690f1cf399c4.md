### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): The decisive exchange was CON's dilemma: if the home state's "chokehold" (parentage/passport/visa) is the enforcement mechanism, it must either be used—leaving a newborn stateless—or waived, in which case it is no deterrent at all. PRO's answer in Rebuttal 2 conceded the newborn always receives parentage and documents regardless of fraud, which CON immediately converted into "common ground" showing PRO's signature chokepoint delivers no more deterrence than CON's ban (both end up punishing adults post-hoc via fines/forfeiture). From there CON's closing line, "Do not license what you cannot verify," crystallized the strongest unanswered point: PRO's regime certifies paper compliance (escrow receipts, counsel certificates, confidential interviews) without ever being able to verify the actual power dynamics that determine whether a surrogate can refuse a transfer, reduction, or caesarean — and licensing additionally signals safety to buyers, arguably expanding demand. PRO's rebuttal that a ban "promises to punish demand after it succeeds" is largely symmetrical to its own post-fraud penalty scheme once the stateless-child concession is made, and PRO never squarely answered CON's narrower-enforcement claim (that a ban only needs to police its own nationals' purchasing/brokering, not foreign clinic conditions). PRO's case remained coherent and well-argued throughout, with strong framing early (chokehold, escrow, pre-birth orders) and good pressure questions, but it ceded the single mechanism its case depended on and did not fully recover the comparative advantage in the final two speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 dilemma (stateless child vs. forgiven violation) forced PRO's Rebuttal 2 concession that parentage is always granted regardless of fraud — a concession CON then leveraged in Rebuttal 2 and Closing to show PRO's core enforcement chokepoint collapses into the same post-hoc demand-side penalties as a ban.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B won the decisive enforceability clash. The parentage/chokehold paradox (withhold papers and harm the child; grant them and empty the threat) forced A into pre-clearance-plus-post-birth-rescue, after which B showed remaining tools are weak mobile-firm fines while licensing still certifies and expands demand A cannot verify abroad. A’s underground-harms and pre-conception-gate case stayed strong but never fully neutralized that structural limit on “enforceable” standards.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1: if home-state leverage means withholding parentage/visa, the newborn takes the hit PRO warned against; if the state relents, noncompliance is priced in—so the chokehold is a bluff.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by decisively dismantling Side B's core enforcement mechanism. The motion requires "enforceable" standards, and Side B relied heavily on the home state's ability to withhold parentage and visas as a "chokehold." Side A effectively proved that because the state must ultimately grant parentage to avoid child statelessness (a point Side B conceded under pressure), the chokehold is merely a bluff that agencies can price in. Side A's distinction between "auditing paper" and "auditing power" brilliantly exposed the limits of cross-border regulation, showing that conditional legality merely certifies exploitation rather than preventing it. Side B argued well and framed the pre-clearance mechanism strongly, but could not overcome Side A's proof that the standards are unenforceable in practice once a child is born.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it weaponized Side B's concession that newborns must receive parentage even after fraud. By showing that this reduces Side B's "chokehold" to mere post-hoc fines and license revocations, Side A proved that the regulatory framework just becomes a cost of doing business for agencies, completely neutralizing Side B's primary deterrence argument.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases, but B constructed a more systematic mechanism (pre-conception licensing gates separating destination-state clinic accreditation from home-state agency/bank/insurer control) and sustained it under pressure. B's Q1 was the sharpest moment of the debate: it exposed that A's own claim ("judges will rescue the baby at the airport") undercuts the deterrent logic A later relies on for banning the trade, since the same post-birth rescue applies regardless of legal regime. A's answer (A4.1) pivoted deterrence from status-threat to criminal punishment of buyers, a reasonable but costly concession that B exploited in B4.4 to highlight inconsistency in CON's framing. A's strongest material — the volume/scale argument ("fewer beginnings is better protection than paperwork") and the "legal market only between permissive states" critique — landed real blows and were never fully quantified away by B, but B's closing reframed the volume claim as unproven "hope" rather than a demonstrated mechanism, leaving that clash live but not clearly won by A. On balance, B's case progressed more cleanly across rounds, resolved pressure with direct, correctly formatted answers, and left fewer internal tensions exposed than A's shifting deterrence rationale.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question Q1 ("If judges will always regularise a baby's status once born, how does your ban deter buyers... more than a pre-pregnancy gate?") forced CON to abandon its own airport-rescue argument as the basis for ban deterrence, a pivot B then used in B4.4 to expose inconsistency in CON's case.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): A won a competitive debate by keeping the decisive comparison live: residual cases exist under either regime, and conditional pre-pregnancy gates attach usable sticks (escrow, independent counsel, insurance, advance parentage/citizenship route, license/market denial) that a ban deliberately discards. B landed real damage on multi-jurisdiction enforceability, airport regularization, and volume reduction, but never fully neutralized A’s residual-harm and leverage points; A’s pressure answers and second rebuttal narrowed the clash more cleanly than B’s later volume-and-pipeline reframes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1–A2.2 turning B’s cross-border enforcement critique against the ban, then specifying home-state recognition/payment gates as pre-pregnancy leverage rather than foreign clinic raids.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully attacking the "enforceable" prong of the proposition. Side B's enforcement mechanism relied heavily on the destination state inspecting the clinics, which Side A effectively undermined by pointing out the host country's financial incentive to ignore abuses and act as a rubber stamp. Side A's alternative—a ban enforced at the buyer's home country by making the trade uninsurable, unadvertisable, and criminal—presented a more plausible and robust enforcement mechanism. While Side B's harm-reduction argument regarding the underground market was strong, Side A's framing that conditional legality scales exploitation by legitimizing it, combined with the sharp critique that B's model merely shifts the race to the bottom among permissive states, provided a cleaner comparative advantage on the core clash of enforceability and overall harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (A4.3) decisively dismantled Side B's split-enforcement answer by highlighting the destination state's inherent conflict of interest in policing a profitable industry, and pointing out that B's legal market simply becomes a race to the bottom among permissive states.
