### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): CON (A) built a stronger practical case by consistently pressing the workability of the proposed ban. Its central move — that any line between "ordinary relevance" and "banned inferred psychographic targeting" collapses because modern ad delivery systems (lookalike audiences, engagement optimization) perform the same kind of latent-trait inference internally — was never fully resolved by PRO. PRO's attempt in Rebuttal 2 to supply a bright-line test ("psychographic score > X," auditable logs) was a clever original move, but CON's A4.3–A4.5 showed that campaigns typically don't request or see such explicit scores, so the test either bans ordinary platform optimization or becomes a cosmetic relabeling exercise. PRO's closing reasserted the moral stakes (autonomy, structural fairness) but did not repair this operational gap, leaving CON's enforceability objection as a live, unanswered route to victory. On the autonomy point, CON's "resonance opens deliberation, it does not close it" framing, backed by the "proves too much" rebuttal to PRO's affective-priming claim, was sharper and more consistently maintained across rounds than PRO's assertion that inference-based targeting bypasses rational scrutiny. The fairness/democratization exchange was closer to a wash, but since the motion demands a workable prohibition, CON's practical critique carries more decisive weight.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.3–A4.5 response to PRO's "psychographic score > X" enforcement test, showing that ordinary platform-level optimization already performs the same latent-trait inference the ban targets, was the most damaging and least-rebutted exchange of the debate.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): A won by holding the decisive clashes: PRO never proved that inferred resonance bypasses reason rather than opens a contestable claim, and A’s workability dilemma (overbroad ban on ordinary platform optimization vs cosmetic label-ban) survived B’s attempted “psychographic score > X” audit line. B’s fairness and autonomy frames were real but weakened once A inverted cost advantages and reduced “manipulation” to ordinary attention-capture. Comparative weighing favored regulating deception/stolen data/hidden sponsors over outlawing relevance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 (A4.3–A4.5) turning B’s enforceability reply against itself: modern delivery rarely uploads a score; platforms learn latent responsiveness, so the ban is either overbroad or evadable.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing a trilemma that Side A never resolved: a ban on inferred psychological profiles either captures ordinary platform optimization (making it overbroad and harmful to challenger campaigns) or is easily evaded by moving the inference inside the platform (making it cosmetic). B also effectively blunted A's manipulation claim by showing that resonance and attention are doorways to deliberation, not substitutes for it, and that voters retain the ability to reject resonant claims. A's closing repeated the harms but did not answer B's workability or relevance objections, leaving B with the cleaner live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3-B4.5, where CON demonstrated that PRO's proposed API audit fails because modern ad platforms infer user responsiveness organically during delivery, meaning the "psychographic score > X" test either prohibits normal engagement optimization or allows campaigns to evade the ban entirely by letting the platform do the inferring.

B, by contrast, respected format constraints, answered pressure questions with clear A1/A2 structure, and closed with a tight, memorable crystallization of the clash. Overall B presented the more disciplined, consistent, and ultimately more persuasive case.
