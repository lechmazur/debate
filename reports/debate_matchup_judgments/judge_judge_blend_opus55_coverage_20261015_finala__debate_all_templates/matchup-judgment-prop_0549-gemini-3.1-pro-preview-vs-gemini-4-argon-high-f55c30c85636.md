### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): B won by keeping a tighter comparative story: synthetic opioid economics, replaceability, and enforcement backfire made A’s scarcity theory look like high-effort low-yield whack-a-mole, while MAT/harm reduction still saved lives short of total market eradication. A’s strongest live route—casual deception victims whom MAT cannot reach—landed early and forced real answers, but B largely met it with naloxone/test strips plus the claim that most solitary deaths still involve established dependence, and A never fully answered the “fractions of a cent / single shipment slips through” math. Across rounds B adapted more cleanly; A repeated bottleneck assertions without neutralizing B’s decisive cost and backfire pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 (esp. B4.3–B4.5): answers solitary/casual-user pressure, then flips A’s perfection demand onto supply-side arithmetic and dealer-bust backfire.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by establishing that the economics of synthetic opioids make supply-side interdiction structurally futile over a five-year window, while successfully defending the comparative efficacy of treatment and harm reduction. B's core rebuttal—that a microscopic lethal dose costs fractions of a cent, so even aggressive precursor seizures cannot price cartels out of production—materially undermined A's theory of change and was never recovered. B also effectively neutralized A's "deception epidemic" argument by showing that interdiction cannot achieve 100% containment, making reactive tools the only realistic protection for remaining leakage, and turned A's penalty argument by showing severe trafficking charges undermine bystander 911 calls. A's closing that "you cannot medicate an addiction that does not exist" was rhetorically sharp, but B had already demonstrated that harm reduction (test strips, naloxone) addresses non-OUD deceptive-use victims while MAT protects the bulk of at-risk users continuously.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's economic rebuttal in Turn 7 (B4.3) demonstrating that because a lethal fentanyl dose costs fractions of a cent, tripling precursor expenses would add less than a penny to a ten-dollar pill, thereby collapsing Side A's claim that precursor interdiction can meaningfully raise costs or reduce street supply, while accelerating a pivot to deadlier unregulated synthetics.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A built a much stronger comparative case by grounding their argument in the inescapable economics and logistics of synthetic opioids. A effectively neutralized B's interdiction strategy by demonstrating that fentanyl's microscopic volume and negligible per-dose cost make supply-side bottlenecks mathematically illusory, while highlighting how enforcement scrambles markets and increases potency risks. B's focus on deceptive counterfeit pills was rhetorically compelling, but B failed to prove that interdiction could reliably stop these pills from reaching users, and B's dismissal of withdrawal-driven behavior weakened their response to A's pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of the cost and bottleneck argument for precursor interdiction, using the math of fentanyl's microscopic dose cost to show that seizures cannot price cartels out of production and only accelerate pivots to deadlier synthetics like nitazenes.
