### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but B built a tighter, more coherent line: platforms are functionally intermediaries holding customer assets, securities law already provides enforceable mechanisms (segregation, capital rules, fiduciary duty, antifraud provisions with extraterritorial reach), and existing law need not force every listed token to be deemed a security—only the venue's intermediary function triggers registration. Crucially, B's A1/A2 answers in Rebuttal 2 directly rebutted A's pressure questions (explaining that exchanges can register while trading non-securities, and that U.S. securities antifraud rules reach foreign solicitation of U.S. customers), closing the two biggest holes A tried to open. A's strongest move—conceding that proof-of-reserves rules "do not yet exist"—was a serious self-inflicted wound that B exploited effectively in both Rebuttal 2 and Closing, framing A's alternative as speculative and unproven versus B's "already works" framework. A's rebuttals were solid (Madoff point, self-custody point, incumbent-moat point) but never fully neutralized the concession that its preferred framework has no track record, while B's framework's weaknesses (e.g., that current SEC reach didn't actually stop FTX in practice) were less sharply pressed by A. B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly ("proven shield vs. policy wish"), while A's closing largely restated points without fully resolving the proof-of-reserves gap it had conceded. This gives B a clear but not overwhelming edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2 answers) directly dismantling A's two pressure questions—clarifying that venue-level registration doesn't require treating every token as a security, and that U.S. securities antifraud law extraterritorially reached FTX's solicitation of U.S. customers—combined with A's own concession in the same round that proof-of-reserves rules "do not yet exist," which B then weaponized in Closing to brand A's alternative as unproven speculation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A won by demonstrating that major crypto platforms functionally operate as securities intermediaries and that existing broker-dealer/exchange rules provide proven structural protections—segregation, capital requirements, fiduciary duties—that the current light framework lacks. Side B’s strongest points were regulatory mismatch and overbreadth, but B fatally conceded that its key proposed safeguards (proof-of-reserves, tailored custody rules) “do not yet exist,” leaving it defending a speculative regime against an existing, enforceable framework. A’s pressure questions exposed this gap, and A crystallized the advantage in closing by showing that only securities law offers immediate investor recourse rather than a policy wish.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Q2 in the Pressure Questions round and the subsequent exchange: A asked B to name one platform penalized under the “tailored” proof-of-reserves rules. B admitted none exist because those rules are not yet in place, confirming that the distinct category lacks enforceable safeguards. A leveraged this to establish that the proposition defends an existing protective regime while the opposition relies on an untested alternative.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): PRO (B) kept the clash on functional intermediation - platforms holding billions, executing trades, profiting from flow - and showed securities rules already provide segregation, capital, and antifraud tools.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Pressure Q2 demanding a platform penalized under tailored proof-of-reserves rules, and A's concession in Rebuttal 2 A2 that no such rules exist, collapsed CON's alternative into a speculative wish versus existing securities safeguards.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): B built a tighter, more coherent case by separating the "asset classification" question from the "intermediary function" question from the outset, and sustained that functional test through every round. B's clarification that the motion targets platforms performing custody/matching/execution (not every token's legal status) defused A's recurring "not every token is a security" objection, and B's commodity/forex-broker analogy showed the framework need not force literal securities registration on non-security tokens. Crucially, in the custodial-vs-decentralized exchange, A's claim that "most platforms blend custodial and non-custodial features" was meant to expose a loophole in B's carve-out, but B correctly turned it around: blending is itself a reason to regulate the custodial core, and A never recovered from having its own point weaponized against it. A's strongest material — offshore flight risk and the claim that CFTC/FTC/state tools already existed for FTX — was raised but never fully reconciled with the fact that those tools evidently failed to prevent the collapse, a tension B highlighted effectively in closing. A's "tailored framework" alternative remained vague throughout (custody rules, disclosure, leverage limits) without ever specifying how it differs operationally from broker-dealer obligations, making B's concrete functional ask easier to credit. Both sides executed the Q&A format properly, so no execution penalties either way, but B's rebuttals did more actual damage to A's core logic than vice versa.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5/A2.5 exchange: A argued that platforms "blend custodial and non-custodial features" to attack B's bright-line carve-out for decentralized protocols, but B flipped this into proof that the custodial core must be regulated regardless of a decentralized front-end — turning A's own point into self-defeating support for B's position.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by establishing a clear functional test: platforms that custody assets, match orders, and execute trades for retail investors perform the economic role of broker-dealers and exchanges, regardless of token labels. This framework directly answered Side B's asset-classification worries by analogizing to commodities and foreign exchange, while B never fully explained what its vague "tailored framework" entailed beyond the status quo. A also neutralized B's offshore-flight argument and showed that B could not explain how its preferred regime would prevent the next FTX-style commingling scandal. B's insistence that securities law would force every token through an equity-style registration apparatus became less persuasive as the debate progressed because A persuasively separated intermediary conduct rules from underlying asset definitions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.3) was the most decisive exchange. A directly answered B's pressure question about commodities and utilities by pointing out that traditional broker-dealers already handle non-securities under analogous conduct frameworks, and then showed that the offshore-flight argument proves too much because it would justify abolishing all financial oversight. This combination defanged B's two strongest objections and left B without a live path to victory.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side B sustained the decisive functional test: platforms that custody funds, match orders, and execute for retail are intermediaries and should bear broker-dealer/exchange obligations like segregation, net capital, and surveillance. Side A conceded blended custodial models but never answered B's pressure about what existing authority would have routinely examined FTX books, and its core claim that securities rules presuppose asset registration was neutralized by B's asset vs intermediary distinction. Offshore "flight" was turned into a proves-too-much argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1-B2.6 / B4.1-B4.2 - separating token label from intermediary function and showing structural safeguards prevent commingling, directly undercutting A's fraud-only and misclassification arguments.
