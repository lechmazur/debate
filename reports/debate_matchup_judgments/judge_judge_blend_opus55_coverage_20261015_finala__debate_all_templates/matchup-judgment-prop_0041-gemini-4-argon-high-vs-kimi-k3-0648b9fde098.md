### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases, but CON (A) controlled the central comparative question more effectively: "the urgent/vulnerable shopper pays list price under both regimes, so a ban only strips away discounts that help others." This was introduced early, sharpened via a well-aimed pressure question, and never fully dislodged. PRO's best counter—that uniform pricing forces competitive pressure to lower the single public price, while a capped/disclosed regime lets retailers inflate the anchor and quietly discount flexible shoppers—was a genuinely strong mechanism (the "divide and conquer" point), but it remained somewhat asserted rather than fully worked through against CON's parallel claim that competition should discipline the anchor just as it disciplines a uniform sticker. CON also offered a concrete counter-plan (price cap + audited disclosure) that absorbed much of PRO's harm scenarios (gouging, anchor inflation) by redirecting them to more targeted remedies, and handled the "contradiction" charge about transparency adequately. PRO's closing restated rather than extended its mechanism, leaving CON's repeated, rhetorically crisp framing as the last clear word on the central clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Question Q1 ("name one concrete way her checkout total falls under your ban") and PRO's attempted answer in Rebuttal 2 (competition forces the single list price down) were the pivotal exchange; PRO's mechanism was plausible but never fully rebutted CON's parallel claim that the same competitive logic should discipline a capped anchor, leaving the asymmetry only asserted rather than proven.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): CON won a close, high-quality exchange by locking the decisive comparison: the urgent/captive shopper pays list under both regimes, so the ban's real effect is killing discounts rather than cutting that shopper's total. PRO's divide-and-conquer mechanism (inflated anchors + private vouchers) was coherent and memorable, but never escaped CON's structural point that a high uniform price remains fully legal under the ban and that competition, disclosure, and a list-price cap can target true extraction without outlawing the voucher. Both sides answered pressure cleanly; CON weighed and closed more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's sustained list-price comparison (esp. A2.2/A4.5 and A5.1–A5.4): ban does not lower the midnight parent's total, only eliminates the voucher—while uniform high prices stay legal.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO won the central pooling clash. CON argued the midnight parent pays list either way and the ban only raises others, proposing a list-price cap. PRO answered directly in B4 that the list price is not fixed: with selective discounting retailers inflate the anchor and voucher the elastic shopper, while a uniform-price rule forces the store to lower its single public price to keep comparison shoppers, thereby dragging down the inelastic shopper's price. That divide-and-conquer mechanism answered CON's Q1 while CON's competition-will-police-the-anchor answer never explained why it doesn't lose flexible shoppers via targeted vouchers. B also handled the enforcement and elasticity presses more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 A1/A2 - audit inputs vs auditing cap and the uniform-price shield: explains why anchor inflation is sustainable under a cap but not under a ban because discrimination lets the retailer keep elastic shoppers with private vouchers while stranding the urgent buyer, directly neutralizing CON's core list-price cap frame.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): B built a crisp, memorable principle (visibility = legitimacy) and pressed it hard, extracting what it framed as a concession in A4.1 that personalized pricing needs a public ceiling to be defensible. But A's PQ1 exposed that B's ban, as written, covers individualized base prices with no ceiling requirement at all—B never actually proposed or defended a ceiling mandate, only argued it would make things better, leaving a gap between the motion as written and B's defense of it. A pressed this gap in Rebuttal 2 (A4.1) and Closing, arguing the ban as stated destroys real discounts (cart-abandonment, grocery rebates) without fixing monopolist exploitation, which B's own PQ2 example conceded the ban does nothing for ('does not lower the price by a single cent'). A's closing crystallized this into a clean weighing claim: verification without affordability is a hollow bargain. B's closing restated the visibility principle well but did not fully close the gap between the motion's actual scope and the narrower 'profile-keyed number' framing it kept retreating to, nor did it answer A's point that uniform uniform discounts get gamed away. A's case ended up with the cleaner, more complete path given the motion as literally worded, despite B's rhetorical strength and tighter thematic consistency.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question 1 ("does your ban outlaw cart-abandonment deals and grocery rebates, or do you concede those should be legal?") forced B into A1's admission that individualized pricing is banned outright, which A then used in Rebuttal 2 and Closing to show B's defense had narrowed to a ceiling-anchored exception the actual motion does not contain.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a competitive debate by keeping the live decision on the motion as written: a blanket ban’s overreach. A’s public-ceiling / private-floor retail model, plus the behavioral story that hesitation often tracks budget constraints and one-click full-price shoppers track higher WTP, remained live through the close. B landed sharper pressure and a cleaner verification frame, and forced A toward sticker-price dependence, but never fully neutralized A’s core comparison—that ban-surviving “anyone can claim” offers get gamed, force higher baselines, and kill real targeted relief for price-sensitive families. B’s monopoly/desperation path was answered as a sector-regulation problem the ban does not fix. A made the comparative cost of the proposition clearer than B made its necessity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3 vs B4.1: A shows that salvaging only uniform cart-abandonment/posted rules collapses the poverty-relief mechanism B claimed still exists, because affluent shoppers game universal offers and profiled discounts must die under the ban.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): CON (A) won the affordability vs verification clash. PRO’s verification principle was powerful, but A resolved the alleged opacity contradiction with public ceiling/private floor, flipped the vulnerability story (tight budgets signal hesitation and get discounts), and showed a less restrictive fix (mandated visible ceiling + existing gouging/discrimination enforcement + sector caps). PRO conceded “profiled coupons die” and admitted its carve-out for conduct-based deals collapses under its own willingness-to-pay definition, while failing to show a ban actually lowers the monopoly price it worries about.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1-A2.4 and A4.1-A4.3: public ceiling/private floors resolves B2.3 contradiction, inverts inelasticity to show affluent pay full price while budget shoppers signal sensitivity, and exposes that B’s uniform-conduct exception is still willingness-to-pay-based and that banning profiled discounts raises base prices.
