### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, competitive cases, but A sustained a sharper, more consistent attack on the treaty's core mechanism. A's dual-use/verification argument (missile defense and co-orbital servicers sharing the same kill-chain as ASATs) was pressed hard across every round and never fully neutralized; B's "objective indicators" answer still rested on authorization and declared intent, which A exploited directly ("a state can strike its own defunct satellite and call it disposal research"). A also effectively weaponized B's own concession that "no enforcement mechanism can literally rewind a violation," using it in both Rebuttal 2 and Closing to argue the treaty disarms the transparent while leaving the covert untouched. B's best moves—the reversible-inconvenience-vs-irreversible-debris frame and the "fatal dilemma" attempt to trap A between "destructive testing is decisive" and "it's easily replaced"—were real contributions, and A's answer to the dilemma (distinguishing partial proximity validation from full terminal-intercept validation) only partially closes that gap. But B's Rebuttal 2 was explicitly clipped before reaching its concluding weighing sentence, a genuine execution cost per the judging rules, and B's Closing reframe ("tester bears risk financially, orbit bears it physically") never squarely answers A's sharpest line that the "state most willing to cheat retains both the weapon and the option to fragment a satellite when it matters most." That asymmetry argument was the decisive, unresolved clash, and A controlled it more effectively through the debate's progression.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 turning B's own admission—"No enforcement mechanism can literally rewind a violation"—back against B's "fatal dilemma," combined with A's distinction between partial (proximity) and full (terminal-intercept) capability validation, which defused B's attempted logical trap while reinforcing the covert-pathway asymmetry that A's Closing drove home.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the decisive comparison clear: irreversible shared orbital pollution versus bounded, partly substitutable demonstration costs. A’s reciprocity/externality framing and the dilemma that CON cannot treat destructive orbital proof as both uniquely necessary and nearly replaceable survived the exchange better than B’s strong asymmetry and dual-use critiques, which were real but overweighted imperfect enforcement relative to the motion’s cascade-prevention aim. B’s practical and co-orbital pressure was sharp and A’s clipped R2 was a cost, yet A still answered the live burdens more cleanly on who should absorb uncertainty in a commons.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s dilemma in R2: if MD/proximity nearly validate the chain, compliant states lose little by forgoing debris-making impacts; if orbital destruction is uniquely decisive, it is uniquely observable—and B cannot hold both at once.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A won by delivering sharper comparative weighing and more damaging rebuttals. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but B's bright-line rule ("kinetic strike against an orbital target, regardless of predicted persistence") survived pressure better than A's alternative. B's answer to Q1 clearly distinguished its observable, trajectory-based trigger from intent-reading, directly defusing A's "trilemma" framing even though A continued to press it in closing without fully grappling with that third option. A's strongest point—that the treaty leaves wartime use and possession untouched, the scenario most likely to actually cause a cascade—was never fully resolved, but B's "we can't stop everything, but we remove routine guaranteed peacetime harm and rehearsal" response was a reasonable partial answer that kept the resolution's core logic alive. Critically, A's own answer to B's pressure question (A1 in Rebuttal 2) conceded that compliance under its behavior-based alternative is often confirmed only after debris already exists, undercutting its claim to be meaningfully preventive rather than merely punitive — exactly the vulnerability B's "prevention before fragmentation" framing was built to exploit. B's closing crystallized this irreversibility asymmetry more effectively than A's closing rebutted it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to its own Q1 pressure question (A1 in Turn 7), clarifying the ban triggers on an observable orbital-target kinetic strike rather than predicted persistence or inferred intent, combined with A's concession in its own Q1 answer (Turn 8) that compliance under its alternative is partly confirmed only after debris already exists — this exchange decided which side's verification story was more credible.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON won by keeping the decisive clash on instrument fit: the proposition bans all kinetic ASAT tests while leaving possession and wartime use legal, and PRO never closed dual-use, verification, and underinclusiveness cleanly. PRO’s irreversibility and chokepoint case were strong and well grounded, especially on orbital physics and China/Russia examples, but CON repeatedly forced a live trilemma—intent, all orbital strikes, or persistent debris—and showed PRO narrowing the text whenever verification got hard. The cleaner comparative vote is for CON’s harm-based alternative over an overbroad peacetime label that leaves the cascade pathway open in crisis.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s sustained exposure of PRO’s definitional slide (all tests → only orbital strikes with fragmentation) culminating in the closing trilemma against A’s A1 bright-line answer.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a much cleaner and more decisive framing of the core clash: the irreversibility of orbital fragmentation. Side B's primary offensive argument was that a categorical ban on "all" kinetic ASAT tests is overbroad because it bans low-altitude tests where debris supposedly burns up quickly. Side A brilliantly neutralized this by arguing that orbital mechanics and intercept failures make "safe" kinetic strikes a myth, and that we cannot trust a tester's pre-impact physics predictions. A's framing—"prevention before fragmentation beats punishment after it"—was highly memorable and effectively shifted the burden back to B. Side B's alternative (a behavior-based rule banning only "persistent" debris) was left sounding naive and unenforceable, as it requires arguing about debris lifespans after the irreversible harm has already occurred. Furthermore, A handled B's critiques regarding dual-use missile defense and wartime use with standard, robust test-ban logic, whereas B got bogged down in treaty-drafting trilemmas that A easily navigated. A's rhetorical clarity and sharp rebuttals made the decision easy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled Side B's "safe low-altitude test" argument by emphasizing that fragmentation is irreversible, predictions can fail, and a bright-line physical ban is vastly superior to an after-the-fact liability regime.
