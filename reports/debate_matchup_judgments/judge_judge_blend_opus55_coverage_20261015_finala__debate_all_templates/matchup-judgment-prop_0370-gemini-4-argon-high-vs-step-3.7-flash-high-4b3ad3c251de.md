### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built a coherent case around property rights, cost-shifting, and the "voluntary opt-in already satisfies preference, so mandate only coerces dissenters" logic, then used Pressure Questions to expose a real tension in CON's position: CON argued personal phones enable "instant access" to off-duty workers, which only makes sense if personal phones are harder to escape than company devices. CON's answer in A4.2 then explicitly conceded exactly this—that a personal device holds transit passes, medical alerts, and family contacts, making pings seen "immediately," while a company device "can be physically left in a locker or powered off," creating a boundary. This concession directly undercut CON's own earlier claim that reachability problems exist "regardless of device," a contradiction CON never repaired in its final closing (A5), which simply reasserted the device-neutral claim as if the concession hadn't happened. PRO's closing (B5) correctly seized on this self-defeating admission to crystallize the clash. CON's case also leaned on confident but ungrounded claims ("surveys...consistently find," blanket containerization/remote-wipe safety, GDPR/CCPA as reliable deterrents) without acknowledging PRO's rebuttal that litigation holds and imperfect sandboxing undercut those safeguards. Additionally, CON's Opening and first Rebuttal were both clipped for exceeding word limits, a real execution cost that cut off argument development at key moments. PRO's rebuttals were sharper, more responsive, and better exploited the live contradiction, making this a solid, clear win for PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2's point that "you do not need an employment mandate to get people to do something they already want to do," combined with the Pressure Questions exchange and CON's resulting self-contradictory admission in A4.2 (personal phones are inescapable vs. company devices can be locked away), which PRO decisively exploited in its closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins because CON conceded in Rebuttal 2 that employers prefer personal phones because workers cannot turn them off, admitting a company device can be left in a locker while a personal phone is an inescapable off-duty leash, and simultaneously confessed that managers routinely coerce workers into installing apps. These admissions directly contradicted CONs claim that routine requirements protect worker choice. PRO showed the same software runs on either device, so the mandate serves only to shift hardware costs and enable unpaid reachability, while PROs voluntary opt-in model preserves genuine choice without coercion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PROs Pressure Question 2 and the following Rebuttal 2 answer were decisive: they forced CON to admit that a company device can be physically left in a locker or powered off, while a personal phone cannot, revealing that the real purpose of a personal-device mandate is off-duty reachability, not operational necessity or worker convenience.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO wins because it proves routine mandate coerces only holdouts: if many prefer one phone, voluntary opt-in preserves convenience while ending cost-shifting. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): A (CON) built the stronger path by exploiting a genuine contradiction in B's case: B simultaneously argued that mandatory apps expose banking data, medical records, and off-duty life to surveillance, yet also claimed only a "tiny number" of workers would opt out even when protected from retaliation. A pressed this relentlessly across rebuttals and pressure questions, and B's attempted clarification (that the tiny-opt-out figure only applies once retaliation is removed) actually reinforced A's point: if workers freely choose to keep the apps even without coercion, the severity of the alleged privacy harms is undercut. B's late pivot in closing to a pure autonomy/property-rights argument ("even if every app were perfectly safe...") was a substantive retreat from its own empirically-loaded opening, and A's closing explicitly and effectively called this out. B also leaned on suspiciously precise, unverifiable claims (a named EFF statistic, an unnamed "major national delivery firm" breach) that read as invented sourcing, weakening its grounding score, while A's mechanism-based reasoning about OS sandboxing and permissions was more cautious and plausible. B's Rebuttal 1 was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, costing it some completeness. Both sides executed the pressure-question format correctly, but A used the exchange more decisively to expose and sustain a contradiction that B never fully neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 (A2.1–A2.2), later reinforced in Rebuttal 2 (A4.2–A4.3), which caught B's simultaneous claims that mandatory apps are deeply invasive yet only a "tiny number" of workers would opt out even without coercion — a contradiction B's subsequent clarification could not cleanly resolve and that forced B into a weaker, late-stage principle-only defense in closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side A wins by correctly framing the proposition as a protection against coerced employer access to personal property and data, not a ban on workplace apps. A successfully showed that making personal phone installation a routine condition of employment exploits a power imbalance that eliminates meaningful worker choice. When B attacked A with an apparent contradiction—arguing that A’s own "tiny number" opt-out claim proved workers found the apps safe—A’s Rebuttal 2 turned this decisively by clarifying that the low opt-out rate applied only under a protected, retaliation-free rule, which proved the rule would be minimally disruptive while still safeguarding the minority who need protection. This neutralized B’s strongest attack and underscored why the proposition is necessary: under current conditions, workers cannot safely refuse. B’s practical arguments about e-waste and hardware redundancy were deflated by this same clarification, and B’s repeated insistence that company phones would not stop off-duty tracking never fully answered A’s broader case about cost-shifting, personal data breach exposure, and the principled boundary between work and private life. A’s progression from empirical risks in the Opening to a principled "even if perfectly safe" stance in the Closing gave the debate a clear moral and logical anchor that B never successfully dislodged.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 clarification that the "tiny number" of opt-outs assumes a protected, coercion-free rule—neutralizing B's contradiction attack by showing the proposition is necessary precisely because current coercion prevents refusal, while remaining minimally disruptive to employers.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won the core technical and tradeoff clash. It showed modern OS sandboxing blocks claimed access to banking/medical data, argued off-duty tracking is a management-policy problem not solved by who owns the device (a company phone carried 24/7 enables same surveillance), and trapped PRO in a fatal contradiction: PRO painted apps as mass invasions yet said only a tiny fraction would opt out when retaliation is removed. PRO's answers that basic chat apps still demand broad permissions and could be updated later were speculative and never rebutted the sandbox or e-waste/login-auth practicality points. PRO's repeated precise EFF 60% stat and breach anecdote were poorly grounded versus CON's mechanism-based defense.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 contradiction exploit - if routine apps truly looted photos/banking/children's medical records and policed grocery trips, opt-outs would be massive, not "tiny"; B4.3's clarification that under protected choice almost everyone would still keep apps conceded safety/convenience and collapsed PRO's surveillance narrative.
