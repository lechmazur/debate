### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): PRO built a clean, consistent case (omission is itself a choice; the motion requires inclusion, not primacy) and used comparative examples effectively. The decisive exchange came in Pressure Questions: PRO's Q1 forced CON to admit its "anti-erasure floor" alternative also requires naming which events are "major," and PRO's closing (B5.2) pressed this into a reductio—showing CON's ban-vs-mandate distinction collapses once the floor has any teeth. CON's answer (A5.3) restated the distinction without resolving why a no-deletion duty escapes the same list-making problem it condemns elsewhere. CON also never squarely answered PRO's asymmetry point (B3.2/B5.4): why compelled achievement-teaching is acceptable but compelled honesty is not; CON's "doubling state control" line sidesteps rather than answers. CON scored real points on the "bare atrocities without corrective context" risk (A3.2/A4.4), and PRO's answer there (facts "refuse to stay bare") was the weakest moment in its case, slightly hand-wavy. But on balance PRO neutralized CON's central distinguishing mechanism more successfully than CON neutralized PRO's core framing, and PRO's closing synthesized the clash more sharply, leaving a cleaner path to the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question (B3.1) and follow-up closing (B5.2), showing CON's "ban erasure" alternative necessarily requires the same major-event list-making CON calls dangerous when framed as a mandate—this exchange most damaged CON's core distinguishing argument and was never fully resolved by CON's reply.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won by successfully exposing a structural contradiction in Side A's counter-proposal. Side B effectively argued that Side A's 'anti-erasure' rules, to have any teeth, must specify which events cannot be omitted, thereby collapsing into the very mandate Side A opposed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing crystallization that Side A's 'anti-erasure floor', the moment it imposes a duty to teach and checks compliance, becomes the exact mandate Side A condemns.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won by decisively dismantling Side A's primary alternative. Side A relied heavily on "anti-erasure" rules as a less intrusive way to ensure hard history is taught without a statutory mandate. Side B effectively trapped Side A by pointing out that an anti-erasure rule with teeth is functionally identical to a mandate (as it must define what cannot be erased and enforce a minimum standard of coverage), while one without teeth is merely the status quo. Side A never successfully escaped this structural dilemma, and its closing attempt to separate "prohibition" from "prescription" rang hollow. Side B also effectively highlighted the asymmetry in Side A's position regarding state compulsion in teaching achievements versus atrocities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's persistent and logically airtight dismantling of Side A's "anti-erasure" alternative, culminating in the closing argument that a prohibition on omission inherently requires a prescription of content, causing Side A's core distinction to collapse.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases, but CON (A) landed the more damaging cumulative rebuttals. A's concrete examples (Japan's softened Nanjing coverage despite inclusion, Turkey's denial, the US 1619/1776 swing) directly undercut PRO's central mechanism — that a requirement secures honesty rather than mere topic inclusion — and PRO's own A2 answer conceded "no mandate can guarantee perfect interpretation," which A exploited effectively. A's "floor is a ceiling" point (codifying a state-authored list of "major" atrocities implicitly certifies the rest as minor, with statutory backing) and the "shield covers the state's text, not the teacher who goes beyond it" rebuttal were never squarely neutralized by PRO, including in PRO's closing. PRO's strongest move — "power already writes the curriculum, so the only question is whether it may write itself innocent" — was turned against it in A's closing as a concession that the mandate adds nothing beyond cost when institutions are captured, and adds nothing beyond what free institutions already deliver when they are not. PRO's Pressure Questions were sharp and correctly answered in A1/A2 format, and A's clipped final rebuttal is a real execution cost, but the substantive exchange by that point already favored A's framing. The debate was close and both sides argued the actual motion without evasion, but A's path to victory survived the exchange more intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 "A2" answer — pointing out that nothing in PRO's model stops a government from teaching atrocity as aberration or necessity "now with a statute blessing the spin," paired with the unanswered "shield covers the text, not the teacher" point — most damaged PRO's live route, since PRO's own concession that no mandate secures interpretation left the floor's practical benefit resting almost entirely on litigability, which A's capture argument also partially undercut.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively weaponizing the mechanics of state power against Side B's proposal. While B successfully argued that public schools are already state-controlled, A proved that handing the state a mandate to narrate its own crimes results in 'domestication' and state-sanctioned spin. A's argument that the mandate only shields the state's spun words, leaving honest teachers just as exposed, decisively defeated B's teacher-shield claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 argument that the mandate protects only the state's own words, meaning a teacher who goes beyond the state's spun narrative remains completely exposed and is now insubordinate.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by decisively shifting the core clash from "deletion" to "domestication." Side A successfully argues that a state mandate does not prevent a bad-faith government from minimizing or spinning its own atrocities, but rather grants that spin statutory authority. Side A's distinction between the state as a referee (via courts and professional standards) and as a narrator is highly persuasive, and its rebuttals sharply dismantle PRO's claims about teacher shields and factual floors. Side B's case relies on the somewhat naive assumption that a legislative mandate can bind the very state apparatus it seeks to constrain, which Side A exploits effectively throughout the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of PRO's "teacher shield" and "factual floor" arguments in Rebuttal 2, specifically the sharp observation that "the shield covers the text, not the teacher" and that nationalist curricula already engage in "ritual confession" that domesticates atrocities rather than shattering victimhood narratives.
