### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): B won a close debate by tying skewed admissions to the distribution of a scarce, career-shaping credential. B also gave the clearer policy distinction between trusting a fairness engine and auditing an inheritance institution, despite a clipping penalty in its first rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to the hospital analogy was decisive: universities do not merely receive an unequal population but actively select entrants and allocate credentials that open elite labor markets. That response made composition part of the causal mechanism rather than just evidence of inequality before enrollment.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): CON won a competitive debate by forcing a clearer decision rule: compare what the university uniquely changes versus the closed-inheritance baseline, and treat composition as intake evidence rather than the verdict. PRO’s strongest path—aggregate seat flow, credential laundering, and audit-by-default policy—remained live, but was undercut by CON’s asymmetry and counterfactual (marginal asset for outsiders; ratification for the already advantaged) and by CON’s demolition of PRO’s population-share falsifier as an impossible pre-university standard. On policy, CON kept the tighter coherence: PRO’s own reforms (end legacy, condition subsidy) are fairness enforcement, so demoting the mission removes the standard that justifies them. PRO’s clipped R1 cost some force; CON’s later speeches narrowed and resolved the clash more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3–B4.4: PRO’s population-share test makes mobility definitionally unreachable and the demotion/audit move collapses into the fairness mission it renames.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): CON won by disputing PRO's compositional metric and establishing counterfactual marginal effects as the proper standard. CON showed that elite universities do not cause wealthy students to consolidate status they would not otherwise retain, while they do provide low-income students with transformative mobility assets—substituting a contestable credential for closed inheritance. CON also exposed a damaging policy contradiction: PRO's own reforms (ending legacy preferences, conditioning subsidies on access) presuppose a fairness mission that PRO simultaneously argued should be demoted. PRO's clipped first rebuttal compounded this by truncating the policy development needed to resolve the pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's sustained pressure that PRO's reforms require treating elite universities as fairness engines, most sharply crystallized in B4.4: "You cannot hold institutions to a job you have declared is not theirs." This undermined PRO's attempt to distinguish "audit by default" from enforcing a fairness mission.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the aggregate institutional function decisive. It conceded genuine mobility for low-income admits but showed that elite universities also actively convert prior wealth into admissions advantages, prestigious credentials, and network access for a much larger affluent cohort. Side A's marginal-causation framework undercounted this added credentialing and networking effect. B also defended a coherent conditional policy rather than a crude withdrawal of aid.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response that selection itself can constitute reproduction was decisive: universities are not merely cameras recording upstream inequality when they choose wealth-readable criteria and then certify the result as merit. This directly weakened A's claim that affluent outcomes should be attributed almost entirely to family background.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): A won by owning the motion’s comparative “more than” test: elite cohorts mainly certify and compound prior advantage, so a few real climbs do not make the institutions engines of fairness. A forced a live burden on aggregate cohort effect versus B’s per-admit counterfactual, answered B’s pressure with a concrete mobility threshold and non-hostage aid rules, and turned B’s reform concessions into evidence that current practice is inheritance until proven otherwise. B was strong on individual trajectory change and the risk of harming aid, but never fully neutralized volume-and-legitimation weighing or the proposition’s treatment frame; conditioning became A’s presumption, not a full CON victory path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 / Closing on the “more than” metric: counting only changed low-income trajectories while writing off wealthy status-preservation makes reproduction vanish by definition; policy subsidizes the whole institution.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that elite universities' aggregate function—selecting overwhelmingly from the affluent, converting inherited advantage into meritocratic credentials, and legitimizing status at scale—constitutes reproduction that outweighs their marginal mobility effects. A successfully absorbed B's counterfactual per-student argument by framing it as a selective spotlight on exceptions, and defended its policy framework as a coherent inheritance model that protects low-income aid as a floor. B's "blame the camera" reframe was strong but did not fully neutralize A's causal claim that the university actively certifies advantage rather than merely reflecting it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4) directly answered Side B's pressure questions by defining measurable thresholds for mobility dominance and rebutting the claim that aid must be a casualty, turning the "aid hostage" argument back on B by making access a condition for tax privilege rather than a negotiable budget line.
