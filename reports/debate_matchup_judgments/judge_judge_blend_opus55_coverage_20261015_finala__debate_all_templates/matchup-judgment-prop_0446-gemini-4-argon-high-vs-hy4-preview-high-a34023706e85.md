### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and used the pressure-question round properly, with clear A1/A2 answers. CON's recurring "moving milestone" critique (that retirement under PRO's model is conditioned on finance/replacement power arriving, making the "common timeline" effectively soft) was a strong, sustained point, but PRO partially neutralized it by distinguishing the immediate, unconditional freeze on new coal from the milestone-governed retirement schedule. PRO's closing identified a sharper, less-resolved tension in CON's own alternative: CON claimed its carbon-price-and-border-adjustment "club" works "even if China and India never sign" [A4.5], yet also relied on domestic carbon pricing to cut China/India's own grid emissions [A4.1]—and PRO correctly noted that tariffs cannot touch the "vast majority" of coal burned domestically in non-member economies [B4.4]. CON's late attempt to patch this ("decarbonizes its own members first… pulls others in" [A5.2]) was vague and did not squarely answer how a club that excludes the largest emerging coal burners actually retires their domestic baseload coal, leaving PRO's core practical objection more live at the end than CON's symmetrical critique of PRO's mechanism. Because the debate ultimately turns on which side's mechanism can plausibly retire coal where it is actually burned (China, India, Indonesia), PRO's final framing and rebuttal left a cleaner, more damaging unanswered point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing catch that CON's "club works even if China and India never sign" [A4.5] contradicts CON's reliance on domestic carbon pricing to curb China/India's own coal use, since border tariffs admittedly cannot touch domestic grid emissions [B4.4]-[B5.2], was the most decisive and least-rebutted exchange of the debate.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO maintained a live grand-bargain path: binding common deadline solves free-riding and capital-market signaling while differentiated, milestone-backed finance avoids blackouts. CON's strongest 'moving deadline' pressure was answered with unconditional new-coal freeze and enforceable interim funding, and bankable guarantees for upfront capital. CON's club alternative collapsed when PRO showed border adjustments don't touch domestic coal where most coal is burned in China/India, and self-funding via domestic carbon price recreates the voter backlash CON warned about, leaving PRO with the cleaner comparative route to retiring young fleets.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B4.4/B5.2-3 attack that CON's smaller club 'works even if China and India never sign' leaves domestic coal untouched and forces developing nations to self-fund through regressive price hikes - exposing a fatal contradiction in CON's alternative.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully defending the logic of the "grand bargain" (binding finance in exchange for a binding timeline) and delivering a decisive critique of Side B's alternative. Side A effectively demonstrated that Side B's "smaller club" with border adjustments cannot address the massive domestic coal consumption of non-member emerging economies, a vulnerability Side B failed to adequately overcome. Side B's strongest point—that the common timeline becomes a moving target if retirement is contingent on replacement power—was mitigated by Side A's clarification that interim milestones and the freeze on new coal remain binding. Side A's closing crystallized the comparative advantage more cleanly, showing why a rich-country tariff club cannot solve a global prisoner's dilemma.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which dismantled Side B's "smaller club" alternative by pointing out that border tariffs only affect traded goods and cannot force the retirement of domestic coal plants in non-member emerging economies like India and China.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (A) landed the most damaging exchanges of the debate: it forced PRO to concede that donor finance could be "penalized" within the treaty (making ratification by wealthy legislatures even less plausible) and that milestones would be "suspended or recalibrated" if support fell short. CON then correctly argued this recalibration concession undercuts PRO's central "credible market signal" claim, since investors cannot trust a deadline that bends whenever aid falters. PRO's closing attempt to rescue this ("the bar on new unabated coal... remains") was a late, somewhat thin patch rather than a full resolution. PRO's own framework ("ceiling on delay, not floor on ambition") was a clever reframe that defused the literal "identical deadline" strawman charge, and PRO consistently pressed that CON's alternative ("differentiated timelines" without binding force) is just the voluntary coordination that already produced delay — a charge CON never answered with a real mechanism, only repeated assertion. That unanswered point keeps PRO's coordination-failure framing alive, but it is outweighed by the more concrete, feasibility-based damage CON inflicted on the treaty's actual design (ratification realism and the recalibration loophole), which goes to whether the proposition as written is workable at all. CON's closing speeches did contain a minor execution flaw (several broken/nonexistent reference tags), which cost it a bit on grounding discipline, but this did not offset the substantive rebuttal advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3-A4.4's exploitation of PRO's "penalize donors" and "recalibrate milestones" concessions (B4.1-B4.2) was the most decisive exchange: it turned PRO's attempt to make binding support credible into evidence that the treaty is both unratifiable and self-defeating, a point PRO's closing only partially addressed.

B never resolved why wealthy fleets should share the same final date rather than exit earlier voluntarily under its own ceiling model.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 – binding finance to guarantee credibility guarantees rejection, while suspending milestones when finance fails concedes the deadline is not binding, collapsing B's core investor-signal argument.

Side B forces Side A into a trilemma: an early timeline causes blackouts in developing nations, a late timeline gives wealthy nations decades of slack, and binding financial support is politically unratifiable. When Side A attempts to escape this by allowing phaseout milestones to be "recalibrated" if finance falls short, Side B brilliantly capitalizes on this concession to show that the "ironclad market signal" is actually conditional and suspendable, destroying Side A's core mechanism for stopping coal investment. Side B's alternative of differentiated timelines emerges as the more realistic and effective path.
