### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides built a coherent, well-structured case, and B's "rebate trap" (narrow relief hurts captive workers, broad relief guts both revenue and traffic reduction) was a genuinely sharp pressure point that A had to answer repeatedly. But A's answers were more substantive: it reframed exemption design as a tunable, correctable policy problem versus a veto with "no tuning knob," and it consistently pressed the core asymmetry CON never fully resolved—that the status quo is not neutral but an "invisible toll" (crawling buses, delayed ambulances, unpriced pollution) that also falls on non-drivers and low-income transit riders. B's strongest counter (bus lanes/signal priority can fix throughput without pricing) was logically exposed by A as not reducing car volume and not funding transit/rebates, and B never cleanly rebutted that structural gap in Rebuttal 2 or Closing, instead re-asserting the trap. A's closing crystallized the clash cleanly (design constraint vs. veto, tuning problem vs. no-knob status quo), while B's closing, though sharp, mostly reasserted the trap without engaging A's "invisible toll" asymmetry or explaining why bus lanes alone solve congestion volume. Both answered pressure questions directly and in format. Overall A's case had a cleaner, more complete mechanism (price + revenue + compensation + adjustment) while B's alternative (bus lanes + transit-first) depended on assuming away the core scarcity problem pricing is meant to solve, giving A a clear but not overwhelming edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 answer to the "rebate trap" (A4.1-A4.4), framing exemption calibration as a correctable design problem versus B's "veto with no destination," directly met the sharpest pressure point in the debate, though B's subsequent closing reassertion kept the tension alive without fully resolving it.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO won a competitive debate by owning the motion as written: opposition is a design constraint, not a veto, and the live comparison is an explicit toll versus the invisible one already paid in delay, emissions, and unreliable buses. CON landed the sharpest fairness hits—sequencing, middle-income captives, diversion, and the exemption trap after PRO widened relief—but never escaped the freeze problem (no workable threshold without blocking demand management indefinitely) or showed that bus lanes alone ration the bottleneck or fund alternatives. PRO’s answers under pressure and closing crystallize a governing mechanism; CON’s brake lacks a destination.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s A4/A5 trap turn after PRO’s B4.1 sliding-scale retreat: expand fixed-shift rebates and peak gains/revenue collapse; keep them narrow and you dock captive workers—plus diversion irony on perimeter bus lanes.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): PRO won by maintaining a coherent comparative framework: congestion itself is a regressive, unpriced toll on time and health, whereas pricing generates revenue and policy levers to compensate captives, shift discretionary trips, and adjust the scheme over time. CON landed sharp blows with the exemption trap and sequencing objections, but never squarely answered why bus lanes and progressive taxes alone could reduce total vehicle demand or fund the transit they demanded be built first. By the closings, PRO’s "tuning problem vs. veto" crystallization showed a live governing mechanism, while CON’s alternative remained underdeveloped and effectively conceded a veto to driver opposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s sustained reframing of unpriced congestion as a regressive "invisible toll" on bus riders and poorer neighborhoods, which CON never offset with a credible alternative revenue source or mechanism to reduce central vehicle demand.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides built sophisticated cases, but PRO more effectively controlled the decisive definitional clash. CON's core move—distinguishing Stockholm/London's trials and referendums from "imposing despite strong opposition"—was clever but ultimately self-undermining: PRO's rebuttal and Q1 pressed that launching any pilot still requires imposing the fee over pre-launch driver opposition, and CON's answer (A1 in Rebuttal 2) mostly restated the trial/permanence distinction without squarely refuting that the trial itself instantiates the very action the motion describes. PRO also landed a sharp, unanswered double-standard point in closing: CON accepted that parking permits/exemptions can protect aides and tradespeople without collapsing the policy, yet denied that equivalent congestion-pricing exemptions could do the same. CON's equity and legitimacy arguments were real and forced PRO to make concessions about exemptions, but PRO's response—that vulnerable drivers are a small share, so targeted relief preserves most revenue and traffic benefits—directly answered the dilemma CON posed, and CON's closing reply ("small shares are exactly who lose") was more assertion than rebuttal. PRO's pressure questions were sharper and more clearly exploited the opponent's own concessions, and PRO's closing crystallized the clash more decisively than CON's recap-heavy closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 in Pressure Questions, asking how a city can ever "prove the policy works" without first imposing it over pre-launch opposition, exposed that CON's Stockholm/London trial-and-referendum model still required imposing the charge despite driver opposition — a tension CON's subsequent answer did not fully resolve.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO won a competitive debate by turning CON’s own “prove it works, then consent” frame into a catch-22: experience is impossible if strong pre-launch driver opposition is treated as a veto. PRO kept the clash on tragedy-of-the-commons physics, induced demand, and majority-interest democracy, while adequately answering the exemption dilemma via nonlinearity and narrow targeted relief. CON was sharp on regressivity and Stockholm/London safeguards, but the reversible-trial reframe still concedes first imposition over driver anger—the motion’s actual decision rule—so PRO had the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B3.1/B4.1: forcing CON’s “prove-then-consent” concession to admit that testing requires imposing the charge over fierce driver opposition first.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by showing that any proof-then-consent model requires initially imposing pricing over driver opposition, turning Side B's own concession against them. A also exposed a double standard on equity: B's parking reform alternatives impose the same costs and backlash without solving induced demand. B's reading of the motion as requiring permanent irreversible imposition was strained, whereas A's framing—that "even when" simply refuses a minority veto—better fits the text and the evidence from Stockholm and London.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A turning Side B's "prove it works" concession to show that even reversible trials and electoral mandates require imposing the charge over strong driver opposition, and exposing the double standard on exemptions between parking reform and congestion pricing.
