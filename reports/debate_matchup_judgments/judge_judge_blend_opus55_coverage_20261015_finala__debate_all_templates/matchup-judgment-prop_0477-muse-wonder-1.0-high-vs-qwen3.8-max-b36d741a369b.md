### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-sequenced cases, but B's path survived the exchange more intact. A's strongest move was the "deterrence by denial" framing (cuts become expensive and visible if routes are diversified) and the sharp point that private operators won't fund wartime-level redundancy. B never fully dislodged the denial logic, but B landed two decisive counters that A could not fully neutralize: (1) a concrete non-defense-budget funding mechanism (levies, licensing conditions, user-funded contracts) that directly answers the "who pays" pressure without conceding the large defense-to-cable shift the motion demands, and (2) the internal tension in A's final position—A retreated to "defer only marginal/duplicative platforms" while still claiming this funds a serious wartime backstop, which B's closing ("PRO's answer assumes escorts are untouched while shrinking the force that generates escorts") exposed as incoherent: either the cut is marginal and can't fund "much more," or it's substantial and undercuts the very sea control needed to protect cables and repair ships in contested waters. A's answers to the pressure questions were vaguer ("marginal," "duplicative") than B's concrete levy/licensing mechanism, making B's rebuttal to the funding question land more cleanly. A's closing reasserted the denial framing but did not resolve this scale/consistency problem, leaving B with the cleaner, more resolved comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 / Closing point that A's "marginal platform" deferral is self-undermining — either too small to fund the demanded resilience or large enough to erode the sea control needed to protect cables and repair operations — directly exploited an unresolved tension A never cleanly answered.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that deniable cable attacks are better countered through resilience and denial than by relying primarily on attribution and retaliation. Side A offered a plausible targeted funding alternative and correctly stressed sea control, but repeatedly treated the proposed shift as disarmament after B explicitly preserved a sufficient escort and combat core. B made the cleaner marginal comparison: extra visible platforms face diminishing returns, while concentrated cable vulnerabilities remain major failure points on which those platforms themselves depend.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer that no navy can patrol every cable, whereas diverse routes and dispersed landings can make successful coercion require many visible cuts, directly weakened A’s enforcement-centered case. Its later distinction between preserving core sea control and postponing marginal procurements answered A’s strongest pressure.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO built a cleaner path to victory by establishing that gray-zone cable attacks are cheap and deniable, making visible platforms poor preventive tools, and showing that deterrence-by-denial through redundancy and dispersed infrastructure is the more robust response. CON’s alternative funding mechanisms and platform-dependency arguments were plausible but did not fully neutralize the attribution problem or the scale of investment PRO showed was necessary for simultaneous cuts. PRO’s defense of the trade as a shift from marginal, duplicative visible platforms rather than core sea control preserved the logic of the motion against CON’s attempts to frame it as disarmament.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 1 reframing from “catch the cutter” to “make the cut fail” (A2.3–A2.4), which blunted CON’s central claim that visible platforms deter gray-zone cable attacks and established deterrence-by-denial as the decisive standard for judging the trade-off.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and engaged directly, but B built the more durable structural trap. B's "dilemma" framing—either PRO's resilience spend is modest (and already matched by existing commercial redundancy, as shown by the Baltic/Red Sea examples PRO itself introduced) or it is serious (and then no longer cheap, undermining PRO's cost-comparison claim)—was never cleanly escaped. When pressure questions forced PRO to specify a benchmark ("two or three plausible near-simultaneous cuts," funded by deferring a squadron increment), B's closing correctly showed this concedes that the stated threat is already absorbed by current rerouting, making the "much more investment" ask look like marginal convenience rather than decisive risk reduction. B also introduced a sharper weighing frame late but legitimately (reversible disruption vs. irreversible military/territorial loss) that PRO's final rebuttal and closing did not substantively rebut, instead reasserting that exposed cables make "every platform less effective" without specifying how. PRO's case remained strong on the grey-zone coercion narrative and the attribution-gap point, and it scored real hits noting platforms can't restore severed data links and themselves depend on networks—but B's attribution answer (deep cuts need specialized vessels, narrowing suspects and making patrols/sensors more useful, not less) blunted that attack. On balance B controlled the comparative framing more effectively across rounds and left PRO's central ask looking either redundant or self-underfunded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 "dilemma" move (B4.1/B4.4), sharpened by B's Pressure Questions and crystallized in B5.1: showing that PRO's own stated benchmark (two or three cuts) is the scale already absorbed by existing commercial rerouting, so the proposed reallocation buys convenience rather than the deterrence-by-denial PRO claims.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by exposing an unresolved scale-and-cost dilemma. If the proposal handles only a few cuts, existing rerouting and targeted repair measures already capture much of its benefit; if it must withstand sustained coordinated sabotage, Side B never established that deferring one marginal platform could fund the necessary routes, vessels, crews, and monitoring. Side A also gave visible platforms a credible multi-mission value beyond cable defense. Side B established the importance of cables and the logic of deterrence by denial, but asserted rather than demonstrated that substantially greater spending was the superior marginal trade.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to Side B’s two-or-three-cut benchmark was decisive: it argued that this limited resilience resembles what existing redundancy already provides, while the larger repeated-cut scenario would cost much more and require the same naval presence Side B proposed deferring.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a cleaner comparative at the point of likeliest attack. It framed undersea cables as the likely first target of grey-zone coercion, argued that visible platforms are ill-suited to prevent deniable deep-sea cuts, and specified a concrete marginal trade (deferring one squadron increment or frigate batch) to fund strategic redundancy and repair surge. Side B’s strongest move was a dilemma framing that the plan was either redundant or unaffordable, but Side A escaped this in its second rebuttal by defining a limited benchmark (2–3 near-simultaneous cuts) and showing that cheap stockpiles alone cannot create geographic diversity or surge crews. Side B never fully overcame the core problem that the platforms it defended depend on the very connectivity Side A sought to harden, and its closing assertion that recent rerouting already met the benchmark begged the question of whether commercial peacetime resilience equals strategic wartime resilience. Side A’s progression, direct answers to pressure questions, and sharper weighing on the marginal trade gave it the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over Side B’s pressure question demanding specificity (Turn 6 Q1): Side A answered in its second rebuttal with a concrete marginal trade—absorbing 2–3 near-simultaneous cuts by deferring one squadron increment or frigate batch—and Side B countered that recent disruptions already met that benchmark through rerouting. This clash defined whether the proposed resource shift bought genuine new resilience or merely duplicated existing commercial slack.
