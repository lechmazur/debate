### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but CON's structural "asymmetry" argument—that price/e-waste gains require broad, sustained behavioral change while safety/security harms need only a shift in who performs marginal repairs—went largely unanswered after being pressed in Rebuttal 1 and restated in Rebuttal 2 and closing. CON also answered PRO's sharpest pressure question (naming a disabled theft protection) with a concrete, specific mechanism—parts pairing enabling donor-part legitimization—rather than dodging, which blunted PRO's "speculative harm" framing. PRO's "trap" argument (that CON's harms and denial of benefits are mutually exclusive) was clever but was directly neutralized by CON's low-volume/high-risk-per-job response, and PRO never fully re-engaged that rebuttal in the final rounds. CON also tied its case more tightly to the motion's explicit five-year window, arguing persuasively that risk changes immediately with the rule while savings require years of behavior change—an argument PRO's closing didn't squarely dismantle. PRO still landed strong points (software locks vs. existing aftermarket parts, manufacturer capitulation as evidence prices can fall, longer update pledges), keeping this close, but CON's answers under pressure and sharper time-frame alignment give it a modest edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's "trap" argument in Rebuttal 2 (B2.1–B2.2), explaining that benefit-scale and harm-scale are not symmetric, combined with its direct, specific Q1 answer naming parts pairing as the disabled protection—this pairing of rebuttal and pressure-answer most effectively neutralized PRO's central structural attack.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively winning the clash on theft and security, while effectively mitigating Side A's price and e-waste impacts. B's answer to A's pressure question cleanly demonstrated that pairing bans directly disable the primary deterrent against parts harvesting, a point A failed to overcome in closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4's answer to A's first pressure question beautifully turned A's challenge by explaining that parts pairing itself is the exact theft deterrent being disabled, making stolen components suddenly valuable.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): CON won by keeping the five-year clock and asymmetric burdens live: benefits need broad, sustained price drops plus owner behavior change against upgrades/trade-ins, while risks rise from rule changes themselves (pairing/tools access, untrained battery work) even at modest repair volume. PRO’s aftermarket-plus-pairing mechanism and status-quo kitchen-table counter were strong on price installability, but did not close magnitude on e-waste or neutralize the donor-value/theft path. CON’s Rebuttal 2 answers and closing crystallize a cleaner vote under the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1/B4.1 scaling + pairing-as-separate-deterrent answering A’s “no theft protection disabled” pressure: harms need less volume than benefits and pairing (not Activation Lock) raises stolen-part payoff.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B built a more disciplined, cumulative case. B's opening grounded claims in concrete, plausible real-world patterns (Apple's self-repair program, NY e-bike battery fires, the Massachusetts telematics dispute), giving the harms side texture that A's opening lacked. The decisive exchange came in Rebuttal 1/2: B exposed that A's price-reduction mechanism (third-party/refurbished parts flooding the market) is the same channel B says drives the fire and theft risk, and sharpened the security point by distinguishing activation lock (device-level) from parts pairing (component-level) — a distinction A never fully dissolved. When A introduced new safeguards (owner-authenticated calibration, donor-serial blocklists) in Rebuttal 2 to patch this, B correctly flagged these as aspirational mechanisms absent from any actual statute, turning A's own concession ("no enacted law has measurably cut prices") into a closing weapon: benefits are projected, harms are observed. A's closing reframe ("competition or lawlessness") was clean but didn't answer why the safeguards it relies on exist nowhere in current law, leaving B's "savings realized only where risks are paid" framing as the more convincing resolution of the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 point that A's own price mechanism (third-party/refurbished parts) is identical to B's risk mechanism, sharpened by distinguishing activation lock from parts pairing — a distinction A never cleanly rebutted and which B leveraged again in Rebuttal 2 to label A's new safeguards as unenacted aspiration.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B won by successfully merging Side A's benefit mechanisms into Side B's risk mechanisms, arguing that cheap third-party parts are precisely the uncertified components that cause fires. Side B also effectively dismantled Side A's security mitigations by pointing out that donor-part blocklists do not exist in current legislation and victims do not report component serial numbers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2 brilliantly reframed the debate by pointing out that Side A's price mechanism (cheap third-party parts) is exactly the channel through which Side B's harms (battery fires) flow.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B won by collapsing PRO’s price/e-waste pathway into the same third-party/parts channel that carries fire and theft-market risk, then forcing A to defend safeguards that were not shown to be in actual statutes and to concede no enacted law has yet shown measurable price or waste cuts. That left A’s five-year net-benefit case as projection against observed risk patterns. A’s Apple-voluntary-versus-mandated-competition distinction and authentication-preserving reframe were strong, but did not neutralize B’s statutory-and-timeline pressure or the two-tier-market weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s two-tier reply to A’s price/flood contradiction and the follow-on “safeguards not on the books” strike in Rebuttal 2/Closing—turning A’s savings mechanism into the risk mechanism while treating mid-debate calibration/blocklist details as aspirational rather than legal.
