  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive response was that universities can require nondiscrimination, research integrity, and clinical safety as conduct without demanding personalized declarations of belief. This undercut A’s central claim that prohibiting loyalty statements would disable basic academic safeguards.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): PRO maintained the decisive conduct vs belief distinction throughout. CON never answered why a behavioral rule needs a personal belief statement, leaving its central route - that the ban disarms protections for viewpoint diversity - neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 dismantling CON's equivalence between professional duties and loyalty oaths - "You must not discriminate" vs "You must articulate your commitment" [A2.4] and that contracts already regulate conduct without pledges [A4.4].

Side A's pressure questions exposed the weakness of Side B's redefinition, and their subsequent speeches cleanly crystallized the stakes, proving that standard employment contracts and civil rights laws are sufficient to regulate conduct without resorting to compelled speech.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where they sharply distinguished between behavioral rules (regulating conduct) and ideological loyalty statements (policing belief), effectively neutralizing Side B's overbreadth argument and exposing the absurdity of classifying clinical safety protocols as "ideological" pledges.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by consistently distinguishing compelled ideological affirmation from evaluating conduct, teaching ability, mentoring, and compliance with anti-discrimination rules. Side A’s litigation and ambiguity concerns had force, but they depended on treating ordinary questions about supporting students as loyalty statements, despite B repeatedly explaining that the motion bans demanded professions of belief rather than competency assessment. Side A never supplied a persuasive limiting principle for when ideological affirmation becomes impermissible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal directly answered the strongest overbreadth challenge: universities can examine a dean’s hiring, mentoring, funding, and enforcement record without requiring assent to a contested causal theory. Side A’s response that faculty must acknowledge structural inequity effectively reinforced B’s concern that a particular worldview could become an employment prerequisite.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A PRO won the central definitional clash. B argued ban would block Title IX/equity assessment and cause litigation, but A drew a clear, repeatable line: conduct/compliance/record vs compelled affirmation of contested theories (physics professor, dean evaluated by actions not oaths). A answered pressure directly with court doctrine and alternative evaluation, while B conceded the extreme example was poor practice and labeled it a strawman without refuting that it occurs. A crystallized First Amendment conduct/belief distinction; B never rehabilitated why that distinction fails.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1-A2.2 plus A4.1/ A4.3 where PRO turns CON's Title IX/vagueness attack into conduct vs belief test and shows hiring can be judged by record not oath - directly neutralizing CON's parade-of-litigation and pedagogical-competence routes.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A built a much cleaner and more principled case by strictly distinguishing between behavioral compliance (which is permissible) and compelled ideological belief (which is not). Side A's answers to the pressure questions were exceptionally sharp, particularly the point that a dean's commitment to women in STEM can be evaluated through their concrete track record and actions rather than requiring an oath to a specific sociological theory. Side B relied too heavily on a slippery slope argument about litigation and struggled to defend the most extreme versions of loyalty statements when pressed, making Side A's route to victory much clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled Side B's pressure questions by explaining that a dean's support for women in STEM should be measured by their actual hiring and mentoring record, not by forcing them to profess a specific ideological theory about the causes of gender disparity.
