### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): The debate turned on a dilemma CON (Side A) raised in its opening and never let go of: a right to disconnect is either optional, in which case non-disclosure becomes a negative signal and the "clean" workers PRO wants to protect still get pooled with risk, or mandatory, in which case portability for good actors disappears and bad actors gain a statutory tool for evasion. PRO (Side B) tried two escapes — "default severance so there's nothing to decline" and a "falsifiability plus process" test for what may travel — but CON's second rebuttal exposed that the falsifiability/process test is simply CON's own "reform the score, with notice, expiry, and contest" position restated, collapsing PRO's distinct "disconnect" right into a description of regulated data portability. PRO's "default severance" answer also never actually solved the volunteering-creates-inference problem, since strong applicants will still disclose references and leave non-disclosers exposed regardless of statutory inference bans. CON's whisper-network point (forbidding visible score pushes judgment into less accountable channels) also went substantively unanswered. On top of the substantive gap, PRO's speeches were repeatedly clipped for exceeding word limits at crucial junctures (end of opening, end of both rebuttals, and the closing), cutting off its own conclusions at exactly the moments weighing should have happened — a real execution cost under the judging rules. CON, by contrast, stayed within limits, maintained a consistent thesis (relevance, accuracy, notice, and time-limits as the proper fix) across every speech, and used the pressure-question round effectively to sharpen the same dilemma it opened with. This adds up to a clear, non-trivial win for CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point that PRO's "falsifiability plus process" exception for cross-context transfer is simply "reform, not disconnect," and that aggregating verified claims into a score doesn't become illegitimate just because it's composite, was the decisive exchange — it showed PRO's own refined standard had quietly converged on CON's proposed framework.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side A won by exposing an unresolved contradiction in B's model. If favorable histories remain voluntarily portable, withholding them becomes an adverse signal; if that inference is genuinely prevented, portability loses much of its value. A also gave a coherent alternative of relevance limits, notice, expiry, and pre-decision contestability, then showed that B's exceptions for specific, checkable conduct effectively concede regulated cross-context transfer. B's vivid case for severing opaque composite scores remained persuasive, but it did not establish why a broad right to disconnect was superior to A's targeted regulation. B's repeated clipped turns were an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered both pressure questions with a regulatory mechanism, then turned B's falsifiability-and-process exception into a concession that justified reputation may cross contexts. Its renewed portability-versus-silence dilemma remained unanswered.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by showing that PRO's default severance either fails to protect good workers from adverse inference or destroys the portability it claims to support, and that PRO's falsifiability-plus-process exception for fraud and specific claims is indistinguishable from CON's own reform alternative. CON's regulated-transfer model—relevance limits, notice, contestability, and expiry—more coherently addresses the harms of cross-context scoring without blinding legitimate decision-makers or pushing reputation into unaccountable whisper networks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 [A4.3-A4.5], which demonstrated that PRO's exception for specific checkable conduct claims traveling with notice, expiry, and contest is simply regulated reputation under another name, while the default break cannot simultaneously preserve voluntary portability and prevent non-disclosure from becoming a negative signal in competitive screening markets.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but A more effectively forced the decisive clash: whether the motion's text (a right to "disconnect," backed by non-penalization) actually matches B's eventual narrow construction (block only fused cross-domain algorithmic scores while leaving context-specific records, references, and bans untouched). A tracked B's own concessions across rounds (P2.5's admission that accurate data can be blocked, P4.1's carve-outs, P4.2's "algorithmic horn") and used them to show B was quietly defending a narrower relevance-limiting regime rather than the broad right as written. A also landed an adverse-selection/cost-shifting point (honest applicants subsidize exits via deposits and blanket screening) that B never squarely answered in its final turns, leaving a live, unrebutted cost on the table going into closing. B's case was well-framed, vivid, and grounded in plausible analogies (juvenile records, credit report aging), and it offered a workable definitional test for "algorithmic reputation system," but its repeated narrowing of scope gave A continual ammunition, and its closing recapped themes without resolving the cost-shifting or textual-breadth pressure. A's performance is weakened somewhat by two clipped turns (opening and Rebuttal 2) that cut off content before the judge could see it, an execution cost that trims A's margin, but the substantive content that did land was sharper and more responsive to B's live concessions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A2.3–A2.8), which turned B's own line "Disconnect is the backstop when even accurate data should not travel" into an admitted suppression concession, combined with the unanswered adverse-selection/cost-shifting argument, did the most damage to B's path to victory.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B narrowly won by grounding the right in cross-context algorithmic reuse rather than erasure of underlying records. Its distinction between relevant, purpose-specific checks and portable fused profiles answered much of Side A’s strongest breadth objection and preserved a clear autonomy and irrelevance harm. Side A raised serious unresolved concerns about adverse selection and relevant fraud warnings across platforms, but repeatedly characterized restricted automated reuse as a gag even though publication, references, and source records remained available. Side A’s two clipped turns were also a modest execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered both horns by separating source facts from portable algorithmic profiles and automated reuse from independent human inquiry. That made the proposed right look workable rather than like the universal amnesia regime Side A attacked, though cross-platform fraud sharing remained a live weakness.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B successfully defended a textually grounded, narrow reading of the motion that targets cross-context algorithmic fusion rather than all records, while Side A’s broader interpretation strained the text. B cleanly answered A’s definitional pressure in Rebuttal 2 with a workable algorithmic/portable distinction and neutralized adverse-selection fears by showing context-specific checks remain available. A’s case was undermined by two clipped speeches and an overreach that equated disconnect with a gag on truthful warnings—a framing B persuasively rebutted by showing source facts and references stay lawful while only automated cross-domain scoring is blocked. B’s closing crystallized the clash better: the core harm is accurate data misused forever in the wrong place, not merely bad data, and only a legal right prevents compulsory surrender of lifelong fused scores.
