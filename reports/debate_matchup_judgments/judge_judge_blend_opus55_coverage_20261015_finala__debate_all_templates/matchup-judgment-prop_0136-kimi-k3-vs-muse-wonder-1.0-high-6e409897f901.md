### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Both sides built genuinely competitive cases using the same East Asian examples (Japan, Korea, Singapore) to opposite ends, which made this a tight debate. CON's strongest throughline was deliverability: structural fixes are slow, often fail to materialize (Seoul housing worsened despite decades of policy), and cash can hit the fertility window now. PRO's strongest throughline was that CON's own mechanism for cash — "pull first births earlier so seconds become possible" — still depends on recurring structural support to convert into a second child, a point CON conceded ground on (A4.1's hedge "not pure timing, particularly for second births" was never developed) and B exploited decisively in the closing ("timing buys the opportunity — only structure converts it"). The pressure-question exchange also favored PRO: when asked to name a place where a bonus raised completed cohort fertility, CON could only argue the test can't yet be run, while PRO offered a more substantive (if contestable) answer citing the Japan–Korea divergence and municipal childcare patterns. CON's rebuttal that PRO's "deliverable" housing fixes (rental allowances, queue priority) are themselves disguised cash transfers was sharp, but PRO's closing partially neutralized it by reframing recurring monthly relief as structurally different from a one-off payment. On balance, PRO crystallized the decisive mechanism more clearly and handled pressure more directly, giving it a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing move isolating that CON's own "earlier first births enable second births" mechanism still requires ongoing structural support to convert into completed fertility — a point CON never squarely rebutted after conceding "pulling planned births forward" was their best mechanism.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully arguing that while cash bonuses might shift the timing of the first birth, the structural conditions of housing and childcare are required to convert that timing into a second child. Side A effectively leveraged the Japan-Korea divergence to prove structural impacts and cornered Side B into admitting their "large bonus" solution is untested in the region.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in Rebuttal 2 and Closing that "Timing buys the opportunity — only structure converts it" perfectly neutralized Side B's best argument about the biological window.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): B won a close, high-quality debate by keeping the decisive comparison on completed fertility rather than speed of payout. A’s deliverability and late-first-birth timing arguments were sharp and forced real concessions, but B repeatedly converted A’s own second-birth logic into a structural requirement (space and recurring care), showed Korea’s long bonus record as a failed timing-only experiment, and answered pressure with concrete in-window housing/care instruments. A’s late “Korea only tried small bonuses” defense partly undercut its claim, while B’s weighing—bonuses shift when; structure decides how many—stayed cleaner through the close.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, mechanism-based cases, but CON (A) was consistently sharper in comparative weighing. A's opening used concrete, plausible real-world cases (Korea, Singapore, Nordics failing despite huge housing/childcare investment vs. Russia/Hungary/Quebec rebounds from cash) to ground the "will" framing of the motion—that housing/childcare reform is undeliverable in practice, while cash transfers are statute-and-bank-transfer fast. PRO (B) offered a sound structural rebuttal (under-delivery vs. lever failure, Nordic floor still double Korea's, Akashi as a delivered example), and effectively answered pressure questions with A1/A2 formatting. But B's own best examples (Russia, Hungary) were shown to be housing/education-restricted cash, which A turned into a decisive point: if cash-as-housing is what works, cash delivers that mechanism faster than construction ever has. B raised the "defend the cheque as written" distinction but never fully closed that door, and its closing failed to answer A's "will"/deliverability framing or the charge that Akashi's free services are themselves cash-equivalents undermining B's lone empirical case. A's final closing crystallized this cleanly and went unanswered, giving it the stronger, more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 turn on Russia/Hungary ("the active ingredient was the payment, not the concrete... their distinction is bookkeeping, not mechanism") combined with the closing line "conversion is my point" was the most decisive exchange, neutralizing B's strongest evidence and reframing it as support for cash's deliverability advantage.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side A won by effectively neutralizing Side B's best empirical examples (Russia and Hungary), successfully arguing that they functioned as long-term housing policies rather than pure one-off cash bonuses. Side A also cleanly answered Side B's biological timing argument by pointing out that extra fertile years do not produce a second child if the couple is still trapped in a cramped rental.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and Closing brilliantly reframed Side B's success stories (Russia's maternity capital and Hungary's loan forgiveness) as proof that long-term housing costs are the true bottleneck, structurally damaging Side B's defense of unrestricted one-off cheques.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a competitive debate by making the motion’s “will” decisive: deliverability and observed East Asian under-delivery (Korea/Singapore) remained live after exchange, while cash had clearer short-run rebounds and a coherent late-age timing/biology path. Side B had the cleaner mechanism story and stronger contamination of Russia/Hungary as housing-linked instruments, plus a solid Akashi answer, but never closed the scale gap between aspirational supply levers and what governments have actually pulled.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Russia/Hungary reclassification (housing-restricted capital / forgiven housing debt ≠ pure one-off cheques) was the sharpest single hit; A’s follow-through on “will”/undelivered bottlenecks still decided the comparison.
