### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases—A on physical non-equivalence and moral hazard, B on cost-efficiency and Global South capital flows—but A's pressure questions landed a genuine blow. A's Q1 forced B to either defend or abandon its Rebuttal 1 claim that firms would "finance both simultaneously" (expensive domestic retrofits plus cheap offsets). B's A1 answer effectively conceded that no executive would pay for retrofits while unlimited cheap offsets exist, which directly undercut B's own prior argument and validated A's moral-hazard thesis. B never repaired this contradiction in Rebuttal 2 or Closing, instead re-asserting efficiency framing without reconciling it with the admitted incentive problem. A also responded more directly to B's Global South capital-flow argument by pointing to manufacturing-heavy value chains (phones, apparel, data centers), while B's claim that most value chains are domestic was only partially defended. B's permanence defense (verified renewables/gas destruction) was a fair counter to A's biological-storage critique, but it arrived late and didn't fully neutralize A's baseline-manipulation point, which A kept pressing throughout. Overall A had the tighter, more exploited clash and a cleaner closing narrative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Q1 ("why would any profit-driven executive pay... when the law lets them buy the exact same badge for pennies") exposed a real contradiction in B's Rebuttal 1 claim that firms would simultaneously fund cheap offsets and expensive retrofits—B's answer essentially conceded the point, and A capitalized on it in Rebuttal 2 and Closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that unlimited offsets undermine the very in-value-chain transition net-zero requires. Side A offered a credible cost-efficiency case, but assumed rather than demonstrated that firms would later fund expensive internal changes, inadequately answered additionality and permanence concerns, and mislabeled avoided renewable emissions as permanently neutralizing continued fossil emissions. B also effectively answered the hard-to-abate and Global South objections, though its nonexistent reference IDs and late numerical benchmark were minor execution costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B decisively exploited Side A’s concession that executives will not fund expensive retrofits while cheap offsets provide the same net-zero badge. That concession validated B’s crowding-out mechanism and weakened A’s unsupported claim that external credits and internal transition would proceed simultaneously.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by effectively dismantling Side A's core economic efficiency argument. Side B correctly identified that allowing unlimited cheap offsets destroys the incentive for corporations to decarbonize their own operations, a point Side A inadvertently conceded. Side B also successfully countered Side A's geographic arguments by pointing out that global value chains inherently include the Global South, meaning in-value-chain reductions still direct capital to developing economies but for durable industrial upgrades rather than fragile offset projects. Side B's framing of "net-zero" as requiring actual operational decarbonization rather than just funding external reductions was much more aligned with the physical and practical realities of climate policy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal in Rebuttal 2 and the closing, where it seized on Side A's concession that executives won't pay for expensive internal retrofits if cheap offsets are available, framing this not as a flaw of the cap, but as the exact moral hazard the cap is designed to eliminate.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B landed the more decisive exchanges. A's strongest moves—moral hazard, additionality/verification concerns—were real, but A's defense of the cap under pressure revealed internal tension: claiming that a firm emitting two tons while permanently removing two tons is still "growing carbon debt" conflates absolute output growth with net atmospheric addition, and B caught this directly in [B4.4]. A's claim that companies abandoning pledges "cools the planet" was an overclaim B exploited sharply and memorably in closing ("canceling a press release does not cool the planet"). B also used A's own airline example against it (jet fuel as the overwhelming majority of aviation emissions, not a residual sliver) and consistently reframed the debate around atmospheric impact versus "semantic purity," which is the more outcome-relevant lens for judging a motion about legal policy. A never fully resolved why verified, permanent, ton-for-ton removal should still be capped once quality is assured, repeatedly falling back on "moral hazard" without addressing the scarcity-of-removal-capacity argument that could have shored this up. Both sides handled the Q&A format correctly, but B's questions exposed sharper tensions and A's answers, while direct, left the central contradiction (quality vs. quantity) unresolved through to the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's [B4.4]-[B4.6] exchange, catching A's claim that a firm removing exactly as much as it emits is still "growing carbon debt," and reframing the tradeoff as real climate finance versus marketing-label purity, was the most decisive moment, reinforced in B's closing line that "canceling a press release does not cool the planet."
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by consistently comparing physical atmospheric outcomes rather than merely claim integrity. It showed that the proposal regulates a voluntary label, not emissions, so hard-to-abate firms can exit the claim while continuing to emit and ceasing offset purchases. Side B never established why firms would instead transform or keep financing projects without receiving credit, and its assertion that dropping a misleading label itself cools the planet was unsupported. Side A incurred a minor execution cost from several nonexistent P-reference labels and somewhat overstated how much finance would disappear, but its core comparison remained decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange answered Side B’s concession that ton-for-ton permanent removal makes the math correct. A showed that two tons emitted and two permanently removed still yield zero net atmospheric addition, whereas exiting the voluntary claim can leave the same gross emissions with no removals. Side B’s moral-hazard framing did not rebut that direct physical comparison.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins decisively by exposing the practical flaw in Side B's mechanism. Because the proposition regulates the "net-zero" label rather than mandating absolute operational cuts, Side A convincingly argues that hard-to-abate industries will simply drop the label ("greenhushing") rather than fund uncredited offsets as corporate "philanthropy." Side A's distinction between regulating offset quality versus quantity is highly effective, demonstrating that capping high-quality, permanent removals sacrifices actual atmospheric benefit for semantic purity. Side B's reliance on idealized corporate behavior and the argument that dropping the label is an "honest admission" fails to overcome Side A's core physical reality: canceling a marketing claim does not reduce emissions, but it does evaporate the private climate finance needed for carbon removal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.4-A4.6), where they dismantle Side B's concession that firms might "exit the claim." Side A clearly contrasts the physical outcomes: under Side B's cap, a firm emits two tons and removes zero to avoid the label; under Side A's quality standard, the firm emits two tons and permanently removes two tons to keep the label, resulting in a vastly superior atmospheric outcome.
