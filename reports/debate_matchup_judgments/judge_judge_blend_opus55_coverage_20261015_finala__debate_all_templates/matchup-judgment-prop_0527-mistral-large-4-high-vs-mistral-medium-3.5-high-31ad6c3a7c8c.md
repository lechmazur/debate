### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): A built a tighter, more resilient case: the macro-level framing (no AMC = no drugs at all, so high prices are the cost of any supply) was consistently defended and never squarely dismantled. Crucially, in the pressure-question exchange A demanded a marketed antibiotic from delinkage/prize models; B's answer (zoliflodacin) conceded it is still in Phase 3 trials, not marketed—A capitalized on this directly in closing to show B's key counter-evidence actually proves the pipeline-empty problem rather than solving it. B's strongest live point was the structural critique that AMC pre-purchase removes marginal cost at the point of care, undermining PRO's claim that high prices themselves enforce stewardship, and B pressed this hard as a "fatal contradiction" in closing. But A's answer—that price gatekeeps system-level overuse while protocols govern clinical-level appropriateness—offered a plausible non-contradictory division of labor that B never fully closed off, only reasserted rhetorically. B's equity and opportunity-cost arguments were real but stayed somewhat abstract and were answered (if not perfectly) by A's "cannot negotiate access to drugs that don't exist" framing. On balance, A answered the decisive pressure point more concretely and ended with a cleaner, more memorable decision rule, giving it a clear but not overwhelming edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure question demanding a marketed antibiotic from delinkage, and B's answer naming zoliflodacin while admitting it is still in Phase 3 trials — which A then used in closing to show B's own evidence undercuts its claim that alternatives already work at scale.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by sustaining the central market-failure argument: stewardship suppresses sales, so guaranteed revenue is needed to keep antibiotic development viable. Side A sharply exposed that pre-purchase can eliminate the claimed point-of-use price deterrent, forcing B to rely on stewardship protocols instead. But that damaged only B's secondary conservation claim, not the core case for permitting AMCs. A's proposed alternative remained incomplete: when directly challenged to identify a marketed antibiotic delivered at scale through delinkage, A offered a candidate still in trials. A's closing then overfocused on the price-signal contradiction and neglected its stronger equity and opportunity-cost arguments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's most decisive response was that zoliflodacin remains in trials rather than demonstrating marketed, scalable delinkage. This turned Side A's direct answer into confirmation that its alternative had not yet matched the supply outcome needed to displace AMCs.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins because its core diagnosis of market failure and the urgency of empty pipelines remained the governing frame. While Side A landed a sharp internal critique on stewardship—showing that government pre-purchasing neutralizes price signals at the point of care—this did not ultimately overcome Side B's independent argument that high unit prices are mathematically necessary to fund innovation across deliberately low volumes. Side A's alternative of delinkage, while substantiated by one late-stage candidate, never established a scaled, market-ready replacement, leaving Side B's "no supply, no access" imperative intact. Side B also handled pressure questions more directly, and Side A overstated the stewardship "contradiction" where complementarity is plausible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's turn that AMC pre-purchasing eliminates the marginal cost signal at the hospital level, directly undermining Side B's claim that high unit prices enforce conservation. Side B's response that protocols complement price was adequate but never fully resolved why high prices were justified as a stewardship tool if the AMC structure removes the cost barrier precisely where prescribing decisions are made.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B landed and sustained the single most damaging structural critique: PRO's own flagship evidence (the UK subscription model) is a delinked, availability-based mechanism that explicitly moves away from the high-unit-price, volume-linked architecture the motion asks us to permit. B pressed this "mutually exclusive architectures" point in Rebuttal 1, Pressure Questions, and Rebuttal 2, and PRO's answer—that "AMCs are flexible by design" and high prices merely reflect value without driving volume—never fully dissolved the tension, since PRO kept citing a model whose success stems from decoupling price from payment. PRO also never squarely answered B's "category error" point (vaccines vs. antibiotics) raised in B2.2, instead re-citing the same pneumococcal precedent in Rebuttal 2. PRO's consequentialist framing (pipeline collapse, catastrophic resistance deaths) remained strong and under-rebutted in isolation, but B's closing crystallized why "permit high prices" and "the only proven working model" are in tension, leaving a cleaner, more decisive route to the voter.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's repeated point (B2.1, B3.1, B4.1) that the UK delinked model PRO relies on actually abandons the high-unit-price/volume-linked structure the motion defends was the most decisive exchange; PRO's flexible-design answer mitigated but never fully closed this gap.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that high unit prices need not make developer revenue depend on prescription volume: AMC rewards can be tied to availability, innovation, and stewardship while access is supported through tiering and pooled procurement. Side A effectively attacked mandatory, permanent, volume-linked monopoly pricing, which the proposition did not require, and never established that its preferred alternatives could replace AMCs at scale. B occasionally overstated the evidence, but retained the clearer innovation-versus-inaction comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response to the stewardship objection was decisive: it separated the guaranteed return from units prescribed, making high per-dose prices compatible with low usage. This exposed Side A’s repeated assumption that every permitted AMC must reward sales volume.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing a fatal tension in PRO’s case: PRO cited the UK delinked subscription model as proof AMCs work, but that model explicitly avoids high unit prices, undermining the proposition’s core premise that high prices must be accepted. CON also landed a damaging category error attack on PRO’s pneumococcal precedent and showed that high unit prices breed global resistance through rationing, negating PRO’s equity response. PRO’s evasive answer to CON’s pressure question about per-dose incentives—retreating to delinked models rather than defending high unit prices—confirmed the contradiction. While PRO’s economic intuition had some force, CON successfully shifted the debate to whether high unit prices destroy the very value of antibiotics, and PRO never recovered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s turn of the UK pilot and pneumococcal precedent: showing that PRO’s best example avoided high unit prices while the vaccine analogy failed the antibiotic stewardship test, collapsing PRO’s track-record and stewardship defenses simultaneously.
