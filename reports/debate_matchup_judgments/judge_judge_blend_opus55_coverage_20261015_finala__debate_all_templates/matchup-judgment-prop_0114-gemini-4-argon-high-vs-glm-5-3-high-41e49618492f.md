### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON (A) landed the decisive blow: it exposed a real contradiction in PRO's credit design, which was narrowed to "time-limited, prior-attachment" credits to dodge fraud/fiscal objections in Rebuttal 1, but then had to become "extended, intensity-scaled" credits in Rebuttal 2 to cover the decade-long caregivers PRO had championed. PRO never resolved this tension beyond calling it a "design conversation," which left the audit/exit-subsidy critique live. A also gave more concrete, immediate answers to the pressure question about what happens before retirement (home-health aides, respite care today) versus PRO's deferred pension-credit payoff, which PRO itself conceded pays "zero cash until retirement." PRO's framing (ledger, handrail, "extraction") was rhetorically vivid and its fiscal-precedent argument (military/parental-leave credits) was solid, but its closing mostly reasserted the symbolic framing rather than repairing the exposed design contradiction. PRO's opening was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost. On balance, CON made the clash easier to resolve and more convincingly tied policy mechanism to outcome.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3-4.4), catching PRO's shift from "time-limited, prior-attachment" credits to "extended, intensity-scaled" credits and showing PRO could not have both narrow verifiability and full coverage of long-term caregivers without reviving the very fraud/exit-subsidy problems it had claimed to solve.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): A won a competitive debate by keeping the motion’s core comparison live: residual unpaid care still occurs after any services build-out, and only credits write that labor into the same pension ledger that already credits military service and parental leave. B landed real damage on career-exit subsidies, auditing, and A’s shifting guardrails, but never neutralized residual care or showed that a flat floor (equal for non-caregivers) escapes “invisibility.” A’s pressure answers and closing crystallization made the choice easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s residual-care weighing (services reduce volume but never erase the ledger zero) vs B’s later exposure of A’s time-limit/attachment contradiction.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully anchoring the debate to the specific mechanism and language of the motion. The decisive clash was over Side A's alternative of a universal pension floor. Side B effectively demonstrated that a flat universal floor pays a decade-long dementia caregiver the exact same amount as someone who never performed care work, thereby officially preserving the "invisibility" the motion explicitly seeks to eliminate. While Side A raised strong practical concerns about administrative burdens and workforce exit, Side B's rebuttals—that all social programs require eligibility lines and that social-insurance credits can carry interim health coverage—were sufficient to neutralize these attacks. Side B's rhetorical framing of "recognition versus extraction" and "counting the work versus erasing it" cleanly captured the core stakes of the proposition, whereas Side A's attempt to redefine "visibility" as "providing services today" felt like a pivot away from the pension credit mechanism specified in the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's universal pension floor alternative in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, pointing out that a flat floor fails to distinguish care labor from non-care labor, thus leaving the core harm of the motion (treating care as invisible) entirely unresolved.
