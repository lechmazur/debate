### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing that OS control over local permissions does not translate into reliable, surgical control over recommendations generated on remote servers. Side A repeatedly asserted auditing, browser filtering, privacy-preserving credentials, and crisis exemptions without adequately explaining how they would distinguish harmful feeds from search, education, and peer support. Side B also preserved the abuse and older-teen autonomy impacts against those exemptions. Side A framed corporate exploitation powerfully, but its proposed mechanism remained the weaker link.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest response was that technical weakness and coercive power are compatible: the system can fail to identify harmful algorithms while still enabling crude app and browser lockouts. This directly defeated Side A's claimed contradiction and unified B's feasibility and abuse objections.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): CON maintained three live routes that PRO never neutralized: OS cannot surgically audit remote ML feeds, so non-bypassable enforcement forces either blind trust in developers or blunt browser/app lockouts; mandatory OS age beacons harm privacy; and non-bypassable age gates let controlling parents isolate 17-year-olds from peer forums, not just hotlines. PRO's answers relied on asserted auditing and crisis exemptions without explaining how to preserve search/adaptive learning or forum discovery. The blunt-instrument reframe resolved the alleged contradiction and won the weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1-B2.2 and B4.3: camera-permission false equivalence, search is itself algorithmic, browser loophole dilemma, and the blunt-instrument pathology that an OS can fail at surgical curation while succeeding at crude isolation.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by decisively dismantling Side B's technical mechanism. Side A correctly points out that an operating system manages local hardware and cannot audit proprietary, real-time machine learning models on remote cloud servers, rendering Side B's "API-verifiable" and "app store audit" claims technically brittle. Side A also effectively defends the vulnerable teen impact, noting that non-bypassable age limits allow abusive parents to lock teens out of peer support networks, and sharply rebuts Side B's "confiscation" defense by noting controlling parents use devices for tracking. Side B's rhetoric was strong, but its core mechanism relied on technical assumptions that Side A successfully exposed as unworkable without causing massive collateral damage to the open web and educational tools.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantles Side B's claim that app stores can audit cloud algorithms, explaining that this is the exact pathology of a blunt instrument that forces scorched-earth lockouts, and refutes the crisis exemption by explaining how vulnerable teens actually use peer forums rather than just dialing hotlines.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A narrowly won by presenting a workable middle ground: accurate age tiers and limits on behavioral profiling while preserving search, messaging, and contextual discovery. B raised serious concerns about abusive parents and centralized enforcement, but repeatedly treated “cannot easily bypass” as an airtight information blockade and did not fully answer A’s narrower, content-neutral model. A’s nonexistent reference IDs and weak claim that stronger controls give abusive parents no new power kept the result close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A most effectively turned B’s distinction between contextual discovery and exploitative profiling into support for A’s model: topic-based recommendations could remain while watch-history and dwell-time profiling were disabled. B’s strongest response was exposing A’s initial neglect of age-limit misuse, but A’s birth-date-linked tier clarification substantially narrowed that objection.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won the central recommendation clash: A showed disabling behavioral profiling is distinct from blocking info, keeps search/messaging/community, and directly counters self-harm spirals, while CON's lifeline argument requires the same profiling. CON's strongest age-tier abuse point was blunted by A1/A4 - parents already have total hardware control, and an OS flag is a less-invasive middle ground than bans or spyware, with age tied to birthdate and 18+ gating. B never solved how app loopholes protect teens whose parents control the device.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1/2 move that algorithmic discovery can't infer unstated struggles without exploitative behavioral profiling - exactly what drives harms - plus the Do Not Track / tracking-ban analogy for enforceable, viewpoint-neutral OS flag.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B built a much stronger and more technically plausible case. They successfully differentiated between blocking access and blocking behavioral profiling, neutralizing Side A's "information vacuum" argument. Side B also effectively countered the "abusive parent" argument by pointing out that hardware control already exists, making the OS switch a middle ground rather than a new threat. Side A's admission that app-level bypasses are "essential safety valves" was a strategic misstep that Side B capitalized on to show Side A was defending corporate engagement loops over parental authority.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal to Side A's claim that teens need algorithmic discovery to find support they cannot articulate. Side B pointed out that an app cannot infer unstated struggles without the exact behavioral profiling that fuels self-harm spirals, turning Side A's defense of algorithms against them.
