### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but PRO (B) kept tighter control of the actual motion—the "when possible" qualifier—repeatedly forcing CON to admit that its strongest arguments (24/7 staffing cost, abuse risk, continuous supervision) applied mainly to extreme cases already carved out by the motion, not the general default. CON's rebuttals never cleanly answered PRO's Pressure Question about why round-the-clock institutional care would be cheaper than targeted hourly home support for the majority of seniors, instead reframing around "crisis cost" without engaging the arithmetic. Worse, CON's answer to Q2 conceded that its model requires "early facility placement... while they still have cognitive reserve to adapt"—a concession PRO exploited devastatingly in closing, reframing CON's position as mandating premature loss of autonomy years before necessity. CON's closing reasserted the "default/burden-of-proof" framing but never neutralized this admission or the cost-math gap, leaving PRO's comparative story clearer and more resolved at the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing use of CON's own "early facility placement isn't uprooting—it's continuity" line from Rebuttal 2 was the decisive moment: it turned CON's attempt to answer a pressure question into an admission that institutional-default care means stripping autonomy before it is actually needed, which CON never recovered from.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A won by consistently enforcing the motion’s “when possible” qualifier and distinguishing supported home care from unpaid family abandonment. Side B raised legitimate transition and safety concerns, but repeatedly assumed that low-needs elders require continuous one-on-one supervision and ultimately defended early institutional placement. A provided the clearer proportional rule: match support to present needs, monitor decline professionally, and transition when home care is no longer safe.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s decisive response was that Side B’s cost and safety case depended on treating every elder as needing 24/7 supervision. A then turned B’s defense of “early facility placement” into a clear consequence of the institutional default: sacrificing capable years and autonomy to insure against future decline.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): B won by holding the motion's actual text—"when possible" and organize around supporting home care—against A's slide into preemptive institutionalization for progressive cases. B repeatedly showed that family burnout and crisis transitions are costs of unfunded status-quo home care, not of the funded support the motion endorses, and crystallized A's "early facility placement" concession as guaranteeing loss of autonomy years before 24/7 need. A raised real intermittent-vs-continuous and default-burden pressures, but underweighted the large low-need population and treated edge cases as the governing default.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4: unsupported family collapse is not the motion; "when possible" plus funded aides/respite matches needs and monitors decline without preemptive uprooting.

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): The debate's decisive clash was A's labor-scarcity/staffing-scale argument against B's "properly funded home care" vision. A pressed this hard across every round, forcing B to admit that adequate support could require "overnight aides" and "24/7 support"—which A then used to expose an apparent tension with B's earlier framing of support as merely "part-time" in the pressure round. B never squarely answered the workforce-scarcity math, instead reasserting that investment would simply solve the problem and pivoting back to the normative burden-of-proof frame. That frame is rhetorically strong but B's closing still hadn't neutralized A's core mechanism claim that dispersed one-on-one care is logistically unsustainable at scale while pooled institutional staffing multiplies caregiver reach. A also executed sharper, more specific rebuttals (quoting B's own language back at them, e.g. the "strawman" vs "24/7" tension) and maintained a consistent causal story about economies of scale and visibility/safety. B's case on dignity, institutionalization syndrome, and burden of proof remained compelling but increasingly abstract once feasibility was challenged, and B's answers to pressure questions were adequate but didn't fully resolve the scaling problem A kept foregrounding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1), which caught B's shift from dismissing 24/7 staffing as a "strawman" in the pressure round to then promising "overnight aides" and "24/7 support" to answer the 2 a.m. safety question—exposing an unresolved tension in B's feasibility claims that B's closing did not fully repair.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): A won narrowly by better matching the qualified proposition. B compellingly showed that intensive home care can exhaust scarce labor, but repeatedly treated high-risk elders needing continuous assistance as representative of all cases. A preserved a coherent home-first rule with proactive assessment and institutional exceptions. B's case supported institutions when home care is unsafe or impractical, not making them the default whenever home care remains feasible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange concerned nighttime care. B's 2:00 a.m. challenge exposed a real scalability problem, but B overstated A's answer by turning care that “may be part-time” into a promise of only part-time care. B had the stronger moral framing on dignity and burden of proof, but never neutralized A’s scale arithmetic or the 2:00 a.m. gap without promising the continuous one-on-one staffing B earlier dismissed. A’s like-with-like comparison and contradiction capture left the cleaner reason to reject organizing the system around home care as the default.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 (A4.1/A4.3): pinning B’s shift from calling 24/7 one-on-one a strawman to then requiring overnight/24-7 home aides, and converting that into the workforce-scale dilemma.
