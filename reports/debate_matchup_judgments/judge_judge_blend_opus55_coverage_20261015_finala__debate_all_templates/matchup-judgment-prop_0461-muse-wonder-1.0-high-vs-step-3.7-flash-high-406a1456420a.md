### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): A built a single, well-developed structural dilemma—that any mandate must either preserve flexible, independent discovery (risking re-identification of rare vulnerable cohorts via differencing) or lock researchers into pre-registered, platform-mediated queries (making "independence" nominal and self-auditing in substance)—and sustained it across every round. The pressure questions round was decisive: A's Q1/Q2 forced B to specify who defines cohorts and who runs the accreditation body, and B's detailed answers (pre-registered protocols, ethics-board review, cross-stakeholder accreditation) effectively conceded the trade-off A had been pressing, which A then exploited cleanly in Rebuttal 2 ("pre-registration... ends flexible discovery... keep it and independence means permission to ask pre-approved questions"). B's case leaned heavily on the Instagram example and the DSA precedent to establish urgency and feasibility, which are strong rhetorical anchors, but B never squarely resolved the reidentification/differencing mechanism beyond asserting that calibrated privacy budgets and synthetic data make risk "negligible," and never fully answered why a platform-administered or board-administered aggregation pipeline isn't just relocated capture. B's Rebuttal 1 being clipped for exceeding the word limit is also a real execution cost that cut off a developing point. A's closing crystallized the unresolved tension precisely, while B's closing largely restated the "false tradeoff" framing without directly defeating the self-auditing/narrow-discovery critique. On balance, A provided the cleaner, more rigorously defended path to a verdict.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 response to B's pressure-question answers (A4.3-A4.4): pointing out that B's own safeguards—pre-registered protocols and a credentialed accreditation board—confirm rather than resolve A's core dilemma, since they trade away flexible discovery for privacy and relocate gatekeeping rather than eliminating it.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won by establishing a persistent privacy-utility-discovery tradeoff that Side A mostly denied rather than resolved. A’s proposed safeguards were responsive, but pre-registration and privacy budgets restricted exploratory research, while accreditation preserved the gatekeeping problem. B also offered a comparatively narrower alternative through compelled, confidential audits. A’s clipped rebuttal and overconfident claims that safeguards “eliminate” risk further weakened its case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s decisive response was that A’s own safeguards prove the dilemma: fixed protocols prevent flexible discovery, but loosening them restores re-identification and manipulation risks. This directly converted A’s proposed solution into support for B’s central objection.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively exposing the inherent trade-off between privacy and flexible discovery, particularly for rare and vulnerable cohorts. When Side B proposed pre-registered protocols and strict querying bans to solve the re-identification risk, Side A sharply demonstrated that this destroys the independence and flexibility required to discover unexpected harms, turning the mandate into a scripted, gatekept regime. Side A also provided a more plausible defense of its alternative (targeted audits via subpoena) and effectively capitalized on Side B's logical concessions and a clipped rebuttal turn.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it used Side B's own answers (pre-registered protocols and bans on ad-hoc querying) to prove that the proposed mandate sacrifices independent discovery for scripted, gatekept data, effectively neutralizing Side B's claim that the privacy-oversight trade-off is eliminated.
