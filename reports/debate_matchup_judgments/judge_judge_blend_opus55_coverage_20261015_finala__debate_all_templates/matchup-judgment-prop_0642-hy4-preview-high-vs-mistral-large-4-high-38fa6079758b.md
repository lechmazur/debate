### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases—CON on externality/spillover harms from under-provision, PRO on hidden costs and inefficiency of mandated minimums—but PRO sustained a tighter, more mechanistically grounded rebuttal arc. PRO's recurring point that mandated stalls are often bundled, empty, or insufficient for two-car households (so minimums don't even reliably prevent spillover) was never squarely resolved by CON, whose best answer ("unbundle the cost") implicitly conceded the point while still defending the floor. PRO's answers to CON's pressure questions (permit-district priority for residents, impact fees/spillover plans as developer liability) were concrete and directly responsive, whereas CON's answer to PRO's parallel question about ceiling flexibility leaned on an asserted "political difficulty" claim without much support. CON's case also suffered an execution cost: Rebuttal 1 was clipped mid-sentence before its strongest closing line landed, weakening the delivery of its asymmetry-of-harm argument at a key moment. The debate stayed genuinely contested through both pressure rounds and closings, but PRO narrowed the dispute more effectively and left fewer open threads.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 point that minimums don't actually guarantee off-street parking use (bundled/empty/insufficient stalls) directly undercut CON's central claim that minimums "internalize" parking demand, and CON's response (blaming bundling while still defending the mandate) never fully closed that gap.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that a maximum is a calibrated ceiling, not a prohibition or zero-parking mandate. Side A’s core scenario depended on developers providing no parking while still collecting the same rent, but it never adequately answered B’s point that tenants, lenders, resale incentives, and curb restrictions make parking valuable in car-dependent markets. B also weighed the unavoidable housing, land, and driving costs of excessive minimums against spillover that can be addressed directly. A made strong points about inelastic car demand and physical shortages, but treated every ceiling as necessarily below need and its first rebuttal was clipped.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s challenge that ceilings can be varied just as minimums can be calibrated was decisive. A answered that floors provide a safer buffer, but did not establish why adaptable ceilings must remain below demand or why costly overbuilding is merely harmless surplus.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B wins a closely contested debate by successfully defending the flexibility of maximums (as ceilings, not bans) and providing concrete mechanisms (residential permits, impact fees) that neutralize Side A's primary offense regarding developer free-riding and neighborhood spillover. Side A argues well on the asymmetry of harm, but Side B exposes a logical gap in Side A's case: if minimums require unbundling to avoid empty stalls (as Side A concedes), those unbundled stalls will still result in street spillover unless the street is managed, effectively forcing Side A to rely on the very curb management solutions they criticized Side B for using.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's answers in Rebuttal 2 regarding impact fees and residential permits cleanly neutralized Side A's pressure questions about developer liability and rationing by income.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): A built a consistent structural argument—that a maximum is a ceiling, never a floor, and therefore cannot guarantee a development absorbs the parking demand it creates—and pressed this point relentlessly across every round. Crucially, A exploited the motion's "even in car-dependent regions" qualifier effectively: surface lots are cheap in these areas, undercutting B's central affordability/hidden-tax argument exactly where the motion demands PRO prove its case hardest, while the spillover/externality risk (unpriced curb space as a free commons) is most acute there. B's main counters—that market discipline (vacancy risk, rent premiums) will make developers build adequate parking anyway, and that curb pricing can solve spillover—never fully closed this gap. The vacancy-risk argument doesn't address that spillover costs fall on neighbors and taxpayers, not the developer, and the curb-pricing fix was rightly flagged by A as a policy outside the motion's actual mechanism, something B never squarely resolved. A's pressure question exposed real tension in B's "high caps rarely bind" framing, and A's answers to B's questions directly engaged the externality point rather than dodging it. Both sides executed the format well, but A's tighter, more consistently weighted argument made the debate easier to decide in its favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 point that B's defense ("cities can price curb parking and issue permits") is a companion policy outside the actual motion, forcing B to concede in Rebuttal 2 that "nothing stops a capped development from using public streets"—effectively admitting the externality problem A raised was never resolved by the proposition as written.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by identifying the unresolved curb-parking externality. In car-dependent regions with free street parking, developers may save money by underproviding spaces while shifting costs onto neighbors and taxpayers. Side B relied on vacancy and rent incentives but conceded that nothing directly prevents spillover, and its proposed curb pricing was outside the policy change. A also exposed the weak calibration dilemma: a loose maximum mainly removes the floor, while a binding one risks constraining needed supply.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange was its answer to B’s market-discipline claim: because curb parking is a free commons, renters can still occupy units while developers externalize parking demand. This directly undercut B’s assertion that vacancies would reliably force adequate provision.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively leveraging the "car-dependent regions" clause to explain why market forces would fail without minimums. A's argument that developers will underbuild because unpriced street parking acts as a free substitute cleanly neutralized B's reliance on market discipline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal that the market cannot discipline developers when the true cost (the curb) is free decisively undermined B's core "market incentives" mechanism.
