### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, with A landing a genuinely sharp logical tension (the "pincer" effect of paying the levy on inputs while facing retaliatory tariffs on exports, and the claim that "leverage" and "hold firm" cannot both be true). But B answered that tension directly (holding firm is what makes the threat of retaliation non-credible in the long run) and, crucially, exploited A's concession that voluntary alliances only cover "willing partners," leaving the core leakage problem against unwilling competitors unresolved—a gap A never closed, instead retreating to "cheaper power and faster permitting," which doesn't answer the asymmetric-cost mechanism. B's closing cleverly repurposed A's own language ("until Brussels folds") to reframe retaliation as a test of resolve rather than proof of policy failure, giving B the sharper final framing. A's case also suffered a real execution cost: Rebuttal 1 was clipped for exceeding the word limit, cutting off argument development the judge never fully saw. On balance B maintained a clearer, more consistently defended throughline and handled the decisive clash slightly better.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5's response to A2.5 ("the leverage story and hold-firm clause cannot both be the strategy") — B reframed holding firm as the precondition for leverage rather than a contradiction, and then in closing turned A's "tariffs held until Brussels folds" into evidence that retaliation is a test of resolve, not proof of failure. A's pincer-effect rebuttal (A2.1-A2.2, reiterated in A4.2/A5.4) was the strongest counter-pressure but was weakened by the clipped turn and never fully dislodged B's willing-partners exploit.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A built a clearer path to victory by establishing that a broad scope prevents circumvention through downstream goods, that voluntary alliances cannot compel unwilling emitters, and that holding firm against retaliation generates leverage rather than surrendering Europe's climate sovereignty. Side B's "pincer" argument and retaliation fears were sharp but undermined by a clipped first rebuttal and an inability to explain how unwilling competitors would be constrained without a border price. Side A crystallized the debate around manageable friction versus permanent industrial bleed, giving the cleaner reason to vote PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pincer argument—that a broad levy taxes imported intermediates and invites retaliatory duties on finished exports, squeezing European manufacturers—forced Side A to defend home-market protection versus export losses, crystallizing the central tradeoff.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO wins the central value-chain clash: taxing only raw steel/aluminum would punish EU downstream makers while leaving foreign finished machines untaxed, so breadth closes the loophole. CON concedes leakage is real but its alternative of cheaper power + voluntary alliances leaves unwilling emitters unchecked, and its pincer argument is answered by home-market protection + revenue retention incentive. PRO also resolves the leverage/hold-firm contradiction — holding firm is what makes leverage credible — while CON's clipped R1 and repeat of that contradiction loses momentum.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B in R1 turning A's "shelters a narrow upstream slice" claim [A1.3] against itself to show only a broad industrial scope protects downstream EU manufacturers.

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued densely and avoided fake citations, but A built a more sustained and precise critique of the motion as written. A repeatedly exploited the gap between "most imported industrial goods" and B's narrowed scope to measurable commodities, and its key rebuttal — that resource shuffling guts the climate case for the core commodities B relies on while unauditable complex goods trigger the retaliation B concedes is real — directly dismantled B's rhetorically clever "illusion and apocalypse cannot both be true" framing by showing both effects operate simultaneously across different product segments. B's closing never squarely answered this segmentation point, instead re-asserting the same dichotomy A had already defused. B also conceded away export rebates under pressure, which let A press the export-damage argument (cars, aircraft, aerospace) largely unanswered beyond the assertion that this is "the smaller loss." Additionally, B's first rebuttal was flagged as clipped for exceeding the word limit, an explicit execution cost. Pressure-question answers were formatted correctly on both sides, but A's answers more directly reinforced its central structural argument, while B's answers mostly restated prior claims (equivalence, home-market logic) without resolving the scope/measurement tension A kept pressing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.2), arguing that resource shuffling and trade-war retaliation are not mutually exclusive but hit different product segments simultaneously, directly neutralized B's "illusion and apocalypse cannot both be true" framing — a point B's closing never adequately re-engaged.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B demonstrated that across the breadth of most imported industrial goods the policy simultaneously triggers retaliation on complex manufactures and allows resource shuffling on primary commodities, producing an economic self-harm with negligible global climate gain. By dropping export rebates and conceding retaliatory damage to cars, aircraft, and agriculture, Side A conceded the industrial protection rationale and never fully resolved the deindustrialization pressure, while its clipped first rebuttal left key burdens underdeveloped.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s explanation that the levy yields a climate illusion on primary commodities via resource shuffling and a trade war on complex goods via punitive defaults at the same time, cleanly neutralizing Side A’s attempted contradiction and capturing the decisive cost of the motion.

A exposes that "most" goods creates two simultaneous failures: primary metals can be resource-shuffled to Europe for climate illusion, while complex machinery/electronics cannot audit sub-tier smelters and face punitive defaults that trigger retaliation. That segmentation directly answers B's core dilemma "cannot be both illusion and permanent war" [B3.1][B5.2] and survives pressure. B's shift from comparable carbon price [B2.4] to vague equivalence [B4.5] and dropping export rebates [B2.5] leaves European exporters paying full carbon on inputs, zero relief abroad, plus tariffs on cars/aircraft/farms unanswered, making net deindustrialization the stronger live route. B's R1 being clipped is an execution cost.
