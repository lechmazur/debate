- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by making the live mechanism clash decisive: gene-editing is not generic-pill compulsory licensing, so a paper pathway cannot treat patients without the innovator’s tacit know-how, and market-revocation “leverage” is an empty threat if firms exit. Clear competitive CON win on operative comparative burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 on B4: walking-away empties exclusivity leverage; rich-country “not huge volume” kills the accessible-volume innovation offset; patient’s-own-cells blocks generic import copy.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won the feasibility clash. Gene-editing for sickle cell is autologous - patient cells harvested, edited, reinfused - so there are no generic hubs to authorize and no finished product to import. PRO's market-access leverage concedes domestic law can't seize foreign tacit know-how and collapses to revoking exclusivity, which A shows makes firms walk away. PRO also conceded rich markets offer "not huge volume" just lower price, undercutting its earlier volume-offsets-price-cap claim and leaving the innovation harm alive. PRO's moral veto frame never overcame the technical non-transferability argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2/A4 dismantling pill-vs-cell analogy and exposing PRO's volume concession: pills copy from patent disclosure, gene therapies need unpatented vectors and active training, can't be bulk-imported, and PRO's A1 leverage is an empty bluff that drives exit while pooled procurement doesn't cut autologous clinical cost.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B won by effectively dismantling Side A's core analogy between gene-editing therapies and traditional chemical pills. Side B demonstrated strong grounding in the actual mechanics of autologous cell therapies (like current CRISPR treatments for sickle cell), explaining why compulsory licensing and generic manufacturing cannot simply bypass the originator's tacit know-how and complex clinical procedures. Side A struggled to overcome this biological and logistical reality, relying on generic solutions like "regional pooling" and "domestic conditionality." Side B sharply rebutted these by pointing out that biotech firms would simply walk away from a single market rather than surrender global trade secrets, and that pooling patients does not reduce the massive per-patient hospital costs of bespoke cell processing. Side B's pressure questions successfully exposed the flaws in Side A's volume and leverage arguments, leading to a decisive victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side A's "domestic conditionality" leverage by pointing out that a biotech firm will rationally walk away from a smaller, price-capped market rather than surrender its global proprietary know-how and trade secrets to a competitor, effectively neutralizing Side A's primary enforcement mechanism.

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO won by forcing CON’s decisive concession that even fully funded public facilities remain illegal without a license, then showing CON’s preferred alternatives (voluntary licenses, tiered pricing, public manufacturing) either need the same statutory hammer or collapse under CON’s own “costs survive zero royalties” claim.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s R2 (A1/A2 + contradiction turn): public manufacturing is still barred without a license, so CON cannot praise capacity investment while opposing the pathway that unlocks it, and zero-royalty clinical costs would doom CON’s alternatives equally.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO won the necessity-vs-sufficiency clash. PRO then answered the empty-clean-room objection by defining the pathway to include dossiers, vector protocols and tech transfer, and used leverage to show voluntary/tiered deals need the credible hammer. CON’s cost-dominance claim in A2.2 undercut its own alternatives, a contradiction PRO exploited in B4.3-B4.5, while CON never differentiated why narrow existing compulsory licensing would avoid the need for the modern pathway the proposition requires.

Side A successfully traps Side B on the necessity of legal permission to build public manufacturing capacity for complex biologics. When Side B pivots to existing compulsory licensing, Side A sharply reminds the judge of Side B's opening argument that traditional patent-only licenses fail to transfer the necessary biologic manufacturing data, leaving an empty clean room. Side A's comparative weighing was cleaner, more adaptive, and far more damaging to the opponent's live routes to victory.
