### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but A's throughline was sharper and better defended under pressure. A's central structural point—that sustaining a veto takes only a blocking minority while renewal requires an affirmative majority—was never fully neutralized; B's parliamentary-repeal and appropriations fallbacks were good counters, but A's closing exposed the key gap: many coercive emergency powers (protest bans, censorship, surveillance) require no funding, so appropriations leverage doesn't reach them, and parliamentary backbenchers rarely topple their own government to claw back drifting authority. B's strongest point—that automatic cliffs risk a legal vacuum during genuine crises when legislatures can't convene—was answered with fast-track/remote-voting mechanics and narrow impossibility extensions that, while not in the literal proposition, are plausible standard legislative self-governance rather than executive discretion, and A defended this directly when pressed (A4.1). B's 'rubber-stamp vs. deadlock' contradiction charge was answered reasonably (different failure modes under different incentive conditions), but A's closing reframe—that renewal is compulsory for powers still needed and contestable for those that aren't—gave a cleaner resolution of the dilemma B tried to force. Both closings crystallized the clash well, but A's synthesis left fewer live, unanswered threats to its case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing rebuttal (A5.2) that appropriations control cannot stop cost-free coercive decrees (protest bans, censorship) directly undercut B's key fallback safeguard (legislative control via funding/repeal), and B never adequately answered this specific gap.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that automatic renewal corrects the decisive default-setting problem: a legislative majority may be unable to withdraw powers protected by an executive veto, while renewal requires affirmative current consent. Side A established a real risk of accidental lapse, but Side B plausibly answered it through legislative procedures, political incentives, and the ability to renew necessary powers while narrowing abusive ones. Side A's alternatives remained weaker for low-cost coercive powers that cannot effectively be defunded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange distinguished politically compulsory renewal of genuinely necessary crisis functions from later renewal of protest bans or surveillance. This undercut Side A's rubber-stamp-versus-dangerous-cliff dilemma by explaining how renewal creates leverage to preserve essential authority while removing abusive provisions.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): A won the decisive clash on sticky defaults and institutional math: without sunsets, ending broad emergency power is harder than starting it, and a one-third veto minority can outlast a later majority. B’s cliff/deadlock risk and rubber-stamp dilemma were real and well pressed, and B resolved the alleged contradiction cleanly, but A’s answers on legislative procedure control, later-stage leverage, and especially the inadequacy of defunding zero-cost coercion left B’s main escape hatches weaker. B never fully neutralized the inverted-burden problem for presidential-style systems or free emergency decrees, while A better explained why day-one tailoring is weaker than forced later review. Clear but competitive PRO edge after full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s closing strike on appropriations (legal coercion needing no money) plus sustained 55%-vs-1/3 veto math against B’s “majority can always control later” route.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and engaged the clash directly, but B did slightly more damaging work by the end. B's cleverest move was turning A's own flagship historical example (post-9/11 surveillance sunsets) into evidence that renewal votes can entrench power by giving executives political cover and inducing greater judicial deference — a point A never fully neutralized. B also pressed a concrete operational-chaos argument (quarantines, banking backstops, and wartime coordination cannot survive a midnight lapse) that A's "normal laws remain, reauthorize tomorrow" response never squarely answered, especially in A's closing, which reverted to the loan metaphor rather than confronting this live threat. A did land a strong counter by exposing the tension in B's framework (agenda control blocks revocation but somehow not renewal) and resolving it cleverly by noting the same fast-track fix should apply symmetrically — a sharp piece of reasoning — but B's "zero months on silence" answer (via privileged petition) and its vivid closing on operational catastrophe left the debate tilted narrowly toward B's practical case against automatic, calendar-triggered lapses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's reuse of A's own 9/11 surveillance-sunset example to show renewal votes can manufacture political cover and judicial deference, combined with B's closing "cannot vote on Tuesday to un-crash a financial system that froze on Monday" rebuttal, which A's case never fully answered.

Side A established a serious risk of deadline-driven hostage-taking and offered a sophisticated privileged-revocation alternative, but that model still permits powers to continue whenever nobody petitions or a revocation majority cannot form. B showed why availability of repeal is weaker than requiring the executive to justify continuation. A's operational-cliff concern kept the debate competitive, especially after B minimized a possible one-day lapse, but early fast-track votes and adjustable renewal periods substantially reduced that risk.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second pressure question and B4.5 most effectively exposed the weakness in A's alternative: a forced revocation mechanism works only when opponents initiate and win, leaving silence as consent. A's claim of “zero months on silence alone” did not fully answer the possibility that no petition occurs or no revocation majority forms.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the decisive comparison on what happens when legislatures do nothing, and by placing the burden of proof on continuing extraordinary power. A’s failure-mode weighing—visible, reversible lapse versus indefinite unowned drift—remained cleaner after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.6) answering the midnight-hostage and judicial-deference pressures, then locking the symmetry: if privileged votes can force revocation, they can structure renewal, and the safer default is expiry rather than silent continuation.
