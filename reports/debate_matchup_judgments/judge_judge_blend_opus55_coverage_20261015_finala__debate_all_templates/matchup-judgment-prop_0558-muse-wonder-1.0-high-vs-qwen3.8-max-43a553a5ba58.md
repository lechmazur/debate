### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): The debate came down to whether "reviewable detention" (CON) is meaningfully different from "indefinite detention with paperwork" (PRO's framing). A's pressure question in Round 3 was sharp: at what point does repeated "not yet" review become indefinite in practice? B's A1 answer conceded detention should end when removal is no longer foreseeable and no risk remains, but never explained what legal mechanism forces that conclusion absent a ban — B's own system allows the state to perpetually assert "foreseeable" or "unresolved." A exploited this directly in closing, arguing B's concession "has no teeth" without a hard rule. B's strongest counter — that case management is a "gamble" for the uncooperative/dangerous and that no tool guarantees removability — was real and unanswered in full, but A's response (intensive supervision, breach triggers custody, proportionality) gave a workable rebuttal that kept the clash alive rather than conceding. B also repeatedly asserted the public "bears the risk" without grappling with A's point that indefinite detention also eventually ends (court order, cost, health) often badly, undercutting B's safety framing. On balance, A had the cleaner, more falsifiable standard (finite, evidence-renewed custody) and more directly answered CON's toughest case; B had a strong structural objection but didn't fully close the loop on why a ban specifically guts enforcement beyond what reviewable-but-proven detention already requires.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure question (A3.1) asking what principled stopping point exists under CON's model, and B's answer in A1 (B4.1) conceding an endpoint in principle but failing to supply an enforceable trigger — which A then weaponized in closing as "proof overruled by nothing but another review."
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully pressing the dilemma of the hardest cases—uncooperative flight risks and dangerous individuals. By forcing Side B to concede that case management cannot guarantee removability for those who destroy documents, Side A effectively framed the proposition as a choice between rigorous, reviewable custody and an arbitrary clock that mandates release regardless of persistent risk or noncompliance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal, which capitalized on Side B's answer to Q2 by highlighting that if case management cannot secure removability, Side B's model effectively replaces enforcement with an expiration date.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): A won a competitive debate by forcing the decisive clash onto limits with teeth. A made liberty the default and showed that B’s “lawful, reviewable detention” collapses into indefinite custody unless “not yet” can ever become “must release.” A’s Pressure Q1 and answers A1/A2 were especially clean: incomplete suspicion cannot buy infinity, and a demand for guaranteed removability proves too much. B landed real hard-case pressure—no case-management guarantee, risk shifted to the public, custody preserving later removal—and A did concede no short-of-custody guarantee. But A still kept the stronger comparative path: renewable, proven, time-limited custody for demonstrated danger/flight risk, without an unfinishable justification loop. B’s closings recycled the gamble frame without neutralizing A’s teeth point as well as A crystallized B’s concession that detention should end when purpose/risk disappear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1–A2.3: folding B’s “reviewable detention” into indefinite detention with paperwork, then demanding an outer limit that can ever force release.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but B's pressure questions exposed a genuine structural tension in A's position: when pressed for "the day" an obstructing, unidentified person must be released, A answered "no exact day" and conceded detention could continue while necessary for removal or danger. B seized on this in Rebuttal 2 and the closing, arguing this collapses PRO's "prohibition" into the same judicially-reviewed detention B proposes—meaning either the ban doesn't really bite (so the motion fails to deliver what it promises) or it does bite and rewards sabotage. A never cleanly resolved this dilemma; its burden-shifting defense (release as default vs. detention as default) is a real distinction in principle, but A did not use it decisively to show why outcomes differ, nor did it fully rebut B's sharpest practical point—that case management is weakest exactly at the moment of a final removal order, when a breach record is not removal. B's closing crystallized this cleanly ("a calendar or a judge") while A's closing largely restated its framework without closing the loophole. B's rebuttals were more incisive and did more direct damage to A's live route to victory, giving B the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.4), which quoted A's own "no exact day" concession back at it to argue PRO's model is functionally indistinguishable from "reviewed detention," undermining the motion's central promise of a genuine prohibition.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A won by forcing Side B into a lethal dilemma regarding the definition of a 'prohibition' on indefinite detention. When B conceded that there is 'no exact day' for release if a detainee obstructs, A effectively demonstrated that B's model collapses into A's 'reviewed detention' counter-model. Furthermore, A directly answered B's pressure questions, distinguishing between foreign-caused delay (which A agreed merits release) and detainee sabotage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Turn 8, Side A's Rebuttal 2, where A capitalizes on B's 'no exact day' concession to prove that B has either adopted A's model of reviewed detention or must defend rewarding sabotage.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): CON won by forcing a live dilemma PRO never escaped: either obstruction/unknown identity can keep custody going (collapsing PRO into reviewed detention) or a real ban eventually forces release despite sabotage (enforcement failure). PRO’s liberty/default framing and foreign-government pressure were strong, but answers like “no exact day” and necessity-based custody confirmed CON’s collapse charge. CON’s judge-vs-calendar distinction and external-vs-self-created obstruction split decided the clash more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s B2.1/B4.4 dilemma: obstruction exceptions make the ban non-prohibitive; without them, sabotage earns release—then crystallized against PRO’s “no exact day” answer.
