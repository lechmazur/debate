### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B won by distinguishing the timescale of discovery from the timescale of correction. A persuasively established that short cycles distort research, but never adequately showed why long funding must eliminate meaningful external checkpoints. Its answer that deans would reallocate funds on merit assumed away B’s concern about internal hierarchy and delayed exit. B offered the cleaner comparison: competitive bias is contingent and reformable, whereas block-grant lock-in is structural. B’s clipped rebuttal and both sides’ nonexistent C-reference IDs impose execution costs, but do not overturn its substantive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s incumbency argument was decisive: competitive systems may favor incumbents, but block grants formally immobilize funds within incumbent institutions. The closing formulation that a long competitive grant is “a block grant wearing a costume” whose costume has an exit sharply exposed the missing corrective mechanism in A’s model.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO successfully framed the debate around the intrinsic timescale of discovery, showing that CON's "mid-term checkpoints" inevitably recreate the behavioral distortions of short cycles. CON made a strong case for external exits and the dangers of institutional silos, but PRO convincingly leveraged the prompt's specific tradeoff to argue that losing short-term exits is the necessary price for high-risk science.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4.3 effectively neutralized CON's proposed "longer grants with checkpoints" by explaining that any mid-term survival checkpoint forces researchers to design for interim publishable units, collapsing CON's attempted middle ground.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully neutralizing Side B's primary alternative. Side B advocated for reformed, longer competitive grants with mid-term checkpoints to maintain accountability and cross-institutional mobility. Side A delivered a decisive structural critique: mid-term checkpoints inherently force researchers to produce interim publishable units, recreating the exact incrementalist distortion that block grants aim to solve. Side B's closing assertion that a checkpoint is an "option, not a paper mill" failed to explain how external reviewers could evaluate progress without demanding interim deliverables. While Side B made strong points about block grants creating "immobile silos" and protecting internal incumbents, Side A's framing of "accountability matched to the timescale of discovery" and the "block grant in a costume" trap gave A the cleaner comparative path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's argument that Side B's proposed "seven-year competitive award with go/pivot/stop reviews" is merely a "block grant wearing a costume" that forces researchers to manufacture interim papers, effectively collapsing Side B's reformed competition model into the very system it sought to avoid, but with added distortions.
