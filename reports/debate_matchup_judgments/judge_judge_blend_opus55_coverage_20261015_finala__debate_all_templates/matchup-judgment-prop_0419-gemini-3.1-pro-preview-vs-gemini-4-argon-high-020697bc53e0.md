### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON (Side A) built a tighter, more internally consistent case: it identified that a guaranteed "fast path" triggered by self-selected categories (billing/safety/cancellation) destroys the triage that protects genuine emergencies, and it pressed this point relentlessly without PRO ever resolving it. CON's sharpest move was exploiting PRO's own concession that small businesses could comply via cheap outsourced answering services — CON correctly noted this directly undercuts PRO's central mechanism (a human who can "exercise nuanced judgment and override broken algorithms"), since outsourced scripted agents have no such authority. PRO never squarely resolved this tension in rebuttal 2 or closing, instead retreating to the general claim that a human "breaks the cycle" even while escalating, which doesn't answer the queue-jumping or powerless-agent critiques. PRO's opening and normative framing (asymmetry, high stakes, "digital fortress") were compelling, and its pressure questions were sharp, but its answers to CON's pressure questions and its rebuttals relied more on assertion ("theoretical inconvenience," "basic sorting menu") than on resolving the mechanics CON kept hammering. CON's alternative policy proposal (click-to-cancel, outcome-based regulation) also gave a cleaner competing path that PRO never fully dislodged.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal 2 point [A4.5], turning PRO's own concession about outsourced third-party answering services against PRO's core claim that humans provide irreplaceable contextual judgment, was the most decisive exchange — it exposed an unresolved internal contradiction in PRO's case that was never repaired.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won by making the decisive operational clash clearer and tighter across rounds: a guaranteed fast human bypass for billing/cancellations/safety destroys the triage needed to keep genuine emergencies clear, while PRO’s own concessions (basic menu self-selection plus outsourced answering/ticketing) still leave users with powerless middlemen and flooded queues. A’s harm story on chatbot walls and high-stakes cases stayed vivid but never neutralized B’s queue math, retention-trap history, small-business scaling, or superior alternative of outcome rules (click-to-cancel, deadlines, fines). B’s later speeches sharpened those pressures into a cleaner reason to reject the mandate as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 collapse of A’s pressure answers: basic “safety/billing” menus are not real triage and cheap third-party agents lack override power, so the mandate both worsens queues and fails to deliver the nuanced human judgment A claimed was essential.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B wins by exposing a structural collapse in Side A's case. B showed that A's mandate requires human access but not human authority, and A's concession that cheap third-party answering services satisfy the mandate undermined A's core claim that human judgment is the needed escape hatch. B's queue-flooding argument demonstrated that destroying automated triage could perversely bury genuine safety emergencies behind routine callers, and A's response—dismissing staffing as merely a problem for companies to solve—failed to engage the operational reality. B's alternative of outcome regulation (click-to-cancel, strict dispute deadlines) provided a cleaner mechanism to address the same consumer harms without the unintended consequences.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal exploiting Side A's concession on third-party answering services: B demonstrated that these powerless middlemen lack authority to override algorithms, collapsing A's distinction between human judgment and automated loops, while combining this with the queue-flooding argument to show the mandate would neither be fast nor effective.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly and complied with the pressure-question format, but PRO built a more coherent throughline: the "friction is free, human time costs money" incentive mechanism was introduced early, defended under pressure, and used to directly rebut CON's gridlock predictions by explaining why routine demand would be automated away, leaving the human fast path reserved for genuine disputes. PRO also landed a sharp contradiction catch (CON claiming automation already resolves instantly while also predicting flooding), and CON's answer—that habitual/distrust-driven demand persists regardless of automation quality—was asserted but never reconciled with its own claim that automation works well, and went unrebutted mainly because it arrived in CON's closing. More importantly, under pressure (B's Q1), CON effectively conceded that complex billing/safety cases still require human back-office investigation, undermining its central alternative of mandating "automated resolutions" instead of human access. CON's "biological chatbot" and cost/bottleneck arguments were strong and kept the debate close, but PRO's economic-incentive framing gave a cleaner answer to "why would this actually fix anything," which is the decisive comparative question the debate turned on.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's contradiction catch in Rebuttal 1 (B2.1), paired with CON's concession under pressure (A4.1) that complex disputes still need human back-office investigation, did the most damage to CON's alternative "automated resolution" proposal.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): A won by keeping the decisive clash on free digital friction versus costly human time, showing that the mandate forces honest automation for routine exits while still covering novel billing and safety cases bots cannot auto-resolve. B raised real operational worries about baseline demand, Tier-1 limits, and queue pressure, but never fully neutralized A's incentive flip or the hole in pure automated-resolution alternatives; A's cleaner comparative weighing made the proposition easier to affirm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 contradiction: if bots already issue instant cancellations/refunds, the predicted human flood disappears, turning B's cost/bottleneck case against itself.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO won by landing the decisive economic incentive reframe and keeping their core route to victory alive through the final speech. CON raised legitimate concerns about gridlock and cost, but PRO successfully exposed a tension in CON's case—if automated resolutions are instant and flawless, consumers would not flood a human line. This let PRO argue that mandating a human fast path actually incentivizes companies to make automated exits work honestly, while still guaranteeing humans for unscripted crises. PRO's queue-management answers and payroll-cost argument blunted CON's gridlock fears adequately, whereas CON never fully answered how a bot could autonomously resolve a contested billing dispute or novel safety hazard without human access. CON's closing largely repeated earlier assertions rather than resolving PRO's live incentive story.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's first rebuttal caught the contradiction between CON's claim that AI can provide instant automated resolutions and CON's simultaneous prediction of massive call-center gridlock, then flipped it into a clean incentive argument: human payroll costs force companies to automate routine tasks honestly.
