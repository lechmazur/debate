- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won a competitive debate by forcing a cleaner comparative choice: enrollment funding’s core harm is structural (same check for dropout and graduate), while CON’s cream-skimming/mission worries were framed as adjustable design risks the motion permits.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO Rebuttal 2 / Closing: CON’s own concession that enrollment wastes money on dropouts plus “nothing prevents a high Pell premium” flips cream-skimming into a dial, while enrollment’s harm remains the payment rule itself.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO won by showing that CON's predicted harms—cream-skimming and mission distortion—depend on implementation choices the motion does not require, whereas PRO's harm (taxpayers paying equally for dropouts and graduates) is structurally embedded in enrollment funding. CON never answered what a university loses when a student borrows and drops out, conceding only that the waste is "visible" and "bounded." PRO's "visibility without consequence" frame revealed that this flaw self-perpetuates because money never moves when students fail. PRO also exploited CON's concession that nothing in the motion prevents a high Pell-completer premium, collapsing CON's claim that the motion structurally mandates access cuts. CON's "designer discretion" critique had force, but could not overcome PRO's decisive comparison between adjustable design risks and a built-in moral hazard.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7), where PRO directly answered CON's pressure questions and then turned them to show that CON had conceded enrollment funding wastes money on dropouts while offering no financial consequence for failure, and that CON's cream-skimming fear was a "dial" addressable within the motion's text—sharpening the clash into built-in harm versus fixable risk.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO kept a live, unanswered harm: under enrollment-only funding a university loses nothing when a student borrows, drops in October, and leaves with debt and no degree - a concession B makes explicit [B2.10].   Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's debt-without-degree + per-graduate math reversal: enrollment funding equals revenue on census day with zero loss on dropout, while "rather than enrollment alone" with a high Pell completer premium makes the disadvantaged graduate the most valuable admit, collapsing B's marginal-dollar cream-skimming claim.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): CON won the decisive clash on incentives and motion fidelity. PRO’s case for ends-over-enrollment was sharp and memorable, but never neutralized CON’s core structural attack: graduation-plus-earnings both reward intake (selectivity/cream-skimming), peer/field benchmarks still fail to hold constant who is admitted, and lag plus fixed-budget competition still cut resources at weaker regional campuses. PRO’s late “reward without punishment / keep the base” defense and [B5.1] concession that CON’s flaw is real further undercut the proposition as written (“rather than enrollment alone”), leaving CON with the cleaner comparison: patch weak completion with floors/enforcement while money still follows every student through the door.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s A2/A4 sequence crushing dual-metric gaming (selectivity lifts both metrics; peer benchmarks inflate, not shrink, premiums) and then forcing PRO’s soft hybrid redesign off the motion’s wording.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing that Side A’s escape hatch—keeping base funding for flat numbers—collapses into enrollment-plus-a-bonus, violating the motion’s text, while the purer version would punish the campuses that need funds most. B also exposed that peer earnings benchmarks still incentivize cream-skimming, since wealthier admittees inflate the local premium for reasons the university did not supply. A never repaired the anti-gaming defense or recovered from B’s sharper institutional-framing dilemma.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal [B4.5–B4.8] turned A’s “base funding” defense into a concession that the model is effectively enrollment funding with an outcomes bonus bolted on, violating the motion, and then showed that regional peer benchmarks fail to stop gaming because wealthier students naturally out-earn the local high-school baseline.

PRO’s claim that selectivity cannot raise both metrics [A2.3] was directly refuted, and its peer-benchmark fix still rewards admitting wealthier students [B4.8]. PRO’s answer to flat numbers – keep base funding and reward upside [A4.1] – concedes the motion’s “rather than enrollment” wording, leaving a fixed-budget relative cut that B exposed [B4.5-B4.7]. Enrollment paying schools to say yes vs outcome paying them to say no was the cleaner mechanism story.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5-B4.8: showing PRO’s “flat keeps base, upside bonus” is just enrollment funding with a bonus, contradicting “rather than enrollment,” and that same-region baseline doesn’t block gaming by admitting more advantaged students.
