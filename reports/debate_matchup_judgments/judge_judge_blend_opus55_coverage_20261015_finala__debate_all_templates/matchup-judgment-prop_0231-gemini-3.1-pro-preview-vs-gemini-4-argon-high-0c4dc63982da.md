### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): CON built a more original and mechanistically grounded case by distinguishing the Zurich/Frankfurt opioid model from the stimulant-driven crises PRO claims to address, and by exposing a genuine operational contradiction: either police enforce the perimeter (scaring off clients) or tacitly tolerate dealing nearby (creating a cartel safe zone). PRO's pressure questions were good, but its answers (A4.1-A4.2) effectively conceded the "tolerance zone" dynamic and offered only vague appeals to "de-escalation" and "calm rooms" without explaining how short, compulsive stimulant cycles are interrupted. CON's rebuttals repeatedly returned to these unresolved points, while PRO largely repeated its "dead can't rehab" and "comparative baseline" framing without closing the specific mechanistic gaps CON identified, including the unaddressed point that cardiac emergencies inside a site still require ambulance transport, undercutting PRO's cost-savings claim. Both sides executed the format correctly and avoided fake sourcing, but CON did more work to sharpen the decisive clash and left PRO's core analogy and enforcement logic visibly strained by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1-B2.4 and B4.1-B4.4 sequence, pressing the "honey pot/safe zone" contradiction and the stimulant-cycling mechanism, which PRO's A4.1-A4.2 answers conceded more than refuted.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): CON won by exposing operational and pharmacological flaws in PRO's model that materially weakened both halves of the motion. CON demonstrated that supervised sites create a legal paradox for policing that protects street-level dealing, and that the stimulant-driven crises in Latin America and Europe cannot be managed indoors because crack and cocaine paste produce rapid cycles of craving and street hustling that defy containment. These rebuttals undercut PRO's claims to reduce both overdose deaths and public disorder. While PRO's comparative framing was excellent, CON successfully shifted the comparison from site versus open-air market to institutionalized decline versus investment in actual recovery, showing that the sites may actively anchor disorder and cannibalize scarce detox resources.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pharmacological critique showing that the heroin-based Zurich/Frankfurt model fails for stimulant crises: crack and cocaine paste's rapid cycling, lack of antidote, and compulsive street-hustling patterns mean supervised sites cannot reliably prevent fatalities or contain public disorder for the very populations the motion targets (B1.4-B1.5, B2.3, B4.3-B4.4).
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won by introducing a highly effective, mechanistically grounded critique regarding the pharmacological mismatch between the heroin-focused European model and the stimulant-driven crises in Latin America. Side A convincingly argued that the short half-life and neurochemical cravings of smokable stimulants undermine PRO's "resting room" solution, leading to a volatile cycle of street hustling that worsens public disorder. Furthermore, Side A decisively won the policing clash by exposing the operational contradiction of protecting a consumption site while trying to arrest the dealers immediately outside it, cleanly rebutting PRO's "personal possession" loophole. While Side B relied on strong rhetorical framing, Side A's structural and resource-allocation arguments proved more robust and logically sound.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to PRO's policing strategy, pointing out that ignoring "personal possession" outside the clinic simply hands cartel runners a legal loophole to conduct micro-transactions with impunity on the clinic's doorstep, effectively neutralizing PRO's public order claims.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-defended cases, but PRO's core framing proved more durable across the exchange. PRO's baseline argument—that open drug scenes and cartel presence already exist wherever such sites would be sited, so the real comparative question is "where does the dose happen"—was never fully dismantled by CON, who kept asserting "hyper-concentration" without directly refuting that the areas in question are already saturated with dealing. PRO's survival-first logic ("a dead person has zero percent chance of recovery") landed as the decisive answer to CON's "holding pattern" and multi-dose-per-day objections, since CON never explained why partial coverage of overdose risk is not still a net reduction in deaths. CON's strongest moments were real: the Zurich distinction (prescription programs vs. mere consumption rooms) and the pressure-question catch that PRO's "safe passage" answer undercuts its own "strict security perimeter" claim were sharp and exposed a genuine tension. PRO's response to that catch (distinguishing personal-dose users from traffickers) was adequate but not fully convincing, leaving a live wound. Still, on balance PRO maintained the clearer throughline, answered more of the clash directly, and reframed the debate's central tradeoff in a way CON's closing could not overturn, only reassert as "the wrong question" without refuting it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's catch in Rebuttal 2 (B4.3-B4.4) that PRO's "safe passage" admission contradicts its own "strict security perimeter" claim was the sharpest single exchange, but PRO's survival-first framing ("zero percent chance of recovery") from Rebuttal 1 onward proved the more decisive and durable point across the whole debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by successfully controlling the baseline comparison. The motion asks whether cities should permit regulated supervised consumption sites to reduce overdose deaths and public disorder. Side A persuasively frames the choice not as "clinics vs. a drug-free utopia," but as "clinics vs. unregulated public streets." Side B repeatedly attacks the model by comparing it to idealized alternatives—prescription heroin programs, rapid detox, uncorrupt policing—but never rebuts the core intuition that, for severe users not yet ready for abstinence, moving consumption indoors saves lives and cleans adjacent streets compared to the status quo. Side A's survival-prerequisite argument ("a dead person has a zero percent chance of recovery") withstands Side B's "holding pattern" critique because Side B never explains how a user who dies in an alley at 2:00 PM reaches Side B's preferred treatment later. Side B's sharpest attack—that sites institutionalize cartel markets via "safe passage"—is blunted when Side A distinguishes personal-use transport from bulk dealing and notes that the open-air market already exists in precisely these neighborhoods. Side B's closing misstep references "P4.1" and "P5.2," revealing slightly sloppy execution, while Side A crystallizes the clash cleanly by forcing Side B to concede that banning sites prevents zero cartel transactions and merely forces all consumption back into public view.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's answer to Side B's pressure questions in Turn 7 (A4.1–A4.2), where PRO distinguished between a user carrying a personal dose and a trafficker selling bulk narcotics, and reframed the security-perimeter challenge as a reason to free police to target dealers rather than arrest sick users. This exchange neutralized CON's central "state-sanctioned retail hub" narrative by showing that the relevant comparison was the existing chaotic baseline, not a cartel-free utopia.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO built a more pragmatic and persuasive case by establishing a realistic baseline (existing open-air drug scenes) and demonstrating how supervised sites directly reduce overdose deaths and public disorder relative to that baseline. CON's arguments about "honey pots" and the Zurich prescription model were sharp, but PRO effectively neutralized them by pointing out that banning sites does not eliminate dealing, it merely forces consumption to remain public and lethal. PRO's weighing of immediate survival as a prerequisite for recovery was decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal to CON's "honey pot" and Zurich arguments, specifically the framing that CON makes "the perfect the enemy of the living" and that banning clinics prevents zero cartel transactions while forcing all consumption onto the streets.
