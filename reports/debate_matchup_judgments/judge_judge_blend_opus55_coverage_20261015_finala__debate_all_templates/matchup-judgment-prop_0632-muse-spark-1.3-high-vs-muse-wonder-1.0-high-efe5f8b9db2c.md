### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but A built a tighter structural case around the motion's own cost-control clause and sustained pressure on it across rounds. A's key move—that a legal coverage mandate undercuts the "walk-away" leverage that makes value-based/outcomes contracts credible—was pressed hard in Rebuttal 1 and crystallized into a genuine dilemma in Rebuttal 2 (A4.3): either the "preferred drug" exclusion is real, in which case patients who need the non-preferred option are still denied, or it isn't, in which case leverage evaporates. B's closing never returned to resolve this dilemma, instead retreating to the moral "disease discrimination" framing. A also landed an underaddressed disanalogy: statins/vaccines are cheap and finite while GLP-1s are branded, expensive, and open-ended, and the hepatitis-C comparison (cited by B as proof prices fall with competition) was undercut by A's point that hep-C drugs were curative and finite, unlike indefinite obesity therapy—a point B never directly rebutted. B's case was emotionally resonant and reasonably well-mechanized (BMI/comorbidity thresholds, reauthorization, rebates), and its pressure questions were sharp, but its answers to A's pressure questions (A4.1/A4.2) exposed rather than closed the scale and leverage problems, and B's final speech did not re-engage the sharpest live clash. That left A with the cleaner, more defended path to victory on the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3), pointing out that B's "cover at least one preferred drug, tier the rival punitively" mechanism either still forces coverage of the holdout for patients who can't tolerate the preferred drug, or genuinely excludes them, meaning leverage and universal access cannot both survive—left unresolved by B's closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing a structural flaw in PRO's value-based pricing mechanism: once coverage is legally mandated, insurers cannot credibly walk away from the drug class, and formulary preference fails when patients cannot tolerate the contracted product, forcing coverage of expensive holdouts anyway. PRO never fully repaired this tension, while CON sustained a coherent critique of scale, price, and leverage through the final speech.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2 [A4.3] showing that PRO's preferred-drug leverage model either denies patients the non-contracted option or evaporates when the mandate forces coverage of clinically indicated holdouts, undermining the entire value-contract mechanism.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively dismantling Side B's core analogies (statins, vaccines) through clear economic arithmetic regarding unit cost and duration, and by exposing the fatal flaw in Side B's leverage argument. Side A convincingly showed that a coverage mandate inherently destroys the walk-away power necessary for value-based pricing to work. Side B's attempt to salvage leverage via formulary tiering was effectively neutralized in the final rebuttals when Side A pointed out that accommodating patients who cannot tolerate the preferred drug forces the insurer back to the holdout, restoring the manufacturer's pricing power.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they exposed the contradiction in Side B's definition of the mandate: if insurers only have to cover one preferred drug, patients who cannot tolerate it are denied; if they must cover the holdout for those patients, the manufacturer's leverage is restored and the insurer's walk-away power is destroyed.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): A built a tighter, more sustained case: the scale argument (broad FDA labels mean "clinically indicated" is not a narrow gate, so coverage mandates translate into mass, recurring spending) was never squarely defused — B's "clinical gates/continuation review" answer conceded that documented need still triggers coverage, which is exactly A's point. A's attack on the statin analogy (cheap generics vs. expensive patented biologics) landed cleanly and B never recovered it or revisited it. A also pressed a genuine structural tension in the mandate's cost-control mechanism: with only two dominant, patent-protected manufacturers, "outcomes-based contracts" have weak teeth without a credible threat to exclude the whole class, and B's answer (manufacturers will "compete to be preferred") didn't grapple with the duopoly point. B's strongest original contribution — the collective-action/Medicare-churn argument — was partially blunted by A's reply that commercial payers still front costs for savings realized elsewhere, and B's response (benefits accrue in a few years too) was asserted rather than demonstrated. B's pressure questions were sharp and did expose a real soft spot (exclusion hasn't actually produced affordable prices), and A's answer walking that back to "it has contained rollout, not cut prices" created an internal tension A never fully resolved — but B's closing didn't exploit that opening, letting A's reframing stand. On balance, A answered more of the load-bearing clash points and ended with the cleaner, more consistent throughline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal turning the statin/antidepressant analogy back on B (cheap generics justify broad coverage; expensive patented biologics do not) was the single most damaging and unanswered exchange, since B never revisited or repaired that comparison after it was exposed.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing that the mandate would trigger massive, certain pharmacy spending on tens of millions of eligible adults, while the promised value-based pricing mechanism was undermined by the removal of class-level negotiation leverage and the administrative burden of tracking outcomes across a fluctuating metric like weight. PRO’s downstream-savings frame was blunted by the generic-vs.-branded cost distinction and the timing mismatch between commercial payers and Medicare.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s takedown of the statin analogy by pointing out statins are pennies-a-day generics while anti-obesity drugs are expensive branded biologics, gutting PRO’s attempt to normalize indefinite mass preventive pharmacotherapy.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively dismantling the economic assumptions of Side B's case. A effectively highlighted that "clinically indicated" under FDA labels encompasses tens of millions of patients, making the mandate a massive, certain cost that dwarfs the probabilistic downstream savings. A also provided a much stronger analysis of PBM leverage, explaining that mandating coverage destroys the threat of class-wide exclusion, which is the primary tool for negotiating deep rebates in a duopoly market. Side B's reliance on "clinical gates" and outcomes-based contracts felt insufficient against A's rigorous arithmetic and realistic market analysis.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's "leverage" argument, clarifying that the threat to exclude the entire class is what secures real rebates, and that a mandate guarantees volume to a duopoly, reducing outcomes-based rebates to easily gamed, administratively burdensome token discounts.
