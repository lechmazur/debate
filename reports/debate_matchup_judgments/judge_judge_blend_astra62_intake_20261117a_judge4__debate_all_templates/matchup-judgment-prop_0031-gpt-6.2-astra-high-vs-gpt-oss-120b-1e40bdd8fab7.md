### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins clearly. It drew a sharp, consistent line: ban single-family-only districts, not single-family houses. It showed that service capacity is better governed by direct limits such as connection caps and dwelling ceilings than by building type, and that bonuses can sit on top of a duplex baseline. CON (Side A) undercut its own case several times. In A2.4 it said a total ban "delivers certainty" and stops developers from locking out competition, which is PRO's argument. In A4.4 it conceded that a duplex right does not change underwriting, then in its closing restored the lender-premium claim as its "decisive" harm. In A4.2 it defended a by-right duplex bonus, which PRO correctly showed already satisfies the motion. CON also leaned on unsupported authority, such as "documented in countless city council minutes" and lenders "assigning higher risk weights." Its ownership-fragmentation answer to Q1 was neatly rebutted: forty detached houses also mean forty titles and forty mortgages.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's sewer-cap response (B2.3–B2.5), sharpened in Rebuttal 2 (B4.3–B4.5) and the closing, was decisive. It showed that a forty-dwelling ceiling handles capacity directly. It also turned CON's "by-right modest bonus" into a concession: if a qualifying duplex can be built by right, the district is no longer single-family-only. That left CON without a coherent position on the motion as written.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A dominated every clash with airtight logic, disciplined mechanisms, and surgical refutation, while Side B repeatedly contradicted itself. Side A cleanly distinguished banning single-family exclusivity from banning detached houses, showed that total dwelling caps and form rules handle infrastructure and streetscape more precisely than single-unit mandates, and kept density bonuses for capacity above the baseline. Side B asserted an unsubstantiated mortgage-rate penalty that it contradicted in B4.4 before repeating in B5.1, accidentally argued for a total ban and against bonuses in B2.4, retreated in B4.2 to a by-right duplex rule that abandons single-family-only zoning, and miscalculated in B4.1 by claiming twenty duplexes create more titles and mortgages than forty detached houses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side B claimed duplexes overwhelm schools and roads [B2.1], Side A asked in Q1 [A3.1] what planning problem forty detached houses avoid that twenty duplexes create when household capacity is fixed at forty. Forced off headcount in [B4.1], Side B argued that twenty duplexes multiply titles, mortgages, and owners—and Side A effortlessly closed the trap in [A5.2] by pointing out that forty detached houses and twenty separately owned duplexes both produce forty titles and forty mortgages.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): A consistently defended the actual distinction between banning exclusive districts and banning detached houses, offering direct capacity limits, published rules, and by-right duplexes as a coherent alternative. A therefore left B defending little beyond exclusivity itself.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s forty-household comparison exposed building type as a poor proxy for service capacity. B’s response shifted to implausible ownership concerns and a by-right bonus that effectively satisfied A’s rule, while A’s closing cleanly dispatched the duplicated-title argument.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): CON (A) wins decisively. PRO's case rests on the claim that banning single-family-only districts creates a mandatory two-unit floor. A dismantled that early with the "one or two dwellings permitted" district example: removing a ceiling does not install a floor. PRO never recovered. In Rebuttal 2, PRO asserted that a house-or-duplex district "functions as" single-family-only zoning. That contradicts the plain meaning of "only," and it effectively concedes that the ban requires a separate minimum, which was exactly A's point. A also showed that the ban applies only to new districts and guarantees no immediate supply. The concrete sewer-capacity scenario gave A a clear comparative mechanism: the bonus with an automatic trigger delivers the same sequence of homes, while a true minimum could delay even the first home. PRO's Minneapolis evidence carried precise, unverified figures ("over 7,000 units," "neighbors stagnant," "newly zoned parcels"). It was repeated without addressing A's causality challenge and also mischaracterizes Minneapolis's reform. PRO's own Q1 and Q2 oddly conceded A's framing. PRO's closing repeated the disproven floor claim rather than answering it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1/A4.1 and the closing planner test: a district permitting a house or a duplex complies with the ban, and nothing exists to grant a variance from. This collapsed PRO's "compulsory floor" premise, which every PRO benefit depended on.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won dominantly by pairing exact textual precision on the motion with sound municipal land-use mechanics, while exposing a fatal conceptual error at the center of Side A's case. Side A repeatedly conflated banning single-family-only districts (forbidding a one-unit ceiling) with banning detached houses (imposing a compulsory two-unit floor), going so far in [A4.1] as to claim that a district allowing either a house or a duplex still counts as single-family-only. Side A also undermined itself in Turn 5 by phrasing its pressure questions as challenges to its own position, and invented unsupported claims in [A4.3] that Minneapolis compelled multi-unit construction on newly zoned parcels. Side B refuted every claim cleanly and also won the even-if weighing: using its sewer-capacity model to show that automatic, ordinance-triggered density bonuses match the ban where service exists while allowing a temporary single-family designation where capacity lags—whereas enforcing Side A's supposed two-unit minimum would only delay the first home.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's one-sentence ordinance test in [B2.1]—sharpened in [B4.1] ("'Only' describes what the district excludes, not what an owner chooses") and [B5.1]—decisively collapsed Side A's core claim that builders would need a variance to construct one house, while [B2.4]-[B2.5] proved that even a separate two-unit minimum could not make an unfinished sewer main carry sewage and would reduce near-term housing.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won decisively by exposing PRO’s central equivocation: banning single-family-only districts removes a one-unit ceiling but does not require a two-unit minimum. A district permitting either houses or duplexes complies with the ban, so a detached house needs no variance. Side A merely repeated and intensified this error while adding unsupported claims about Minneapolis parcels. B’s sewer-capacity example also showed how an automatic bonus can deliver usable flexibility without delaying construction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s “one or two dwellings are permitted per lot” ordinance directly dismantled A’s compulsory-floor mechanism; the planner-and-variance formulation in the closing made the same decisive distinction especially clear.
