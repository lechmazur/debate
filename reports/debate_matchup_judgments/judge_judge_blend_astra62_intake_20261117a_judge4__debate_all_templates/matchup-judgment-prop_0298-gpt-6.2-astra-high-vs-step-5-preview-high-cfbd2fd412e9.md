### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins because CON's final position effectively accepts the motion as written. The proposition asks whether governments should digitize records and publicly fund access rather than leave recovery mostly to private markets. It does not require state ownership of a single search platform. PRO set this up from the opening: public funding paired with community control and confidentiality safeguards. That design defused CON's "state monopoly" and "gatekeeper" framing before it gained traction. PRO's central causal point was never squarely answered: companies lack incentives to index low-demand registers, and library subscriptions cannot add missing records to an index. In A2, CON conceded it would fund searchable transcription and bar exclusive private control. That concedes public funding of discovery. CON then tried to recast the motion as a contest over state custody. That is evasive reframing, because the motion turns on who pays for and drives recovery, not on which ministry holds the servers. CON did land fair points about how fragile legislative budgets are. However, PRO answered them with concrete structures: independent deposit copies, nation-held credentials, and confidential review. PRO also honestly conceded the limits of these remedies and noted that a subscription cannot bind a legislature either. PRO's closing cleanly crystallized CON's concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Q2/A2 exchange was decisive. PRO asked whether CON would fund an archivist to make an unindexed wartime register searchable or stop at preservation. CON answered "I would fund searchable transcription." PRO's closing then used that answer to show that CON's preferred model is publicly funded discovery, which is the motion. This exposed CON's custody-versus-funding distinction as a reframe rather than a rebuttal.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): A defended the proposition more cleanly: public funding of access can be separated from state custody. B repeatedly attacked a state-owned/default-gatekeeper version of the motion, but A's independent repositories and Indigenous-custodian grants removed the control problem while keeping public funding. B's Rebuttal 2 admission that he would fund searchable transcription effectively validated A's core point that missing records need public-funded indexing, not mere preservation. B's remaining distinction between funding work and state custody was less grounded in the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's use of B's admission that he would fund searchable transcription was decisive. It exposed that B's main objection targeted state custody rather than public funding, while A's independent-depository and Indigenous-custodian answers neutralized the future-legislature risk B pressed.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by holding the motion as written while making the right concessions. B's opening insight — commercial indexing follows expected subscriptions, so records reconnecting only a few families go unmade searchable — was never really broken; A's library-card answer only opens existing databases, a point B pressed consistently. A's strongest line was that B's safe models (Indigenous custody, independent depositories) amount to CON's targeted-grant approach, allegedly abandoning the motion. But the proposition requires governments to digitize and publicly fund access, not to own a monopoly search box, and B's closing landed the decisive blow: A's own A2 answer — "I would fund searchable transcription" — concedes the substance of the motion (public funding of discovery) while resisting a state-monopoly strawman B never defended and had disclaimed as early as [B1.5]. B answered pressure questions with concrete, honest structures (deposit copies, confidential review routes, nation-as-grantee contracts) and admitted limits plainly, while A's rebuttals leaned on reinterpreting the motion as "state gatekeeper." Both sides were well-grounded and rhetorically sharp; B made the debate easier to decide on the actual question.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 claimed B had collapsed into CON's model by relocating custody; B's Closing reversed it by quoting A's own concession to "fund searchable transcription," showing that the real dispute — public funding of discovery versus leaving it mostly private — had been conceded by A, leaving only a custody issue the motion never required.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins by establishing a precise counter-model and the decisive mechanism dispute. Its model is publicly funded custodians, referral services, preservation, fee waivers and targeted scanning, with broad searchable indexing of open records left mostly to private services. CON's key insight was that scanning removes handling cost but not interpretation or disclosure review. CON also argued that many descendants of one series does not mean many requests for the same pages. Together these points framed the real question as which investment shortens a family's actual wait. PRO (B) leaned on "lottery," "amortization," and "commercially dead records" framing. It never engaged the claim that disclosure, not retrieval, is the bottleneck for sealed records. PRO's answers under pressure also weakened its case. A1 sidestepped the prioritization question by declaring the choice false and listing every category of record. A2 conceded no override of the community, converging with CON's own position, which CON pointed out. PRO repeatedly mischaracterized CON's keeper as unpaid after CON had specified that keepers are paid. The closing largely repeated earlier lines. PRO's best point, that markets abandon exactly these records, was neutralized by CON's reply that commercial neglect tells us who must help, not what to buy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3–A4.5) was the most decisive exchange. Using PRO's own sealed guardianship ledger, CON showed that a scan does not identify which entries concern the same child or what may be released. It also showed that "thousands of descendants" is not the same as repeated retrieval. This answered PRO's amortization argument directly, and PRO never responded to it.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B wins by reframing the actual bottleneck: scanning fixes handling and legibility, but the binding cost for restricted records is individual disclosure and interpretation. PRO never fully answered B's sealed-ledger point that many descendants do not mean repeated retrieval of the same pages. B also turned PRO's community-consent safeguard into a concession that confidential funded searches, not statewide indexing, satisfy the right.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal 2, especially that a scan opens the ledger without telling staff which entries concern the same child or what can be released, and that PRO now offers the same community-consent search; this narrowed the debate to what actually shortens a family's wait.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Both sides were disciplined and responsive, but the decisive clash—whether digitization actually shortens a family's wait—was controlled by A. A's core analytical move, that a name index requires paying someone to interpret entries nobody has requested while the per-request disclosure judgment survives scanning regardless, was never squarely broken by B. B's amortization claim ("a scan fixes the file once") was effectively blunted by A's point that "thousands of descendants in one series does not mean thousands of requests for the same pages." B's strongest route—the "lottery vs. infrastructure" framing—was answered directly when A specified paid keepers, a publicly funded referral service that follows leads across jurisdictions, and scanning where retrieval is the bottleneck. B's later speeches, especially the closing, largely restated the amortization and market-failure lines rather than resolving A's marginal-cost weighing, while A's speeches consistently narrowed the dispute ("what matters is repeated work actually avoided") and ended with a crisp decision rule: spend the next public dollar on what stands between the family and an answer. B's rhetoric was more memorable, but A did the cleaner comparative work on the live clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange starting at [A2.3]/[B4.1] through A's Rebuttal 2 and closing: B claimed scanning amortizes costs at scale, and A countered that indexing demands interpretive labor on unrequested entries and that disclosure review— not retrieval—is the compounding step, culminating in "a thousand descendants... are not a thousand requests to retrieve the same page." B never fully recovered this ground.
