### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): A wins the central comparison: restricting reachable, trusted promoters can reduce recruitment even when offshore casinos remain accessible. B raises genuine overbreadth concerns, but repeatedly demands operator compliance or deliberate targeting without adequately answering that mechanism.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: A decisively answered B's claim that fining creators cannot make offshore products safer: the prohibition targets the persuasive recruitment channel, not operator compliance. B largely repeated its objection rather than explaining why reducing that recruitment would be ineffective or outweighed by the restriction's costs.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins solidly by pairing a grounded real-world causal story with superior rebuttal, pressure-question execution, and comparative weighing. Side A (CON) gained real traction on the literal breadth of "audiences likely to include minors," rightly noting that the motion itself states no percentage threshold and could capture mixed-audience adult creators. However, Side B mitigated that overbreadth concern—both by pointing to standard regulatory audience-composition thresholds and by showing that even a 5% minor share on a major channel can expose tens of thousands of children while leaving licensed, regulated sponsorships untouched—and dismantled Side A's alternative model. Specifically, Side B showed that operator-side duties (licensing, deposit limits, age assurance) cannot touch unlicensed offshore crypto casinos outside domestic jurisdiction, and exposed Side A's "deliberate youth targeting" test as practically unenforceable when Side A failed in Rebuttal 2 to name any evidence that could prove intent once demographic analytics and loss-share promo codes are excluded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B2.5] and Pressure Questions [B3.1]-[B3.2], Side B pointed out that Side A's operator-side safeguards assume a compliant licensed bookmaker rather than an offshore crypto casino built to evade gates, and asked what evidence could ever prove "deliberate youth targeting" if creator analytics and revenue-share links do not count. When Side A's [A4.1]-[A4.3] neither established leverage over offshore operators nor identified a single workable proof of targeting intent, Side B's closing [B5.1]-[B5.5] decisively crystallized Side A's counter-model as "duties for parties beyond reach and an unprovable test for the one within it."
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side B built the cleaner path to victory: knowable audience composition can trigger protection without proof of a confession of intent, and promoter liability addresses recruitment harm even when offshore operators never comply. Side A raised genuine vagueness and overbreadth concerns, but over-relied on statutory drafting objections, deliberate intent, and the irrelevant demand that creator liability force operator compliance. B answered both jurisdictional scenarios and supplied the stronger low-cost comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 directly answered A1 and A2: operator enforcement and promoter liability are complements when operators are reachable; when they are not, liability still removes the trusted code-and-revenue-share recruitment channel. A's response largely repeated that this would not secure operator compliance, missing B's narrower exposure-reduction claim.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): A narrowly wins on the distinction between preventing persuasion and blocking transactions: operator safeguards do not replace restrictions on paid recruitment. B sharply challenged the prohibition's breadth, but A supplied a genuine adult-gated exception. More importantly, B never established its decisive claim that removing domestic promotions simply leaves minors watching equivalent offshore material. Surviving reposts do not demonstrate unchanged exposure or future promotional activity. A's inconsistent scope explanations, weak first pressure question, and nonexistent reference IDs substantially limit the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's explanation that prohibition removes sponsorship, affiliate incentives, and repeated trusted recommendations answered the migration objection. B's memorable “same clip” response treated possible residual exposure as proof that persuasion would not decrease, leaving A's central mechanism standing.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won solidly by collapsing Side B's core claim that the prohibition is narrow and administrable. Using B's own premise that algorithmic feeds inevitably mix audiences, A showed that the audience prong acquits no creator on the open internet—a point confirmed when B could only exempt a closed 21+ gated channel—while showing B's "high-risk" criteria ("uncertain returns," bonuses, sportsbooks) encompass commercial gambling generally. Although B made a credible point that active creator endorsements generate stronger youth persuasion than passive reposts, A paired stronger comparative weighing on operator-side levers with cleaner execution, whereas B contradicted itself on audience control and cited nonexistent "C" reference IDs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A2.1]-[A2.4] and [A4.2]-[A4.4], Side A turned Side B's own claim about blind algorithmic distribution against B's conjunctive defense, proving the demographic prong never fails on open feeds and then using B's lone acquittal (a 21+ gated channel) and "uncertain returns" test to show that both limiting prongs collapse.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): A won a clear but competitive debate. B preserved a plausible child-persuasion rationale and explained why removing trusted sponsored funnels could matter, but A more effectively exposed the unstable limits: B’s sole clear acquittal was an already age-gated adult channel, while its high-risk examples risked covering gambling generally. A also supplied the sharper enforcement comparison and directly answered both pressure questions. B reduced, but did not overcome, the scope and substitution concerns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it used B’s 21-plus gated-channel exception to argue that the audience prong excludes virtually no open-feed creator, then pressed the surviving-repost mechanism to contend that the ban removes accountable promotion more reliably than it reduces minors’ exposure.
