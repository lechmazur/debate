### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON wins a competitive debate on sharper clash management. It reframed bundles as cross-subsidy and risk-sharing rather than lock-in, then extracted two concessions and pressed them consistently. First, PRO conceded that customizers "pay different rates" or face a "recalculated price." Second, PRO allowed seasonal or minimum-commitment terms for sports. CON converted both into the claim that the motion relocates the penalty for change rather than removing it, and that it hollows out flexibility where cable-style friction is felt most. PRO's best responses had real force. "A choice that may cost more is not lock-in" is a strong line, and the "proves too much" reply on content economics is reasonable. Even so, PRO's answers on sports grew muddled: supply-side licensing alongside consumer monthly pausing was asserted rather than explained. Both of PRO's main rebuttals were also clipped, so its conclusions were cut off at key moments. CON's administration-burden point was somewhat overstated, but its financing-risk mechanism was never squarely neutralized beyond assertion. PRO argued the motion as written fairly, but CON made the decisive tradeoff clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1–A2.3 decided the debate. CON seized on PRO's B2.2 admission that customizers pay different rates and its B2.4 allowance of minimum commitments for volatile content. It argued that flexibility either costs the discount or vanishes for sports, the very case where it matters most. PRO's A1/A2 answers in B4 reframed this as "a different subscription" but never fully escaped the bind.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): B wins by distinguishing optional, transparently repriced customization from a guarantee that every modification preserves the original discount. A repeatedly attacks that guarantee and treats optional controls as mandatory monthly administration. A's financing-risk mechanism remains credible, but it does not establish that those costs outweigh reduced friction and avoiding whole-bundle cancellations. B better connects its comparative case to the proposition, although its two clipped rebuttals and shifting treatment of sports commitments keep the win competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer that intact bundles retain their discount while modified bundles receive transparent new prices undercuts A's central claim that losing the original discount defeats flexibility. The motion promises easier adjustment, not unchanged subsidies. A's strongest response exposed the tension between B's earlier minimum-commitment language and its monthly sports-flexibility promise.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A better contested the cable-lock-in analogy and converted Side B's concessions on recalculated prices and seasonal limits into a clearer welfare comparison: flexibility relocates rather than removes costs, while stable bundles function as risk-sharing. Side B's choice principle remained intuitive, but clipped rebuttals left the financing and administration clash less fully answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 used B's own "recalculated price" answer to show the bundle discount is revoked at the moment flexibility is used, reframing the motion as risk transfer rather than liberation.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) held the motion as written: make pausing, splitting, and reconfiguring easier under disclosed terms, rather than impose cable-style friction. CON (A) raised real concerns about lost bundle discounts, administrative burden, and new conditional pricing. Its "redundant or disruptive" dilemma was a fair move. But in A4.1 CON said it would prohibit neither pausing a sports tier nor separating a kids' account. It also endorsed disclosed reactivation terms and changes taking effect at the next billing date. That largely concedes the proposition's substance. CON's remaining objection, against making a "monthly reset the default architecture," fights a stronger version of the motion than PRO defended. PRO had already rejected that version in B4.1 by describing a published pause policy rather than a monthly auction. CON's closing did land one point: PRO's "without penalty" sits uneasily with its concession that separating can forfeit bundle savings. That tension is minor, because PRO had already framed lost savings as an honest consequence rather than a penalty. Both sides lost an opening or rebuttal to clipping, so execution costs roughly cancel. Overall, PRO gave the cleaner reason to vote and made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was the Pressure Questions round and its answers. PRO's Q1 asked which consumer action CON would actually prohibit. CON answered in A4.1 that it would prohibit neither pausing nor splitting. PRO then reframed in B4.5: the family in A2.2 that pauses sports and finds stand-alone services more expensive has gained the decisive information, the bundle's real value. That turned CON's main example into support for PRO.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): B wins the proposition as written by distinguishing optional monthly flexibility from mandatory renegotiation. Its published-policy answer allows disclosed discount forfeiture without engineered exit friction. A raises plausible administrative-cost and pricing-stability concerns that B only partly answers, but repeatedly attacks compulsory restructuring rather than easier changes. Under pressure, A permits pausing and splitting on disclosed terms at the next billing date—substantially conceding B’s practical model. B therefore provides the clearer reason to support the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal explicitly concedes that splitting need not preserve the bundle discount, separating legitimate pricing consequences from lock-in. A’s closing dilemma fails to overcome that distinction and conflicts with A’s own earlier description of discount forfeiture as ordinary accounting rather than lock-in.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B won by defending the proposition as written: easier monthly pause, split, and reconfiguration under disclosed terms, not mandatory churn. B answered the rights and pricing objections, treated lost bundle discounts as an honest consequence of unbundling, and turned the sports-tier example into a reason that easy exit reveals a bundle’s real value. Side A’s welfare case and closing dilemma were sharp, and B’s “without penalty” close was sloppy, but A would not bar the very actions the motion concerns and mainly opposed a stronger architecture than B kept on the table.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered that advertised access must be preserved while the bundle discount need not survive unbundling, and recast A’s more-expensive stand-alone example as useful information rather than a reason to keep exit costly.
