### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won on the motion's comparative "more" by exploiting a real evidentiary asymmetry: randomized patrol trials show marginal gains, while CON (A) conceded in Rebuttal 2 that it had "no comparable trial showing a citywide sentencing advantage" and could not give a reliable share of violence its policy reaches. A fought well on the margins — the point that trials compare against ordinary coverage rather than equal-cost custody, and that counting passersby mistakes breadth for violence prevented — but never supplied a positive reason to rank sentencing above patrol, only reasons for uncertainty. B's dilemma in Rebuttal 2 ("every time CON makes sentencing more defensible, it makes sentencing smaller") forced A into defending a narrow, late-acting policy, and A's honest concessions left that route intact. A's opening reframe (incapacitation through opportunity, private and grievance violence) was genuinely original and created early pressure, but B narrowed it effectively by showing most violence clusters publicly and that many active shooters are never convicted. Both sides followed format cleanly and avoided fake precision; B's England and Wales example for A was cautious and plausible. B made the debate easier to decide and closed with a memorable, earned weighing ("a smaller proven gain still beats an unmeasured one"), which A's closing contested but could not fully overturn.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Q1 asking what evidence shows sentence extensions reduce citywide violence net of replacement and post-release effects. A's answer ("I have no comparable trial showing a citywide sentencing advantage") became the decisive concession, which B crystallized in the closing: CON's policy is not merely unmeasured but small by design.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins a clear, competitive debate by proving the comparative scale required by the word "more." Side B anchored its case in spatial crime concentration, immediate deterrence over distant severity, randomized patrol trials showing crime drops with diffusion of benefits, and low shooting clearance rates. Side A argued with admirable analytical precision and epistemic honesty—sharply distinguishing dealer replacement from feud replacement and exposing Side B's loose "protects all passersby" phrasing—and only loses ground because narrowing sentencing to a modest post-base-sentence aggravation for recent repeat violent convictions shrank its citywide reach.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: In Turn 7 ([B4.3]-[B4.6]), Side B granted Side A's modest sentencing model rather than fighting a strawman, then showed that every move Side A made to keep sentencing defensible—short extensions to avoid aging out, personal feuds to avoid replacement, and prior violent convictions to avoid over-breadth—made the policy smaller, late-acting, and unable to reach the unconvicted shooters driving citywide gun violence.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Both sides ran disciplined, well-matched cases built on marginal logic rather than fake precision. B's strongest surviving routes were the symmetric-test principle (incapacitation must also predict violence during the added years) and the cost/testability comparison. But A consistently created sharper comparative pressure: A2.2's argument that a repeat offender's own history is the most direct forecast available, A4.3's reasonable lowering of the displacement burden ("enough displacement plus decay"), and above all the exploitation of B's two concessions — that field evidence does not establish a separate effect on shootings [B4.1] and that post-release violence is only "a serious possibility" [B5.1]. A's closing crystallized those concessions into a clean weighing: on the gravest violence, one policy rests on a person-specific forecast and the other on a gap. B's responses (violent-crime reductions at hot spots, place patterns predicting timing better than convictions) were good but never fully repaired the shootings concession in a motion about violent crime. B's cost-symmetry point [B4.5] was A's weakest answered spot, but A's dilution answer was at least responsive. Format compliance was clean on both sides (proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 structure). A razor-thin but real edge to CON on clash resolution and crystallization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's second rebuttal and closing: after B conceded in A1 that the field evidence "does not establish a separate effect on shootings" [B4.1], A hammered that B's case "thins out exactly where the violence is gravest" [A4.5] and then welded it to B's own admission that post-release violence is "a serious possibility" [B5.1], framing the final choice as a person-specific forecast versus a gap. B's symmetric-test counter in B4.4 ("apply the same test to both") was the best attempt at repair but could not undo the evidence concession.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a high-level, competitive debate through sharper marginal and resource comparisons. Side B showed that sentence extensions only take effect years later after the base sentence ends, exposed Side A's apples-to-oranges contrast between a year of prison and an hour of patrol, and demonstrated that equivalent funds buy many targeted shifts protecting crowds from any attacker rather than incapacitating just one aging offender. Side B also convincingly answered displacement and dilution critiques—explaining that robbers cannot move concentrated passenger streams to empty bus stops and that covering unpatrolled arrival windows expands prevention at high-harm sites—while turning tight accountability into an outcome-auditing tool focused on injuries and robberies. Side A framed the severity of retaliatory shootings and domestic violence memorably, but relied on an asymmetric standard of proof on shootings in closing and never overcame Side B's dollar-for-dollar comparative math.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's takedown of Side A's unit and coverage framing across [B3.1], [B4.5], and [B5.1]-[B5.2]: after Side A contrasted a year of custody protecting every street with one patrol hour on one block, Side B forced an equal-budget comparison across many high-risk shifts and noted that custody protects every street from only one person—so listing every street he cannot visit makes an attack during the added year no more likely.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B built the stronger comparative case by consistently focusing on marginal benefits: added prison years prevent only offenses that would otherwise occur after ordinary release, while targeted patrol can be tested against violent-crime outcomes and adjusted. Side A landed effective attacks on displacement, expansion dilution, and weak evidence about shootings, but often asserted rather than demonstrated that these effects would outweigh incapacitation and treated continuous custody as though it guaranteed substantial counterfactual prevention. B answered both pressure questions directly, candidly acknowledged evidential limits, and exposed A’s asymmetrical comparisons without relying on inflated precision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both questions directly, applied the same counterfactual-prevention test to patrol and custody, and replaced A’s patrol-hour-versus-prison-year comparison with a dollar-for-dollar comparison across recurring high-risk opportunities.
