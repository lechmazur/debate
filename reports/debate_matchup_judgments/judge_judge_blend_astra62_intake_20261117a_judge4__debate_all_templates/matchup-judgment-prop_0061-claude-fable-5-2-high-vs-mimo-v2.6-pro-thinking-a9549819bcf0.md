- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate. B's embrace of a smaller, consolidated country is a defensible reframe, yet it sits close to the managed decline the motion contrasts against immigration, and B never fully resolved who staffs the care beds. A's closings crystallized the clash ("no workers and no year"); B's closing repeated earlier points more than it advanced new weighing. Both sides had clipped turns, roughly offsetting execution costs; both used pressure questions and A1/A2 format correctly.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B won a narrow decision by making the motion's own wording — "prioritize immigration rather than rely mainly on the alternatives" — the decisive weighing axis and holding A to it across every round. A's strongest ground (care work is relational, automation frees rather than adds hands, only immigration adds people/consumers/taxpayers now) was genuinely damaging, and A's "trap" callout against B's full-offset standard was a fair reframe. But B extracted two real concessions: A repeatedly declined to claim immigration covers the larger share of the demographic gap (calling the levers "complements"), and A answered B's retention trilemma by accepting higher pay and lighter workloads — B's own mechanism — leaving "status pathways" as the only difference, which B effectively gutted as either mobility-restricting or temporary. B also gave concrete, plausible domestic sources (returners, part-time hours capped by tax thresholds, reallocation from low-value work), blunting A's "adds a single worker" test enough to survive. Both sides suffered clipped rebuttals (B twice, A once), which kept this competitive, and A's timing argument kept the outcome close. B's cleaner comparative discipline on the motion as written tips it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal (B4.6–B4.9): after A's answers, B showed A had twice declined to claim immigration covers the majority of the gap and had adopted B's wage-and-workload mechanism wholesale, then closed the "route to status" loophole by showing status either tethers the migrant to care or dissolves once they settle — converting A's best sector into B's treadmill.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins a high-level, competitive debate by framing the motion's priority test cleanly and winning the central clash over elder care and labor adaptation. Conceding selective, supplementary immigration early on, Side A showed that population decline leaves a shrinking workforce under either realist scenario, making permanent output-per-worker gains and cross-sector labor reallocation the main event rather than an ever-expanding migration treadmill constrained by a weak yen, language barriers, and aging source countries. Side B (PRO) mounted smart arguments comparing inflows to shrinking birth cohorts, highlighting unpaid female family care, and pressing SME adoption delays, while both sides incurred minor execution blemishes (two small end-of-turn clips for A; one clip and a nonexistent C-prefix reference ID for B). Ultimately, Side A proved that Side B's own non-exploitative residency model cannot retain care workers without Side A's wage and productivity mechanisms doing the primary work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's elder-care dilemma and reallocation rebuttal ([A2.5–A2.9] and [A4.1–A4.9]): Side A showed that once migrants receive permanent residency and full mobility, they leave low-paid care just like domestic workers unless higher wages and productivity lighten the job—even turning Side B's "care as a route to status" defense into a sectoral recruitment treadmill—while explaining concretely how retail automation ("Self-checkout does not lift a patient. It frees the cashier who can") and uncapping female part-time hours supply domestic care labor.

B effectively exposed A’s excessive full-offset arithmetic and made a strong timing and labor-addition case, but never squarely established why immigration was the primary strategy rather than a necessary supplement. A’s closing crystallized that dependency more decisively, though B’s argument that A merely redistributes a shrinking workforce remained a live strength.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both pressure questions concretely, then used B’s “complements, not rivals” answer and acceptance of higher pay and lighter workloads to argue that immigration covered only part of the problem and depended on A’s mechanisms to retain care workers.
