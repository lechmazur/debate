### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): This was an unusually even, high-quality exchange. A built the textbook case (volatility smoothing, intergenerational preservation, rule-based accountability) and set a strong trap: CON's "lean stabilization reserve" is fund logic, and if rules can protect a dividend they can protect a fund. B defused that trap better than any other pressure point in the debate, drawing a principled line between a bounded, capped smoothing account and an open-ended discretionary portfolio, and warning that "fund logic" was expanding until it no longer named the motion. B also held the framing terrain throughout—weak institutions in developing countries, exactly the setting the motion specifies—and neutralized Botswana/Chile with the precondition argument. A's best counter was the visibility inversion (the missing bond, the unfunded payroll, the pledged royalty are cash's invisible losses), which landed well, but B's answer that shrinking checks under a public formula make adjustment and borrowing unmistakably political held up. B's closing crystallization (B5.2)—that once both sides accept rules, the only remaining difference is default retention of a large discretionary principal in institutions fragile states cannot safely run—was the cleanest voting issue on the table. A's mirror-image closing was strong but slightly less decisive. Razor-thin edge to B on framing control, the buffer distinction, and final weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The A3.1 pressure question ("what principled line separates your lean reserve from a sovereign wealth fund?") was A's sharpest weapon, but B4.1-B4.2 answered it head-on with the bounded-versus-open-ended distinction and the definitional warning, then flipped the symmetry back in B5.2: if rules can protect a multi-billion portfolio, they can protect a capped reserve plus dividend—leaving discretionary principal retention as the real, unsafe difference. That exchange decided the debate's central clash.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): B wins the comparison between visibility and stabilization. A persuasively identifies additional opportunities for capture in retained public assets, but never establishes why a sovereign fund must be open-ended and discretionary while its preferred reserve can be capped and rule-bound. B exploits that inconsistency and explains why noticing smaller checks does not replace savings available during a bust. A answers the pressure directly, but its acceptance of shrinking dividends leaves B's stabilization advantage standing. B understates cash's human-capital benefits, yet its broader management case survives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's closing directly challenges A's reserve distinction: the same safeguards could constrain a fund. Paired with the observation that a visible dividend cut reveals distress without supplying additional resources to cushion it, this materially weakens A's final route to victory.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side B wins a clear but highly competitive debate by establishing institutional realism as a prerequisite to Side A's macroeconomic and intergenerational goals. Side A made strong arguments on non-tradable inflation, public infrastructure, pro-cyclical bust pain, and the tension in Side B's "lean buffer." However, once Side A conceded that ministries and courts may be captured, Side B proved a crucial asymmetry: while upstream royalty skimming threatens both models, retaining principal in an open-ended fund adds low-visibility downstream chokepoints—collateralized borrowing, connected fees, and ministry procurement—that silently drain wealth before it ever funds clinics or teachers, whereas universal cash puts resources directly into poor households and creates millions of immediate watchdogs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's governance defense across [B2.1], [B2.6], and [B5.1]: after Side A argued that corruption affects the whole chain and pitched sovereign funds as offering rule-based "control before liquidation" [A4.1-A4.3, A5.1], Side B cleanly separated symmetric upstream theft from asymmetric post-deposit discretion and flipped Side A's closing crux on its head—showing that in weak states, "control before liquidation" belongs not to honest trustees, but to the very rulers incentivized to pledge or raid the fund out of public view.
