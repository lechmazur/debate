CON then exploited that concession relentlessly in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, showing that the motion's breadth strips actual complainants of a remedy they may want, in exchange for only a contingent, unguaranteed increase in third-party pattern visibility (since PRO itself admitted unenforceability "guarantees no disclosure"). CON's Rebuttal 1 being clipped for exceeding the word limit is a real execution cost, but the core argument survived into later rounds, limiting the damage.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but PRO built the more resilient structure. PRO's core distinction—courts can protect genuine privacy (medical records, witnesses, employer's own disclosures) through ordinary contract/defamation mechanisms without enforcing a gag specifically on the victim's account of her own abuse—survived CON's attacks. CON's strongest asset, the "reciprocal/mutual confidentiality as an exchange" argument, was undercut when PRO exposed it as question-begging (B4.5: CON's "one-sided nullification" assumes employers are entitled to buy silence in the first place, which is the very proposition at issue). CON never cleanly answered that circularity charge in Rebuttal 2 or Closing, instead repeating the "package/paternalism" framing. PRO also handled the Pressure Questions cleanly, biting the bullet on the "involving disputed allegations" issue and turning it into a clarity advantage rather than a liability, while CON's answers (contract breach remedies, right-to-respond clauses) felt like patches rather than systemic solutions to the asymmetric-burden problem PRO raised (vulnerable workers having to litigate coercion after the fact). PRO's closing crystallized the clash sharply ("settlement finality is not speech finality"), while CON's closing largely restated earlier framing without resolving the circularity or burden-of-review gaps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B4.5 rebuttal, calling out that CON's "one-sided nullification" argument presupposes employers are entitled to purchase silence—the very question the debate was about—went unanswered and undermined CON's central reciprocity framework for the rest of the debate.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON won the core trade-off. PRO proved secrecy can hide patterns, but CON showed categorical unenforceability also destroys genuinely mutual, counseled, claimant-requested confidentiality and its compensation premium. PRO's A1 admission that even unadjudicated allegations void NDAs, plus A2 claim that employer duties stay enforceable while claimant's promise becomes worthless, left unanswered consideration-failure and paternalism costs. CON's breach/right-to-respond answer and tailored alternative (preserve police/regulator/court reporting, strike coercion/obstruction) kept public protection without sacrificing agency.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 & 2 on breadth and one-sided nullification - arguing disputed allegations trigger invalidity and defamation is no substitute - was the decisive exchange against PRO's premium-for-silence turn.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built a more compelling and logically cohesive case, effectively framing the core clash around whether courts should enforce the purchase of a victim's future voice. Side B successfully neutralized Side A's "mutual confidentiality" and "reduced settlement" arguments by highlighting the practical burden such agreements place on victims who must later litigate to escape asymmetric gags. Side B's handling of pressure questions was sharper and more direct, while Side A relied on theoretically possible but practically burdensome contract remedies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they decisively contrasted the risk allocation of both models, pointing out that CON's framework forces victims to return to court to prove coercion or breach, whereas PRO's bright-line rule simply removes the employer's power to compel silence.
