### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins by keeping the debate tied to the motion's actual test: the gap between what advocates expected and what cameras delivered. CON (A) argued with polish, but its main comparison, "cameras versus the former evidentiary vacuum," only shows that accountability improved at all. PRO exposed this cleanly in B2.1. CON then tried to narrow advocates' expectations to "evidentiary infrastructure," but conceded in A4.1 that communities also expected deterrence and consequences. PRO exploited that concession in the closing. PRO's strongest structural move was the reply to CON's own pressure question. If resistant institutions blunted the tool, that is the expectation gap, because advocates sold cameras specifically to pierce departmental self-protection. CON's counter, that resistance alone cannot show a shortfall, was reasonable but never established that the delivered gains matched the expectations. CON also leaned on what policies "can" do, which PRO fairly labeled ideal rulebooks rather than actual practice. CON's closing line about official narratives losing automatic finality was memorable, but it still measured gains against zero rather than against expectations. PRO had some loose generalizations, such as the claim that agencies routinely release exonerating footage within days. Still, its case was more responsive to the burden as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.2 answering A3.2: PRO turned CON's framing that "resistant institutions blunted a useful evidentiary tool" into an admission of the expectation gap. Cameras were promoted precisely to overcome institutional self-protection, so their domestication is the shortfall. CON's A4.4 reply never fully neutralized this point.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): A better maintains the motion’s comparison: improvement over an evidentiary vacuum does not establish improvement matching advocates’ expectations. A also persuasively treats departmental resistance as part of cameras’ actual accountability performance, rather than a separate failure to discount. B effectively challenges inflated expectations and establishes genuine evidentiary benefits, but repeatedly substitutes usefulness for fulfillment of expectations. A’s sweeping, thinly substantiated characterization of advocates’ original promises keeps this a competitive rather than overwhelming win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.2 directly turns B’s institutional-resistance defense: departments blunting a useful tool can explain why accountability underdelivered. B correctly responds that resistance alone cannot establish an expectation gap, but does not adequately reconcile its modest evidentiary benchmark with the deterrence and consequences it acknowledges advocates expected.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side A won by keeping the debate fixed on the motion’s actual test: whether advocates’ original accountability expectations were met. Side B’s “achieved floors” and “evidentiary infrastructure” framing was clean and epistemically disciplined, but it increasingly looked like a retrospective narrowing of the promise rather than a full answer to the unmet deterrence-and-consequences expectations that A highlighted.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins by building a clear distinction between visibility and answerability, and between what footage makes possible and what is reliably routine. Because the motion is comparative, PRO did not need to show cameras failed, only that they fell short of the promises attached to them. PRO named those promises: deterrence, consistent discipline, and public trust. CON's main strategy was to narrow advocates' expectations to an evidentiary breakthrough. That narrowing was partly persuasive, but PRO flagged it repeatedly as shrinking the benchmark until cameras could not miss it. PRO's Q2 then asked for evidence that review routinely produces discipline or reform. CON answered mostly with confident assertion ("routinely sustained," "pressure cities to tighten policies") rather than grounded mechanism. CON's strongest moves were the deterrence-under-uncertainty argument (officers cannot know which stop will be scrutinized) and the alleged contradiction that advocates knew legal limits. PRO answered the contradiction cleanly in its closing: separate legal campaigns do not erase the broader expectations placed on cameras. CON's closing line that accountability improved "every bit as promised" overreaches and is less credible than PRO's calibrated concession of real but bounded gains.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 in Rebuttal 2 conceded that stopping a tainted prosecution is real accountability, then distinguished case-specific correction from institutional change in sanctions, supervision, and repeat conduct. It also offered a concrete, realistic benchmark. This neutralized CON's best question and reframed CON's examples as winning a "smaller motion."
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B built the stronger comparative case through external discovery, complaint review, and constraints on fabricated reports, directly challenging A’s separation of documentation from accountability. A correctly argued that genuine benefits can coexist with disappointed expectations, but largely asserted the expected transformation and actual shortfall rather than establishing their scale. B also better explained why potential later scrutiny matters outside existing proceedings. Its sweeping claims about routine compliance and fully satisfied expectations limit the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s discovery-and-subpoena response undermined A’s claim that departmental control keeps footage from producing independent accountability. When A narrowed the objection to encounters outside legal proceedings, B answered with uncertainty about future complaints and the role of fabricated reports in enabling abuse—a stronger response than merely citing successful cases.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side A won by keeping the debate focused on the motion’s comparative test: whether cameras delivered the broader accountability advocates expected, not merely whether footage sometimes helps. B made strong points about legal discovery, missing-footage inferences, and deterrence, but A successfully framed those gains as contingent and case-specific rather than dependable institutional accountability. A’s visibility-versus-answerability closing gave the cleaner reason to vote PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal turned B’s dropped-charge and suppression examples into a concession that cameras can correct individual cases without routinely sanctioning officers or changing institutions; B’s deterrence answer helped, but left the routine-encounter burden partly unresolved.
