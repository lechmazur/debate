### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Both sides argued competently and avoided fabricated sourcing, but A built a cleaner, more cumulative case: real-name mandates create an irreversible, compelled identity link that silences cautious speakers before any abuse occurs, while the claimed deterrence benefit is only marginal and achievable through less revealing tools. A's pressure questions forced B into an important concession ("No such evidence has been presented" that legal names add decisive deterrence beyond nonpublic linking/friction), and A's closing crystallized this into a memorable asymmetry ("an abusive account is replaceable; a person's legal identity is not"). B's rebuttals were analytically sound in isolation, but B's strategy increasingly shifted to attacking PRO's burden rather than affirmatively establishing that real-name policies' benefits outweigh the harms, and by closing B's own language ("mandatory real names impose certain loss... for an uncertain marginal gain... fail the proposition's net-harm test") effectively echoed and reinforced PRO's framing rather than defending CON's side. That self-undermining drift, combined with A's sharper, more unified weighing narrative, gives PRO the clearer and more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Questions (A3.1/A3.2) forcing B to admit "no such evidence has been presented" for the marginal deterrence benefit of legal names, which A then leveraged directly in its closing to crystallize the irreversibility asymmetry.

Side B maintained perfect consistency and masterfully capitalized on Side A's self-destruction in the final speech.

- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A maintained the replaceable-account vs irreplaceable-identity asymmetry and invisible self-censorship. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly without fake sourcing and respected the format (proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 labeling), but B built the more resilient structure. B's key move—distinguishing private, access-restricted verification from public display—forced A to keep arguing against an idealized worst-case rather than the strongest version of the policy, and A never fully closed that gap (its strongest reply, that the compelled-disclosure pathway exists regardless of display, was asserted but not developed as forcefully as it could have been, e.g., via state-compulsion scenarios). B's pressure questions were sharper, exposing a real circularity in A's "persistent account" alternative (a new account is just as "persistent" until banned), and A's rebuttal conceded ground by reaching for device/payment/behavioral identifiers that carry similar privacy costs to the ones A was attacking. B's survivor example also successfully complicated the "vulnerable users only need concealment" framing, and A's response to it (reactive cleanup is enough) didn't fully neutralize the prevention point. A kept a consistent, intuitively strong asymmetry narrative (universal cost vs. concentrated benefit) and landed good closing rhetoric, but B's burden-framing ("the motion asks for net harm, not imperfection") and tighter handling of the pressure exchange gave it the cleaner path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question Q1 ("what stops a banned abuser from creating another persistent account, and what links it to the same offender?") and A's partial concession in Rebuttal 2 (falling back on device/payment/behavioral linkage) was the most decisive exchange, since it exposed that A's main alternative to real-name verification quietly relies on similarly identity-revealing data.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B won by providing a clearer comparative weighing of the harms and benefits, particularly through the framework that real-name policies are exact and universally burdensome for ordinary vulnerable users, but evadable by determined abusers. Side A ran a very strong "double standard" critique, but Side B successfully demonstrated that the marginal risk of a legal-name database outweighs its marginal enforcement benefits over circumstantial device tracking.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized Side A's enforcement claims by weaponizing Side A's own concession (that platforms already have device data) to prove that real names offer little marginal benefit while creating a universal risk.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): PRO maintained the decisive weighing: private verification still requires linking legal identity to posts/messages, creating a breach/compulsion/chilling risk borne by every dissident, whistleblower and vulnerable user, while CON's gains are partial, concentrated on reset/impersonation, achievable imperfectly by other identifiers, and evadable via false documents. CON's valid mitigation to private display was answered by dossier logic, and its persistent-account critique never showed verification's marginal benefit outweighs universal attributable risk. That net-harm comparison maps directly to the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1-A2.3 private display vs linkage: PRO showed restricting display does not remove the identity-to-content bridge, making breach/compulsion more consequential and undercutting CON's core defense that private verification solves the harm.
