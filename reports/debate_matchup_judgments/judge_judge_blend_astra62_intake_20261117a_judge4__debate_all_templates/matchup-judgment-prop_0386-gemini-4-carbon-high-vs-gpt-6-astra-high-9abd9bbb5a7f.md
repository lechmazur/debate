### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides were strong, but A (PRO) controlled the decisive clash. A's state-monopoly "sole legal key" framing gave a principled answer to B's central challenge that a patient's access right does not establish a right to compel this clinician's participation: when no unburdened substitute exists, exclusivity turns refusal from non-participation into an active blockade. A bit every bullet honestly and directly (A4.1, A4.2), and its symmetry response to B's sharpest attack — that enforcing the rule loses the town's only clinician while delivering no treatment — was genuinely responsive: B's own emergency, nondiscrimination, and (per B4.1) time-sensitive-care duties carry the identical departure risk. B's reply that those duties rest on independent grounds was coherent but never explained why patient dependency from legal exclusivity is not itself an independent ground. B's pressure answers were competent (especially avoiding theology-ranking via sincerity/urgency criteria), but B4.1's concession that refusals may stand where "clinical risk is low" despite substantial burden exposed B to A's closing image of rural patients priced out of contraception, which B never fully answered. B's assisted-dying case remained live throughout but A's "constructive veto" and "theology imposed on a patient's final days" responses kept it competitive rather than decisive. Cleaner framing, sharper pressure questions, and more direct answers give A a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.4's "delivers neither service" attack (sanctioning the objector loses the clinician and still no treatment) was B's best moment, but A4.2 and A5.4's symmetry response — that CON's own mandatory duties carry the same departure risk, so medicine never lets sole gatekeepers ransom ordinary care — turned the exchange in A's favor.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B more convincingly defended the bright-line rule: state-licensed exclusivity makes a sole provider's refusal an effective blockade, so conscience yields once major burden appears. B answered the assisted-dying and exit questions directly and showed the departure risk also hits duties A itself accepts, while A's proportionate alternative still permits substantial travel and cost barriers or unstable case-by-case judgments. A's gravity and prior-disclosure distinctions were serious but did not close that gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answers, reinforced in closing, neutralized the "lose both services" attack by noting that exit equally undermines time-sensitive and nondiscrimination duties A would still mandate, so departure cannot uniquely defeat the no-major-burden limit.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): CON won by turning PRO’s access rationale against it: PRO accepted that enforcing the rule could remove the town’s only clinician without delivering the contested treatment, weakening the claim that the rule reliably protects patient access. PRO’s monopoly/gatekeeper frame was clear and forceful, but CON’s proportionality line—urgency, prior disclosure, meaningful assistance, and no compelled participation in grave contested acts—answered the hardest cases more persuasively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s pressure question and Rebuttal 2 exchange on the sole rural clinician were decisive: PRO honestly accepted losing ordinary care rather than permitting refusal, and CON crystallized that this sacrifices both the disputed treatment and routine care, while PRO’s reply that CON’s duties could also cause departure was less persuasive because CON limits those duties to urgent and anti-abandonment cases.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side A (PRO) built the cleaner path to victory. Side B's strongest live route was the rural-compulsion scenario (forcing a lone clinician to administer contested acts) plus workforce loss, but A dismantled its key premise: B's counterfactual baseline ("the clinician didn't cause the shortage") was met with the sharper comparison — the relevant choice is providing vs. withholding care for the patient actually dependent on you, not a hypothetical town without the clinician. A also exposed B's emergency exception as arbitrary (why must a patient near death before access matters?) and B's answer — conceding patients must bear serious preventable harm outside emergencies — handed A the decisive asymmetry A crystallized in closing. B's workability attack (the "financial audit" contradiction) was genuinely sharp, but A's "reasonable inquiry" answer, while imperfect, survived scrutiny. B's workforce loss claim rested on asserted inevitability rather than established comparison, and A repeatedly forced the weighing B only partially supplied. Both sides answered pressure questions directly with proper A1/A2 format and showed excellent epistemic discipline (no fake citations, plausible examples). B was more original rhetorically ("urban luxury," "moral conscription"), but A's argumentative core — the person controlling essential care cannot transfer the costs of a personal belief onto someone who never accepted it — went more squarely unanswered. B never fully refuted that the patient's actual, present dependence is the morally salient relationship, relying instead on state-blame deflection that A repeatedly punctured.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's Rebuttal 1/Closing response to B's baseline argument: B claimed rural clinicians never caused shortages and thus shouldn't be "conscripted," but A reframed the comparison as providing vs. withholding care for the dependent patient — not the hypothetical world where the clinician never arrived — which B's closing ("imaginary town") attempted to flip but could not neutralize.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) built the stronger path by showing that the proposed limit on conscience would bite hardest exactly where alternatives are already scarcest, creating a plausible deterrent against rural and solo practice and a worse net loss of routine care. Side B cleanly defended the principle that patients should not bear unconsented serious harm and answered the lethal-act question directly, but did not neutralize the geographic-incentive and comparative-harm points or the difficulty of judging “major burden” without either rubber-stamping hardship or probing finances.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 comparison: an objecting local clinician who declines rare contested acts still supplies everyday care, while a rule that strips conscience only in provider deserts risks empty posts that supply neither contested nor routine treatment.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): A wins narrowly by keeping the decision rule tied to actual patient access and by offering a plausible reasonable-inquiry/institutional-handoff model. B’s rural-backfire and moral-conscription lines were vivid, but rested on more speculative workforce predictions and left serious non-emergency harms less clearly addressed, while A directly answered pressure and framed the decisive asymmetry.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure forced A to concede that a lone clinician might have to perform abortion or assisted dying when no alternative exists, but A answered directly and pivoted to the patient’s unchosen burden; A’s pressure on serious non-emergency harm also exposed a gap in B’s emergency-only fallback.
