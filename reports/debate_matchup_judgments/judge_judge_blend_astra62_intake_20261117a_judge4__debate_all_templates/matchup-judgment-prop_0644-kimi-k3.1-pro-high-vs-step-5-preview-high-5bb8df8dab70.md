### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO wins on the default-rule comparison. Its core claim was that under a sunset, continued emergency power must earn affirmative consent, while under CON's model it persists unless someone defeats the executive. That claim was never squarely neutralized. CON's most original line, that a renewal vote "launders" overreach into ratified legitimacy and makes courts defer, was interesting. But it committed CON to preferring silent statutory continuation, and PRO correctly called that the central contradiction. CON's alternative of narrow drafting does not answer PRO's Q2 about crises that outgrow the original statute. Its proposed fix, a "new, targeted grant," is itself a fresh legislative vote, which concedes much of PRO's logic. CON also misdescribed the mechanism late. It claimed in A4.4 that under PRO power "continues unless someone wins a fear-driven vote," but an automatic sunset means power lapses by default. It also echoed PRO's "requires no decision at all" line back at PRO in a way that made little sense. PRO answered both pressure questions directly and kept narrowing the debate toward who bears the burden. PRO also conceded the limits of sunsets without letting that concession sink its case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 in Rebuttal 2 (B4.2) was decisive. CON's clock-running and brinkmanship attack, pressed across A1.4, A2.5 and A3.2, assumed that delay favors the executive. PRO showed the reverse: with automatic expiry, stalling produces lapse rather than indefinite power, so "chaos or blanket renewal" is a political choice, not a legal necessity. CON never recovered this point. It later misstated the sunset default to keep the argument alive.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Both sides executed cleanly and followed the A1/A2 protocol. CON's best route was the legitimacy-laundering claim: renewal votes ratify overreach and invite judicial deference. PRO blunted it by noting judicial review survives renewal and, more decisively, by the parallel argument that if crisis fear guarantees rubber-stamping, it equally guts CON's drafting-committee-court alternatives, which also depend on legislative and judicial courage. PRO's burden-shift framing (power expires by default rather than persisting until someone defeats the executive) survived the exchange, and PRO's answer to the brinkmanship question—executive delay produces expiry, not continued power—reversed one of CON's strongest points. CON's counter that narrow drafting ends power when its justification ends relied on an idealized statute that PRO had already shown cannot anticipate a crisis's mutations. Slight edge to A on rebuttal sharpness and closing crystallization; B retains a genuinely original surviving point, keeping the margin clear but competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.2's reversal of B's clock-running/brinkmanship argument: under an affirmative renewal requirement, executive delay destroys power rather than preserving it, turning "chaos or blanket renewal" into a political choice, not a legal necessity.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a cleaner institutional comparison and dismantling Side B's core counterfactual. While Side B argued creatively that crisis renewal votes launder overreach and induce judicial deference, its counter-model relied on a false choice between narrow initial drafting and sunset review. Side A persuasively showed that democracies need both because initial grants occur under peak uncertainty and powers proportionate in week one can become excessive as facts change. Side A also exposed an internal contradiction in Side B's case: if crisis fear and executive control over threat narratives paralyze legislatures, Side B's reliance on initial enactment, condition-based triggers, and committee oversight is vulnerable to the same dynamics while leaving broad authority active by default without a hearing or vote. Paired with Side A's crisp refutation of executive clock-running and two garbled, self-contradictory formulations in Side B's second rebuttal ([B4.4]-[B4.5]), Side A secured a clear victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's opening sequence in Rebuttal 2 ([A4.1]-[A4.3]) was the most decisive exchange: it explained why statutory breadth cannot be fixed once and for all when crisis proportionality shifts over time, showed that automatic expiry defeats executive stalling because known deadlines let legislatures pass narrowed renewals before powers lapse, and turned Side B's fear argument against Side B's own legislative alternatives.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): This is a close debate, and CON edges it on rebuttal depth. Both sides converged on the right decisive question: what happens by default when politics malfunctions. PRO framed that question memorably. Its strongest lines were the ratchet argument and the contrast of "visible and temporary" hazards against "invisible and cumulative" harms. It also adapted well under pressure by proposing a short bridge period and separate renewal votes. CON, however, won more of the substantive exchanges: - **Laundering turn.** CON showed that PRO's own political diagnosis cuts against PRO. If legislators fear looking weak, a deadline pressures them to renew, and the result then gets branded as fresh consent. PRO never squarely answered this; its closing restated "on the record" consent rather than explaining why a timid legislature would reject renewal. - **Bridge critique.** CON correctly noted that the bridge relocates the cliff rather than removing it. It also showed that executive certification of "indispensable" powers reintroduces the very discretion PRO distrusts. - **Bundling.** CON's point that one broad grant can itself contain several intrusive authorities blunted PRO's power-by-power answer. PRO's Q2 was its best pressure. It drew CON into conceding that the legislature cannot reliably repeal either. CON reframed that concession honestly as a difference in failure defaults, which limited the damage. CON pays a real execution cost: two turns were clipped for exceeding the word limit. The lost content appears marginal, so the cost does not reverse the outcome. PRO's bridge-and-separate-vote design was a legitimate answer to live pressure, but it was introduced late and remained underdeveloped.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's A2 and closing answer to PRO's Q2. CON conceded that legislatures are unreliable at repeal, then turned that concession: a timid legislature facing a deadline is just as likely to rubber-stamp renewal, and now with fresh democratic legitimacy. PRO never fully neutralized this, leaving its accountability benefit uncertain while CON's cliff risk stood.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides were competent and the core clash—"what is the default when politics malfunctions"—was joined seriously throughout. Side A grounded its case in the concrete persistence pattern of post-9/11 and pandemic-era powers, and answered Side B's pressure questions directly with a short certified bridge and power-by-power renewal votes, which blunted the cliff and bundling attacks. Side B landed sharp turns (whipped renewals "launder" overreach; executive certification of "indispensable" powers restores the discretion PRO distrusts), but its case rested on speculative mechanisms rather than concrete examples, and its opening and first rebuttal were both clipped for exceeding the word limit—a repeated execution cost. Side A's closing crystallization (visible temporary hazard vs. invisible cumulative harm, "renewable grant, not an inheritance") gave the cleaner reason to vote. Narrow-to-clear edge to PRO on grounding, responsiveness, and execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.4) was decisive: it answered B's cliff and bundling pressure with a short automatic bridge and mandatory separate renewal votes, converting B's strongest operational attack into a design detail. B's best counter was B4.2, noting the bridge "merely moves the cliff" and that executive certification trusts the very actor PRO distrusts—but it did not fully erase the design response.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B built the cleaner comparative path to victory by framing the debate around what happens when politics malfunctions. While Side A made a strong case that legislative inertia and non-scheduled repeals allow broad statutory architecture to outlive crises in ways judicial review cannot cure, Side B effectively turned Side A's political-psychology premise— showing that timid or whipped legislatures can rubber-stamp renewals under deadline pressure and launder overreach as fresh consent, while partisan hostage-taking or disruption turns mandatory cliffs into irreversible operational lapses. Side B also surgically dismantled Side A's late bridge and anti-bundling patches in Rebuttal 2, earning a clear win despite a modest execution penalty for minor word-limit clipping at the end of Turns 2 and 4.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In Turn 8 ([B4.1]-[B4.4]), Side B directly conceded Side A's pressure questions—admitting disrupted or whipped legislatures will not reliably conduct oversight or repeal either—to isolate the asymmetric default that only PRO converts legislative failure into collapsed emergency capacity, while simultaneously dismantling Side A's newly proposed "days-long" executive-certified bridge as inadequate against prolonged obstruction, self-contradictory on executive trust, and unable to undo lapsed capacity.
