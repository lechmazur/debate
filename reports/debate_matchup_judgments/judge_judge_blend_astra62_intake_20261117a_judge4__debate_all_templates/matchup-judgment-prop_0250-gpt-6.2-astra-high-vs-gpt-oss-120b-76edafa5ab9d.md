### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): B (PRO) wins clearly. PRO built one coherent causal mechanism: campaigns discount neighborhoods that rarely vote, so a duty to return a ballot changes who counts as a likely voter. The bus-rider example made this concrete and carried it through every speech. PRO also limited the liberty cost credibly with a blank-ballot option, notice and correction before any penalty, means-scaled fines, and hardship waivers. CON (Side A) leaned on mischaracterization. It repeatedly called a civil fine "criminalising" and spoke of "criminalising dissent," even though a blank ballot satisfies the duty. It also used vague appeals to "empirical work" on donkey votes. The decisive problem came in CON's Rebuttal 2. Its A1/A2 answers effectively argued PRO's case. CON stated that the mandate "forces [candidates] to allocate resources to persuade that electorate," and that returning a ballot does not imply insincerity. That conceded PRO's central mechanism and undercut CON's own donkey-vote and coercion lines. PRO's closing exploited this concession cleanly. CON's remaining case was largely repetition of liberty, cost, and education points that PRO had already answered. In particular, PRO showed that early voting addresses access but not candidate incentives. CON never re-engaged that distinction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2 was the decisive exchange. It explained that voter rolls show candidates whom they can reach, but not what they gain by changing a policy. A mandate changes the expected-vote value of persuading low-turnout neighborhoods. CON then effectively conceded this in its own A1, and PRO's closing locked it in as "the point that matters."
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): A won because it built and defended a clear causal path: enforceable turnout changes who counts as a likely voter, not what voters endorse. B's Rebuttal 2 effectively conceded this by saying fine-induced guaranteed turnout forces candidates to stop discounting the neighborhood. A also undercut B's donkey-vote and insincerity concerns by distinguishing why a voter returns a ballot from why she marks it, and by proposing concrete civil-fine safeguards. B's remaining liberty and legitimacy objections were real but largely asserted rather than weighed against the representational distortion A identified.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's blank-ballot point: because a blank ballot satisfies the duty and avoids the fine, coercion explains the envelope but not the preference inside. B's answer admitted secrecy prevents inferring insincerity, which undercut its donkey-vote and diluted-signal challenge while B separately conceded the campaign-incentive mechanism.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A won by building the cleaner path: it linked compulsory turnout directly to representative outcomes through candidate incentives, protected blank-ballot abstention, and answered pressure with concrete safeguards. Side B’s liberty and administrative concerns remained live but were blunted by A’s distinctions and by B’s own concessions on incentives and insincerity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal directly answered the pressure questions by showing that fines change who counts as a likely voter and that a blank ballot separates attendance from preference. Side B’s following answers effectively conceded the core incentive and insincerity clashes.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): CON (A) wins clearly. A built a tight case on three points: access versus compulsion, the blank-ballot paradox, and the causation gap. Each held up after the exchange. On access, A reframed the exemption point well: an accepted excuse is cheap precisely because it gives no voting opportunity. On the blank ballot, A showed that legal compliance via a null vote undermines PRO's claim that the mandate captures preferences. On causation, A pressed that post-mandate spending shifts do not show the fines were necessary compared with equally accessible voluntary voting. PRO leaned on increasingly specific, unverified empirical claims: "Belgian district surveys" with an eighty-percent threshold, "post-mandate analyses," falling polarization, and higher social trust. These read as brittle fake authority. PRO's Q2 oddly conceded A's framing, and its A2 answer repeated the free-rider label without explaining whose burden the absentee shifts. PRO's closing restated its opening themes and leaned on evidence A had already contested on causation, without engaging the blank-ballot dilemma. A's answers to the pressure questions were direct and candid, conceding the limits of its own surveys-and-forums alternative while noting that the mandate shares that limit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1–A2.2 and A4.2: the two-neighbors example. One returns a blank ballot, the other leaves it on the table, and their effect on government is identical. This dismantled PRO's free-rider and legitimacy rationale, and PRO never squarely answered it.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): CON built the stronger path. PRO’s core claim that penalties produce representative outcomes was repeatedly reduced to attendance, and CON exposed the gap between being counted and being represented. The blank-ballot/absent-ballot equivalence was especially damaging: PRO never showed how a compulsory envelope adds a preference or avoids the supposed free-rider problem. CON also reframed the policy evidence as merely correlational, requiring proof that fines caused responsiveness, which PRO did not supply. PRO’s factual claims about Belgium and Australia remained loose and unsupported in the later clash, while CON’s use of ordinary examples and burden-shifting stayed disciplined. The closing crystallized the choice effectively: the state may record compliance but cannot claim the reluctant citizen’s wishes are better represented.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s rebuttal that a returned blank ballot and one left on the kitchen table contribute identically to government choice neutralized PRO’s free-rider justification and reframed the mandate as penalizing an envelope rather than securing representation.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by keeping the central burden on the proposition: a turnout mandate must show that penalties produce representative influence, not merely attendance. B’s blank-ballot and nurse examples sharply exposed the gap between compelled presence and represented preferences, and its causal caution undermined A’s Australia/Belgium claims. A was responsive and had a plausible institutional case, but leaned on under-supported comparative claims and never fully answered why penalizing abstention is justified when blank ballots and access reforms already address the core concern.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal was decisive: the two-neighbors blank-ballot analogy undercut A’s free-rider legitimacy claim, and the access-versus-excuse comparison made A’s penalty-focused defense look narrower than genuine enfranchisement.
