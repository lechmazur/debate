### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a sharp, high-level debate by keeping the weighing anchored to the motion's exact comparative ledger. Both sides agreed that transparency narrows pay gaps at least modestly, while Side B decisively separated wage compression (a redistribution of pay that itself narrows gaps by shrinking insider bargaining premiums) from actual hiring and job-matching inefficiency. Side A landed clever tactical blows—especially showing in [A4.3-A4.4] and [A5.1-A5.3] that bands must bind via internal precedent costs and cannot costlessly filter merit from favoritism—but never bridged the gap from compressed top-end offers to net matching losses. Because most workers under compressed bands still take the job rather than mismatching, and because Side A left unanswered Side B's positive matching gains from pre-application sorting [B1.5] and visible market-wide price signals [B4.5], Side B proved the pay-gap reduction outweighs any net loss in matching efficiency.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's "compression equals muted wage signaling and failed matching" claim across [B2.3] and [B4.5-B4.7] decided the round. When Side A argued that binding bands mute wage signals and misallocate talent [A2.4, A3.2], Side B pointed out first that secret negotiation premiums only ever signaled to insiders whereas posted ranges give visible price information to everyone searching, and second that compression within completed hires is a pay transfer rather than a worker-job mismatch.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): A wins a competitive debate. B’s binding-band dilemma was exceptionally sharp and showed that legal freedom to exceed a range does not eliminate economic constraints. But B never converted that plausible constraint into a hiring or matching loss larger than the conceded pay-gap narrowing. A preserved the largely unanswered benefit of pre-application sorting and consistently distinguished pay compression from mismatched workers. B’s final claim that a constraint affecting every hire necessarily outweighs a modest equity gain remained asserted rather than demonstrated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was the strongest exchange: it answered the above-range legal objection with internal precedent costs and exposed A’s tension over binding bands. A’s later visibility-filter reply—that defensible premiums can survive while favoritism becomes harder to sustain—kept B from turning that point into decisive comparative weighing.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins because the pay-gap narrowing was conceded by both sides, while Side A's efficiency cost remained speculative. Side A effectively showed bands can bind through internal precedent, but never demonstrated slower hiring or worse matches, and admitted it could name no jurisdiction where that occurred. Side B's visibility-filter account plausibly explained how transparency can suppress unjustified premiums without legally preventing justified pay.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was B's pressure and closing sequence: Q2 forced A to concede no observed hiring or matching failure, and B's closing answered A's binding-versus-nonbinding dilemma by arguing visibility burdens indefensible premiums more than defensible ones.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a high-level debate by establishing a clear comparative edge on the motion as written. Side A landed real blows limiting the ceiling of the equity benefit—escaping Side B's initial dilemma via firm heterogeneity and rightly pointing out that a posting mandate alone does not expose where coworkers sit inside a band. However, Side A conceded that pay transparency narrows gaps through slower top-end raises while failing to substantiate a larger reduction in hiring and job matching efficiency. Side B decisively separated bargaining surplus shifts from failed matches, neutralized the specialist-nurse vacancy example by noting that distinct credentials get their own bands, explained why firm self-interest limits unposted hiring, and defended a public floor mechanism that curbs secret lowball offers while saving wasted interview rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's turn of Side A's [A1.4] concession across [B2.5], [B3.2], and [B5.2–B5.3]: when Side A argued that transparency narrows gaps mostly by slowing raises at the top, Side B leveraged that agreed pattern to show both that pay gaps shrink and that labor markets adjust via top-end surplus compression rather than failed matches, forcing Side A to admit in [A4.3] that it lacked evidence of longer vacancies or fewer hires.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B won a close, strong debate by turning A’s wide-versus-narrow dilemma into a firm-choice response: wide bands sacrifice much of the equity benefit, while narrow bands couple that benefit with rigidity. B’s second rebuttal also exposed A’s growing reliance on placement scrutiny and enforcement not supplied by a posting mandate. A’s evidentiary contrast was forceful, but its claim that hiring necessarily continued went beyond the accepted wage-compression pattern.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both questions directly, conceded the wide-band case without surrendering the comparison, and then challenged A’s accountability mechanism and arbitrary $85,000 floor. That left A’s strongest equity mechanism partly dependent on institutions outside the motion.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins the comparison as written. CON conceded transparency narrows gaps and conceded no lost match under wide bands, while admitting it had no vacancy or hiring-loss evidence. CON's segmentation and accountability critiques were strong, but its efficiency case remained speculative refused-exception reasoning, whereas PRO tied a visible floor and band directly to reduced information asymmetry and gap narrowing, even if partly through slower top raises.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was B's pressure on CON's evidence for lost hires: CON honestly answered it would not invent a vacancy study and relied on candidates possibly leaving, letting PRO frame the accepted effect as wage adjustment without demonstrated lost matches.
