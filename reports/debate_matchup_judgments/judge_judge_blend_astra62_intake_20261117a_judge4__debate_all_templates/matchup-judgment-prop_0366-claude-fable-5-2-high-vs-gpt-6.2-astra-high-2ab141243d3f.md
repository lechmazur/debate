### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A controlled the decisive clash: who pays for cinema's budget gap. A repeatedly extracted concessions — B admitted the window "costs a homebound fan opening night," admitted money beats exclusion when available, and admitted every blockbuster has excluded fans — then crystallized the certainty asymmetry (certain, repeated exclusion vs. a hypothetical, unshown substitution chain). A's closing also pre-empted B's leverage counter by noting the reformed window itself rests on "unwritten cheques" (captioned showings, tested equipment) nobody has agreed to supply. B fought back well: the externality story about small-town screens was genuinely novel, the genre-test reversal in B4.4 ("plays nearly as well at home" predicts switching, not safety) was sharp, and the closing leverage argument (make the rental conditional so the studio has a reason to pay) was B's best repair. B also answered A's pressure question with an actual test and a real example (WW84). But B's exception remained largely decorative in ordinary times, and A's framing made the vote easier: charge the people who profit, not the people locked out. Clear but competitive win for A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.2–A4.5: A's answer to B's revenue-sharing hypothetical — "the obstacle is not simultaneous release, it is a studio declining to write a cheque" — converted B's own scenario into the proposition's core claim that the window socializes cinema's costs onto excluded viewers. B's best counter was B5.1's leverage reversal, arguing that selling the rental first removes the patron's only leverage over the studio.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B narrowly won by reframing the clash as a choice between two audiences’ access risks and defending a more workable conditional rule. A’s moral framing and certain-versus-conditional harm comparison were powerful, but its genre test was economically vulnerable, and its willingness to release without secured matinee funding weakened its revenue-sharing solution. B most effectively exposed the missing leverage in “release first, press the studio later.”
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second pressure question forced A to endorse release even when the accessible matinee would lose its funding. B then answered A’s closing leverage argument by showing that making the rental conditional creates pressure before release, whereas releasing unconditionally leaves the cinema to absorb the immediate loss.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): A won by defining the motion modestly, making opening-weekend exclusion a certain repeated harm, and forcing B to defend theatrical-first as a fallback when studios refuse financial support. B’s leverage and shift-covering points were strong, but its cinema harms remained conditional and its exception/test did not fully rebut the fairness burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s answer to the revenue-share hypothetical, carried into the closing, turned B’s studio-commitment concession into the central weighing point: money can protect matinees, so the window charges excluded viewers for a cost studios could pay.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and honestly, but Side B built the more decisive route to victory. B's core convergence argument went unanswered: A conceded that windows should be protected where screens are at risk, and B showed that the "handful of releases that carry a screen" are precisely the big titles the motion concerns, making A's position collapse into theatrical-first with exceptions. B's "cannot target beneficiaries" point (the day-one rental sells to everyone with a sofa, not just the excluded viewer) was met with an honest but thin answer in A4.3. B's bounded-versus-permanent error weighing, reinforced by the risk-allocation point that studios decide while exhibitors absorb closures, was the cleanest comparative frame in the debate, and B's closing crystallized it rather than adding new material. A's strongest clash — the asymmetry of dismissing the excluded viewer's loss as "a few weeks' wait" — was directly engaged by B in B2.6 and B4.3-4, noting that simultaneity only delivers shared discovery via the very substitution A denies. A answered pressure questions directly and used its word limits well; B suffered two clipped turns as execution costs, which keeps the margin from growing, but the substantive pressure and weighing consistently favored B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal: the observation that A's proposed test requires comparables that only exist after the experiment has been run on someone's cinema, combined with the point that the studio makes the call while workers and owners absorb the closure — "when the decider doesn't carry the downside, the rule should lean toward caution" — plus the fire-insurance analogy, turned A's uncertainty argument into B's strongest ground.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): A won a close, high-quality debate by preserving the motion’s title-by-title comparison: certain opening-night exclusion must be justified by a demonstrable exhibition benefit, with revenue guarantees available where forecasts are uncertain. B’s untargeted-rental and asymmetric-risk objections were powerful, but its closing overreached by treating A’s limited exceptions as equivalent to theatrical-first for almost every big title. B also conceded that shorter windows cannot restore the missed shared cultural moment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second-rebuttal answer that simultaneity does not itself create a shared viewing unless friends stay home—thereby causing substitution—was the sharpest single hit. A nevertheless answered the broader risk pressure with concrete screening tests and studio-backed revenue floors.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B won by turning the debate into a question of irreversible risk. It conceded the accessibility loss but framed it as bounded and partially remediable, while casting simultaneous release as an untargeted subsidy to households that might otherwise have bought tickets, with possible permanent harm to cinemas. Side A had the stronger moral image and a clean access argument, but its title-by-title test and revenue-floor fix never fully answered the uncertainty problem or the point that the films most likely to need protection are the big titles themselves. B’s clipping costs hurt it, but not enough to overturn its clearer route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s rebuttals turned Side A’s couch example against it: a day-one rental cannot be limited to viewers who genuinely cannot attend, and Side A’s proposed risk test depended on comparables that may not exist before the experiment is run. That exchange made the irreversible-error framing decisive.
