### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): B won by showing that the cap targets owner identity rather than the harmful conduct or concentration itself. A offered a plausible correlated-exit risk and strong rhetoric, but increasingly relied on lease protections, local subcaps, anti-consolidation review, and farmer preferences to make the policy work. B persuasively argued that those measures do the substantive protecting while an institutional cap can merely shift land to large local incumbents.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal directly answered A’s exit challenge with ex ante lease, transfer, and conservation rules that can bind successors. This undercut A’s repeated claim that conduct regulation necessarily acts only after harm, while B’s substitution argument showed that changing titleholders need not change rents, consolidation, or stewardship.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully arguing that capping institutional ownership creates a 'substitution problem' that empowers large local incumbents, and by proving that conduct rules can legally constrain the 'exit option' Side B relied on. Side B's reliance on companion rules to fix the cap's flaws ultimately conceded that the cap itself is an untargeted mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A1) decisively neutralized Side B's strongest claim by detailing how lease and transfer regimes (consent rules, covenants binding successors) can directly regulate an owner's exit option.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): CON won the targeting clash. PRO's cap requires anti-consolidation and local subcaps to avoid incumbent capture, conceding that ownership identity alone is not decisive. CON's substitution problem showed that removing institutional buyers likely transfers land to concentrated local holdings that replicate the same harms, while institutions can still exert control through leases. PRO's correlated-exit mechanism survived but was not enough to overcome the demonstration that the motion conflates label with behavior. Razor-thin because both argued with exceptional discipline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's substitution problem in B4-B5: removing institutional owners does not prevent concentrated control or conduct harms, it merely shifts them to local buyers, making the cap redundant and potentially distortionary.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): A won a clear but competitive debate by preserving the distinction between conduct regulation, per-controller limits, and an aggregate cap on financially driven institutional ownership. B compellingly exposed evasion and category problems, but overstated that counting beneficial ownership necessarily converts A's proposal into B's general control regime. A showed that many institutions could remain individually subdominant while collectively absorbing the land base, a structural concern B's alternatives never squarely resolved. A's mistaken C3.1 reference and reliance on companion policies were execution costs, but not decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing answer to B's claimed fatal concession was decisive: looking through shells and options is anti-evasion, while the cap still uniquely limits the aggregate share held by a defined investor class. B largely repeated that this became a general control cap without answering A's collective-accumulation distinction.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A won by effectively exposing a structural flaw in the motion's focus on "ownership" rather than "control." When Side B attempted to close loopholes by stating the cap should include options and management contracts, Side A decisively weaponized this concession to show Side B had abandoned the proposition for Side A's control-based alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 flawlessly capitalized on Side B's attempt to include options and management contracts under the definition of ownership, framing it as a fatal concession that control, not title, is the necessary metric.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): CON won by exposing that the ownership cap is the wrong instrument: it is either evaded through options and shells or, if enforced via beneficial-control provisions, collapses into the control-based regulation CON advocated. PRO’s aggregate-ceiling argument remained live but was undermined by concessions that blurred the “non-farming institutional” category, while CON’s dilemma—unnecessary if companion policies work, harmful if they don’t—landed cleanly and went largely unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 and Closing showing that PRO’s concessions on beneficial control and family holding companies convert the title cap into a general control cap, making the motion’s investor-class label arbitrary and porous.
