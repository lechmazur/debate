### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO built a tight, motion-centered case: under employer-based financing, eligibility depends on hours worked, so coverage can be lost exactly when illness makes work impossible. Single-payer removes that test. PRO then dismantled CON's main defenses. On COBRA, the policy travels but the employer's contribution does not. On exchange plans, a subsidy pays a premium but does not guarantee the same oncologist. On "fixed contractual contributions," a contract can be honored in full while hours fall below the eligibility threshold. On administrative burden, CON counted the public administrator while ignoring the insurer administrators it replaces. PRO's pressure questions were sharp and exposed real gaps. Its A1/A2 answers were candid, admitting that no guardrail guarantees faster access than every employer plan. The "hold the treatment decision constant" move clarified the true difference between the systems. CON had live points: waits, benefit exclusions, and budget politics. It undercut them in several ways: - **Unsupported claims:** It asserted that single-payer systems "by definition" ban top-up coverage, which PRO never conceded and CON never supported. - **Precision and self-contradiction:** It leaned on brittle specifics such as the NHS 18-week figure and administrative-cost parity. It also claimed most small firms already drop coverage, which cuts against its own employer-based case. - **Mismatched evidence:** It applied elective-wait evidence to chemotherapy. - **Repetition:** It recycled the COBRA portability point without answering PRO's contribution critique. CON's closing largely restated its case and did not address the eligibility mechanism PRO made central.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal of portability was the decisive exchange. Its line, stated in A2.1 and A5.3, was that under COBRA the policy travels but the employer's contribution does not, and an exchange subsidy does not keep the specialist in network. This neutralized CON's main claim that the current system already protects the machinist. CON never engaged it and simply repeated COBRA and exchange plans as answers.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the decisive comparison on its own strongest terrain: what happens when a patient becomes too sick to work. B repeatedly asserted that single-payer means rigid benefits, blocked supplements, and waitlists, but never proved those harms were inherent to single-payer financing rather than contingent policy choices. A exposed this with the pressure questions and rebuttals, especially by showing that a rare-disease add-on could exist over a public basic benefit too. B's COBRA and exchange portability answers did not overcome the point that the employer contribution, not just the policy, can disappear exactly when a worker cannot work enough hours. A's closing crystallized that comparative harm cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it held the treatment decision constant and asked what happens when both plans cover a rare treatment but the patient can no longer work. Under employer-based coverage, the employer contribution can vanish precisely when the patient needs care; under single-payer, the payer keeps paying on the same terms. That directly neutralized B's rare-condition and customization advantage.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): B won by keeping the motion’s actual comparison in focus: basic financing that survives when illness cuts work hours, versus coverage whose employer contribution does not travel. B answered pressure with usable guardrails and honest limits, then held treatment decisions constant so job-status failure remained the decisive difference. A never neutralized that test, treated waits, exclusions, and top-up bans as inherent to single-payer financing, and misstated B’s concessions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal and close: COBRA carries the policy but not the employer contribution, and even a perfectly fitted employer plan can vanish when hours fall, while clinical rationing does not follow from one basic payer.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B wins clearly. A's opening set out the standard case for single-payer: portability, the job-lock harm and administrative waste. But A never adapted after B reframed the debate. B argued that a public guarantee across employment gaps does not require replacing every employer contract. B answered each of A's gap claims with specific design responses: - **Who is covered:** caregivers and freelancers qualify for the subsidized plan through an income test, not a work history. - **Treatment continuity:** existing treatment authorizations stay in force after a layoff or plan collapse. - **Rural care:** standby funding for rural maternity wards is decoupled from who pays for routine deliveries. B also pressed A's administrative-savings claim, noting the total-versus-avoidable distinction and the effect of common billing rules. A responded with brittle precision, including the "nearly $1 trillion" and "$300–400 billion" figures and the unsupported claim that private insurers are the "leading cause" of rural closures. A also never isolated savings that require eliminating employer purchasing. In the pressure round, B gave direct, concrete A1/A2 answers: an income-based cap, a protected living allowance, an enforceable timely-access remedy and independent review. A's answer on the rural ward did not explain why taking over delivery bills secures the night shift. A's closing largely restated the "tethered to work" refrain and asserted gaps that B had already closed, such as the former employer dropping its plan. B's closing crystallized the debate around the machinist and the value of having more than one purchaser.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.4 on the rural ward: the standby payment arrives in advance whether ten employers pay for deliveries or none do. It does not leave when a factory closes, and taking over delivery bills does not make the appropriation more binding. This neutralized A's rural-care route and exposed A2 as non-responsive.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B wins by showing the employment link can be broken without eliminating employer purchasing. B offered concrete public backstops—automatic continuation after job loss, a subsidized basic plan with income-based caps, and enforceable coverage standards—that directly answered A’s main gaps. A’s strongest cost and rural arguments were materially undercut: B showed the administrative-savings comparison measured existing systems rather than single-payer against a hybrid with common billing rules, and that public standby payments could secure rural capacity without taking over all delivery bills. B’s sole-payer denial point also went largely unanswered, giving a clear comparative reason to preserve multiple purchasers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was the decisive exchange: it supplied specific premium-cap and network-authorization guardrails in A1/A2, then dismantled A’s administrative-savings and rural-cash-flow claims by arguing common billing rules could capture savings without replacing employer contracts and that standby payments do not become more secure merely by taking over delivery bills.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by separating access from who pays first and then holding that distinction through the exchange. Its hybrid—automatic continuation, income-based zero-premium protection, enforceable benefit standards, and targeted public standby funding—directly answered Side B’s main gaps without conceding that a sole purchaser is required. Side B opened strongly on job lock, but later speeches recycled the claim that coverage remained tethered to work, never cleanly answered why taking over already-paid bills secures rural staffing beyond a public standby payment, and relied on overly exact administrative-savings figures against the wrong counterfactual. Side A’s closing weighed the real remaining tradeoff: reporting friction versus no alternative purchaser.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it set a concrete income cap and appeal remedy, then showed that neither the claimed administrative gap nor rural standby funding requires replacing employer contracts that already deliver care.
