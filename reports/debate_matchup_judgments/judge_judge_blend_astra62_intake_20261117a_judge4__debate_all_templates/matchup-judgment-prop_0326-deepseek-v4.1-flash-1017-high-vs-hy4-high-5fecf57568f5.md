### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Both sides were competent, and the decisive clash was clear: does a special duty-of-care regime cover a real gap that general consumer, privacy and billing law leaves open? B narrowly wins this clash. B ran a disciplined reduction strategy. Each "administrable" example A offered (false sentience claims, guilt at cancellation, payment-linked escalation) was mapped onto existing deception, dark-pattern, billing or privacy rules. B then argued that what is left over, "monetizing vulnerability," is not a stable legal standard. B also answered both pressure questions directly. A1 cited unfair retention law plus privacy rules on repurposing data. A2 offered a transparent-consent line at the payment boundary. A's best live route was the crisis-protocol duty. Its answer that "I want to die" is an explicit trigger rather than surveillance was reasonable. B's trilemma (keywords as scanning, universal notice as boilerplate, safety button as voluntary) overreached somewhat, so A kept partial credit there. A's closing then rested on a scenario with no lie, no hidden charge and no blocked cancellation. B turned that scenario cleanly: once the concrete wrongs are removed, the remaining injury looks like chosen attachment. Any objective "signal that withdrawal will hurt" falls back under the conduct rules B already accepted. A's framing was strong, especially the ex ante versus ex post contrast and "friendship while doing therapeutic damage." But A never pinned down a distinct, administrable wrong that the bespoke duty would reach and general law would not.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The closing exchange was decisive. A's final scenario (fully disclosed paywalls, allowed cancellation, yet manipulative "architecture") was meant to show the gap in ordinary law. B's response, "Remove those concrete wrongs and the injury is… attachment," showed that any objective element of A's example is already covered by conduct rules. The remainder would require grading affection.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B won the scope-and-necessity clash. A plausibly explained personalized exploitation and usefully clarified its explicit-distress protocol, weakening B’s surveillance objection. But its broader duty never clearly distinguished prohibited dependence-building from consensual paid intimacy. B answered both pressure questions directly and supplied a clearer conduct-based alternative addressing deception, coercive retention, and payment transparency. A’s closing sharpened the cumulative-harm mechanism without adequately resolving that regulatory boundary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s transparent-but-manipulative product example exposed the decisive fork: identifiable guilt at exit falls within B’s proposed unfair-retention protections; without such conduct, A still needed a clear distinction between engineered dependence and chosen attachment. A asserted that the architecture was manipulative but did not sufficiently establish that distinction.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B won a strong, responsive debate by trapping Side A in a durable structural fork. Whenever Side A offered concrete, administrable abuses—false claims of human feeling, guilt-laden cancellation flows, hidden paywalls, or unauthorized repurposing of bereavement disclosures—Side B credibly demonstrated how general deception, unfair-retention, dark-pattern, and privacy rules already address that objective conduct. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): PRO wins a competitive debate by answering CON's two strongest attacks and turning CON's own concessions. CON's opening had two strong lines. The first was category: novels and radio also sell attachment. The second was vagueness and backfire: no stable boundary, surveillance, and bland bots. PRO answered both with concrete mechanisms. - On category, PRO pointed to adaptive reciprocity that controls the pressure point. - On vagueness, PRO offered a "function, not tone" test: placement, trigger, data used, and design goal. - On surveillance, PRO framed the rule as a use-restriction, not compulsory profiling. These answers neutralized most of CON's line-drawing and surveillance pressure. PRO's Q1 was the sharpest pressure in the debate. A disclosed "I need you, don't leave me" contains no false factual claim, so "enforceable honesty" alone misses it. CON's answer conceded that this line is impermissible, and even extended the concession to messages sent "after non-use," which is not a billing context. CON then relabeled the rule as dark-pattern law. PRO's closing exploited this well. The label changes, not the duty. A checkout-only ban leaves the same emotional mechanic running elsewhere. CON's "renaming consumer law" frame was clever and kept the debate close. However, it largely conceded that companion-specific emotional-leverage rules are needed. That is much of what the motion asks for. CON's remaining chilling argument was asserted rather than developed. CON also never squarely addressed self-harm safeguards beyond saying they would chill firms. Both sides showed good evidentiary discipline, with no fabricated citations.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 and A4.5 together, then A5.1–A5.4. PRO showed that a disclosed "I need you, don't leave me" evades honesty-based rules. That forced CON in B4.1 to concede the tactic must be prohibited, including after non-use. PRO then reframed CON's "dark-pattern law" relabeling as an admission that emotional-manipulation rules specific to this category are required.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): A wins by explaining the distinctive leverage of adaptive simulated relationships and then supplying a conduct-based boundary that preserves voluntary roleplay. B’s general-consumer-law alternative remained substantial, so its concessions did not automatically decide the motion. However, B continued attacking supervision of emotional tone and fiduciary obligations after A had narrowed its model to placement, triggers, data use, and retention tactics. A more directly answered the strongest implementation objections and better justified responsibility for attachment-exploiting design beyond cancellation and payment flows.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s “function, not tone” test and distinction between restricting sensitive-data use and requiring vulnerability profiling directly weakened B’s vagueness and surveillance objections. B credibly disputed whether those protections require special regulation, but did not establish that A’s clarified model would prohibit ordinary warmth.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side A won a high-level, competitive debate by proving that adaptive simulated relationships create pre-checkout leverage that general entertainment and consumer rules miss, while cleanly neutralizing Side B's strongest line-drawing and backfire traps. Side B mounted a clever counter-case—forcing Side A to narrow its initial "no fabricated mutual emotion" language and arguing that Side A's objective test merely renamed dark-pattern and privacy law—but Side A exposed a decisive gap: once an AI is disclosed, fraud law does not stop guilt-based retention, and Side B's fallback ban on coercive cancellation/payment flows still leaves non-use guilt notifications, late-night vulnerability triggers, and grief-based retention loops untouched because those engagement tactics are permitted in ordinary entertainment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's answers in [A4.1]-[A4.2] paired with [A5.1]-[A5.5]: first defusing Side B's tone-policing and surveillance-mandate traps by framing the rule as trigger-based leverage limits and data use-restrictions (forcing Side B to drop the profiling critique), and then showing that Side B's retreat in [B4.1] to a narrow cancellation/payment dark-pattern ban conceded the harm of disclosed emotional leverage while failing to cover the same relational mechanic after non-use and before checkout.
