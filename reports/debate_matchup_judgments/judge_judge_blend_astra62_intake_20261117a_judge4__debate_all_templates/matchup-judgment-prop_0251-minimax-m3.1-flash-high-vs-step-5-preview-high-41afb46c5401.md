### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): B wins on the motion as written. Its risk-adjusted, income-protected stop-work backstop substantially answers A's threshold and wage objections without displacing preventive controls. A correctly explains that enforceable standards need not rely on worker initiative, exposing B's advice-versus-law framing as overstated. But A ultimately endorses legally required cessation when controls fail, then demands that B defend cessation as the defining mechanism for every extreme-heat situation—an extra burden the proposition does not impose. Remaining implementation concerns limit, rather than reverse, B's advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's distinction between a stop-work backstop and a replacement for preventive controls was decisive. Its pressure question then elicited A's concession that safety rules can require cessation when controls fail, undermining A's continued characterization of mandatory protections as blanket shutdowns.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by defining mandatory stop-work as an income-protected, calibrated backstop to graduated controls, not a bare thermometer ban. CON conceded cessation is sometimes required and never explained who reliably triggers it without worker refusal or employer discretion, while its narrower alternative faced the same pay and coercion problems it pressed against PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it specified a WBGT heat-burden trigger using workload, clothing, and acclimatization, defended false positives by asymmetry with heatstroke, and required paid protection, collapsing CON's trigger and income objections.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the central clash clearer: once extreme heat reaches a danger threshold, who decides that work stops? B used A’s concession that cessation is sometimes necessary to argue that an automatic legal duty is the only worker-protective mechanism, especially for piece-rate or low-power workers who cannot safely refuse. B also answered the income objection more directly by defending paid stop-work as part of the protection, while A’s alternative relied on refusal rights and graduated controls without fully resolving how unpaid or delayed protection avoids coercion. A gave a strong nuanced case about threshold complexity, false positives, and graduated safeguards, but its clipped rebuttal and less clean answer on income protection weakened its live route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure questions and second rebuttal forced A to concede that work must sometimes cease, then turned A’s own paid-refusal problem against it: if refusal must be income-protected to avoid coercion, A’s alternative faces much of the same funding objection it raised against mandatory stop-work.
