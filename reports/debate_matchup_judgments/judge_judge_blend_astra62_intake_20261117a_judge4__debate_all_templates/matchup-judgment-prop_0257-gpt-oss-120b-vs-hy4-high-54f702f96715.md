### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): CON wins clearly. It built a sharp dilemma and pressed it consistently through every round: digital "ownership" is either enforced by continuing platform control, which makes it a license with remedies, or it allows perfect duplication that cannibalizes the launch market. PRO never escaped this dilemma. Its crypto-escrow answer relied on voluntary key erasure and an optional wipe tool, and CON showed that this either collapses into platform control or fails to prevent duplication. PRO's preservation answer misdescribed fair use and archival law by claiming a buyer's lawful possession authorizes library copying and lending, and CON's paperback-scan comparison exposed the flaw. PRO also has execution costs. Its opening and first rebuttal were both clipped. It relied on a vaguely specified "2020 cloud-gaming shutdown" described as purchases vanishing outright, which is brittle given that refunds were common in comparable cases. It invoked loose constitutional property claims. Its own Q2 conceded CON's framing. In the closing, PRO repeated the shutdown point without answering CON's "inert file" response about patches, servers, and clearances. CON also gave a concrete refund-bond mechanism in A1 and closed with a memorable crystallization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1–B2.2 rebuttal ("bits have no master") turned PRO's specific-copy defense into a forced choice. Either ownership requires ongoing verification, which is a license, or it permits duplication. CON's B4.3 then dismantled the escrow answer: "A missing paperback proves itself; a deleted bitstring does not."
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B built and sustained a decisive dilemma: a transferable digital "specific copy" is either enforceable only through continuing platform control (verification, key rotation, deletion checks), which contradicts meaningful ownership, or it coexists with retained backups, making resale duplication. Side A's cryptographic escrow answer was its best attempt, but B dismantled it cleanly ("a missing paperback proves itself; a deleted bitstring does not"), and B equally broke A's preservation claim by noting that owning a paperback never authorized libraries to scan and lend copies. A's closing failed to re-engage the dilemma, merely reasserting certainty and dismissing price effects as speculative, while B's closing crystallized the clash sharply and offered a concrete alternative remedy path (refunds, export, migration duties). Both sides followed the A1/A2 and Q1/Q2 formats properly, but A also suffered clipping costs on its opening and first rebuttal, and its later speeches narrowed rather than resolved the core dispute. B made the debate easier to decide and left its central route unneutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3-B4.4: Side B's dissection of A's escrow mechanism and preservation claim. B showed that unverifiable key erasure means the sold file and retained copy coexist, and that "lawful possession" cannot smuggle institutional reproduction rights—an exchange A never recovered from.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): A won by making the central clash about enforceability: PRO’s transferable digital copy either requires ongoing platform verification, which is the licensed control PRO rejects, or permits retained backups and thus duplication. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): A wins clearly. A built a precise model: a durable baseline copy with backup and verified transfer, while patches, streaming and cloud features stay separately paid services. That model answered B's update, fragmentation and pricing claims directly. A2.5 turned B's own "near-zero marginal cost" premise against its pricing argument. A's Q1 forced the decisive concession at B4.1–B4.2: revocation is not needed for patches, low prices, or piracy control, and is only an "ancillary tool." A's Q2 drew the admission that a buyer keeps nothing after delisting or shutdown. B's closing then reverted to claiming revocation is necessary for patches and security, contradicting its own A1. B also lost execution points: three of its turns were clipped for exceeding the word limit. B leaned on an asserted "law already treats these as licenses" and an "impossibility" claim that A neutralized by noting the seller controls the keys. A answered B's questions directly with A1/A2 and narrowed the clash cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's Q1, asking which benefit actually requires revoking a paid copy. B answered at B4.1–B4.2 that none does and that revocation is merely "ancillary." A's closing at A5.1 seized on that word and showed that revocation is seller leverage rather than technical necessity. This collapsed B's core case, and B's closing then contradicted its own concession.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A (PRO) won decisively. A's case was coherent from opening to closing: ownership as durable rights with verified transfer, the unbundling answer (baseline copy plus paid services), and the inversion of the preservation argument. A answered B's pressure questions directly with A1/A2 labels and used clean weighing ("CON protects platform manageability; ownership protects the paid-for thing"). The decisive break came in B's second rebuttal, where B conceded that none of its claimed benefits—patches, codecs, low prices, piracy control—require revocation, calling it an "ancillary tool," and then admitted the buyer retains no surviving claim after delisting or shutdown. A's closing seized both concessions effectively. B compounded the damage with three clipped speeches (opening, both rebuttals) and a closing that contradicted its own concession by reasserting that only a revocable license can guarantee functionality—exactly what B had just admitted was false. B's "logical impossibility" and categorical claims about law and encryption were also overconfident and were dismantled by A's point that architecture is not a legal limit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 / closing sequence: A's pressure question asking which benefit requires revocation forced B to concede revocation is unnecessary ("ancillary tool" [B4.2]) and that buyers retain no claim after delisting [B4.3-B4.4]. A's closing crystallized this into the memorable "a purchase with a discretionary off-button is a rental mislabeled as a sale," leaving B with only a contradiction to answer.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the decisive clash clear: whether a paid digital purchase should leave buyers with a durable asset or only seller-controlled access.
