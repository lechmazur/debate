### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins because it more cleanly dismantled PRO’s core safety claim: the most harmful scammers can bypass ID checks with stolen credentials, and verified badges may then make them more trusted. A also gave a sharper comparative weighing by showing that permanent ID/biometric storage poses irreversible, targeted harms to marginalized users, and it offered concrete less intrusive alternatives. B kept asserting a “modest delay vs. widespread harm” tradeoff but never fully neutralized the false-security and breach objections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s turn-four rebuttal that PRO’s admitted exception for sophisticated scammers is exactly the life-ruining harm, and that verification would possibly supercharge those actors with trusted badges while stockpiling permanent identity data, was the decisive exchange B failed to overcome.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by making the decisive weighing stick: the severe harms Side B led with are exactly those professional actors can bypass with stolen credentials, while a verified badge and permanent ID storage create false trust and irreversible risk for the users B claims to protect. B never resolved that contradiction, weakly equated messages with legal identity, and dismissed narrower tools without showing why mandatory checks are the only scale-matching fix. A's answers and close left a clearer reason to reject the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttals distinguishing life-ruining scams from low-effort catfishing, then answering B's pressure with asymmetric risk and less intrusive alternatives, left B's "vast benefit, marginal cost" tradeoff unneutralized.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A built the stronger comparative case: sophisticated scammers could bypass verification and exploit the credibility of verified badges, while permanent ID or biometric storage creates asymmetric, potentially irreversible risks for vulnerable users. A answered both pressure questions directly and supplied less intrusive alternatives. B engaged consistently but relied on unsupported assertions that low-effort fraud causes most harm and that added breach risk is marginal, never squarely neutralizing A’s central weighing argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it distinguished anonymity’s protective value for targets from its value to predators, answered the demand for alternatives with targeted moderation and detection tools, and exposed B’s unsupported dismissal of professional scammers as outliers.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): PRO won by reframing the debate around the current zero-effort access lane that enables mass scams and harassment, while showing that privacy, breach, and exclusion costs are design problems rather than necessary features of verification. CON had relevant costs, but its key empirical claims—that AI already neutralizes low-effort scams and that sophisticated fraud dominates harm—were less grounded, and PRO successfully undercut them by noting reactive tools act only after harmful accounts are already live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's rebuttal that zero-knowledge/tiered verification defuses the Ashley Madison-type breach and mass-exclusion objections, and that scamming at scale depends on cheap zero-friction accounts that verification can remove before AI detection ever sees a message.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the motion's tradeoff in focus: a one-time verification hurdle that raises the cost of mass, low-effort scam and harassment accounts, while treating privacy and exclusion as design constraints rather than fatal objections. B distinguished pseudonymity from zero-accountability anonymity and answered both pressure questions with a concrete scale-economics comparison. Side A raised real residual risks—breach surfaces, exclusion, deepfake bypasses, and a verification halo—but largely repeated the photo-ID honeypot case after B had narrowed it, and never squarely neutralized why leaving a zero-effort on-ramp open is better than imperfect upstream friction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal was decisive: it answered A's evidence demand by explaining that verification collapses cheap account farming before AI detection ever sees a message, then showed A's net-harm math depended on permanent ID storage and public legal identity that the proposal did not require.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): B built the stronger comparative case by explaining how verification raises the operating cost of mass fraud before victims are contacted, while distinguishing platform verification from public identification and permanent raw-ID storage. A raised genuine privacy, exclusion, and false-confidence concerns, but too often repeated them as settled facts, overstated the proven effectiveness of existing tools, and did not show why those tools could not complement verification. B sometimes overclaimed that design choices would eliminate risks, yet answered the central pressure more directly and weighed one-time friction against recurring harm more convincingly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: its A1/A2 answers combined the scalability economics of fake-account operations with tiered, pseudonymous verification, undercutting A’s assumed honeypot, public-identity, and universal photo-ID exclusions. A’s reply largely reasserted bypass and AI-detection claims without establishing negligible marginal benefit.
