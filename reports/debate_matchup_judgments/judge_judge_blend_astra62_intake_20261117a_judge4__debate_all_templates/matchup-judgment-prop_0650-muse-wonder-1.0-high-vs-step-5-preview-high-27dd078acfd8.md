### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won clearly in a high-quality debate by dismantling every mechanism Side B (PRO) used to bridge consumer glucose tracking to public-health gains. While Side B smartly conceded the lack of long-term trial data in low-risk users and pivoted to mechanistic habits and catching hidden prediabetes, Side A answered both routes decisively: basic dietary and movement habits do not require continuous sensors, and higher-risk subgroups with rising A1c or obesity can be identified through cheap, validated screening for targeted care rather than mass monitoring. Meanwhile, Side B left key structural critiques unanswered—specifically that chasing flatter curves can worsen nutrition (avoiding fruit/oats or adding fat), that meal-scoring apps keep nudging users past symptom resolution, and that multiplying reassurance visits and follow-up labs across millions of healthy users creates a systemic clinical burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The multi-turn clash over "relative flattening" and stopping rules ([B2.3] -> [A2.2-A2.3] -> [A3.1] -> [B4.1] -> [A4.2, A4.4]): Side A first broke Side B's step-count analogy by showing that a flatter glucose curve in healthy people is not monotonically healthier (since avoiding oats/bananas or loading fat flattens spikes), and then dismantled Side B's "symptom graduation" stopping rule by noting that confounded afternoon fatigue triggers renewed restriction while meal-scoring apps never stop flagging excursions to optimize.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B more convincingly showed that speculative, non-unique benefits of widespread CGM use among non-diabetics do not outweigh built-in medicalization of normal variation, misattribution, and vigilance without a validated target or stopping rule. Side A conceded the lack of durable outcome evidence and never established that the device adds population prevention beyond ordinary habits or cheaper targeted screening, while Side B kept the motion from collapsing into selective high-risk use and weighed concentrated possible benefit against diffuse certain costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal, which rejected symptom relief as proof of public-health benefit or device necessity and showed that possible value for a higher-risk minority does not justify widespread adoption.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won by making the decisive clash about added value and scale. A argued plausible preventive feedback, but B consistently showed that the claimed benefits mostly reduce to ordinary diet and exercise advice, while the distinct CGM layer lacks a validated non-diabetic target, encourages ongoing vigilance, and is least needed by the low-risk majority. B also handled A’s best turn about hidden higher-risk non-diabetics by preserving a targeted-use alternative without conceding widespread adoption. That left B with the cleaner vote: speculative, non-device-specific benefits for many versus more certain over-medicalization and anxiety costs at scale.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 cleanly answered A’s stopping-rule and symptom-relief move by conceding that feeling steadier may matter, but denying that this proves the glucose reading caused the crash or that continuous monitoring was necessary. It also reframed A’s high-risk subgroup point as support for targeted use, not widespread adoption.
