### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON wins because PRO's levy shrinks under pressure into a narrow, incumbent-targeting mechanism that adds no distinct worker benefit. PRO's strongest fairness principle was that the firm generating transition costs should pay, but CON exposed that entrants, imports, and attrition escape the trigger, leaving only legacy employers with an employment history liable. PRO's fallback principle—direct responsibility for an existing employment relationship—is a layoff rationale, not a robotics-specific one. CON's alternative of broad profit taxation reaches every automator without causation disputes, while PRO conceded general revenue can provide the same worker floor. The clipped Side A speeches are an execution cost, but the visible arguments were sufficient to carry the decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's most decisive rebuttal was that PRO had admitted no exclusive benefit is required and general revenue can provide a universal floor, so the levy adds little for workers beyond cause-auditing and a penalty on firms that once hired and now modernize openly.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): CON reframed the debate as a choice of collection mechanism once PRO conceded a universal floor and no exclusive worker benefit. B showed the levy exempts entrants, imports, attrition and contractors, penalizes only catchable incumbents, and requires costly causation audits, while profit taxes reach all automators and layoff-based charges create dismissal incentives without proving robotics. PRO plausibly answered the modest-versus-behavioral dilemma and defended direct employer responsibility, but never justified why the robotics trigger is better than those broader instruments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: PRO's 'direct responsibility for an existing employment relationship' is a principle about layoffs, not robots, so severance and layoff-rated insurance achieve the incentive without robot-causation disputes, while taxed profits already capture automation savings from every automator.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A wins narrowly. PRO kept a principled fairness case: firms that directly replace workers with robotics should help fund transition. But CON turned the decisive clash toward implementation and necessity. It showed the robotics trigger is vulnerable to avoidance through attrition, timing, outsourcing, greenfield entrants, and imports; that the principle behind the levy sounds more like general layoff responsibility than robotics-specific responsibility; and that broad profit or layoff-cost mechanisms could support displaced workers without robot-by-robot causation disputes. PRO answered pressure directly and honestly, especially on direct employer responsibility and no exclusive worker benefit, but those concessions helped CON argue that the robot trigger adds administrative friction without giving workers extra help. Side A’s repeated clipping was a real execution cost and kept the margin small, but its closing gave the cleaner reason to reject the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange came after Side B’s pressure answers: B conceded that no exclusive worker benefit was required and grounded the levy in direct responsibility for an existing employment relationship. Side A then reframed that principle as a general layoff obligation, not a robotics-specific justification, arguing that the word “robotics” mainly creates audits, avoidance, and asymmetry against incumbent employers.
