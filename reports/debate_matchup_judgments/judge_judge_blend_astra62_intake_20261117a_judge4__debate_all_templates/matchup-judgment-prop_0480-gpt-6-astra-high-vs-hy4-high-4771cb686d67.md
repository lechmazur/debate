### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): This was a close, high-quality exchange between two competent debaters. Side B (PRO) won a narrow decision because its core mechanism survived the exchange: in structurally scarce sectors like childcare, transit, and healthcare, cash adds demand without adding capacity, and Side A effectively conceded this in its second rebuttal ("I do not assume that everywhere"). Side A's best route—the opportunity cost of phased provision and the charge that PRO counted service promises as delivered benefits while treating purchased necessities as mere receipts—was answered head-on in Side B's rebuttal 2 and closing: waiting exists under both budgets, cash merely hides it in prices and queues, and phased capacity is delivered, auditable capacity rather than a promise. Side B's crystallization that "sustained cash pays the bill again; sustained services remove part of it" also survived Side A's "both are recurring" rejoinder, since a funded place eliminates the underlying bill rather than partially supplementing it. Side A's night-shift parent mismatch example remained live but only as one concrete case against a systemic case. Both sides followed format (proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 structure), avoided fake sourcing, and weighed rather than widened. Side B's slightly cleaner resolution of the decisive clash and more memorable framing earn the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was Side A's pressure question "who waits when the fixed budget cannot deliver?" followed by Side A's rebuttal 2 argument that phased provision means some households surrender real purchasing power for "a claim on the next phase." Side B's rebuttal 2 answer—that waiting exists under either budget, cash simply hides it in full nurseries, rent screens, and appointment lists, while public provision makes the wait visible and enforceable—was the turning point that blunted Side A's strongest route and set up the winning closing line about shortening the queue rather than winning a turn in it.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A wins a clear but competitive debate by proving the structural economics of the proposition. Across all four named domains, Side A showed that transit, broadband, childcare, and healthcare rely on shared physical capacity and pooled risk that individual cash checks cannot build—a point Side B largely had to concede in [B1.8] and [B4.1]. Side B mounted a sharp challenge on marginal opportunity cost, rightly catching Side A's overreaches in [A4.1]-[A4.2] (counting baseline access twice, clustering routes while claiming need-based rationing, and treating sustained cash as one-off) and keeping heterogeneous hardships like eviction risk live. However, Side B never overcame two decisive macro mechanisms in Side A's case: flat unconditional grants dilute a fixed budget across unequal contingencies rather than pooling lumpy risks, and injecting population-wide cash into supply-constrained markets reshuffles queues and bids up prices rather than expanding capacity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's pressure in [A2.2] and [A3.1] asking how recurring cash ever creates bus routes, rural lines, or evening-care slots forced Side B to concede in [B4.1] that transfers alone cannot materialize those systems, allowing Side A in [A5.2]-[A5.5] to show that while both budgets involve waiting, cash hides scarcity in prices and queues whereas public provision actually shortens the queue.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B wins the usable-benefit comparison. A powerfully shows cash alone cannot create scarce slots, routes, or clinicians, but B repeatedly exposes A's asymmetry: promised, phased capacity is counted as delivered while sustained cash is dismissed as a one-off receipt.
