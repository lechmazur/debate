### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): This was a close, competent debate. PRO built a clear framework: whoever can safely walk away is safest. It cleanly won the comparison against full prohibition. It also landed a sharp turn that regulation multiplies the discretion CON criticized in seller immunity. However, PRO's case rested heavily on idealized carve-outs for seller infrastructure. CON's most developed line was the "contamination" or institutional-chilling argument. If purchase is criminal, landlords, banks, platforms, and guards behave cautiously whatever the statute's carve-outs say. That point was pressed in the questions, answered directly in A1, and remained largely standing. PRO's reply was that carve-outs can be written and that CON assumes a lawful buyer. That answers the legal question but not the behavioral one. CON also kept alive the point that buyer fear produces rushed, isolated encounters. PRO answered that harm mostly after the fact, through reporting, rather than by preventing it. PRO's critique of manager power in legal markets was strong, but CON's "change employer, invoke contract, use the courts" levers partially blunted it. PRO's closing line, "the core illegality is the point," was rhetorically bold but let CON frame it as a moral label rather than a safety design. On balance, CON left a slightly more intact route to victory against the claim that the compromise is safer than a regulated market.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A1 in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive exchange. It answered PRO's Q1 directly: carve-outs do not erase police habits, bank refusals, platform bans, or landlord fear, and "the state does not need to convict the landlord to make the landlord cautious." This shifted the dispute from statutory text to institutional behavior. PRO never fully reclaimed that ground. Its discretion-multiplication turn [A4.4] was the strongest counter, but it addressed CON's regulatory alternative rather than the chilling effect itself.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): B wins the legal-market comparison. A establishes a real improvement over full prohibition and effectively answers the claim that safety services must be criminal by specifying exemptions. But B’s mechanisms—rushed screening, concealed clients, and reduced income weakening refusal—remain insufficiently answered. A’s reporting protections do not establish an advantage over lawful work, while its attacks on managers and regulatory discretion do not demonstrate that criminalizing customers is safer. B overstates the inevitability of legal contamination, limiting the margin, but gives the clearer comparative safety case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s argument that buyer fear is something “the seller pays for” directly challenges A’s central risk-transfer claim. A answers with reporting immunity and exit support, but does not adequately explain how remaining workers escape the screening and bargaining costs of a criminalized customer base.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): B established a credible safety improvement over full prohibition and offered a sharp gatekeeper critique of regulation. A won the decisive comparison with a fully legal market: seller immunity does not prevent criminalized purchase from shaping client behavior and deterring landlords, platforms, banks, and security providers. B’s proposed carve-outs answered formal liability but not these practical effects, nor why independent legal sellers could not retain reporting protections while gaining open screening and lawful infrastructure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions and turned B’s “one bright rule” into a permissions regime surrounded by institutional caution. B’s closing assertion that core illegality was the point then let A frame the model as a moral designation rather than a demonstrated safety advantage.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): CON (Side B) won because it built a concrete causal chain early: buyer fear becomes seller risk, which removes screening, deposits, shared premises and bad-client lists. CON then sharpened this into a structural dilemma that PRO never escaped. The ban either mines sellers' safety records for evidence, which drives those safeguards underground, or it walls them off, which reduces deterrence to after-the-fact harm reports. PRO's best route was the two-tier licensing problem: undocumented and poor sellers excluded from a legal market. CON blunted this with a "no license gates protection" standard and argued it applies equally to a regulated legal market. PRO also leaned on claims that were mostly asserted rather than defended against CON's specific counters: - that buyer fear gives sellers leverage; - that thinner demand strengthens sellers; - that the asymmetric model reduces exploitation. CON had argued that scarcity makes refusal more expensive and that rule-accepting buyers are the ones most deterred, and PRO did not answer either point directly. PRO's closing reframe of seller safeguards as "buyer comfort" was clever but was cleanly inverted by CON. CON also kept the debate focused on the harder comparison the motion requires, against a fully legal market, while conceding the easy comparison against prohibition. PRO's answers were competent and honest but more reactive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.3/B4.3 enforcement dilemma decided the debate. A purchase ban needs purchase evidence, so the state must either use sellers' calendars, texts and premises against buyers, which makes sellers hide them, or firewall them off, which makes enforcement symbolic. This converted PRO's "protected work" and "firewall" answers into concessions that the ban either endangers safeguards or barely deters.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B wins the decisive comparison with a legal market. A establishes benefits over full prohibition, but does not show why those benefits require criminalizing buyers. B explains how buyer criminalization threatens screening, records, and sellers' bargaining power, while directly answering licensing pressure with protections independent of registration. A's firewall response leaves effective purchase deterrence underexplained, and its repeated “buyer comfort” framing misses the seller-safety function of those safeguards. B's claim that safer clients are disproportionately deterred remains speculative, but its central mechanism and comparative rebuttals are substantially stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response to A's protected-records firewall exposes the unresolved enforcement tradeoff: using transaction records risks undermining safeguards; insulating them weakens A's deterrence story. B then sharply identifies screening and records as seller protections, not merely buyer conveniences.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): A built the stronger comparative case: criminalizing purchase turns screening records and shared premises into potential evidence, forcing a choice between weak enforcement and concealed safety networks. A also answered B’s exclusion concern with a legal model where licensing cannot gate protection, preserving seller rights without criminalizing every booking. B established advantages over full prohibition but relied too heavily on an asserted enforcement firewall and repeated licensing objections after A had answered them.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s enforcement-dilemma rebuttal, sharpened in A2.3 and A4.3, was decisive: protected safety records make routine buyer deterrence weak, while using those records encourages concealment. B’s harm-report answer addressed violence after the fact but never squarely restored the pre-harm safeguards or credible routine deterrence.
