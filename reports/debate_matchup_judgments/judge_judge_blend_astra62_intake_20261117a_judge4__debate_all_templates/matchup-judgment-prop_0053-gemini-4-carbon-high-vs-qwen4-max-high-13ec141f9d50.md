### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): PRO built an intuitive case: parasocial priming, youth vulnerability, and two layers of protection where each covers the other's gap. CON did more damaging comparative work. It showed that both of PRO's layers attach to the same self-declared paid tag, so they share a single blind spot. Organic and gifted content, which PRO exempts, keeps doing the priming. CON also argued that warnings written into a sponsor's glamour edit can read as legitimizing. Its most effective move was showing that PRO's own enforcement answers end at the licensed clinic: regulator thresholds, certification under penalty, and illegal stealth gifting. That made the feed layer look like decoration that borrows its force from CON's counter-based alternative. PRO's Q1 found a real tension between CON's ban on elective injectables and its displacement claim. CON answered it well. It noted that PRO had itself conceded parental consent makes minors lawful customers for some procedures, then posed a clean dilemma: if minors can lawfully buy, the gate removes a sanctionable seller; if clinics must refuse them, the ads cause only priming, which lives in exempted content. PRO's closing recast this as CON "defending clinics pitching surgeries to sixteen-year-olds," which was rhetorically pointed but did not resolve the dilemma. PRO's "roughly a quarter" threshold also gave CON an easy turn: the trigger would permit a campaign whose audience is one in four children. PRO still has residual weight on the harm of youth-targeted commercial priming, but CON made the decision easier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to Q1 (B4.1–B4.2) was the most decisive exchange. It turned PRO's parental-consent concession into a dilemma: either the gate removes a lawful, sanctionable advertiser while leaving the back-alley injector, or the ads cause only priming that lives in content PRO exempts. Together with B5.1's point that all of PRO's enforcement terminates at the licensed provider, this undercut PRO's main path.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Both sides executed at a high level with sharp, well-structured exchanges. Side B (PRO) won the decisive clashes: the layered-defense logic (gate catches honest accounts, warnings catch birthday-liars) survived Side A's futility attack, and B's pressure Q2 forced the concession that clinics hide risks because "risk talk costs conversions," which B then reversed into "if plain risk kills bookings, warnings are not decorative." B also answered both pressure questions directly with labeled A1/A2 responses, and its alcohol/gambling analogy — pairing counter checks with youth-marketing limits — reframed Side A's counter-only alternative as complementary rather than exclusive, leaving Side A's "put the teeth at the clinic" case unable to explain why the feed layer should be rejected rather than added. Side A landed real blows: the parental-consent catch using B1.3 against B's displacement premise was excellent, and the "compliance perimeter around advertisers" framing was memorable. But A's core alternative never fully answered B's pre-needle harm story (body dissatisfaction, manufactured demand, queued-up eighteen-year-olds), and A's claim that priming "lives only in exempted organic content" was weakened by B's gifted-content-is-commercial-speech response. Narrow but clear edge to B on clash resolution, pressure handling, and burden defense.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): PRO wins a competitive debate. Its central claim is that demand is formed on the feed long before the clinic door, so consent at the counter cannot undo persuasion built upstream. That framing stayed live throughout. PRO also kept the motion appropriately narrow: no ID mandates, and a trigger tied to campaigns sold on teen-inclusive reach. It honestly conceded that gates are leaky and then argued that the right measure is reduced exposure, not perfection. CON built the most sophisticated opposition case, with three main lines: - **Clinic gatekeeping:** minors cannot buy procedures without clinics, fees, and parental consent. - **Age-gating dilemma:** gates either rely on easily faked birthdates or demand intrusive ID. - **Displacement:** suppressed sponsored posts get replaced by organic or covert content carrying no warnings. CON's A1 answer, separating sponsored reach from total teen exposure, was coherent. However, its displacement claim that "algorithms never leave blank space" was asserted more than shown. It also never fully answered PRO's point that removing the paid multiplier reduces the scale of commercial persuasion. The "stamp of legitimacy" argument remained thin against PRO's question about why identical risk language protects on a consent form but launders in a caption. CON's B4.3 argument was that a parent facing bills and a necrosis waiver would refuse if refusal were warranted, so caption text is pointless. PRO turned this in closing: if even that does not stop parents, the clinic is a formality rather than a safeguard, which strengthens the case for upstream warnings. Combined with the irreversibility asymmetry, this gave PRO the cleaner decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange concerned where risk information belongs. PRO's Q2 asked why clinic-stage risk information protects while the same feed-stage information supposedly acts as a "stamp of legitimacy." In closing, PRO turned CON's own B4.3 against it: if parents facing bills, a practitioner, and a necrosis waiver still sign, the counter is a formality after the decision is made. PRO then added the point that the text is the same and only the timing differs. That turn reframed CON's main substitute, clinical consent, as an argument for the motion.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a competitive, well-matched debate. B honestly absorbed pressure ("some minors get through... a gate is a filter, not a wall"), reframed the clash around timing and burden placement, and dismantled A's core substitute — clinical consent — by showing risk information delivered after two years of manufactured preference is a formality, not a safeguard. B's closing crystallization ("One side demands certainty from a warning. The other demands it from a child") resolved the exchange memorably. Side A's best route — organic displacement into warning-free vlogs — was genuinely live, and A's reconciliation of the leaky-vs-crushing gate contradiction in A4.1 was coherent, but B's paid-multiplier/enforcement-gap distinction blunted it, and A's banner-blindness/legitimacy-stamp argument was the weaker flank that B's "the text didn't change, the timing did" exchange exposed. Both sides followed format (proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 structure) and avoided fake sourcing. B made the debate easier to decide by conceding real costs and winning the weighing comparison; A left its strongest ground (displacement) underdeveloped relative to its weaker clinic-consent centerpiece.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's B4.4/B5.1 dismantling of clinical consent: if a parent facing steep bills, a practitioner, and a necrosis-naming waiver still signs, consent is a formality performed after the decision — making the case for moving the warning upstream and forcing A into the strained reply that caption text could succeed where a live waiver fails.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): B built the stronger case for incremental protection: earlier warnings address omitted risks, while imperfect gates can reduce commercially amplified exposure without universal identification. A successfully distinguished sponsored-post reach from total teenage exposure and challenged B’s misreading of its parental-consent conditional. However, A’s decisive displacement argument depended on automatic, equally persuasive replacement and effectively zero warning benefit, neither established. B’s upstream mechanism survived, although its oversimplification of warning context and adult-access costs kept the result competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s distinction between deceptive advertising and truthful promotion that omits material risks exposed a gap in A’s fraud-enforcement alternative. Its later distinction between organic storytelling and clinic-funded amplification also challenged displacement directly: showing that unpaid videos can go viral did not establish that they fully offset reduced commercial promotion.
