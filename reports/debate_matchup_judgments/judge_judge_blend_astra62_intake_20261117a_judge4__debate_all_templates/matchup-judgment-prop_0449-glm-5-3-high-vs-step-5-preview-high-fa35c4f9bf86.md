### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): A wins narrowly because its free-rider argument survived: conditional assistance does not constrain countries that decline assistance and continue burning coal. B developed substantial grid-readiness and enforcement risks, but increasingly relied on treating finance and neutral adjudication as impermissible amendments, although the proposition does not exclude them. A overstated its case by claiming those safeguards follow automatically from a binding agreement. That weakness, alongside B's unanswered implementation concerns, limits the margin, but A supplied the clearer comparative reason for enforceable consequences.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing distinction between financing willing participants and deterring countries that refuse the bargain directly damaged B's alternative. B acknowledged free riding but never explained how conditional assistance would restrain those holdouts. This was more decisive than B's textual objection to accompanying support.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins a clear but competitive debate by prevailing on the central collective-action clash and proving why non-penalized approaches fail against a binding carbon budget. Side A landed sharp, well-grounded critiques on fast-growing grids needing firm dispatchable power, wealthy states gaming capture-rate thresholds, and the textual asymmetry of a motion that mandates penalties without explicitly guaranteeing transition finance. However, Side B persuasively argued that a multilateral treaty commitment naturally includes dispute settlement and reciprocal bargaining leverage, while exposing a decisive gap in Side A's case: Side A conceded free-riding and past voluntary failure while admitting its preferred path is slower, and its reliance on opt-in conditional finance leaves holdouts free to reject loans, burn cheap coal, and undercut compliant neighbors.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B3.1], Side B asked what compels donors to fund transition support and what stops holdouts from burning cheap coal while others pay; after Side A answered in [A4.1] with milestone-conditioned loans, Side B's closing ([B5.1]-[B5.3]) decisively showed that conditional finance only binds countries that come asking and cannot touch a free-rider that declines the bargain and undercuts transitioning neighbors.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): B won a close debate by preserving the strongest comparative argument for penalties: A’s conditional-finance alternative disciplines willing recipients but leaves countries that refuse the bargain free to continue burning coal. A landed substantial blows on grid feasibility, geopolitical capture, and the motion’s lack of guaranteed assistance or arbitration, but never fully answered that free-rider gap. B’s claims that support and neutral dispute settlement would structurally accompany penalties were sometimes too assumptive, keeping the margin modest.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first pressure question exposed the central weakness in A’s alternative. A answered with finance conditioned on verified milestones, but B’s closing successfully distinguished consent-based leverage over participants from consequences for nonparticipants, making that unresolved comparison decisive.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): B better distinguished the need to retire coal from the case for this enforcement mechanism. A exposed voluntary finance’s weakness against captive state-backed lending, but did not adequately explain why penalties would overcome security-driven coal dependence without passing costs to households. B directly challenged that assumption and offered conditional finance as a credible, though incomplete, comparison. A’s reciprocity argument answered some legitimacy concerns, and B overstated that coercion cannot work. Nevertheless, B applied stronger pressure to implementation and affordability, while A’s answers largely assumed those problems away.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to “plant owners, not households” distinguished formal targets from economic incidence: restricting finance can still harm electricity consumers. This directly weakened A’s claimed safeguards, and A’s closing did not repair the gap.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a competitive, well-argued debate by exposing the political and institutional breakdown at the core of Side B's mechanism. While Side B smartly identified captive, state-backed coal builds as the key gap in market-led transitions, its defense of enforcing penalties without unanimity walked into a trap that Side A exploited across the rest of the debate: without China or India consenting, a "global commitment" shrinks into a wealthy-importer sanctions club ("administered by participating members") that provokes retaliation and fractures climate cooperation rather than shutting down domestic coal grids. Side A also answered Side B's pressure questions honestly—conceding consent-based retirement contracts and lender withdrawals are imperfect while showing why signed deals outperform rejected sanctions—whereas Side B dodged naming affected countries or a concrete legitimate adjudicator and relied on idealized promises of front-loaded finance and rebates to dismiss grid and price harms in poorer states.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In Turn 4 ([A2.1]-[A2.4]), Side A took Side B's two sharpest Turn 3 defenses—that penalties "do not require unanimity" [B2.1] and are "nearly free" where markets work [B2.3]—and turned both into major liabilities, showing the first reduces a global commitment to unilateral rich-bloc coercion and the second proves the penalties land exclusively on capital-poor, captive grids.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): A won narrowly by turning B’s non-unanimity defense into the debate’s central liability: enforcement “administered by participating members” looked less like a global commitment than a sanctions club, and B never identified a convincingly legitimate enforcer or fully answered the household and grid costs. A’s redundancy-where-markets-work versus coercion-where-they-do-not dilemma remained forceful. B’s strongest argument—that consent-based finance cannot reach captive, state-backed coal lock-in—was never completely neutralized, so A’s alternative did not establish a reliable 2040 path and the margin stayed small.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal most decisively answered the legitimacy question directly, then quoted B’s “participating members” enforcement model back as confirmation of the sanctions-club objection. B’s best counter was its closing exposure that A’s contracts bind only willing parties and therefore miss the captive-finance cases motivating penalties.
