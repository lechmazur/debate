CON asserted that apprehensions track attempts and that displacement is "deterrence working," but it offered no reasoning for why route-shifting equals net reduction beyond restating the claim. CON's magnet-effect and Spain/Germany claims relied on coincidence. "Well-documented in multiple countries," a specific Germany 2020 initiative, and a closing-stage "after 2007" US claim all read as unsupported authority. Some of this was introduced late, against the format rules. CON also repeatedly argued that legal channels fail because they are "too narrow," which PRO effectively turned into support for expanding them. PRO paid real costs. Its first rebuttal was clipped. Its Q1 awkwardly misattributed the displacement argument. Its A1 conceded it lacked controlled evidence. However, PRO handled that concession honestly by pointing out that the comparative burden cuts both ways. Its 100-worker/80-permit illustration directly answered the cap question. CON's second rebuttal and closing largely repeated the opening list of weaknesses without adapting.

PRO then noted that CON's own admission that pathways are "too narrow" concedes that the problem is insufficient legal capacity, not that legal channels as such underperform enforcement. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): CON wins because it defined the decisive clash: the motion is comparative and causal, so PRO had to show that expanding legal pathways produces a larger net reduction in irregular migration than added enforcement. PRO never met that burden. PRO relied on repeated assertions. It said Canada's program "demonstrably reduced unauthorized crossings." Later it claimed enforcement and demand "remained constant." Both are unsupported specifics presented as settled fact, and the second is close to fake precision. PRO's core mechanism, "each visa eliminates one irregular crossing," assumes the counterfactual instead of proving it. CON exposed this cleanly in its closing. PRO also repeated its "root cause" and "arms race" framing with little progression from the opening to the closing. It did not engage seriously with CON's points about excluded sectors, nationalities, quotas and periods. CON had weaknesses: - Its rebuttal was clipped for exceeding the word limit. - Its positive case for enforcement was also mostly mechanism-based rather than evidenced. - It partly conceded that fully demand-matching visas could displace irregular workers. However, CON contained that concession by tying it back to the comparative burden. Its 100-worker farm example clarified the marginal-effect question. Overall, CON narrowed the debate more effectively and answered pressure more honestly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's closing attack on PRO's conversion claim was decisive. PRO argued that each legal visa removes one irregular crossing. CON showed that this assumes the very counterfactual the motion requires, since a visa holder might otherwise have stayed home or used another legal route. Combined with CON's earlier point that sectoral declines cannot separate legalization from changes in demand or enforcement, this left PRO's route to victory resting on unproven assertion.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won by relentlessly exposing the missing counterfactual in PRO's case: sectoral examples show legal recruitment exists, not that expansion reduces irregular migration more than enforcement. B's farm-needing-100-workers example and the "marginal effect" framing gave the debate a clean decision rule, and the closing crystallized the decisive clash—PRO's "each visa eliminates one irregular crossing" is precisely the assumption it needed to prove. Side A's answers grew more brittle under pressure, asserting that Canadian "enforcement intensity and labor demand remained constant" without grounding, and its speeches largely restated the conversion mechanism rather than answering the causal-identification critique. B also handled pressure questions honestly in A1/A2 format, conceding narrow ground (full coverage could displace irregular workers) while keeping the comparative burden on PRO, which the motion's wording supports. B's main weakness is thin positive evidence for enforcement, but its burden argument—that the proposition asserts superiority and PRO never established it—remains a live route to victory that A never neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1–B4.3 and the closing: B dismantled A's claim that sectoral declines isolate the causal effect of legal pathways, then in the closing zeroed in on A's assertion that each visa eliminates one irregular crossing, showing the counterfactual was the entire unproven motion.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins a clear but competitive debate through superior analytical progression, sharper rebuttal, and better epistemic discipline. Side B (PRO) repeated a single hydraulic thesis across all four speeches—that seasonal labor pathways satisfy pull factors while border enforcement merely shifts smuggling routes—without answering Side A's core structural critiques: seasonal visas leave non-seasonal and excluded flows untouched, firms still favor irregular labor for lower cost and flexibility absent enforcement, and issuing legal visas does not prove recipients would otherwise have crossed irregularly. Side B also contradicted its own employer-bound examples (SAWP and H-2A) when pivoting to wage and portability features, and made an unsupported ceteris paribus claim about Canadian border crossings in [B4.1]. Side A's margin is tempered by a minor word-limit clip in Turn 4 and a purely theoretical, burden-shifting answer to Side B's first pressure question rather than offering concrete examples of effective border enforcement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A5.1], building on [A4.3]-[A4.4], Side A quoted Side B's closing claim that "each legal visa issued to a worker who would otherwise migrate irregularly eliminates one irregular crossing" [B5.2] and exposed that this begs the central counterfactual of the motion, since visa counts measure administrative uptake—including workers who would have stayed abroad—while leaving excluded sectors and informal employer demand intact.
