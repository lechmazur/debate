### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, plausible cases without fake sourcing, but B more effectively exposed the weakest links in A's causal chain. A's strongest asset—the reversibility asymmetry (friction is adjustable, viral harm is not)—was a compelling weighing frame, but it never answered two specific attacks B pressed to the end: (1) that staged/limited rollout does not guarantee discovery of failures that only emerge at mass scale, so "slower" may just postpone rather than prevent harm, and (2) that "stronger defaults" (watermarks, labels, reporting channels) are not actually dividends of slowness and could accompany fast deployment, decoupling the motion's two conjuncts. B raised both points clearly in Rebuttal 2 (B4.3, B4.4) and A's closing reasserted the reversibility frame without directly closing either gap. B's pressure questions (mechanism for safety gains, magnitude demonstration) were well-aimed, and B's answers to A's pressure questions (A1/A2 properly formatted) directly engaged rather than dodged. A's opening was richer and more vivid, and B's opening suffered a clipping penalty, but that execution cost was outweighed by B's sharper, more sustained rebuttal work in the middle and late rounds, leaving the decisive counterfactual claim in A's case unresolved at the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 point that limited cohorts/staged rollout may simply miss or postpone discovery of scale-only failures (B4.3), combined with the closing point that "stronger defaults" are not inherently dividends of "slower" shipping (B4.4/B5.2) — both left unanswered in A's final speech — was the most decisive, unneutralized pressure in the debate.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): B won by keeping trust distinct from adoption and making path-dependence plus irreversibility the deciding comparison: preventable branded failures as first mass impression damage trust in a way delayed convenience and adjustable friction do not. A’s net-counterfactual pressure was real and sharp, especially on delay not guaranteeing safer systems and safeguards not requiring slowness, but B answered the live burden more cleanly via exposure control and staged discovery before scale, and used A’s concession on preventable harms more effectively. A’s clipped opening and more abstract ledger style left B’s route easier to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s R2 exposure-control answer to A’s “delay only postpones discovery” charge, paired with the reversible-friction vs non-consenting viral harm asymmetry.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully distinguishing between mere adoption/ubiquity and actual public trust, arguing persuasively that forced reliance on a flawed product breeds wary dependence rather than trust. A's core weighing mechanism—the asymmetry between reversible safeguard friction and irreversible, viral harms from permissive defaults—was highly effective and cleanly answered B's best objections. Side B argued well on the costs of safeguards and the value of real-world learning, but could not fully overcome A's path-dependence argument regarding first impressions and public perception.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it cleanly answered the pressure questions by contrasting "reversible product friction" with "non-consenting, widely shared exposure," effectively neutralizing B's attempt to equate the costs of safeguards with the harms of permissive defaults.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): The pivotal clash was A's distinction between usage/reliance and genuine trust ("a person can use a system daily and still distrust its answers and its owner"). This reframing exposed a real weakness in B's central mechanism, which treated calibrated use, verification habits, and daily adoption as evidence of trust rather than evidence of learned caution. B's best counters—the knowability problem (risks only surface at scale) and the selection-bias critique of staged release (early cohorts skew technical/tolerant)—were strong and not fully neutralized, but A's answer to the "vague slower" pressure question (comparative readiness criteria, published limits, domain-specific defaults like general-not-personalized medical answers) gave a concrete, non-strawman version of the proposition that survived B's paternalism attack. A also handled the pressure-question round with clean A1/A2 compliance and used it to sharpen rather than widen the debate. B fought well and kept real tension alive (especially on nonusers only seeing "a gated product and fear" under PRO's world), but never fully dislodged A's core trust/reliance distinction, which is the more intuitively persuasive reading of the motion's actual metric. A's opening being clipped is a real but minor execution cost, not enough to offset the conceptual edge maintained across rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal distinguishing "learned distrust" from genuine trust (A2.1), reinforced through B's concession that users now "verify outputs," was the most damaging exchange, since it undercut B's foundational claim that exposure alone builds public confidence.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): B won by keeping the motion on public trust rather than user skill: exposure can create calibrated reliance while still teaching preventable-betrayal distrust of makers, especially among nonusers. B neutralized A’s strawman (blanket delay / opaque refusals) with staged readiness and adjustable defaults, answered pressure cleanly, and left A defending a weaker path where usefulness-plus-patches substitute for earned goodwill. A’s clipped opposite cost was small; B’s opening clip was a mild hit but not decisive. The exposure-calibration case stayed live but never outweighed B’s maker-respect and prevention-vs-repair weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1–B2.4: use/familiarity ≠ trust; search-engine analogy fails on fluent high-stakes fabrications; “stronger defaults” ≠ paternalist opacity.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a decisive conceptual distinction between "reliance/adoption" and "trust," successfully arguing that frequent verification and avoidance of sensitive topics constitute learned distrust rather than calibrated trust. Side A also effectively dismantled Side B's strawman that "slower" must mean "blanket delay" by introducing a plausible "staged deployment" counterfactual, and provided concrete, reasonable answers to the pressure questions regarding safety thresholds and default settings. Side B relied too heavily on the assumption that use equates to trust and struggled to account for the trust deficit among non-users who only observe public failures.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where they sharply distinguished between "calibrated reliance" (checking every answer) and actual trust, and successfully defended staged deployments as a way to gather real-world feedback without exposing millions of novices to preventable harms.
