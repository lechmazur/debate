### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side B won because it made the marginal benefits of decriminalization depend on tools that already exist — naloxone, Good Samaritan protections, deflection — while preserving a treatment lever Side A could not persuasively replace. Side B used Oregon and Portugal more effectively to show that compulsion and accountability, not the mere removal of criminal labels, drove outcomes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's enforceability reply was decisive: a civil commission sanction is a paper threat without a real backstop, while a criminal charge carries a warrant, a judge keeping the case open, and escalating consequences. This undercut Side A's attempt to replace criminal leverage with civil summonses.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side A built a tighter, self-consistent case across both halves of the proposition—showing how removing possession arrests reduces solo drug use, eliminates post-jail tolerance-loss deaths, and prevents custody-induced missed shifts that increase pressure to steal. Side B mounted a punchy case for coerced treatment leverage and pressed A effectively on the lack of an isolated fentanyl-era empirical test, but repeatedly shifted its counter-model from punitive enforcement to arrest-and-divert to no-booking deflection. Side A exposed the fatal tension in that retreat: criminal charges only outperform Portugal-style civil summonses if warrants and custody remain credible threats, which preserves both street-level fear and employment disruption while adding treatment only where scarce slots actually exist.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A exposed Side B's claim that Good Samaritan laws cover someone walking with drugs to a friend's house ([A3.2]), forced B to retreat to discretionary pre-arrest deflection with a "charge held in reserve" ([B4.3]), and then closed the trap in [A5.1] by showing that reserve charges only compel treatment if users take the threat of custody seriously everywhere, keeping the incentive to stay home and use alone intact.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B built the stronger comparative case by showing why decriminalization's gains do not automatically outweigh lost deterrence. A's 911 and employment mechanisms were vivid, but B effectively separated emergency immunity from full repeal, so the rescue benefit need not require decriminalization. B also exposed A's key assumption that both policies leave the same number of people in danger with the same habits. A's rebuttals were sharp but sometimes overclaimed, especially on visibility and on enforcement mattering only before risk becomes serious. B answered pressure honestly and redirected the dispute to whether early deterrence can prevent otherwise high-risk use, which left A's net-outcome conclusion under-supported.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was B's rebuttal that a weekend buyer could avoid a fatal exposure without any addiction chain, and that losing a job is a hardship rather than an automatic downstream theft. This undercut A's two headline outcome claims and forced A to rely on an unsupported assumption that both policies produce the same high-risk population.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won a high-level debate through tighter counterfactual reasoning, sharper pressure-question execution, and cleaner rebuttals. Side A built vivid, grounded mechanisms around delayed 911 calls, practical distrust of Good Samaritan statutes, and criminal records blocking legal work, and pivoted smartly in Closing to argue that harm is concentrated among high-frequency users. However, Side A stumbled in Rebuttal 2—prematurely claiming Side B left Q1 and Q2 unanswered before Side B's answering speech, abandoning its strong early point on post-jail tolerance collapse, and arguing fallaciously that occasional buyers become dependent daily users "in both worlds." Side B dismantled Side A's R1 visibility shortcut, exposed the "both worlds" counterfactual error, showed that repeal rescues also depend on a conditional chain, and turned Side A's Closing frequency point by explaining that delaying transitions from casual to daily use averts the exact stream of repeated exposures and unpayable drug bills that generate deaths and theft.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B4.5], Side B dismantled Side A's [A4.3] claim that once occasional buyers become daily users deterrence stops working so they face the same drug bill "in both worlds," cleanly noting that "the person exists in both worlds; the daily bill need not" if upstream penalties prevent or delay the habit from forming—a dynamic insight Side B extended in [B5.1] to turn Side A's Closing frequency framing.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A won by more cleanly weighing a short, concentrated harm pathway against Side B's longer deterrence chain. Punitive enforcement's call delays, records, and collateral costs fall on frequent users who generate most deaths and thefts and whom possession penalties do not move; any forgone weekend purchases sit on the thin edge of risk and do not offset those harms. Side B rightly refused a fixed population and answered the questions, but never showed why preventing or delaying some initiations outweighs worse outcomes for the mass of risk that still arrives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal and closing: CON's penalty "works only while it doesn't matter," trimming low-risk purchases while the high-frequency users who drive deaths and unaffordable bills pass through with slower help and fewer legal ways to pay.
