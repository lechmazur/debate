### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): A built the stronger comparative mechanism: asymmetric complaint risks encourage omissions extending beyond disputed material into legitimate inquiry. B established plausible harms from authority-backed conformity, but largely asserted that indoctrination is routine while legitimate self-censorship is rare. A also adapted more effectively to questioning. The margin remains competitive because B successfully challenged A’s overstatement that content-related harms are readily correctable while omissions are uniquely invisible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A distinguished abusive teaching from controversial content: coercive grading can persist on an approved syllabus. B’s reply that later review cannot undo suppressed student dissent weakened A’s correction argument, but did not answer that distinction or explain why balanced teaching prevents the burdens of bad-faith complaints.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing a clear structural asymmetry in teacher incentives and neutralizing Side A's strongest counter-case. Side A mounted a competitive offense around captive classroom authority—showing that graded, teacher-led indoctrination chills dissenting students in real time and cannot easily be undone by a later board hearing. However, Side B cleanly severed abusive grading misconduct from controversial curriculum content, pointing out that conformity-grading teachers operate on approved syllabi too and that political pressure merely installs a district-blessed side rather than neutrality—a point Side A never answered. Meanwhile, Side A's defense that complaint-driven pressure surgically removes only "partisan hot takes" collapsed in the late rounds when Side B exposed why ex-post complaint reviews fuel pre-emptive omission and why unrecorded omissions cannot simply be dismissed as rare.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In Turn 9 ([B5.1]-[B5.4]), Side B dismantled Side A's Turn 8 defense ([A4.1]-[A4.2]) that post-complaint reviews protect balanced teachers and that skipped primary sources are rare edge cases caught by students comparing notes: Side B showed that enduring a post-complaint investigation is the exact cost pre-emptive omission avoids, students cannot compare notes on lessons no section received, and calling an unrecorded omission "rare" mistakes invisibility for absence.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A won the central comparison: controversial content and teacher abuse leave a witness, record, and possible appeal, while omission leaves no complainant to trigger review. A also showed pressure cannot reliably distinguish indoctrination from hard questions, so B's claim that balanced teachers are safe and over-censorship is rare assumed the accurate filter it needed to prove.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A's Rebuttal 2 and closing use of B's admission that review happens only after a complaint: a student harmed by slanted teaching can at least name the harm, but no one can complain about a question never asked, and cross-class comparisons cannot reveal an omission shared by all teachers.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B wins on comparative scale, not its flawed attempt to relabel every censorship harm as “content.” A plausibly explains chilling effects and answers the fragility charge by treating critical thinking as learned. But it never adequately answers B’s alternative-pedagogy examples or supports its escalation to inquiry disappearing across entire school careers. B also effectively challenges A’s assumption that teacher-delivered misconceptions are easily corrected. B’s exaggerated claims about reversibility and its conflation of the motion’s two harms keep this competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s argument that changing a reading list need not eliminate evidence-weighing exposed A’s central missing comparison. A supplied an overcorrection mechanism, but its closing still assumed near-total suppression rather than explaining why other opportunities to develop critical thinking would also disappear.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A built the cleaner comparative case and won the decisive pedagogical clash. Side A immediately neutralized Side B's opening claim that young students can interrogate authoritative classroom errors, demonstrated that critical thinking is a transferable skill practiced on non-contested topics like disputed lab results and tariffs (which Side B left unanswered), and persuasively weighed recoverable content omissions against self-reinforcing misconceptions. Although Side B made a strong early showing on how ambiguous rules create lingering risk-averse overcorrection, it weakened its back half by asserting without proof that self-censorship halts inquiry across every K-12 subject and by repeatedly misdescribing Side A's case as relying on students' critical thinking to fix flawed lessons.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal [A4.3-A4.8], which turned Side B's point about dropped accurate topics into a sharp asymmetry ("Gaps get filled. Errors defend themselves"), showed via tariffs and lab results why skipping a contested novel does not teach children curiosity is a crime, and exposed Side B's tension between treating students as sturdy enough to spot a teacher's flawed lesson [B1.2] yet fragile enough to be ruined by silence.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B built the cleaner comparison: caution creates recoverable gaps, while authority-taught error installs beliefs that resist correction. B also exposed A's tension between narrow policed content and system-wide chill, and between students sturdy enough to interrogate bad lessons yet fragile enough to be ruined by omissions. A's process-loss answer was strong, but B showed it depended on inflating a skipped topic into an entire K-12 lesson that curiosity is punished, which collapses back into B's content harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: answering A's questions by distinguishing absence from installed error — 'Gaps get filled. Errors defend themselves' — and forcing A to choose between sturdy and fragile students.
