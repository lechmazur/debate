### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins a competitive debate. Its opening split sub-therapeutic growth promotion from short, full-dose veterinary prophylaxis. That move reshaped the whole exchange: PRO's resistance case largely targeted routine low-dose feeding, which CON was also willing to ban. CON then built a concrete alternative of upstream controls on premixes and human-critical classes, plus indication-specific exceptions. PRO never fully neutralized it. CON also turned enforcement back on PRO: once therapeutic antibiotics stay on farms, a "bright-line" ban requires auditing whether a dose came before or after symptoms. PRO's best moves were its strongest weapons: the irreversibility framing, the point that poor families also suffer most from resistant infections, and the turn that CON's surveillance thresholds act only after resistance emerges. These kept PRO competitive, but CON blunted them by noting that a ban does not reduce selection to zero either. CON's welfare and broad-spectrum rescue argument, along with the barn-caesarean and missing-vaccine examples, stayed largely live. PRO's answers there (aseptic technique, teat sealants, diagnostics) were plausible but less responsive to low-resource settings. On affordability, CON's reply that transitional aid expires while higher prices persist landed harder than PRO's appeal to "targeted support."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over enforcement. PRO's A2 claimed that enforcement burdens fall equally on both sides and that a bright-line ban is easier to audit. CON's A1 and A4.3 answered that upstream controls at feed mills and importers can be enforced without inspecting remote barns. A prophylaxis ban, by contrast, needs farm-by-farm policing of whether doses preceded symptoms. PRO's closing only partially addressed this.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won on the weighing layer. B collapsed A's core distinction by noting that recurring "short" preventive courses across millions of animals at every weaning, dry-off, and stress window are population-wide selection, not rare medicine, and B's closing crystallized the cleanest decision rule in the debate: meat prices and yields are corrigible through targeted support, while lost antibiotic effectiveness is irreversible. B also answered pressure questions directly in proper A1/A2 format and landed the "surveillance thresholds after resistance emerges" framing against A's own concession that zero cumulative selection is impossible. Side A (CON) fought well: the therapeutic-dosing paradox (bans force broad-spectrum rescue riskier to human medicine), the enforcement asymmetry (auditing feed mills versus policing whether farmers dosed before or after symptoms), and the LMIC malnutrition costs were genuinely damaging, and A's rebuttals were the sharpest technical exchanges of the debate. But A's final counter that stunting is "just as irreversible" was asserted rather than developed, and A never fully neutralized B's default-changing argument. A narrow but real PRO victory on cleaner crystallization and the irreversibility comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B's Q2 pressure ("what is the safe cumulative limit?") extracting A's admission that zero cumulative selection is impossible, which B's closing converted into the core vote: A manages resistance after it emerges via surveillance thresholds, while B removes the largest avoidable source beforehand. A's counter-reversal on enforcement auditing (feed mills vs. farm-level symptom timing) was the best CON moment but arrived too late to displace B's irreversibility weighing.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B won by narrowing the motion into a more defensible clash: not all prophylaxis is growth promotion, and a blanket ban can force delayed treatment, broader-spectrum rescue use, animal suffering, and malnutrition costs. Side A’s irreversibility and bright-line-default arguments were strong, but B more convincingly answered the enforcement pressure by comparing upstream class/feed-mill controls with the difficulty of policing whether dosing happened before or after symptoms, and B made the comparative harms easier to weigh.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal effectively flipped A’s enforcement-symmetry claim: upstream bans on premixes and human-critical classes sounded more administrable than auditing “early clinical signs,” while tying prophylaxis exceptions to avoided broad-spectrum rescue therapy.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO wins on the core weighing: a commons-style market failure combined with an irreversibility asymmetry. Food costs can be adjusted, but dead antibiotic classes cannot be bought back. CON raised real problems. A ban does not automatically produce better husbandry, imports are hard to verify, and higher meat prices hurt nutrition for the poor. But PRO answered each one directly. It used co-selection to undercut CON's carve-out for "non-human" drugs. It argued that later weaning, lower density and quarantine need little capital. It also exposed a tension in CON's position: CON says low-capacity states lack veterinary infrastructure, yet its alternative requires prescription policing, vaccination and co-resistance monitoring. CON's A4.1 then conceded that husbandry fixes "require little capital" and that dosing concentrates in large integrators. CON's alternative also drifted toward PRO's position: ban growth promotion and require time-limited prophylaxis tied to documented risk. That let PRO argue in closing that a clean ban is simpler and more effective than policing endless risk claims. CON's closing reframe, that the tool does not guarantee its benefit, was reasonable but mostly restated earlier points. CON never fully overcame the irreversibility weighing. CON's nutrition argument stayed partly live, which keeps the margin competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B4.3 and B5.2 turned CON's own capacity argument into a contradiction: weak states cannot run CON's prescription-oversight regime better than a feed-mill-level ban. PRO also seized on CON's concession that husbandry fixes need little capital. Together these neutralized CON's main feasibility and welfare route.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won a well-matched debate on antibiotic prophylaxis bans. Both sides were grounded and responsive, but B consistently converted A's concessions into decisive clash: A's own admission that husbandry fixes "require little capital" undercut the feasibility objection to the ban; A's proposed "time-limited prophylaxis for documented disease risk" was shown to still permit batch-by-batch herd dosing of the very stressors A originally defended, hollowing out A's alternative. B's answers to pressure (A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2) were direct and effective — the hormone-free process-audit precedent answered the residue-detection question, and the capacity-contradiction turn exposed that A's stewardship plan demands more state capacity than banning bulk feed-mill sales. A's best live route was the weak-enforcement/black-market dilemma crystallized in the closing, plus nutrition distribution harm, but B's substitution and irreversibility weighing ("no budget can buy back a working antibiotic") gave the cleaner reason to vote. A was competent throughout but B made the debate easier to decide and landed the sharper turns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.2-B4.3: PRO's answer that later weaning, lower density, and quarantine need zero capital, followed by the turn that CON's own alternative (prescription policing, co-resistance monitoring, husbandry regulation) requires more state capacity than banning bulk preventive sales at feed mills — this contradiction, then exploited in B5 against A4.1's concessions, was the debate's decisive exchange.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A won by making the central trade-off clear: manageable food-price and yield costs versus irreversible loss of antibiotic efficacy. It also pressured Side B's targeted-stewardship alternative on feasibility, relabeling risk, import enforcement, and co-selection. Side B remained plausible on animal welfare, poor consumers, and enforcement limits, but never fully answered how its alternative would police prophylaxis better than a ban under limited state capacity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal was decisive because it answered the detection and husbandry-upgrade pressure with audits, process standards, and low-capital husbandry changes, then turned Side B's low-capacity point against its own oversight model.
