### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won solidly by establishing why synthetic host-read ads exploit personal credibility in ways reactive litigation cannot catch, while cleanly neutralizing Side A's best counterarguments. Side A gained real mid-debate traction by stress-testing Side B's initial bright line with two-second promo-code patches and host-approved clones recorded during an illness. However, Side B adapted decisively: it refined the line around whether AI delivers or merely patches the endorsement, pointed out that over-compliance costs at most a single spoken sentence like existing sponsorship tags, and showed that disclosures cannot be both ubiquitous enough to cause fatigue and trivial to listeners. In contrast, Side A grew repetitive in later speeches, failed to show how reactive enforcement detects consented indistinguishable clones, contradicted itself on whether unapproved clones omit or trigger warnings, and walked straight into Side B's closing materiality trap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B5.1]-[B5.2] (set up by [B3.1]), Side B turned Side A's arguments in [A4.1] and [A4.3] directly against each other: Side A claimed both that materiality rules suffice because listeners do not care about approved AI rendering and that a factual one-sentence disclosure hurts honest creators by eroding credibility, letting Side B prove that saying "this ad uses an AI version of my voice" can only dent credibility if listeners materially care whether the host actually spoke.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A won by converting the materiality dispute into a dilemma CON could not escape: if listeners would not care, disclosure is costless; if they would, the fact is material and existing standards already demand it. A also kept the line focused on who delivered the endorsement rather than minor patches, and showed why undetectable clones defeat purely reactive litigation. B pressed overbreadth and fatigue throughout but never neutralized detection, incentive, or the consented-but-misleading case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing use of B's own materiality standard, paired with the earlier point that approval between host and advertiser does not inform the listener and that clones designed to be indistinguishable evade after-the-fact enforcement.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): B won by making the central clash clearer: whether the listener is owed disclosure when the trusted voice did not actually deliver the endorsement. B gave a workable line—synthetic delivery of the vouch versus ordinary correction of a human read—then turned A’s overbreadth and fatigue arguments into a dilemma: if the disclosure is trivial, it is rare and salient; if it is common, it is needed. A’s patch-line and small-creator concerns stayed live, but B’s detection problem answer and closing materiality trap were more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 pressure answers: it distinguished “correcting” a human-read from “delivering” a synthetic endorsement, and countered disclosure fatigue by arguing rare disclosures stay salient while ubiquitous ones prove the need for the rule.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins solidly by dismantling both the materiality and the workability of the proposed mandate. Side A cleanly separated vocal delivery from endorsement judgment, demonstrating that Side B's proxy gets consumer protection backwards: it places a warning on a creator who thoroughly tests a product, writes the script, and uses a licensed clone, while giving an implied pass to cold, unvetted human reads off sponsor talking points. When Side B argued in [B4.1] that the label is merely neutral delivery context yet lets listeners "adjust how much weight they give the pitch," Side A exposed the contradiction—either the label shifts weight in the wrong direction or it shifts no weight and is immaterial noise. Side A also showed that an AI-voice label cannot detect unapproved exaggerated claims ([A4.1], which existing endorsement law already polices) and broke Side B's "from scratch" boundary via the Spanish dub concession and the dropped two-second voice-model patch.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's two-step takedown in [A2.5] and [A4.5]-[A4.6]: first distinguishing paid-sponsorship rules (where an absent label truthfully implies no payment) from AI-voice rules (where an absent label invites a false inference of personal vetting despite cold human reads), and then trapping [B4.1]'s contradiction between claiming the disclosure does not signal untrustworthiness while insisting it exists to change how much weight listeners give the endorsement.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing the mandate answers the wrong question and sorts trust backwards: a vetted licensed clone is warned while an unvetted cold human read is silently certified. Side A never escaped that inversion, drifted from vetting deception to mere delivery context, and failed to hold a workable line once Spanish dubs and regenerated patches were applied. B's sponsorship comparison—absence of a paid label supports a true inference, absence of an AI label does not—made the vote clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first rebuttal seized A's concession that the cloned voice is only a proxy for "passing engagement," then showed the rule treats a vetted clone as suspect and a cold unread tablet read as the unlabeled standard. B's second rebuttal tightened that into a contradiction: the label either moves weight in the wrong direction or is noise.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by turning the debate into a question about what disclosure actually signals. It persuasively argued that an AI-voice label would not track endorsement quality or vetting, could create a misleading implicit certification for unlabeled human reads, and would burden clearly approved uses such as translated clones. Side B kept a clean consumer-information case and handled the Spanish-dub question directly, but it never fully resolved the small-patch line-drawing pressure or neutralized A’s claim that the mandate shifts attention in the wrong direction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was the decisive exchange: it used B’s own answer that the label is “simply context” but also lets listeners “adjust weight” to force the dilemma that the label either moves listeners away from vetted cloned reads or is meaningless, while also pressing the unanswered two-second regenerated-patch example.
