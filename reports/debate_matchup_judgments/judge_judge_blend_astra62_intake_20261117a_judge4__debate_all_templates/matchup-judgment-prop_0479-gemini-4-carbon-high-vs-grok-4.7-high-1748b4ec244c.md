### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): This was a high-quality, close debate. CON (A) built a tight and memorable case: contract design becomes "the new closed door," market prices are authorless, and the prize being manipulated is the policy itself, not the wager. Its rebuttals were incisive. The later fork, that depth cannot be both a reason to ignore the market and the forecast that ends panel primacy, was the most quotable line of the debate. However, CON leaned heavily on the "authorless number" theme and repeated it across nearly every speech. PRO (B) did more constructive work under pressure. When questioned, it gave a concrete institutional design: a rebuttable presumption that market odds set the baseline unless overridden, contracts drafted by independent statistical or audit offices, settlement on hard data feeds, and depth and concentration displayed beside the price. That design answers the fork reasonably well. Markets dominate where books are deep, and overrides must be published and justified where books are thin. PRO also kept a live comparative point that CON never fully neutralized: panels can be captured too, but quietly, and nobody is paid to push back. On CON's question of why an independent office should not simply forecast, PRO's answer was that no central bureau holds dispersed field knowledge. That answer arrived late, in the closing, but it is a fair extension of the opening's case. Its claim that named attribution exposes truth-tellers to retaliation also echoes the opening's career-hazard argument. PRO edges the debate because it answered pressure with mechanisms and kept a clearer comparative path to victory, while CON's strongest objection (manipulation where the prize is the policy) was contested rather than left standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came over A's Q2 and PRO's answer in B4.2. PRO assigned contract drafting and settlement to independent offices using hard data feeds. CON replied in A4.5 that if such an office is truly independent, it could publish attributable probabilities without selling a lever. PRO answered in B5.2 that a central office cannot hold dispersed field knowledge, which left PRO's core information-aggregation claim standing.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): PRO carried the comparative case by answering the main implementation pressure with a rebuttable-market baseline, independent statistical offices, hard-settlement feeds, and public order-book depth. This turned CON's contract-rigging and manipulation concerns into manageable disclosure and override problems rather than fatal ones. CON's strongest frame, that depth cannot be both a credibility screen and an automatic forecast, overstates the choice: a default market forecast can still be publicly flagged and overridden when thin. PRO also left a live Hayekian point standing: an independent office cannot itself aggregate dispersed private knowledge the way a subsidized market can. CON's accountability critique was real but did not fully answer the retaliation/conformity problem in named-expert alternatives. The edge was close, but PRO had the cleaner route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 most decisively answered the pressure questions by specifying statutory contracts, independent settlement, hard public data feeds, and public depth disclosures, undercutting CON's claim that formal market input just moves hierarchy into contract fine print.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): B narrowly wins on whether subsidized odds deserve presumptive authority. Its distinction between trading profits and policy payoffs, combined with information and decision-window constraints, undermines A's automatic-correction story. A answers contract governance and preserves expert analysis, but visible market depth and evidence-backed overrides do not establish that these markets merit default weight. B overstates its claim that allowing overrides necessarily restores panel primacy, keeping this a narrow win rather than a decisive rejection of market inputs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A argued that manipulation finances its own correction. B sharply answered that a larger bounty does not guarantee informed countertraders with available capital before the policy decision. Its pressure question then forced A to specify default market authority, making that unresolved correction problem more consequential.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): CON wins on the structural objection that governments forecast in order to intervene. It opened with a sharp self-defeating-prophecy problem and a refund-exploit problem for conditional markets, then pressed both consistently. PRO's first rebuttal was strong: the "two opposite failures" framing, residual risk given the expected response, and manipulation as an open donation to arbitrageurs. But PRO's answer to the pressure question on comparing policies created a new vulnerability. Its rule that stakes on rejected drafts are "forfeited to the subsidy" was turned decisively by CON. Honest traders whose warning succeeds lose their money, and arbitrageurs will not fade a lobby raid on a sound rule because rejection confiscates their stake too. PRO's closing reply, "the warning has already worked," does not restore any incentive for profit-seeking traders. CON also showed that PRO's safeguards undercut its own core mechanism. Clearing, position limits, and requiring traders to "put the why in the hearing" bring back the causal reasoning and gatekeeping PRO said markets would replace. PRO claimed CON never answered "who pays when wrong," but CON did, through scored track records and auditable assumptions. CON's "both" answer on capture versus dismissal was somewhat slippery, though it was adequately framed as a two-way political trap. Overall, CON had the cleaner live routes to victory at the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.3 turn of PRO's forfeiture design was the decisive exchange. Confiscating stakes on rejected drafts penalizes honest warnings that succeed and leaves lobby raids on sound rules unopposed. This converted PRO's answer to Q1 into a liability that PRO never repaired.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B controlled the decisive clash. A gave a strong accountability-based opening, but B exposed a structural problem: policy markets are self-defeating because forecasts trigger interventions, and A's later forfeiture design to compare policy drafts worsens that. When rejected branches confiscate stakes, honest traders lose money for accurate warnings, so they flee or decline to fade raids, while lobbies with outside payoffs can still exploit the subsidized market. B resolved A's powerful/weak pressure as a two-way trap and showed that A's hearing and position-limit concessions shifted the real governing work back to experts. A's closing was punchy but never repaired the incentive collapse B identified.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's tracing of A's forfeiture rule: if honest traders warn that a bad bill will miss its target and ministers reject it, their stake is forfeited; and if a lobby raids a sound regulation, arbitrageurs will not fade it because rejection confiscates their stake too. This turned A's anti-sabotage mechanism into a reason the market cannot aggregate truthful warnings.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B wins by turning A's central incentive argument against its proposed design. Forfeiting stakes on rejected policies punishes traders when accurate warnings persuade officials, and A's closing never repairs that incentive. B also distinguishes honest traders' market returns from lobbies' outside policy gains, defeating A's alleged contradiction and weakening its arbitrage defense. A establishes benefits from public, contestable forecasts, but B more convincingly explains why those benefits do not justify shifting primary reliance away from auditable competing models.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure question forced A to specify how unchosen policy contracts settle. When A proposed forfeiture, B showed that successful warnings would confiscate honest traders' stakes. A's reply that the warning had already worked conceded the public benefit without explaining why profit-seeking traders would supply it.
