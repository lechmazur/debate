A’s unsupported claims that every relevant contract produces exclusion and freezes substantially weakened its grounding. B overstated the inevitability of uncapped budget failure, but answered the central pressure more directly and developed the stronger comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to the rebate-delay challenge was decisive: prospective price adjustments and invoice withholding need not require waiting for reimbursement or freezing enrollment. - **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A won solidly by proving that for broad preventive obesity coverage, only outcome-based pricing or spending caps bound total price-times-volume liability, whereas Side B's upfront unit-price model leaves total budget exposure uncapped. ### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): A (CON) wins by keeping the clash on the motion's "only when" precondition. PRO had a plausible affordability case, but it repeatedly mischaracterized CON's alternative as open-ended/full-price reimbursement even after CON clarified that it included rebates, tenders, competition, and overall budgets. CON turned the key issues into a live dilemma: caps either bind and force access levers unless the seller voluntarily cuts price, or they do little; outcome terms add retrospective attribution fights rather than replacing prior authorization. PRO's status quo rationing point proved budget pressure, but not that broad coverage should be legally barred absent these specific contracts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 was decisive: it showed outcome-based rebates would not eliminate prior authorization but add a contested per-patient-year inquiry, and it converted PRO's "manufacturers already hold pricing power" into a reason the motion creates a fresh access veto.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): A better addresses “only”: B establishes the importance of budget protection, but not why affordable coverage negotiated through ordinary discounts must be forbidden. B repeatedly substitutes full-price, unlimited reimbursement for A’s alternative and inadequately answers the manufacturer-refusal problem. However, B’s automatic unit-price adjustment directly rebuts A’s claim that caps necessarily ration, and A never properly engages that mechanism. This keeps the debate competitive, but A’s central objection to making these particular contracts mandatory survives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest response distinguishes evidence of existing rationing from evidence that the proposed contractual gate is necessary. B’s insistence that ordinary procurement has already failed does not establish that conclusion or answer A’s alternative on its actual terms.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by focusing squarely on the motion's mandatory precondition ("only when manufacturers accept") and out-executing Side A in the second half. Side A built a credible fiscal case—showing that uncapped chronic coverage for millions risks budget crowd-out and drives status quo prior-authorization rationing—which kept the debate competitive against Side B's somewhat optimistic view of funding broad prevention within existing budgets. However, Side B decisively won the outcome-based pricing clash (showing risk-adjustment creates audit fights and incentives to avoid complex patients, while clinical stopping rules already address non-responders), exposed how the motion's "or" and seller-refusal dynamics turn bargaining failure into a patient-access cliff rather than breaking manufacturer pricing power, and progressed sharply across rounds while Side A largely repeated its earlier script in late speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Turn 8 dismantling ([B4.3]-[B4.4]) of Side A's proposed risk-adjusted 12-month 5-10% weight-loss metric ([A4.2]). Side B showed that outcome rebates add onto rather than replace front-end eligibility controls, and that "risk-adjusted" metrics simply rename the attribution fight—turning routine chart notes into contested clawback evidence for nausea, depression, or food insecurity while pressuring providers to avoid complex patients. Side A left this completely unanswered in Closing.
