### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): This was a close, high-quality debate, and PRO wins narrowly. PRO built a clear, intuitive case. Primary care is the entrance to the health system, universality closes the gaps that targeted eligibility leaves, and a guarantee can coexist with triage and with private supplementary care. CON's attack was analytically sharper. It argued that a guarantee either constrains spending against higher-value services, such as sight-saving transport, or collapses into mere reconsideration. CON pressed this well in B4.3-B4.4 and in its closing. However, CON's case rested heavily on a stylized fixed-budget hypothetical. CON also conceded in A2 that its alternative offers "no enforceable right to an appointment." PRO exploited that concession cleanly. PRO's answer was that the guarantee is a continuing obligation to expand and arrange access, not a priority ranking above urgent treatment. That answer is not airtight, but it plausibly distinguishes PRO's model from CON's "account for exclusion and revisit" model. As a result, the general proposition remains defended. CON's dilemma reduced the guarantee's practical bite but did not show that guaranteeing a primary-care floor is worse than its targeted alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came in B4.3-B4.4 and its follow-up. CON asked what happens at PRO's "clinically justified deadline": does time reverse a clinical judgment, or is the guarantee just renewable review? PRO's closing replied that the guarantee binds government to build and fund access, not to reverse priorities, and turned CON's own concession of "no enforceable right to an appointment" against it. That kept PRO's floor distinct enough to survive.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined, avoided invented evidence, and engaged each other directly, so the margin is thin. PRO wins narrowly on three points. First, PRO's core mechanism held up through the whole debate. Primary care is the point where people learn whether they need treatment, so financial gating there deters exactly the people whose needs go unidentified. Second, PRO's error-asymmetry argument was never fully neutralized. A mistaken subsidy pays for a visit that would have happened anyway, while a mistaken exclusion means nobody learns the patient needs treatment. CON's reply, that wasted money leaves another patient's treatment unfunded, is real but more diffuse. Third, CON's concessions narrowed its position considerably. It accepted provisional first visits with no retrospective bill, generous presumptions of hardship, and lost local access as a trigger for aid. That left the live dispute mostly about affluent patients and continuing care. PRO's closing used this well: the "come back" framing showed that targeting still leaves continuity of care dependent on a financial judgment that can be wrong or out of date. CON's strongest moves were sharp and kept the round close. Its opportunity-cost framing pressed the question of why public money should fund access people already have. Its B4.3 point showed that under PRO's model, the funding obligation partly follows an affluent patient's choice rather than her need. Its referral-inconsistency challenge also landed. Even so, PRO's A2 answer drew a credible line between eligibility checks before the first visit and checks at referral. At referral, a clinician has already identified the treatment and can document its urgency. Before the first visit, the patient is still deciding whether to seek that judgment at all. Overall, PRO left the cleaner reason to prefer universality as the motion is written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was the referral-inconsistency fight. In B2.5 and B3.2, CON quoted PRO's "targeted help for costly referrals" and asked why eligibility checks are fine before life-saving treatment but not before primary care. In A4.3–A4.4, PRO answered that at referral a clinician has already identified and documented the need. Before the first visit, the patient is deciding whether to seek that judgment at all, and only coverage that holds regardless of a later denial gets her through the door. This forced CON to concede in B4.1 that provisionally authorized visits carry no retrospective bill, which narrowed CON's case. CON's best counter was B4.3's point that PRO's funding obligation follows the affluent patient's choice, but it did not overturn PRO's deterrence logic.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): A razor-thin win for PRO. Both sides were disciplined, format-compliant, and built coherent cases without fake sourcing. B's sharpest route — opportunity cost, and the point that PRO's guarantee creates a funding claim whenever an affluent patient opts into the public system — was directly answered by A (taxes recover contributions; clinical urgency governs the queue) and then reframed into the decisive asymmetry: a mistaken subsidy wastes a visit, a mistaken exclusion means a needed condition is never identified. B's best counter was the "tolerant targeting vs. universal eligibility" reframe in Rebuttal 2, which absorbed much of A's error-cost argument by granting provisional first-visit protection. But A's closing hit the residual weakness squarely: affordability categories are volatile (the worker whose practice drops her insurance), and B's reply that "reassessment need not mean abandonment" was assertion without mechanism. A's continuity framing ("come back" as a treatment plan) crystallized the live clash more cleanly than B's closing, which repeated the opportunity-cost weighing without resolving the churn problem. B landed real comparative pressure throughout, but A neutralized the most important attacks while leaving one live route standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The B4.4 "tolerant targeting, not flawless bureaucracy vs. abandonment" reframe versus A's closing response about workers churning out of the "clearly affordable" category — B's strongest defusal move met a precise, well-timed rejoinder that kept A's identification asymmetry alive.
