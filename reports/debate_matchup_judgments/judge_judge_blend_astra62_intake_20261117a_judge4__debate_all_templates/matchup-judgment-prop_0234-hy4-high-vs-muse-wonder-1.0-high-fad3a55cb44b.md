### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A won a high-level, competitive debate by winning the comparative baseline and cleanly neutralizing Side B's central late-round dilemmas. Side B scored real points on Goodhart-style distortions—particularly variable compensation migration, internal peer-grievance compression, and intermediate title gaming, which Side A only lightly addressed. However, Side B staked its case heavily on two claims that buckled under Side A's pressure: first, that policing sham wide bands equates to substantive government pay control, which Side A refuted by showing that requiring large firms to publish their actual internal role structures bans fake maps without fixing prices; and second, that workers are better served by concrete offers, confidential ex post discrimination audits, and asking interview questions to "judge evasiveness directly." Side A decisively showed that ex post audits cannot prevent ex ante career detours, a single offer reveals nothing about within-band placement before applying or transferring, and verbal claims without a public denominator cannot be verified.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B repeatedly argued that promotion rates are misleading because a low number conflates a "stable senior team" with a "dead end," claiming workers should instead rely on confidential bias audits and interview questions where they "judge evasiveness directly" ([B2.5]-[B2.6], [B4.3]). Side A turned this objection by showing that for an entrant seeking mobility, a stable senior team still means a crowded ladder she needs to be warned about before joining ([A2.5], [A4.3]), while ex post audits arrive too late and manager assurances without a public denominator cannot be checked ([A4.4], [A5.1]-[A5.2]).
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins a close debate by holding the right comparison: one-sided secrecy versus a visible, imperfect constraint, not omniscience versus disclosure. Side A landed a real dilemma—wide bands mislead and anchor, while policing them edges into pay control—and a serious gaming risk, but never squarely answered B's point that a low promotion rate is decision-useful under either story (the ladder is crowded) or that private answers and after-the-fact audits cannot be checked before a worker accepts or transfers. B's distinction between publishing the firm's own structure and setting prices was good enough to keep the mandate from collapsing into wage control.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second-rebuttal reframe that a low promotion rate warns a junior the ladder is crowded whether the cause is a stable senior team or a dead end, paired with the point that an employer's unverifiable answer has no public denominator. Side A kept treating that ambiguity as fatal and did not break it.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): B built the clearer comparative case for decision-useful, checkable information before workers apply, negotiate, or transfer. Its answers distinguished truthful disclosure from salary control and explained why offers, optional questions, and retrospective confidential audits cannot reveal the same structure. A pressed a serious live case about anchoring, gaming, compression, and ambiguous promotion rates, so the win was competitive, but its pay-control dilemma and alternative-information case did not fully neutralize B’s ex ante informational benefit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s wide-versus-narrow band dilemma and promotion-rate ambiguity put B under real pressure; B’s Rebuttal 2 answered both directly, then its closing decisively exposed that A’s offer, questions, and audits still leave workers without a verifiable range or promotion denominator.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side A won a high-level, competitive debate by establishing a cleaner case for baseline visibility and exposing the trigger gap in Side B's alternative. Side A showed that without public bands and promotion rates, applicants negotiate and sort blind while underpaid or stalled employees have no baseline to suspect a pattern—making Side B's independent raw-record reviews hard to invoke without public data and complementary rather than rival to disclosure. Side A also contained the flexibility cost by showing that large firms already track bands and require internal approvals, that most counteroffers sit inside ranges without publicizing individual files, and that secret discretion favors connected insiders over overlooked talent. Side B stayed competitive with an unanswered granularity pincer on promotion rates (fine role-level-location cells are noisy while broad categories hide bottlenecks) and a strong Goodhart's Law critique, keeping the margin close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal across [A3.1], [A4.1-A4.2, A4.5], and [A5.1-A5.3] dismantling Side B's private-review alternative was decisive: after forcing Side B to concede that a worker cannot spot an outlier from her own file alone [B4.1], Side A showed that independent review works only after suspicion exists—whereas under secrecy a floor offer or dead-end role feels like personal bad luck—and that publication gives audits and appeals targets rather than replacing them.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A more convincingly showed that role-level bands and promotion rates are employer-defined, noisy or gameable proxies. Disaggregation recreates thin cells, while required public comparison chills useful exceptions—including equity-enhancing ones—without banning them. Side B’s visibility-first case stayed forceful but did not neutralize that tradeoff or the gap between a public label and the actual decision, so the mandated disclosure was not shown to be worth the flexibility loss.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s first rebuttal was decisive: stable promotion rates and context-sensitive pay cannot both be satisfied, and a rule can eliminate beneficial discretion through precedent, grievance risk, and midpoint defaults without formally banning it.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): B built the cleaner path to victory: disclosure supplies the comparative baseline workers and applicants need, while complementing rather than replacing audits and appeals. B also answered the flexibility objection by distinguishing aggregate disclosure from individual public defenses and explaining why competition limits midpoint retreat. A offered sophisticated pressure on thin cells, employer-controlled categories, and raw-record review, but its alternative remained weaker for detecting patterns and informing choices before harm, and it sometimes overstated how directly disclosure exposes each exception.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2 was decisive: publication creates the trigger and comparator that make targeted review effective, while in-band counteroffers and competitive hiring undercut A’s claim that legitimate discretion would necessarily collapse.
