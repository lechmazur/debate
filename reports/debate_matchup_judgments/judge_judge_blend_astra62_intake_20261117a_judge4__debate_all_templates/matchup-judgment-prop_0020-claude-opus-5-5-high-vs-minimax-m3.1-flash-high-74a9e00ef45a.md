### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won the decisive clash: the counterfactual burden. B showed the realistic pre-euro alternative (EMS collapse, Bundesbank dominance) and repeatedly exposed that A's "floating currencies with coordinated stabilization and joint guarantees" has no mechanism — no guarantor would co-sign debt a floating member can inflate away. A answered B's pressure questions well (floating vs. pegged distinction, own-currency debt can't force default), but this opened the door to B's sharpest rebuttal: the guarantor-exposure argument (B4.1-B4.3), which A never resolved, ending the closing with only the assertion that joint borrowing "does not inherently require" monetary union. B also effectively flipped A's own concession that the euro crisis forced fiscal solidarity into evidence that the euro generates solidarity A's alternative merely assumes. A's best moments — the rigged-exit-clause point and the coercion framing of Greece's 2015 choice — were real pressure, and B's Croatia/Baltics answer was adequate though less clean. B's progression across rounds was tighter: each speech narrowed the dispute to "real alternative vs. imagined one," and the closing crystallized it memorably ("the stronger Europe that does"). Both sides argued within the motion and used plausible background knowledge without fake precision; B simply made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.3: B turned A's floating-currency answer to the pressure question into a trap — if Italy can reprice its own-currency debt at will, Germany will never co-sign it, so the solidarity A's alternative depends on is precisely what monetary union uniquely produced. A's closing ("does not inherently require") conceded the absence of a mechanism.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the comparison anchored in workable alternatives rather than A’s increasingly speculative combination of floating currencies, fiscal rules, and joint guarantees. A made substantial points about asymmetric shocks, austerity, ECB accountability, and euro-exit lock-in, but never established that its preferred fiscal capacity would politically exist without monetary union or outweighed the euro’s repaired crisis framework and international benefits. B directly answered the floating-versus-pegged distinction, exposed the guarantor problem in A’s proposal, and crystallized that unresolved burden decisively in the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s floating-currency answer was decisive: A’s own claim that national governments could devalue or inflate away obligations undermined the willingness of other states to guarantee their debts. B then connected this to competitive devaluation within the single market, leaving A’s closing to assert that the same doubtful guarantees and rules would discipline the system.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by forcing the real counterfactual: pegged systems broke in 1992 and floating autonomy historically meant Bundesbank dominance, inflation, and speculative pressure. PRO's floating-plus-joint-guarantees alternative remained asserted rather than mechanised, especially on why guarantors would back debt a member could devalue away. B also answered the accountability and Greece/Croatia pressure more directly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 guarantor-exposure argument: shared fiscal capacity arrived only because the euro made Italy's failure common, undercutting PRO's claim that solidarity could simply precede monetary union.
