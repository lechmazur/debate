### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): Side A (PRO) wins a competitive debate because its case became steadily clearer, while Side B (CON) relied on attacks that A largely answered. A framed deepfakes as forged evidence rather than opinion. When B demanded a concrete legal test, A answered directly: synthetic or materially altered likeness or voice, false words or conduct, political context, no opening disclosure, and intent or recklessness. That converted B's "undrawable line" objection into a manageable drafting question. A also answered the enforcement question honestly. It conceded that every check is state-run, then argued that rejecting state-run checks would also condemn existing fraud and impersonation law. B pressed hard on several fronts: - **Line-drawing:** juries would have to grade realism and audience reception. - **Liar's dividend:** a ban hands deniers a taxonomy for dismissing real clips. - **Enforcement:** incumbents write the definitions and prosecute their critics. - **Label squeeze:** A called labels too weak, yet made disclosure the legal safe harbor. The label squeeze was B's best moment. However, A's closing drew a credible line between an after-the-fact corrective tag and an opening disclosure that keeps the file from being a lie in the first place. B also weakened its own position. It conceded that unlabeled election-eve fakes are not protected and fell back on "existing law," which narrows rather than defeats the motion. B suffered two clipped turns, including the end of Rebuttal 2. It also mislabeled a reference as "P2.3" when it meant A2.3. B's remaining live point, that resharing strips captions, is real but does not outweigh A's core harm: forged political acts reaching voters at the decisive moment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.4's squeeze, that labels are either too weak to rely on or strong enough to undercut A's dismissal of labeling, was the sharpest attack. A5.1 neutralized much of it by distinguishing a constitutive opening disclosure from a post-circulation corrective label, using the parody-ballot-notice versus forged-notice analogy.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): Side A built the clearer path to victory. Against B's definitional attack, A supplied a genuinely concrete source-and-conduct test (synthetic impersonation + falsity + disclosure safe harbor + intent) that shifted the line away from jury-graded realism. A answered both pressure questions directly and honestly, and its closing defused B's best weapon—the label squeeze—by distinguishing a constitutive opening disclosure from a weak after-circulation tag, anchored in ordinary fraud law. B's strongest surviving routes were enforcement asymmetry and the liar's-dividend taxonomy, but A's answers (fixed status definitions, courts, fewer counterfeits raising denial costs) were at least adequate, and A's closing weighing ("a joke can announce itself; a forged political self cannot") crystallized the stakes more decisively. B was sharp and original—the squeeze and "hands deniers a taxonomy" were excellent—but B's opening and second rebuttal were clipped for word-limit violations, and its endgame relied on restating the asymmetry rather than resolving A's disclosure answer. Both sides argued the motion as written with clean grounding; A made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's B4.4 "squeeze"—if labels are unreliable, A's safe harbor legalizes forgery on an unseen caption; if reliable, A's case against labeling collapses—was the sharpest move of the debate. A's A5.1 response, that an opening disclosure is constitutive (like a clearly marked parody ballot notice) rather than a corrective tag, materially neutralized it and preserved A's forged-evidence framing.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): A made the stronger comparative case: deterrence can reduce deceptive impersonation before circulation, while provenance and rebuttal leave important election-eve vulnerabilities. A also directly answered the institutional pressure with independent adjudication and procedural safeguards; B largely repeated that state involvement necessarily means incumbent control. B exposed genuine chilling risks and uncertainty about enforcement, but its central disclosure dilemma did not survive A's distinction between upfront disclosure and subsequent correction. A's shifting definition and incomplete showing of additional deterrence beyond existing law keep this a competitive rather than decisive win.

A parody safe harbor therefore does not contradict A's criticism of delayed labeling. B's final claim that this merely regulates framing did not defeat that distinction.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): PRO began strongly. It framed deepfakes as "forged evidence," argued that past satire relied on visible artifice, and tried to turn the liar's-dividend point back on CON. Its Q2 also forced a real symmetry problem: forgers ignore fraud law too. CON handled that pressure more effectively. Its A1 drew a clear line: fraud liability punishes a proven wrong with intent and injury, while the ban polices how authentic a depiction looks. Its A2 answered the marginal-value question. The determined foreign or anonymous forger is not deterred by either rule, so the ban's added reach falls mainly on satirists and anonymous critics. CON also landed "a statute is not a watermark" repeatedly and well. That neutralized PRO's claim that illegality restores the presumption that recordings are real. PRO's case was weakened on execution: - Two of its turns were clipped for length, losing content. - Its closing attributed to CON a concession that no process is fast enough, when PRO itself made that admission in A4.1. - It drifted from "deployed to deceive" toward an "objective at publication" test. CON exploited that shift as overbreadth. PRO's deter-before-release frame was its best route to victory. CON squarely contested it, arguing that the ban deters only the reachable while the emergency verification problem stays the same. That leaves CON with the cleaner comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over Q2 and the timing frame. PRO argued that a ban works before release, unlike after-the-fact liability. CON answered (B4.3–B4.5, B5.1) that a ban cannot reach foreign or anonymous interferers either. Both regimes therefore face the same race after release, and only PRO's adds suppression of satire and a removal tool incumbents can use against genuine leaks. That undercut PRO's main claimed advantage.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B won a close, high-quality exchange. A's core route — the 48-hour confession clip, the concession that narrow fraud liability should reach it, and the timing squeeze that only ex ante deterrence works — was sharp and well-crystallized in the closing. But B neutralized it with two decisive moves: (1) the distinction between fraud liability (proven false assertion, intent, concrete injury) and banning a medium/realism level is substantive, changing burden, timing, defenses, and who self-censors; and (2) the non-compliance asymmetry — a statute cannot deter the foreign/anonymous forger who drives the emergency, so both worlds face the same post-release race while only A's adds certain suppression of lawful satire and anonymous dissent. B also landed the strongest counter to A's trust argument ("a statute is not a watermark": incumbents can still demand removal of genuine leaks pending review). A's symmetry question (fraudsters ignore fraud law too) was A's best pressure, but B's answer — non-compliance defeats A's claimed pre-release advantage, not law itself, since B relies on layered tools rather than deterrence alone — held. A additionally suffered execution costs: both the opening and Rebuttal 2 were clipped, cutting off the exemptions detail and final cost framing, while all of B's turns were complete and disciplined.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3–B4.5: answering A's non-compliance question, B conceded liability won't stop every final-hour clip but argued this defeats only A's claimed advantage, since A needs the ban to work before authentication while B's layered tools (verification, removal, provenance) address the same emergency — so if both worlds race after release, only A's adds suppression of satire; and "declaring a file illegal is not a watermark" because statutory elements cannot authenticate a genuine leak in real time, blunting A's trust-restoration claim.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B won the comparative policy clash. A persuasively established that fabricated recordings can threaten elections, but did not establish why its ban would deter the decisive forgery better than B’s targeted fraud liability. B directly answered the noncompliance challenge and exposed A’s false choice between preventive deterrence and punishment afterward: both laws can deter, and both face authentication delays. A’s statutory safeguards also failed to answer the risk of genuine leaks being suppressed during review. B therefore supplied the cleaner reason why the ban’s additional speech costs were not justified.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s “a statute is not a watermark” rebuttal defeated A’s claim that illegality restores trust. After A conceded that timely authentication was unavailable, B showed that statutory elements cannot authenticate a disputed recording and later liability cannot recover a lost news cycle.
