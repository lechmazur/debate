### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B wins by forcing A to concede that capital is fungible and passive money can clear. A's remaining residual-control/network case was then dismantled: rights matter only when exercised as conduct, pro-rata rights are capital, operating help is conduct, and customer introductions are either domestic or already covered. A never identified a scarce capability that travels only through equity, while B's direct conduct-rule alternative and "empty chair" point left screening as a costly proxy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 turned A's residual-control argument into a liability: a Western veto inside a rival firm is our leverage, not a capability transfer, and a vote does not etch a wafer. The piece-by-piece breakdown of pro-rata rights, customer introductions, and operating partners was decisive.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B won the comparative-policy clash. A plausibly distinguished investment relationships from fungible cash, but its eventual defense depended on conduct rules being unable to condition involvement or reach funds. B directly challenged both premises, leaving A without a convincing advantage for transaction screening over targeted conduct restrictions. B's excessive demands for an equity-exclusive capability, overstated substitution claims, and repeated clipping keep this a competitive rather than dominant win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A argued that screening uniquely addresses fund-level governance rights while permitting conditional approval. B answered that conduct controls already permit conditional licences and can bind funds as legal persons: governance rights deliver assistance through their exercise, which those rules can restrict. This undercut A's central claimed regulatory gap.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a high-level debate by systematically narrowing and dismantling Side B's (PRO's) theory of strategic harm. Side B argued well on director fiduciary duties and ex-ante conditioning of deal terms, but its core answer—residual control and fund-level alignment—collapsed under Side A's scrutiny. Side A showed that roadmap vetoes and information rights sit with the Western investor rather than building wafers for the rival, that direct conduct rules can bind funds as legal persons to leave board rights an "empty chair," and that financial alignment applies equally to the passive capital Side B lets through. Side A incurred a small execution cost for getting clipped at the word cap three times, though only the final words were lost, leaving a clear comparative victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side A's pressure question forced Side B to identify an equity-only transfer in [B4.1] ("residual control" via board votes, customer/roadmap vetoes, information rights, and pro-rata rights), Side A dismantled the list in [A4.4]-[A4.9] and [A5.2]-[A5.4]: vetoes sit in Western hands rather than etching wafers, pro-rata rights are just fungible capital again, barring conduct by funds and individuals turns vested board rights into an empty chair, and blaming "alignment" contradicts letting passive money clear.
