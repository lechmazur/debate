### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): CON framed the motion well. It anchored on the retaliation clause and built a coherent "pay twice" story: dearer inputs at home, counter-tariffs abroad. Its price-taker split was also clever. Bulk metals reroute, so EU buyers eat the charge, while specialized EU exports become the targets of counter-tariffs. PRO, however, handled the pressure more directly and turned more of CON's material. On incidence, PRO's A2 was precise. Discounting cuts foreign margins while customs still collects the full per-ton fee. CON's reply simply restated the "treadmill" without engaging that mechanism. PRO's Q1 forced CON to name alternatives: free allocation, equivalence deals, and raw-materials-only coverage. PRO's closing then dismantled each. Free allocation guts domestic pricing. Equivalence leaves non-pricing emitters untouched. Raw-materials-only coverage creates the worst downstream squeeze on European assemblers. PRO also turned CON's bulk-rerouting point into an argument for broad coverage. CON's closing partly misattributed the bulk-rerouting claim to PRO; it was CON's own point. That weakened its "PRO can't have it both ways" punch. PRO's claim that retaliation stays "bounded" remained largely asserted. That is CON's best live route, which keeps this competitive. Still, PRO left the cleaner comparative choice: bounded friction versus forced retreat.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing response to CON's A1 alternatives decided the debate. It showed that the narrower raw-materials-only option worsens the assembler squeeze CON had been emphasizing. It also used CON's own "fungible bulk cargoes" point to argue that only broad coverage closes the leakage. This neutralized CON's scope objection, which was its main route to victory.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Both sides were disciplined, well-grounded, and answered pressure honestly, but PRO held the stronger overall path. B's core mechanism — crediting foreign carbon prices so partners can keep revenue at home — explained why retaliation is bounded and temporary rather than merely asserting it, and A never squarely engaged this off-ramp. B's closing also dismantled A's alternative-policy escape route (free allocation, narrow scope, equivalence) one by one, turning A's own "fungible cargoes" point into a case for broad coverage. A fought well: the goods-differentiation rebuttal (A4.4) cleverly resolved B's contradiction pressure, and the closing dilemma ("either demand matters enough to fight over or polluters don't pay") was the sharpest moment of the debate. But A's escalation and incidence arguments were blunted by B's self-interest cap and the per-ton collection point, and A left B's off-ramp and closing demolition unanswered. Clear but competitive edge to B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Q2 caught a real tension between foreign sellers absorbing the charge and revenue funding compensation; B's A2 answer — the fee is charged per ton of embedded carbon, not on margins, so discounting shrinks exporter profits while customs still collects — was the cleanest escape of the round, though A's later commodity-vs-specialized distinction (A4.4) kept the incidence pressure alive and set up the closing dilemma.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): A sustains the stronger comparative case: charging raw materials while exempting finished goods shifts the competitive disadvantage downstream. B repeatedly emphasizes input costs without adequately answering why narrower coverage avoids that problem. A also answers the pressure questions directly, distinguishing retaliation from unlimited escalation and tax incidence from revenue collection. B’s closing largely repeats false dichotomies rather than overcoming those distinctions. A overstates the certainty that retaliation remains temporary and industrial decline follows rejection, keeping the result competitive, but provides the clearer mechanism and stronger responses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3 directly defeats B’s compensation challenge: exporter discounts reduce foreign margins, not the carbon fee collected per ton. B subsequently returns to the fact that EU importers remit the charge, which does not establish that they bear its entire economic burden.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): CON wins because it held PRO to the motion's actual scope, "most imported industrial goods," and that pressure never went away. PRO kept defending a border adjustment as though it applied to bulk steel and cement. Its recurring answer, that the adjustment adds no cost because domestic steel already pays the ETS price, does not cover electronics, machinery, and chemicals. It also does nothing for European exporters competing abroad. CON's "double squeeze" argument (pricier inputs at home plus counter-tariffs abroad) was only half-answered. PRO showed the levy follows goods into the EU market, but CON correctly replied that export-oriented assembly can relocate to serve non-EU markets. On retaliation, PRO leaned on the motion's "even if" wording as if that settled whether the costs are worth bearing. CON answered the veto question cleanly: purely domestic tools give partners no pretext to retaliate. CON also tied retaliation to concrete transition harms, namely restricted critical minerals and batteries. PRO's best line was that contracts for difference leave foreign carbon unpriced and only socialize the loss. That was a real hit, but CON's A1 gave a plausible reply: closing the carbon cost premium removes foreign mills' advantage inside the EU. PRO's second rebuttal was clipped before the judge saw all of it, which is an execution cost. Its closing was clear but mostly reasserted the CfD and veto framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.2/A4.4 against B2.2/B4.1. CON showed that PRO's "domestic steel already pays" defense does not extend to most industrial goods. It also showed that a border levy offers no protection in export markets, leaving PRO's claim that the policy prevents industrial hollowing-out unresolved.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a competitive, well-fought debate. B's core frame held up across all rounds: the comparator is the status quo where leakage compounds, retaliation is the motion's explicit premise, and the only instrument that prices the imported tonne rather than subsidizing the domestic one is the border adjustment. B's sharpest material move was the input-cost symmetry argument (the adjustment adds no cost beyond what domestic producers already bear, it merely closes the foreign exemption), which blunted A's "double squeeze" at home, and the veto logic, which A never fully escaped — A's answer that domestic policies provide "no pretext" for retaliation only covers the EU's own rules, not the broader leverage question. B's pressure Q1 on CfDs was excellent and forced A into a subsidy-forever position B successfully weighed in closing. A's best live route was the export-market squeeze (relocation of export-oriented assembly escapes both EU input levies and counter-tariffs) and the enforceability of anti-shuffling "consistency requirements" — B4.1's claim that relocation "changes which customs authority collects" only covers goods entering the EU market, and A4.4 exposed that gap. But A's industrial-flight and critical-minerals-restriction scenarios were more speculative cascades, and B's retort that all-or-nothing leakage standards would also defeat the ETS A accepts was effective weighing. B's Rebuttal 2 was clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real but minor execution cost since nearly all content survived. B made the debate easier to decide and controlled the decisive clash: polluter-pays at the border versus open-border subsidies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q1 on carbon contracts for difference — asking how a producer subsidy stops a foreign mill from undercutting the funded plant — forced A into conceding a permanent public payment (A4.1), which B converted into the closing's decisive weighing: a CfD leaves every imported tonne unpriced and the payment never ends, while the adjustment makes foreign decarbonization the cheaper route into the EU market.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won by making the breadth of “most imported industrial goods” consequential: protecting upstream producers can squeeze downstream exporters through higher input costs and retaliatory tariffs. B also explained how contracts for difference address the carbon-cost disadvantage, while A repeatedly asserted that subsidies cannot prevent undercutting without answering that mechanism. A treated “even if” too much as permission to dismiss retaliation rather than a burden to justify enduring it. B overstated near-zero emissions benefits and inevitable conflict, but its concrete comparative case survived substantially better.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B decisively answered A’s claim that relocating assembly cannot escape the border levy: factories serving American and Asian markets can relocate outside Europe and avoid both EU input levies and retaliation against EU-origin exports. A’s answer covered sales into Europe, not this export-oriented relocation, and its closing never repaired the gap.
