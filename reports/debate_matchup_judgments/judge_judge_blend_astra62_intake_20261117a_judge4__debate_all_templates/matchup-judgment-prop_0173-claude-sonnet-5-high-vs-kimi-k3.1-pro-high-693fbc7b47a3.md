  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.3/B4.1 - electric modularity is meaningless if aircraft cannot fly the resolution's routes, and demanding per-dollar precision from the working technology while the alternative has no product is not a tie but a forfeit on cost-effectiveness.

Side A relentlessly and correctly pointed out that an audited invoice is not a cost-effectiveness analysis, and that PRO never actually compared emissions or time saved per dollar. Side A also successfully defended the modularity of aviation investment against HSR's irreversible sunk costs, exposing the fatal logical gap in PRO's comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it explicitly dismantles Side B's claim that "showing homework" (audited rail costs) satisfies the burden of proving cost-effectiveness, pointing out that PRO only provided the denominator (cost) without establishing the numerator (comparative emissions and time savings per dollar).

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by effectively turning Side A's "capital risk profile" framing against them. Side A argued brilliantly that rail is too financially risky and electric planes offer a bounded, cheap failure mode. However, Side B pointed out that a cheap failure delivers zero emissions or time savings, perfectly neutralizing Side A's cost-effectiveness calculus.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing crystallization ("Cheap failure is not cheap success") brilliantly dismantled Side A's argument that electric aviation is better simply because its prototypes can be cancelled before billions are sunk.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON won the cost-effectiveness burden. A kept the debate on US delivery risk: California near $33B → multiples, Texas Central zero ground broken, holding PRO's "deployable now" [B1.1] to admit technology ≠ built corridor and no dollars-per-tonne figure [B4.3]. PRO's battery-weight physics and door-to-door friction points were strong, but CON conceded no fleet at scale [A4.1] and reframed to bounded downside vs sunk mega-project failure, while showing secondary fields like Love/Midway already exist. PRO never produced a US HSR success counter-example and its R1 was clipped, leaving CON's live route of incremental, cancellable fleet upgrades on existing runways vs open-ended ROW litigation intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Q1 on "deployable now" — forcing B to concede California/Texas risks and redefine deployable as proven technology not built line [B4.1] — and B's Q2 on existing vs hypothetical airfields, which exposed A's lighter-security claim as selective infrastructure counting.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively grounding the "cost-effectively" burden in the reality of US infrastructure delivery. By repeatedly highlighting the massive cost overruns and delays of the only two major US high-speed rail attempts (California and Texas Central), A severely damaged B's case. B struggled to counter this, admitting a lack of concrete cost figures for rail and relying on theoretical efficiency. A's reframe of cost-effectiveness as a comparison of capital risk profiles, culminating in the powerful closing argument that rail's failure mode is an "open-ended liability" rather than a "cheap failure," decisively won the central clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in Rebuttal 2 and Closing to Side B's "cheap failure" argument. A successfully turned B's claim that a cancelled aircraft prototype is a bounded downside against B, pointing out that rail's actual failure mode in the US is the most expensive failure on the table, already realized twice with tens of billions spent.
