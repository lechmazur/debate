### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins a clear but competitive debate. CON (A) opened with three reasonable attacks: the internet is only one layer of a stack, "high-quality" is a moving target, and enforceability invites rigidity, gaming and litigation. PRO answered each one directly in Rebuttal 1. Its strongest reply was the prerequisite point: a water supply does not guarantee a kettle either. It also gave a credible mechanism that defuses the litigation fear, namely a statutory duty plus a regulator that updates the floor, with courts only as a backstop. CON's Rebuttal 1 largely restated its opening. Its claim that PRO "retreats to the pipe" mischaracterizes PRO's position, since PRO always defended supply rather than outcomes. CON's best moment came in Rebuttal 2, where it described multi-year tenders with penalty bonds, clawbacks and licence conditions. That was a genuine answer to PRO's Q1 about who guarantees the hardest-to-reach. However, it implicitly concedes that consequence-backed obligations are desirable. It also never answered PRO's sharp dilemma that vouchers and builds also deliver only a pipe. PRO's closing then crystallized the remaining difference: contracts leave the final household negotiable, while a right removes that negotiation. Both sides answered the pressure questions with proper A1/A2 labels. PRO made the debate easier to decide and turned more of CON's lines against it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B4.4 answering CON's "bare pipe" pressure (A3.1/A2.2). PRO argued that if guaranteeing the pipe is pointless because devices and literacy are missing, then CON's own vouchers and builds, which also mainly deliver a pipe, are equally pointless. If the pipe is a valuable foundation, the hardest-to-reach deserve it as a duty. CON never addressed this turn and simply repeated the "pipe" concession claim in its closing.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won clearly by defending a realistic regulatory model of universal service and systematically dismantling Side A's core disanalogies. Side B established that once schooling, healthcare, benefits, and employment assume home broadband, leaving the hardest-to-reach households to market cherry-picking or discretionary procurement makes modern citizenship negotiable. Side A (CON) raised a credible public-choice concern that binding "high-quality" floors invite lobbying for low minimums and legal disputes, and pivoted smartly to enforceable build tenders in [A4.1]. However, Side B blunted the rigidity critique by explaining how utility regulators update standards with phased compliance and judicial backstops, exposed that discretionary tenders still leave the costliest households unprotected when budgets tighten, and completely defeated Side A's over-extended "pipe versus stack" critique.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's refutation and turn of the "pipe versus stack" argument across [B2.1-B2.2] and [B4.1-B4.4]: Side B first showed that water and electricity mandates likewise guarantee only the utility feed rather than taps, kettles, or fridges because supply is the prerequisite for every downstream layer, and then pointed out in [B4.4] that if guaranteeing "only the pipe" were pointless without devices and literacy, Side A's own vouchers and build contracts—which also deliver mainly a pipe—would be equally pointless. Side A never escaped this internal contradiction.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins because it kept the motion's actual choice clearer: a standing, regulator-updated duty to reach the last household, with courts only as a backstop, versus access that remains scoped to tenders, budgets, and profitable builds. Side A landed a real design critique of a justiciable "high-quality" standard and improved its alternative with contracts, levies, and penalty bonds, but never showed those tools bind future governments the way a universal-service duty does, and its stack attack was answered by the prerequisite logic that already governs water and electricity. B's pipe dilemma and closing on the negotiable last household therefore remained the cleaner reason to vote for the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: it answered that the right guarantees the usable pipe, not every device or clinic, then flipped the attack by noting that if a pipe is worthless, Side A's own builds and vouchers are worthless too, while if it is a foundation, the hardest-to-reach deserve it as a duty rather than a residual of profit.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins on the strength of a dilemma it built early and kept sharp: a broadband right either guarantees a technical input at the premises, which dates or simply duplicates regulation, or it guarantees real-world outcomes. No access provider controls those outcomes, because devices, home Wi-Fi, platforms and congestion all intervene. PRO (B) answered with a "functional floor" updated by regulators. That is a reasonable move. But A2.2 landed a pointed follow-up: if courts defer to regulators, the right adds little beyond regulation except lawsuits about deference. PRO never fully answered that. On opportunity cost, PRO said the money comes from separate telecom levies and spectrum revenue. CON pointed out that users still pay those levies and that spectrum money is fungible public money. PRO's "No" to the priority question then sat awkwardly beside its claim that a legal duty "cannot be quietly cancelled." PRO's best weapon was CON's clinic concession. CON answered it directly and plausibly: there is a principled difference between the state's own finite facilities and a universal private entitlement. CON also handled the court-hearing question cleanly by attaching the remedy to the proceeding itself (postponement, telephone or in-person participation, a connected public room). PRO's later speeches largely restated its "essential needs enforceable protection" theme and the clinic point without adapting much. CON's later speeches narrowed the clash and adapted to each answer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A4.3 against B4.1. PRO limited its duty to access delivered "to the premises, like electricity at the meter." CON turned that limit into its dilemma: a line can meet the specification yet still yield an unusable telehealth call. So the right either guarantees a dating technical input or an outcome the provider cannot control. PRO's closing restated the functional floor and the voltage analogy without resolving this.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a clear, competitive debate by showing that converting household high-quality internet into an individually justiciable universal right creates enforceability dilemmas, bad last-mile sequencing, and regressive opportunity costs against basic needs like water, power, and clinics. Side B (PRO) framed the citizenship stakes vividly and smartly used a premises-demarcation analogy to parry end-to-end device objections, but relied on an economically flawed "separate ledger" defense of spectrum receipts and telecom levies and contradicted its own case for un-cancellable legal priority when pressed on trade-offs with water and power.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In Turn 8 ([A4.1]-[A4.4]), Side A cleanly dismantled Side B's rural video-hearing trap—arguing the state owes an enforceable remedy for the court proceeding itself rather than universal home broadband—while exposing Side B's contradiction in claiming its right cannot be cut when budgets tighten yet does not legally outrank competing essentials, alongside the reality that spectrum funds are fungible public money and telecom levies fall on users.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won by forcing the motion’s actual instrument: a justiciable high-quality household right “like water.” PRO’s moral case and premises-level refrigerator analogy were strong, but they never closed the gap between a dating technical floor and outcome guarantees no access provider controls. CON answered both pressure questions with a sharper alternative—enforce the hearing, clinic, and school the state controls, plus adjustable household help—and showed separate levies do not erase scarcity or sequencing costs. That made the vote easier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After PRO confined the duty to a premises connection, CON showed that either collapses back into rigid, dating specs or fails to secure the telehealth, school, and court outcomes PRO used to justify the right; the hearing remedy then fit the state’s actual control better than a universal home entitlement.
