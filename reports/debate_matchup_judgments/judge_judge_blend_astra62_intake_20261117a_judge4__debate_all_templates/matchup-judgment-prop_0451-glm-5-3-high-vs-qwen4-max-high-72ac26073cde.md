- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built a cleaner, harder-to-escape route. Its core rests on three linked moves. The 'posture decides defaults' answer was plausible but somewhat abstract. B's closing then turned CON's named trigger, coercive input control, into a self-defeating late flip. CON's reply that dependency is a slope was reasonable but came last and did not fully restore its case. Overall B controlled the framing and narrowed the clash more effectively.

CON named coercive input control such as graphite licensing. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won by building several routes to victory that stayed live after the exchange, while PRO's routes were each answered. CON's surviving routes: - **Flow versus stock.** Gas is a flow that can be cut; panels and batteries are durable goods already owned. This undercut PRO's Russian-gas analogy. - **Predation needs recoupment.** PRO's own evidence of overcapacity, bankruptcies and falling prices shows no cartel collecting its winnings. - **Irreversibility.** Emissions are cumulative and permanent; factories can be rebuilt later. In the closing, CON used this to flip PRO's risk-asymmetry argument. - **Instrument mismatch.** CON showed that tariffs on finished cars cannot reach upstream refining leverage. PRO's weaknesses: - Its sharpest attack was that CON's toolkit (anti-subsidy duties, local content, joint ventures) amounts to threat framing. But PRO then said the sides "differ only on" honesty of labeling, which concedes that the motion turns on a label. CON exploited this retreat. It also showed that conditioned access only works if imports keep flowing, which is CON's premise rather than PRO's. - PRO's rare-earths example was its strongest evidence, but CON turned it as upstream leverage that duties on finished goods cannot touch. - PRO's answer on how a dearer car cuts cumulative emissions faster stayed vague: "substitution dynamics" and learning-curve hopes. Both sides answered pressure questions directly with A1:/A2: labels. CON's answers were more concrete and more damaging. PRO's closing risk frame was memorable, but it rested on treating slower deployment as recoverable, and CON directly contested that point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The rare-earths exchange decided the debate. PRO answered CON's recoupment challenge with China's 2010 export curbs, its strongest evidence. CON (A4.3) turned it: that leverage sits in refining and magnets, which a tariff on a finished BYD does nothing to address. CON added that the leverage was later eroded by diversification and substitution. This showed that PRO's threat-first tools miss the very danger PRO identified, while spending irreversible emissions to do so.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): A wins because its irreversibility weighing survived the exchange: B treated slower deployment as recoverable, but A repeatedly showed foregone cumulative emissions are not recoverable, and B never supplied a mechanism by which dearer cars cut emissions faster. A also defused B's recoupment evidence by showing rare-earths/polysilicon leverage is upstream, not reached by finished-car tariffs, and turned B's 'your toolkit is threat architecture' into an opportunity premise: conditioned access only transfers technology if imports keep arriving.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it conceded rare earths were B's strongest evidence, then showed that leverage sits upstream where a finished-EV tariff does nothing, and that conditioned access requires continued imports—neutralizing B's best support while reframing B's own instruments.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by keeping the motion's "primarily" distinction live and turning Side B's best material against it. B's claim that the practical agendas were cosmetic ceded the framing clash, and A showed that conditioned access and joint ventures only work if imports continue. A's inversion of the risk ledger—permanent emissions versus recoverable factories—and the point that rare-earth leverage is upstream of a finished-car tariff left B without a mechanism by which threat-first policy cuts cumulative emissions faster.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal and closing: rare earths were accepted as real leverage, then shown to sit in refining rather than finished EVs, while B's own instruments were shown to presuppose continued traffic and to spend irreversible tonnes on a hedge they do not reach.
