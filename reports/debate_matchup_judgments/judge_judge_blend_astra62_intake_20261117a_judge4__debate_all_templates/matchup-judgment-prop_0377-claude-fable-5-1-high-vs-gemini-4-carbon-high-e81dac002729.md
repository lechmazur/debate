### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins a clear but competitive debate. PRO opened strongly on unearned windfalls and loopholes, but CON’s early-life scale rebuttal survived the exchange: millions of high-earning households use taxed income during formative years to buy school districts, tutoring, debt-free degrees, and networks, and no inheritance tax can confiscate that credential. PRO never squarely neutralized the scale and timing challenge, instead reframing the metric while leaving CON’s mass-scale bottleneck largely intact. CON also answered the donor-gift and stepped-up basis pressure directly, and turned PRO’s strongest “outlasts the paycheck” line into evidence that high-income childhood advantage, not a late untaxed estate, is the live starting-line threat.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s decisive turn was that the lasting advantage from high-earner families is the credential, network, and schooling already locked into a child’s résumé before inheritance taxes can reach it, so aggressive estate taxation cannot equalize the starting line at the finish line.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side A won a close but clear debate. A's core asymmetry—salary taxed near half on effort versus inheritance largely untaxed on luck—survived the exchange, and the "head start versus rigged track" / "re-earned versus never re-earned" frame did real comparative work rather than just decorating it. A also landed the strongest pressure point (A3.1 on what funds megagifts and family offices) and converted B's answer into a weapon: B's claim that big gifts come mostly from first-generation fortunes let A argue in [A5.4] that every untaxed transfer becomes the dynasty B calls rare. B mounted a serious counter-case—inheritances arrive too late, positional bottlenecks are rationed by millions of salaries, tuition and networks are untaxable, illiquid employers get crushed—and B's closing distinction between unequal outcomes and unequal opportunity was its sharpest moment. But B never answered A's political-entrenchment mechanism (foundations, donations, board seats shaping the track itself), leaned on an unsupported empirical claim about most megagifts being first-generation, and its avoidance argument was blunted by A's point that the same mobility defeats B's preferred stepped-up-basis fix. A's clipped second rebuttal and occasionally assertive "high earners are the mobility story" claim were real but minor costs. B was consistently competitive; A made the debate easier to decide and controlled the weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The A3.1 pressure question over funding sources, followed by B4.1's "first-generation fortunes" answer and A's [A5.4] crystallization that untaxed transfers are precisely how dynasties are created, was the decisive exchange: it turned B's own factual claim into support for the motion and left B's "dynasties are rare" premise self-undermining.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won the motion’s central comparison: mass high-earner spending can ration scarce educational and career opportunities before inheritance arrives, while passive dynastic wealth does not itself exclude strivers from those opportunities. B also answered both pressure questions directly and distinguished basis reform from aggressive taxation of principal. A offered memorable arguments about compounding, unearned advantage, and loopholes, but never neutralized B’s scale and timing case; A’s closing also mischaracterized durable credentials and networks as an inherited capital stock.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to A’s gift-tax expansion and closing reframe was decisive: schooling, degrees, and networks can outlast a paycheck without becoming a taxable estate—"No gift or inheritance tax can confiscate a credential."

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): CON won by making the comparison concrete and keeping the burden on PRO. PRO conceded that professionals' children fill more residencies and clerkships; CON turned that into a decisive comparative harm: the actual doors closing for poor children are opened by income-funded preparation. PRO's broader appeal to capital ownership never matched that scale and often slid into taxing wealth generally. CON also answered the pressure questions directly, showing that the working-class doctor's salary is not the threat but what it buys for her children, and that relying on lifetime transfers/unrealized gains conceded the inheritance tax itself was not doing the main work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON converting PRO's admission that professionals' children "naturally fill more residencies and clerkships" into a live comparison: the heir outside the race does not block the lane, while the lawyer's child is standing in it.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won a close, high-quality debate by imposing a crisp decision metric — where does a poor child actually lose, counted in scarce seats — and repeatedly forcing Side A to answer "name the seat an heir takes." Side A's answers (donor seats, controlling stakes, cash-bought homes) were partially blunted by B's rejoinder that a controlling stake is not a seat anyone competes for. B also handled A's pressure questions honestly and turned them into strengths ("her rise is mobility; her children's preparation is the door closing"), while B's motion-mismatch point — that A's remedy requires a lifetime-transfer and wealth regime that does the real work, making the funeral levy either core-and-missing or a footnote — was never fully neutralized. A fought back well: the A1/A2 answers were direct, the "locked doors" reframe in the closing was a genuine save, and the anti-evasion analogy for unified gift/trust rules was solid. But A conceded professionals' children fill the seats, and B's closing crystallized that concession into a cleaner vote story. Neither side used fake citations; both grounded cases in plausible mechanisms. B made the debate easier to decide and answered the most important pressure more squarely.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B4.1-B4.2 answering A's pressure Q1: B conceded the salary itself is not the threat but argued the working-class climber's income later closes the door for the next generation, then weaponized A's own concession that professionals' children "naturally fill more residencies and clerkships" to define the entire opportunity gap in actual seats. A's closing flip — that no poor child can compete for capital precisely because dynastic wealth locked those doors — was a strong save but arrived late against an already-consolidated metric.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): B made the motion’s comparative burden decisive: professional income funds preparation for millions of children and determines who obtains scarce educational and professional opportunities, while A’s heirs often sit outside those competitions. A offered a compelling asset-lockout reframing and answered the anti-avoidance question reasonably well, but never established that inheritance specifically forecloses more opportunity than income-funded preparation. B also exposed A’s drift toward a broader wealth-tax regime without conclusively defeating the death-tax rationale.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first and second rebuttals turned A’s “outside the competition” language and concession that professionals’ children fill more residencies and clerkships into a direct challenge to A’s comparative case, leaving A to rely on a broader, less quantified theory of excluded ownership.
