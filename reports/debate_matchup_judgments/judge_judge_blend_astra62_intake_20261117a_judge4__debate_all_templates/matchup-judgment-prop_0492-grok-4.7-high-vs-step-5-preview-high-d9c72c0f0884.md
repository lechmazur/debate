### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): This was a close, high-quality exchange. CON wins narrowly. PRO built a coherent case on three points: the fiscal wedge, a dedicated funding source, and timing. Its A1 answer was its best moment. It offered a concrete trigger: new capital, compensated hours down in that function, output holding or rising. That test exempts vacancies and pure demand decline, and it noticeably narrowed CON's original "undefinable replacement" attack. PRO's A2 also blunted the revenue dilemma by limiting the tax base to events that cut paid hours. CON still kept two live routes standing at the end: - **Causation.** Paid hours can fall while output holds for reasons other than robots, such as product mix, outsourcing, or reorganization. Substitution can also be hidden across firms or functions. PRO never squarely answered this. It conceded that the line would "sometimes miss causation" and replied only that imperfect sight is no excuse. - **The direct alternative.** CON's counter-alternative was to fund adjustment directly through automatic, statutory eligibility. That serves the same goal without having to prove that a robot caused the job loss. PRO's timing argument depends on the tax identifying the injury year reliably, and CON exploited exactly that in its closing. PRO also carries an execution cost: its first rebuttal was clipped before the judge saw its comparative conclusion. CON's second rebuttal was somewhat repetitive. Even so, it made the decision easier: if the trigger misfires, the dedicated-funding advantage collapses, and direct support remains available.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came in Rebuttal 2. PRO's paid-hours/output test (A4.1–A4.2) was a strong, specific answer to the trigger problem. CON then turned that same test against PRO in B4.3. It argued that a before-and-after hours comparison still cannot establish causation, since demand shifts, product mix, outsourcing, or reorganization can produce the same pattern. That left PRO's timing case resting on an identification method PRO later admitted could miss.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B wins a competitive, high-level debate by proving that firm-level "replacement" cannot function as a clean tax trigger and by flipping Side A's closing decision rule on timing. Side A pivoted impressively in Rebuttal 2—upgrading its flawed "net hours" baseline to a joint test of new robotics, falling paid hours, and steady output while deftly answering Side B's revenue-deterrence dilemma by noting that one-time retraining costs are a fraction of shed payroll (a point Side B largely repeated past rather than refuted). However, Side B immediately exposed structural flaws in even that refined trigger: internal reorganization, product-mix shifts, and outsourcing reduce hours while output holds without robotic causation, whereas cross-firm and greenfield automation displace workers without triggering the tax at all. Side A had to concede this as "imperfect sight" in Closing, allowing Side B to seal the comparison: statutory automatic stabilizers funded from broad general taxes (including corporate profits, property, and consumption) pay out immediately upon worker separation without waiting on litigation over whether a robot caused the layoff. Side A also incurred a minor execution cost for a clipped first rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side B demolished Side A's initial "net hours" baseline in [B2.1]-[B2.2] for taxing demand-driven decline and Side A upgraded in [A4.1]-[A4.2] to taxing "paid hours down, output holding or rising," Side B decisively dismantled the upgraded trigger in [B4.3] and [B5.1]-[B5.4]—showing that outsourcing, reorganization, and cross-firm substitution break causation and make separation-triggered stabilizers faster and fairer than adjudicating robotic blame.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by answering the decisive definitional and funding pressures with a concrete test—new robotics, compensated hours down, output holding or rising—and a proportional levy limited to a fraction of the shed payroll for only those displaced workers. That defused the vacancy objection and narrowed Side B's small-versus-large dilemma, while Side B's general-revenue alternative never squarely solved A's timing and funding-competition challenge. Side B showed the test is imperfect on causation, but not that imperfection outweighs a timely, targeted fund.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: its A1 paid-hours/output test exempted vacancies and demand decline, and its A2 answer made revenue scale with the payroll obligation actually shed rather than requiring the tax to fund the whole safety net.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): CON wins clearly. From the opening, CON framed the decisive problem: the tax base is "buying machines" or "labor cost avoided," not harm. It then pressed that point consistently. PRO's main responses were a pollution analogy, a "labor cost avoided" redesign, and a late exit charge. CON took each apart. Pollution is separable from the product, while displacement is the productivity gain itself. A wage-bill or hours trigger taxes contraction and is easy to game through hiring freezes and contractors. An exit charge concedes that the levy disappears exactly when a town needs it. CON also answered PRO's pressure questions directly, naming income, consumption, and corporate profit taxes, and gave a reason they are fairer: automation's benefits spread across consumers and other firms. PRO's strongest line was the moral "subsidy to capital / make the beneficiary pay" framing, which stays rhetorically live. However, PRO never solved the administrability or collection-at-closure problems. PRO also falsely claimed CON was "silent on the departing employer." Its closing misread [B2.5], which CON corrected. PRO's opening was generic and its second rebuttal underused the word limit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 response to PRO's A1/A2 settles the debate. Anchoring the levy to wage-bill, hours, or headcount cuts "taxes contraction," while "same crew, same hours" becomes a loophole. The exit charge "admits the levy collects nothing when the town needs it most." This turned PRO's own redesign into a concession.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B won solidly by proving the proposed tax fails both as an administrable boundary and as a reliable revenue source, while offering a superior fiscal alternative. When Side A tried to rescue the tax base by anchoring it to observable drops in wage bills, hours, or headcount [A4.1] plus an exit charge [A4.2], Side B showed this creates a fatal dilemma: keeping nominal crews while freezing hiring or using contractors collects nothing, whereas taxing falling hours penalizes ordinary contraction and lost contracts, and exit penalties deter local modernization while failing against insolvent closures [B4.5, B4.6]. Side B also neutralized Side A's core fairness claim by noting that funding transition aid through broad taxes—including corporate profit taxes [B4.3, B4.4]—captures genuine automation windfalls without an auditor guessing what caused a drop in hours, and survives local shutdowns. Side A further hurt its closing by inverting [B2.5] in [A5.2], which Side B cleanly exposed [B5.3].
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B4.3]-[B4.6], Side B neutralized Side A's windfall-fairness attack by showing that corporate profit taxes already capture successful automation gains without auditors sorting robots from lost contracts, while dismantling Side A's redesigned triggers ([A4.1]-[A4.2]) as a contraction tax paired with an evasion-prone plant-closing penalty.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B won the mechanism clash. PRO's moral accountability frame never survived the definition problem: taxing wage-bill, hours, or headcount cuts taxes contraction and productivity, is evaded by hiring freezes and contractors, and collects nothing when a plant closes. B answered both pressure questions directly with durable general-revenue and profit-tax funding, while PRO's late exit charge conceded the collection failure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it showed PRO's observable trigger taxes contraction rather than robots, creates a same-crew loophole, and that the exit charge admits the levy disappears exactly when the town needs support.
