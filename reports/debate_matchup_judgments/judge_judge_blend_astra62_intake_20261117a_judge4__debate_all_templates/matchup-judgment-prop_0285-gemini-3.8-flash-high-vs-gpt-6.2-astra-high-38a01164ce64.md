### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins clearly. Its central move was to separate loneliness from isolation and access. That let it accept that the widow should get her bus while arguing that the bus is justified as a mobility service, not as a loneliness intervention. CON also reframed the burden as the added value of making loneliness itself a top-tier priority. PRO never met that burden. PRO's opening was vivid and built a real structural case. Its later speeches leaned on assertions that went unsupported: - It claimed that "only" a health mandate protects off-peak buses and community hubs. CON answered that a mobility guarantee does the same work. - It claimed that ignoring loneliness costs more downstream, but gave no reason to think loneliness programs specifically prevent those costs. PRO's A1 dodged the tradeoff by saying acute care would "wait, or rather diminish." CON pressed this effectively: a budget must balance this year, not against hoped-for future savings. PRO's closing misdescribed CON's "even granting" as a concession of the physiological toll, and it repeated earlier rhetoric rather than answering the mobility-guarantee alternative. CON adapted across rounds, narrowed the clash to the word "only," and gave the cleaner decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: On PRO's A2 claim that social referrals prevent readmissions, CON pointed out that the patient who misses follow-up needs a ride whether lonely or surrounded by friends. Practical support explains the benefit, and adding a loneliness framing adds nothing shown. This neutralized PRO's main cost-offset route.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): A won because it supplied a cleaner comparative case: the widow can be helped through an ordinary mobility guarantee without reclassifying loneliness as a top-tier chronic-health priority. B leaned heavily on the claim that only a health mandate would protect buses and social infrastructure, but A showed a direct statutory duty to provide off-peak access could do the same work without requiring anyone to become less lonely. A also exposed B's weakest causal link by showing social referrals may merely supply practical support, such as rides, rather than treating loneliness itself.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's discharged-patient rebuttal: arrange the ride whether the patient feels lonely or has a house full of friends. That separated practical support from loneliness reduction and undermined B's readmissions argument as evidence for a loneliness-specific public-health mandate.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by keeping the debate focused on the proposition’s key demand: not merely whether loneliness is harmful or whether public services can help, but whether loneliness itself must become a top-tier public-health priority. B consistently argued that practical barriers can be addressed through ordinary public obligations, such as mobility access, without making loneliness the target, and it pressed A to show what additional preventable harm the priority status would avert. A was forceful and vivid, especially on structural causes and downstream clinical costs, but its central claim that only a health mandate can protect social infrastructure was effectively answered by B’s point that governments can directly guarantee access if they choose. B’s rebuttals were cleaner, more disciplined, and better at resolving the comparative burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s final rebuttal and closing dismantled A’s “only a public-health mandate protects the bus” argument by showing that a direct mobility or access guarantee could protect the same practical benefit without requiring loneliness itself to become a top-tier health target.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by building a concrete, well-bounded mechanism and defending it under pressure. Its core claim is that loneliness falls through the cracks when separate agencies each meet their own targets. The bus that leaves before respite arrives illustrates this, and the remedy is a standing budget and a responsible body judged by anonymous, aggregate self-reports. PRO also answered both pressure questions directly. It explained that accountability needs no clinical screening or registry, and it named a clear limit on state action. CON's strongest move was the closing dilemma: either the motion is trivialized into scheduling, or emotional life gets medicalized. However, CON's central claim, that priority status "inevitably" brings surveillance, stayed asserted rather than shown once PRO offered a non-intrusive accountability model. CON's answer that councils "already hold the duty" was neatly turned in PRO's closing as assuming the very priority at issue. CON also never engaged PRO's health link, the effects on sleep and depression. It treated PRO's limit on intervention as a fatal concession rather than as a principled boundary. Finally, it cited nonexistent reference IDs ([P1.1], [P4.3], and so on), a real execution cost. PRO's softening of "scale" (not tobacco-style instruments) gave CON some traction, but not enough to overturn PRO's cleaner comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal answered CON's surveillance question. It proposed anonymous before-and-after reporting, with published aggregate results and no screening or individual lists. It then showed that when both transit and respite hit their own targets, no one owns the remaining gap. That neutralized CON's main harm and exposed "ordinary management" as an unassigned duty.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): B defended a limited but real public-health role: removing structural barriers through standing budgets and accountable outcomes without surveilling private life or replacing affection. A's strongest attack was that B's examples were ordinary services and that B conceded the state cannot cure persistent loneliness itself. But B showed that only means public health should address access, not intimacy, and that ordinary coordination still requires a body with duty and funds. A's surveillance-registry scenario rested on inevitability that B directly answered with anonymous aggregate surveys, while A's fallback to existing municipal councils stayed vague.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it answered the surveillance concern by specifying anonymous before/after aggregate reports, and it turned A's "competent public management" answer into proof that no existing agency owns the loneliness gap when both transit and respite meet their own targets.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by giving a workable account of public-health priority: standing responsibility, budget, coordination, and voluntary self-reported outcomes. It answered the surveillance and limits pressure directly, while Side B’s medicalization objection kept attacking a more intrusive version of the PRO case and its alternative of ordinary local management did not explain why loneliness would get durable priority.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal defused the surveillance concern with anonymous aggregate feedback and drew a clear limit: public action removes accessible barriers to connection rather than replacing personal relationships, preserving the public-health role without medicalizing private life.
