### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides were competent and on-motion. PRO built a strong opening story: cyber conflict mostly hits private infrastructure, the military faces a talent drain, and nations need surge scale. CON won the exchange by narrowing the clash to reliance. The question became whether reserves can serve as the backbone or only as a supplement, and CON repeatedly showed that PRO's model depends on permanent institutions to function. PRO's A2 answer did real damage to its own case. It said civilian intelligence leadership holds the sources and controls escalation, while reservists act on compartmentalized indicators. CON turned that into a concession: PRO's reserve becomes a cleanup tier that depends on a standing, cleared command. CON's dilemma was cleanly stated and never fully escaped. Reservists at a struck utility who stay in place are "local staff renamed." Surge teams from vendors face unfamiliar systems and competing demands. PRO answered with assertions about vendor fluency and pre-negotiated compacts, but those compacts themselves require the permanent body CON described. PRO's best late move, "triage is organized abandonment," was memorable but somewhat unfair, since any force under nationwide attack must prioritize. PRO also leaned on unsupported claims, such as reservists activating "within minutes" and the military hoarding zero-days. CON answered both pressure questions directly. On Q1 it described pairing soldiers with local engineers under pre-authorized agreements. On Q2 it explained how a smaller force sustains operations through central sensing, automation, rotation, and prioritization. Overall, CON made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's [B4.3]–[B4.4] was the decisive exchange. It took PRO's A1/A2 answers and showed a two-way trap. If reservists stay at their own employer, they add a badge, not new defenders. If PRO relies on civilian leadership to hold intelligence and control escalation, it concedes that a permanent cleared command must already exist. This crystallized CON's "supplement, not backbone" frame, and PRO's closing reframed rather than refuted it.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a close, high-quality exchange. B grounded the motion in where cyber conflict actually occurs — private, standardized commercial infrastructure — and answered both pressure questions with concrete mechanisms (in-place liaisons, vendor surge, compartmentalized indicators under civilian intelligence leadership). B's standardization argument (hospitals and grids run shared clouds, directories, and industrial controllers that vendor reservists secure daily) undercut A's central "unfamiliar proprietary systems" barrier, and A never answered it beyond reassertion. A landed sharp blows — the instant/part-time trilemma, the "local staff renamed" dilemma, and the closing leverage of B's concession that permanent cleared institutions must exist — but B's reframing that expulsion and recovery IS the fight, plus the memorable "triage is organized abandonment" against A's exhaustion answer, kept B's route to victory intact while A's core factual premise was weakened. Both sides showed clean epistemic discipline and direct A1/A2 compliance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's secrecy dilemma and "outsiders confronting unfamiliar systems" attacks (A2.6, A4.3) against B's Rebuttal 2 (B4), where B answered both pressure questions directly and argued commercial stacks are standardized enough that vendor reservists are fluent where soldiers are not — a point A reasserted but never dismantled.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side A won the motion as written. The proposition demands reliance on civilian reserves instead of standing military units, and A showed that B's model still depends on permanent command, pre-negotiated access, classified judgment, and guaranteed availability that a part-time roster cannot supply. B made a strong case that attacks hit commercial infrastructure and that industry holds scarce fluency, but its stay-in-place and vendor-surge answers collapsed into either renamed local staff or outsiders with competing duties. A's closing converted that contradiction into a clear vote: reserves can widen a backbone, not replace it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal turned B's own answers against the proposition: if attacked-utility reservists stay put, mobilization adds coordination rather than new defenders, while vendor surge recreates unfamiliar-system and competing-demand problems, and compartmentalized indicators leave escalation to a permanent command the reserve does not provide.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Both sides were sharp and well grounded. A built a compelling frame: the attack surface is civilian, the talent sits in industry, and keeping society running is itself defense. B ultimately had the cleaner path to victory. Its double-counting attack forced A to retreat from "mass on demand" to stay-in-place reservists. A's two-pool repair was then answered persuasively: the deep benches A draws on (cloud, telecom, utilities, managed security providers) are themselves the hardest-hit choke points in a simultaneous attack. B also pressed the internal tension in A's "small cadre," which must defend military command, generate threat indicators, and coordinate tens of thousands of reservists. A's answer relied partly on allied intelligence and commercial telemetry, which B effectively labeled free-riding and post-breach detection. B further noted that A's coordination benefits could come from regulation and funding without replacing standing units, and A never squarely answered this. A's closing restated its frame well but did not resolve the cannibalization or cadre-capacity problems. B's air-defense versus firefighter analogy crystallized the weighing memorably.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3 countered A4.1's two-pool roster by showing that the "deployable" reservists come from cloud, telecom, utility, and security providers, which are the very choke points under simultaneous attack. No idle deep bench exists in week one, so the surge cannibalizes foundational infrastructure. A never adequately answered this.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won the decisive clashes. The double-counting attack on the reserve "surge" survived the exchange: B's two-pool fix (stay-in-place plus deployable teams from deep-bench firms) was directly answered by A4.3's point that cloud firms, telecoms, and utilities are the choke points hit hardest in the simultaneous nationwide attack B itself described, so no idle bench exists at midnight. A also exposed that B's model quietly depends on substantial standing capability — a "cadre" that produces classified indicators, defends military command, and coordinates tens of thousands — plus free-riding on allied intelligence, which undercuts the motion as written ("rather than rely on standing military units"). B's best line, that upstream success does not keep society running, was blunted by A's air-defense/firefighter analogy and by A's observation that states can fund municipal resilience and mandate sector coordination without hollowing out the military. B's original reframes (reserve authority versus day-job loyalty, the "spearhead and switchboard" division of labor) were genuinely strong, and B's B4.6 preemption of the "any cadre proves reliance" move was skillful, but B never fully resolved where the indicators, deterrence, and military-network defense come from after shrinking standing forces. Both sides showed excellent epistemic discipline, honored the Q/A formats, and crystallized well; A simply left the stronger live route intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3's counter to B's two-pool design: the "deployable" surge must be drawn from cloud firms, telecoms, and security providers — the exact infrastructure burning hardest in week one — so pulling those engineers to town halls cannibalizes the foundations every hospital and municipality depends on. This converted B's repair of the double-counting charge back into a live, unneutralized winning point for A.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A better resolved the decisive clash. The proposition requires preferring civilian reserves over reliance on standing military cyber units as the backbone. Side B’s surge-mass case shifted under pressure from broad mobilization to stay-in-place work and then a two-pool model, but never cleanly escaped double-counting. Side A showed that “deployable” reservists would be pulled from the same cloud, telecom, utility, and security providers already under simultaneous attack, while a reduced cadre could not sustain continuous upstream disruption, military-network defense, deterrence, and warning. Local recovery matters, yet that did not justify the substitution the motion demands.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal most decisively answered the two-pool fix: drawing deployable teams from cloud firms, telecoms, utilities, and security providers in week one would strip the foundational systems every hospital and municipality depends on, so the promised spare surge does not exist.
