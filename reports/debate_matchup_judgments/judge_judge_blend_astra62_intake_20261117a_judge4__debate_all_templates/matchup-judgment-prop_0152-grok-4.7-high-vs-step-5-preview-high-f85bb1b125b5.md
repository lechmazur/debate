### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO wins because it defended the proposition as written and turned its conditions into the decisive clash. CON's opening attacked how hard "genuinely voluntary" is to apply. PRO answered by conceding the hard cases (monopoly schools, visas, ruinous exits) and pointing out that the motion already excludes them. It also set an ex ante standard, "no shown alternative, no dress rule," with the burden placed on the institution. PRO's Q1 then forced CON to admit that no legitimate case exists even when exit is easy. That admission showed CON's real position is a categorical ban on dress expectations, not a critique of whether voluntariness can be tested. PRO exploited this cleanly in its closing. CON had real strengths. The "toll booth" framing was memorable. The child-consent argument had force. Its A2 answer, distinguishing curriculum as "content" from dress as "a mark on the body," was the best attempt to escape PRO's consistency trap. However, CON kept describing the test as one applied after signing and enforced by litigation, while PRO had explicitly framed it as fixed in advance and visible at the door. CON never fully grappled with that, so its procedural critique largely repeated its earlier claims. CON's final position also made the conditions in the motion irrelevant rather than defeating them, which made the debate easier to decide for PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Q1 and CON's A1. Asked to name one legitimate case, CON replied "There is no such clean case." This let PRO argue in its closing that CON had abandoned the voluntariness dispute for outright erasure, which neutralized CON's earlier critiques of how the exit conditions would work in practice.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won a high-level debate by defending the proposition's modest, conditional scope ("some room" only when alternatives are realistic) while exposing Side B's overreach into a categorical ban. However, Side A's Pressure Questions forced Side B to assert that no clean voluntary case exists anywhere in schooling or work—even for urban families beside strong public schools or adults holding comparable job offers—and to argue that dress expectations are illegitimate even when exit is easy. Side A persuasively showed why optional individual permission preserves only private display rather than shared communal practice, making strict limits cleaner than total erasure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Pressure Questions [A3.1-A3.2] and crystallization in [A5.1-A5.4] formed the decisive sequence. After neutralizing Side B's monopoly and visa examples in [A2.1-A2.2] ("CON keeps reciting our exclusions and calling them our rule"), Side A asked Side B to name one legitimate clean case and explain why imperfect options would not equally condemn prayer and confessional curriculum. Side B's claim in [B4.1] that no clean case exists even among comparable offers, paired with its admission in [B4.6] that it opposes dress expectations even when leaving is easy, let Side A cleanly frame CON's position as abolishing voluntary association rather than policing coercion.
