### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins clearly. PRO set a sharp burden: name a guest who is better off learning the real price later. PRO then dismantled CON's feasibility case by noting that checkout fees are computed from inputs the guest has already entered, and that the motion targets the listing page, not the search grid. CON's best argument was that variable fees make pre-checkout display inaccurate. That argument depended on search-page totals, and CON effectively conceded the point when its own pressure questions mirrored PRO's and asked what objection "remains." CON's later lines rested on unevidenced chains of second decisions by hosts, platforms, and moderators: anchoring to "from" figures, moderation tools stripping medical exceptions, and baked-in rate hikes. PRO correctly labeled these as compliance choices the motion does not require. CON also claimed "millions" in rebuild costs without support. CON's closing misdescribed the burden by flipping PRO's "no guest better off" line, and it introduced no new answer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B2.1 and B4.1 decided the debate. PRO pointed out that no new information arrives between the listing and checkout screens, so whatever produces the fee at checkout can produce it one screen earlier. Combined with the clarification that the motion targets the listing page rather than the search grid, this collapsed CON's core feasibility objection. CON never recovered from it.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A built a tight case around a simple principle — the number used to attract a click should be the number the guest owes — and capitalized on a fatal flaw in Side B's strategy: B's entire feasibility case attacked disclosure on the initial search grid, which the motion never requires. A's pressure questions exposed this precisely, and B's answers retreated into unevidenced speculation about misleading "from" figures, moderation systems stripping medical exceptions, and hosts inflating base rates. A repeatedly turned these back: each CON harm requires a second voluntary decision the motion doesn't mandate, while the harm on PRO's side occurs by default. B's pressure questions were also misattributed, accusing PRO of arguments B itself had made, which A dispatched cleanly. B's own anchoring argument conceded that numbers shown early change choices — which A weaponized as the reason they must be true. B never answered A's standing burden: name one guest better off learning the real price later. A's progression across rounds was disciplined, with the closing crystallizing the asymmetry between certain structural deception and speculative compliance chains.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2, answering B's pressure questions: A1 dismantled B's feasibility case by noting every "unknowable" variable is known on the listing page, and A2 turned B's moderation-liability speculation back by pointing out A never made that claim — CON did — leaving B's rigidity harm entirely unevidenced.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): B won decisively by tying disclosure to the motion’s actual pre-checkout line: dates and party size can already be entered on the listing page, variable fees can be shown accurately or as schedules, and displaying rules does not eliminate waivers. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON wins clearly. It kept attention on what the motion actually regulates, the specific pre-checkout trigger, and put a dilemma on it that PRO never escaped. If the trigger means the listing page with dates entered, the rule is largely redundant. If it means the earlier browsing surface, there is no honest total yet, and a flat cleaning fee shown without dates gives a misleading signal. PRO's answers kept weakening its own case. It narrowed "house rules" to "material, booking-deciding terms," which CON fairly labeled an admission. It then shifted that materiality line again, calling quiet hours trivial while making pool safety mandatory. Its cure for buried fees, "clear notice that variable fees apply," is essentially the vague disclaimer it had condemned. PRO's central sunk-cost argument was turned in two steps: - CON offered a narrower fix: put the itemized total and material terms above the payment fields. - CON then showed that PRO's own list of sunk labor (dates, guest counts) is entered in search, before the click PRO's rule regulates. Grounding also favored CON. PRO's opening relied on brittle claims ("surveys find an average of 20%"), and its 58% headline came from a one-night stay. PRO also lost material to the clipped opening and repeated "zero burden" and "bait-and-switch" without engaging CON's points on what early numbers mean or who decides materiality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A1 and closing settled the main clash. If sunk effort is the injury, the hours of searching and the dates and guests entered there all precede PRO's trigger, so PRO's rule cannot recover them either. Meanwhile, putting the price above the payment form removes the commitment pressure at lower cost. This left PRO's only stated decisive harm with no remedy that is unique to its rule.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B won by making the decisive clash unavoidable: PRO's harm is sunk effort, but dates and guest counts are entered in search, before any click, so PRO's own trigger cannot recover that effort either — while B's "price first, form second" commitment-point rule eliminates the payment-field trap without regulating the browsing surface. A never broke this dilemma, instead repeating the commitment-bias assertion through Rebuttal 2 and Closing. B also caught a real contradiction — PRO condemned "fees apply" disclaimers, then offered "clear notice that variable fees apply" as its cure — and pressed the materiality problem (who decides which house rules count, per listing, at scale) that A answered only by narrowing the motion and redescribing the rule. B's flat-fee ranking distortion (two-thirds of a one-night rate, a tenth of a week's) was a genuine cost A never priced. A's clipped opening cost it the burden argument, and its later speeches recycled rather than adapted. B's speeches consistently narrowed, weighed, and crystallized ("regulate the commitment, not the click"), making the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's closing turn on PRO's sunk-cost case: if effort already spent is the injury, disclosure must precede the search box — which PRO admits can only carry estimates — so the harm PRO calls decisive is one PRO's own rule does not reach, and PRO never named a dollar lost under commitment-point disclosure.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): A kept the debate on the motion’s specific pre-checkout trigger rather than transparency generally. Its “price first, form second” alternative neutralized the payment and consent harms, while B never squarely answered that search effort is already sunk before either trigger. A also exposed B’s narrowing of “house rules” and tension between condemning vague fee disclaimers and proposing notice that variable fees apply. B presented a plausible consumer-protection case but became repetitive, relied on unsupported opening figures, and suffered a clipped opening.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal answered the sunk-cost pressure directly: placing the total and material terms before any personal or payment fields removes checkout commitment pressure without regulating browsing. A’s closing then sharpened the unanswered point that dates, guests, and search labor are already sunk before B’s trigger.
