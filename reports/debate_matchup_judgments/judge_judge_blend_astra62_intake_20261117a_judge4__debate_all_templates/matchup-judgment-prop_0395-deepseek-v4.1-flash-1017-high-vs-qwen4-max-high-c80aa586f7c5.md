### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins narrowly because it adapted better as the debate developed. Side B (PRO) held a clean, defensible principle throughout: competence, not chronology, should decide who stays. Its "birthdate is perfectly measurable and perfectly irrelevant" line was effective. Its answers to the pressure questions were also direct and honest. But CON did more to reshape the clash: - It exploited PRO's safety-critical concession. - It caught PRO endorsing "fixed-term leadership roles" while condemning calendar-based exits. - It reframed retirement as a term limit rather than a competence test, which blunted PRO's accuracy critique. - It argued that PRO's assessment tools work only where output is measurable. - It extracted two admissions: no clean measure exists for judgement or temperament, and "poor fit" dismissal survives abolition. - It then pressed that PRO's remedy depends on a second, unwon reform (just-cause protection). CON's case has real weaknesses. Its claim that a retirement age acts as a "lock" or quasi-tenure is overstated, since a retirement age does not stop earlier poor-fit dismissals. PRO's closing correctly turned the poor-fit point by calling mandatory retirement an additional guaranteed exit. Even so, CON's comparative framing (verifiable rule versus manipulable discretion) remained more developed. CON also did more rebuttal work across rounds, so it takes a real but thin edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1 and A2.2 did the most damage to PRO's line. A2.1 showed that PRO's own remedies, fixed-term leadership roles and phased retirement, rely on predetermined exits. A2.2 recast retirement as a term limit rather than a diagnostic screen, which neutralized PRO's "screen that never tests the condition" attack. PRO's best counter came in B5.3: if "poor fit" dismissal survives anyway, mandatory retirement is an extra exit rather than a shield. That turn was strong but came late and was only partly developed.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side A wins a clear but competitive debate through stronger comparative logic, epistemic discipline, and rebuttal accuracy. Side B wrote memorably and applied real institutional pressure on HR conflict-aversion, hard-to-measure roles, junior succession, and stigma-free exits. However, Side A dismantled B's "prophylactic screening" defense, honestly defended multi-signal behavioral evaluation as imperfect yet superior to a blind birthdate across most occupations, and exposed a decisive contradiction in B's dismissal story. Side B simultaneously argued that firing declining older workers requires months of humiliating documentation and lawsuits while claiming that post-abolition managers can effortlessly dismiss thriving 68-year-olds via vague "poor fit." When Side A noted that surviving "poor fit" dismissal makes mandatory retirement an extra guaranteed expulsion rather than a shield, Side B overreached in closing by asserting that a retirement age prevents employers from inventing pre-retirement dismissals—an ungrounded claim that left PRO's core comparison firmly ahead.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A first neutralized [B1.3]'s "prophylactic vs. forensic" claim in [A2.2] ("a screen that never tests the condition is not prophylaxis—it is assumption," forcing Side B onto a weaker presidential term-limit analogy) and later inverted Side B's "poor fit" trap in [A5.3], showing that where vague dismissal already exists, mandatory retirement provides no tenure shield and acts only as an additional, irrebuttable expulsion of capable workers.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): PRO kept the decision on whether a birthdate may routinely expel capable workers and showed that imperfect, checkable judgment still beats guaranteed misclassification. CON's sharpest practical points—HR conflict-avoidance, unmeasurable judgment, and the value of a known horizon—were met by comparative weighing, while the term-limit analogy and the claim that a retirement date locks earlier vague dismissal were not fully repaired after PRO turned the poor-fit concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing conversion of CON's poor-fit concession: if vague dismissal already exists, mandatory retirement is an extra unrebuttable exit, not the worker's shield.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins clearly. CON's main case was that an age default is a coordination device. PRO answered by separating rules that attach to the post (notice periods, fixed terms) from rules that attach to the person. It then showed that every coordination benefit CON named, including predictability, openings, and knowledge transfer, can come from age-neutral tools. CON was left defending why age specifically is needed, and it never managed that. CON's concessions hurt it. It said the exit is "not a capability verdict" and praised the "lower payroll cost" of rehiring retirees as consultants. PRO turned both into the claim that the rule's real function is cheapening competent labor. CON's pivot to a "shield" for workers who want to leave without a competence finding was weak. Under abolition, workers can still retire voluntarily, and PRO noted that the unchosen worker simply stays employed. CON's late reliance on "anti-discrimination law" to contest an age-based exit is somewhat incoherent. CON's best point was the critique of tenure caps, that they punish late entrants. However, this applies equally to its own rule, as its teacher answer admitted. PRO also handled the Pressure Questions round better. It cleanly disowned the fixed-term "model" burden and pressed a question CON could not resolve.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The teacher example decided the debate. PRO asked why someone hired at sixty is expelled after five years while a colleague hired at thirty keeps the same post for thirty-five. CON answered that both reach "the same predictable career endpoint." PRO's closing then reframed this as "a plan for a biography, not a plan for a post." That exposed that the rule's remaining function is enforcing a life stage rather than coordinating succession.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side A won solidly by dismantling every pillar of Side B's coordination defense while cleanly answering Side B's best pushback on open-ended contracts. Side A severed post-level rules from birth-certificate rules, exposed through the 60-year-old versus 30-year-old teacher comparison that age cutoffs enforce a biography rather than post-level succession, and turned Side B's post-retirement consulting mechanism into proof that the rule cuts pay and security for competent workers while handing employers unchecked rehiring discretion. Side B mounted a smart critique of universal fixed-term contracts in Rebuttal 1, but Side A clarified that open-ended jobs remain open-ended under abolition, while Side B leaned heavily in late rounds on a muddled tenure-cap comparison and the implausible claim that without mandatory firing a worker who wants to retire must prove incompetence to leave.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's sustained turn of Side B's post-retirement consulting defense ([A2.7], [A3.1], [A4.4], [A5.3]): after Side B argued retirees can return as advisers at lower payroll cost and called the retirement age a shield against employer discretion, Side A showed that selective consulting rehires manufacture unappealable employer discretion, prove capability was never the issue, and leave unselected workers fired rather than protected.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that mandatory retirement’s claimed coordination, dignity, and anti-discretion benefits can be delivered by age-neutral tools, while its distinctive effect is an incontestable exit based on identity. Side A’s shield and succession defenses were damaged by the consulting-rehire contradiction and by treating a shared career endpoint as coordination of a post rather than a biography. Side A stayed competitive on stigma and leverage, but Side B’s concessions, pressure, and closing weighing made the vote clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s years-in-post question, and the closing use of Side A’s “same career endpoint” reply, broke the claim that a birth-year rule coordinates jobs rather than biographies; the consulting-shield collapse reinforced that the default does not protect the unchosen worker.
