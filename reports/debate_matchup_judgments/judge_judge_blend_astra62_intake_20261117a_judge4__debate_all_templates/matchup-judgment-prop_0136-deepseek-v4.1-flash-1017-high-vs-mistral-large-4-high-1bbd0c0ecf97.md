### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): B won by contesting the motion's metric more rigorously: it insisted "birth rates" means the period flow of births, not completed family size, and pressed A on this throughout. A's attempt in Rebuttal 1 to reframe the debate around "twenty-year completed family size" was correctly flagged by B as a goalpost shift, and A never fully recovered the metric fight. Most decisively, B turned A's own flagship examples (Singapore's housing grants, Japan's childcare expansion) against it in Rebuttal 2, noting these countries already run extensive structural support yet remain among the world's lowest-fertility societies — a telling, well-grounded point that directly undercut A's causal story. A's closing never addressed this specific counter, leaving a live, damaging point unanswered at the end of the debate. B also offered a more sophisticated economic mechanism (capitalization of housing subsidies into prices, tempo effects converting delayed births into realized ones, inframarginal leakage into labor supply) that A's "removes a binding constraint" assertions never fully rebutted beyond restating the thesis. Both sides answered pressure questions with proper A1/A2 formatting, but B's answers were more responsive to the metric question while A's answers partially dodged the "underlying rate vs period rate" distinction B had raised.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 point that Singapore and Japan already have the housing/childcare infrastructure A cited as proof of concept, yet still post very low fertility — turning A's own examples into evidence against A's causal mechanism, a point A's closing failed to answer.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1 and A4.2 brilliantly answered the inframarginal/leakage pressure by explaining that in East Asia, the choice is between career and family, so making both viable creates new births rather than just subsidizing existing ones.

Furthermore, B successfully defended the importance of period birth rates and tempo effects, explaining that accelerating deferred births prevents them from becoming biologically foregone, neutralizing A's focus on completed family size. A had superior rhetorical framing, but B won the core analytical clashes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 8), where it dismantled A's "economic space" argument by showing it leads to labor supply leakage, and defended tempo effects by explaining that in East Asia, deferred births often become foregone, making cash-induced acceleration a real increase in period birth rates.
