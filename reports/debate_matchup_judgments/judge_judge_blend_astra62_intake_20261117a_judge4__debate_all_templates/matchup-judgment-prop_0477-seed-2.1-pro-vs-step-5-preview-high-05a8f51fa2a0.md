### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): This was a close debate. PRO opened with a vivid single-point-of-failure frame. However, it leaned on brittle precision ("tens of billions a day," "fewer than 60" repair ships, "less than 1%") and narrowed its burden to "marginal dollars" from "prestige" upgrades. That narrowing is defensible under the motion. Still, PRO left its claim that fixed sensors "make subs far more effective" open to CON's turn that they are a multiplier, not a substitute. CON did more of the clash work. It argued that the platforms that counter hostile UUVs are themselves visible procurements. It answered the satellite point with prioritized military SATCOM. It argued that restoring service under sustained cuts requires repair ships operating in contested waters. It also gave a clean, memorable "insurance vs. defense" frame. PRO's attribution-through-monitoring answer partially blunted the deniability dilemma. Its closing reasserted "prerequisite" framing rather than resolving the SATCOM and escort points. CON wins narrowly on sharper, more adaptive rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.6/A4.3 response on satellites was the most decisive move. It reframed PRO's "less than 1%" aggregate bandwidth claim as irrelevant to prioritized, low-bandwidth military command links. This weakened PRO's core claim that cable loss renders platforms "useless," and PRO answered it only with an assertion in closing.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Close but real PRO win. PRO built a stronger path to the proposition by identifying a genuine gap: commercial operators are not structured for coordinated, deniable state sabotage, and modern defense platforms depend on the cable layer they claim protects it. CON's strongest attack was that shifting resources from visible defense would hollow out undersea dominance, but PRO consistently limited the shift to low-marginal prestige procurements while retaining core submarines, patrol aircraft, and sensors. That reading is fair to the motion's "visible" language. CON's insurance-vs-defense distinction was sharp but overstated: PRO's protection/monitoring component ties directly to attribution and deterrence, and redundancy complements rather than replaces military response. CON also never fully answered the economic and espionage stakes. On balance PRO gave the cleaner and more persuasive route to affirm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal that cable security depends on the undersea-warfare platforms PRO would trim was the most dangerous turn, but PRO's second rebuttal neutralized it by narrowing the shift to prestige-focused overmatch while arguing that fixed route sensors make existing submarines and patrol aircraft more effective rather than replacing them.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won the central tradeoff by distinguishing marginal prestige procurements from core undersea-warfare capabilities, showing coordinated state cuts exceed commercial repair capacity, and explaining how monitoring restores attribution while redundancy keeps platforms functioning. Side B's undersea-dominance and insurance arguments were strong, but they treated all visible procurement as equally protective and did not fully answer A's deniability and dependency case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over deterrence: B argued deniable cuts make cable spending mere insurance, while A answered that persistent sensor monitoring removes plausible deniability through attribution and redundancy preserves operations if deterrence fails.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON opened with its strongest move: the "visible" hardware PRO would defund, such as patrol aircraft and ASW frigates, is exactly what protects cables. PRO answered this cleanly. It said the motion does not require dissolving escorts. It then named in A1 which procurements it would cut: strike-fighter wings, land armor, and amphibious hulls. It said that money would go to sensors, unmanned craft, hardened landings, and repair ships. That specification left CON in a weaker spot. CON had to argue that tanks and amphibious hulls are the essential deterrent to cable sabotage. That is a stretch from its original ASW-centered case, and CON never really explained how land armor deters seabed sabotage. CON's best live route was deterrence by punishment: an unpunished adversary keeps cutting until redundancy is exhausted. This was a real point. PRO met it with a plausible answer: attribution, escalation costs, and denial of payoff. CON then mostly repeated the claim rather than deepening it. PRO also made the weighing clearer. It focused on the marginal dollar, denial of effect, and the fact that no finite fleet can guard every mile of seabed. Its "fist vs. nervous system" framing was memorable and tied to substance. Grounding was roughly even. PRO's "95%" figure is a commonly cited approximation. CON's "often less than an hour" and "dozens every year" are similarly loose but plausible. Both sides used the required A1/A2 format.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2 was the key exchange. It answered CON's zero-sum challenge by naming specific non-cable-relevant cuts: fighter wings, armor, and amphibious hulls. This undercut CON's opening claim that the shifted funds would come from the very ASW and patrol assets that guard cables. CON was pushed into defending strike and land capabilities as cable deterrents, which was a less persuasive position.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO won the central clash by reframing the question as marginal defense value through denial of effect rather than fleet size. CON’s strongest route was that unpunished adversaries can overwhelm redundancy, but PRO answered with attribution, hardening, and repair capacity, showing that a large multi-cable attack loses deniability and the clean blackout payoff. CON also relied on visible ships as the only credible deterrent, yet PRO effectively turned that into a reaction-based strategy: the Baltic ships arrived after damage. CON never fully neutralized PRO’s point that diversified landings and rapid repair narrow the vulnerable window more efficiently than another power-projection platform. The tradeoff argument hurt CON because the specific cuts PRO named were not the seabed interdictors CON needed them to be.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s strongest rebuttal was the denial-of-effect response: visible naval assets responded after the Baltic cuts but did not prevent them, while rerouting protects bandwidth but not shared landing chokepoints. That directly undercut CON’s claim that fleet-heavy procurement is the necessary deterrent and shifted the debate toward monitoring, hardening, and rapid repair rather than constant escort.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): A wins by breaking B's false-tradeoff premise: A specifies cutting strike fighters, armor, and amphibious hulls rather than the ASW/patrol assets that actually intercept saboteurs, then redirects funds to tailored sensors, unmanned craft, hardened landings, and rapid repair. A's geography point—that rerouting protects bandwidth, not single landing stations or island chokepoints—and denial-of-effect deterrence survive B's escalation objection better than B's claim that a finite fleet can guard every seabed mile.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A Rebuttal 2's A1 answer: naming non-cable-relevant visible procurements to cut and cable-specific intercept/recovery assets to buy directly neutralized B's central claim that PRO must defund the very forces that protect cables.
