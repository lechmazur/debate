### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): PRO wins because it set a clear decision frame, "net urban value," and kept a consistent causal story through every round. The core of that story was induced demand on one side and network redistribution on the other. CON's strongest material was its displacement and equity case: essential trips move onto arterials, and night-shift and reverse commuters bear the cost. That material was real, but CON never fully neutralized induced demand. CON ignored it in Rebuttal 1 and then conceded it was "real" in Rebuttal 2. CON's exchange-rate question was clever and initially put PRO on the spot. PRO answered directly with clean A1:/A2: labels. In closing, PRO then showed that the same objection applies to widening. CON's own closing conceded that symmetry, which drained the trap of its force. After that, CON's remaining route was "ongoing use is evidence of function." PRO reframed this persuasively: current volume is not an entitlement to the present geometry. The fiscal clash was largely a wash: an immediate demolition cost against an enlarged maintenance liability. CON's gentrification point drew only a glancing answer, which keeps the margin modest.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange-rate clash decided the debate. CON demanded a measurable rate between a commute minute and a housing unit and then called "net urban value" a slogan. PRO answered that no city decision, including widening, can meet that test. CON's closing admitted that "the same veto applies to widening," which turned CON's main late attack into a neutral point and left PRO's induced-demand asymmetry standing.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear, competitive debate by prevailing on both the evaluative framework and network-demand mechanics. Side B showed why urban widening erodes its own peak relief through induced demand and ramp bottlenecks while locking in corridor severance and maintenance liabilities, and concretely explained in [B4.1] how a restored grid, signal priority, transit lanes, and freight routing handle essential trips without a six-lane pipe. Side A built a strong pragmatic case around upfront demolition costs, off-peak car-dependent workers, and targeted auxiliary-lane fixes inside existing corridors (which Side B left under-addressed), but weakened its stance after conceding induced demand [A4.1], admitting widening also lacks a clean exchange rate [A5.1], and leaving unanswered Side B's critique that current traffic proves demand for access rather than freeway geometry [B5.1].
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's handling of the exchange-rate and ongoing-use clash across [B4.3] and [B5.1]-[B5.2]: rather than inventing fake precision to answer Side A's commute-minute-to-housing question [A3.2], Side B exposed that demand as a veto against multi-dimensional costs that widening fails equally (forcing Side A's concession in [A5.1]), and neutralized [A4.3]'s "ongoing use is not failure" by showing that crowded lanes prove only a need for access, not a permanent entitlement to six highway lanes.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): PRO wins the comparative frame: widening induces peak trips yet creates permanent severance and maintenance costs, while removal can distribute essential trips across a reconnected network. CON's displacement and reverse-commute pressures are serious, but its end-of-life rule leaves current volume as an entitlement and its demand for an exchange rate applies equally to widening, so it does not defeat PRO's more-often default.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 and closing turn CON's exchange-rate challenge back on CON: refusing fake currency is not a slogan, widening also lacks a clean rate, and CON treats trips as fixed under removal while downplaying induced trips under widening.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): PRO opened with a clear induced-demand story and recognizable removal examples. It never adequately answered CON's two central pressures. The first was the bundling asymmetry: PRO credited removal with transit, traffic calming, and housing while treating widening only as unpriced general-purpose lanes. The second was the frequency burden, which requires a showing about the ordinary freeway rather than showcase cases. PRO's A1 dismissed selective widening as "not a serious answer," which conceded the asymmetry rather than fixing it. Its A2 restated existing harms instead of addressing the new costs CON identified: delayed ambulances, displaced freight, and fixed-shift commuters. PRO's closing repeated the claim that widening destroys the grid. CON had already neutralized that with its within-footprint, no-new-takings answer. PRO's Rebuttal 1 was also clipped, an execution cost. CON adapted across rounds and turned PRO's "spillover is a policy failure" line into a concession that removal depends on several hard policies succeeding together. CON also pressed the reversibility asymmetry and closed with a clean decision rule: prove a link is redundant before erasing it. CON's framing of shoulders and merges as "widening" is somewhat generous, but PRO never effectively contested it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1–B2.3 called out PRO's comparison of a removal bundle against a widening caricature and showed that "spillover is a policy failure" concedes removal relies on several difficult complements succeeding at once. PRO never recovered from this; its A1 simply re-asserted the asymmetric comparison.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A built a much cleaner comparative case on the motion's "more often" standard and won every major clash by the final speeches. Side B opened well around end-of-life rebuilds and landmark teardowns, but relied on an asymmetric comparison—bundling removal with ideal transit, housing, and traffic calming while reducing widening to unpriced general-purpose lane expansion. Side A dismantled this double standard, showed that B's showcase examples (Embarcadero, Cheonggyecheon, Park East, Inner Loop) only justify removing redundant spurs rather than ordinary freight and radial corridors, and outperformed B on transportation mechanics by distinguishing throughput and managed bus/HOV capacity from unpriced free-flow lanes. Side A also answered B's pressure questions directly—smartly conceding against any widening that requires new neighborhood takings—whereas Side B incurred a minor clipping cost in R1, partially sidestepped the surface-street spillover question in R2, and repeated earlier claims in Closing without engaging A's R2 answers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A2.1]-[A2.5] and [A4.1]-[A4.3], Side A flipped Side B's "shell game" and "policy failure" claims by exposing B's asymmetric bundle, turning B's Milwaukee and Rochester examples into proof that removal fits only redundant spurs, and neutralizing B's pressure questions via a no-new-takings rule plus priced/bus-priority lanes that deliver throughput without gridlock.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B won the frequency clash: PRO proved bad general-purpose widening and exceptional spur removals, but not that the ordinary freeway is typically redundant. B's selective, within-footprint widening answered induced demand, takings, and transit concerns while exposing PRO's bundled comparison, and B showed a fuller road can still provide more access rather than being automatically obsolete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttals on argumentative asymmetry were decisive: removal could not claim transit, calming, and housing while widening was restricted to unpriced general-purpose lanes, especially when bus/HOV, freight, shoulder, and merge improvements can occur without new takings and can later be priced or reassigned.
