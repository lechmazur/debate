### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side B won a close, high-quality exchange. B's core attack—that advertising mostly moves brand share rather than category consumption—was original and never fully neutralized; A's response (child-targeted promotion builds habits) was plausible but asserted. B's pressure question demanding one measurable restriction succeeded when A honestly conceded it could not name one, and B's "reach is not effect" crystallized that concession into a weighing argument. B also landed a genuine internal-consistency hit: A's own affordability mechanism ("buy fewer items instead of unlocking a deal") routes through individual restraint, the very capacity A called too weak to lead. A's closing reply—that behavior as outcome differs from campaigns as instrument—was the best counter of the debate, and A's fairness/children/default framing plus a real mechanism in A4.1 kept the case live. But B answered pressure questions more decisively (B4.1's "thirty years of brand loyalty," B4.3's lentils-and-a-pot), and B's closing sharpened the choice rather than recapping. A's honest epistemic discipline on unverifiable studies deserves credit but cost strategic ground. B made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3-B4.5: B exposed that A's affordability fix requires individual behavior change ("can buy fewer items"), turning A's own mechanism against its thesis, then converted A's admitted inability to name an effective restriction into the decisive weighing point that "reach is not effect."
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): B gave the stronger comparative mechanism: changing defaults and commercial prompts reduces the repeated attention and resistance that campaigns require. A raised meaningful affordability, leakage, and brand-versus-category objections, but increasingly narrowed the broader motion to advertising bans. B answered affordability concretely and honestly acknowledged evidentiary limits, while correctly challenging A's attribution of historical trends to campaigns. A's closing then repeated a confusion B had already exposed rather than resolving the comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's distinction between behavior as the outcome and campaigns as the instrument decisively answered A's claim that structural policy secretly depends on exhortation. Removing a multibuy prompt can change purchasing without teaching restraint; A's insistence that consumers still decide did not establish campaign superiority.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side A won by keeping the motion on instruments rather than outcomes. Environmental rules can remove commercial prompts and change defaults for children and non-engagers, while campaigns ask households to resist pressures left intact. A gave a concrete affordability design and honestly declined to invent a study. B's brand-share, leakage, and reach-versus-effect hits were sharper and still live, but they established design risk and uncertainty, not a reason to prioritize behavior campaigns. B never showed skills create time, access, or immunity to child marketing, and the closing treated necessary purchasing behavior as if it made campaigns the leading tool.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing distinction was decisive: people must buy differently, but that is the outcome, not a reason to lead with campaigns. Removing a junk multibuy or child-directed prompt changes the choice conditions themselves. B's reach-is-not-effect attack was the sharpest pressure, yet it did not convert mutual evidentiary limits into a vote for behavior campaigns.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined and honest, and both executed the format cleanly. The decisive clash was the obesity-outcome evidence gap: CON correctly noted PRO's examples (reformulation, trans fats) showed intake changes, not sustained weight loss from marketing restrictions specifically. But CON conceded in B4.1 that no population-level sustained weight result exists for behavioral campaigns either, and never fully answered PRO's tie-break weighing — that when evidence ties, priority should go to the lever that is universal, permanent, cheapest at the margin, and strongest where options are worst. CON's "universal exposure to an unproven benefit" rejoinder was sharp but left CON in the same evidentiary hole. PRO also extracted two key concessions: CON admitted the time-poor drop out first (B4.3), and CON's equity fix — convenient delivery, defaults, fewer friction points — was persuasively reframed as "environmental design wearing a campaign's badge." CON's response (B5.2) partially dodged by relabeling it as outreach. PRO's A1 answer noting the motion covers "food environment," not just marketing, was a clean, legitimate rescue of its examples. CON's honesty and epistemic discipline were excellent throughout, and its measurability/adaptability thread was real, but PRO made the comparative choice easier to decide and landed the more damaging concessions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing move (A5.1-A5.2): quoting CON's own concession that "the time-poor are the plausible early dropouts" and arguing that every CON repair of its equity problem — convenient delivery, defaults, reduced friction — borrows the environmental lever. CON's B5.2 relabeling answer was reasonable but never fully escaped the concession, making it the most consequential exchange of the debate.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): B wins on comparative reach and persistence. Neither side established sustained population-weight effects, so A’s demand for that proof cannot itself decide priority. B better explained why changing defaults can reach nonparticipants without repeated adherence, while A’s adaptable support still depends on uptake and retention. A successfully challenged B’s cost overclaims, advertising evidence, and relabeling of convenient outreach, but did not overturn that central comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest rebuttal applied compensation and durability concerns to behavioral campaigns too. A answered with monitoring and maintenance, but B exposed their dependence on continued participation, reinforced by A’s concession that time-poor participants plausibly drop out first.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side A won by keeping the motion as a population-priority choice. Once neither side could show a sustained population-weight trial, universality, persistence without continuous effort, and equity were the cleaner tie-breakers. Side B usefully damaged the causal chain—cross-product substitution, the confounded Chile bundle, and coverage not equaling impact—and was more careful not to invent results, but expanding campaigns into intensive support opened a scalability hole it never closed, and its dropout concession left the floor-versus-enrollees comparison standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's first rebuttal was decisive: it applied relapse symmetrically, then separated thin persuasion from intensive programs that cannot cover a population. B's later admission that the time-poor drop out first confirmed that pressure, and B's evidence-standard replies never fully neutralized it.
