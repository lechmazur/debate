### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins by keeping the debate tied to the motion as written. The motion bans add-on seat fees and premium fare penalties; it does not promise that family seating costs nothing. PRO said this openly, conceded that residual costs could go into a posted base fare, and turned CON's subsidy objection by noting that the status quo already spreads costs as gate disorder, crew time, and pressure on strangers to swap seats. CON's strongest line was the "hidden subsidy, someone pays" point, backed by real scarcity. That line was sharp, but it partly misreads the motion: CON's closing treats "without fees" as if it meant "without cost." CON also never showed that a posted base fare counts as the penalty the motion forbids. PRO answered both pressure questions cleanly and directly. It drew a concrete under-8 line and justified it as a dependency line rather than a crash-safety boundary. That narrowed the dispute and blunted the arbitrariness and safety objections. CON did well to point out that the safety framing retreated, but PRO had already recast its case around supervision and dependency, so the point did not decide the debate. CON's 14B argument (that a paid seat assignment cannot be both a declinable product and movable inventory) was its best original pressure. PRO's disruption analogy partly neutralized it. CON's opening and both rebuttals were clipped for length, which cost it closing lines on live points. That is a real execution penalty. PRO's speeches were complete, tighter, and better adapted to the evolving clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The key exchange was PRO's A1 against CON's subsidy objection. PRO argued that a visible base fare charged equally to everyone is not an add-on or a premium penalty, and that the status quo already socializes costs chaotically. CON replied that this was "my objection stated politely," but it never established that base-fare inclusion breaches the motion's actual wording. That left PRO's reading of the motion standing.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): B more convincingly established adjacency as a baseline condition of transporting dependent children and directly defended incorporating its costs into ordinary fares. However, A repeatedly treated redistribution as automatically defeating a motion that forbids add-ons, not shared operating costs, and demanded an unnecessary crash-safety threshold. B answered the central pressure more effectively; A's repeatedly clipped turns also weakened its execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest response was that a posted base fare is not a hidden surcharge: the question is whether basic supervision belongs in the ordinary service, not whether adjacency is costless. Combined with its account of existing boarding and seat-swapping costs, this substantially weakened A's repeated subsidy objection.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): CON wins a clear but competitive debate. PRO began with an intuitive and memorable frame: a fee for adjacency is a "ransom" on a supervision need the operator itself created. But both of PRO's first two speeches were clipped, which cost it substantive content. PRO also conceded in B2.5 that "no rule is violated" when no pair exists. CON turned that concession into a durable dilemma. Either adjacency is a non-waivable safety condition, which would require reserved seats or denied sales, a rule PRO never fully defended. Or it is a preference that price may ration, in which case the motion becomes a price control that hands priced inventory to families at the basic fare. PRO's A1/A2 answers were reasonable: holdbacks, operator-borne remediation, and narrowing the ban to adjacency alone. These recovered ground, particularly against CON's "premium ladder" point. However, PRO's closing overstated CON's conditional standard as an outright concession, and CON correctly flagged that. CON's insistence that someone bears the allocation cost was never fully answered beyond "the operator pays." That reply addresses who receives the bill, not which passenger loses the seat or how the cost reaches other fares. CON's pivot that rebooking falls short of safety was somewhat overstated, since a rebooked family is still seated together. Even so, CON's case was more consistent and better organized across the rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.1–A2.3 exploited PRO's "no rule is violated when no pair exists" line. The argument was that the safety trump cannot vanish on a full train, so PRO must either accept a strict condition-of-carriage duty or admit adjacency is a priceable preference. That dilemma framed the rest of the debate and was never cleanly escaped.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): B better distinguished necessary supervision from discretionary seat choice and ultimately supplied a coherent holdback-and-rebooking duty. A effectively exposed B's initial availability-only retreat and correctly rejected B's portrayal of a conditional as a concession. However, A's decisive objection to rebooking contradicted its own description of an acceptable safety duty. A established plausible allocation costs, but not why they outweighed providing supervision within the purchased fare. B's unsupported cost minimization, shifting formulation, and mischaracterization of A keep this competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's distinction between guaranteed adjacency and unrestricted seat choice directly weakened A's claim that the rule necessarily gives away the premium seating product. On scarcity, B's operator-funded rebooking answer also survived: A explicitly accepted that remedy for genuine safety obligations before attacking B for offering it.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side A won by exposing a live inconsistency: Side B invoked non-waivable child safety to ban fees and fare penalties, yet treated missing pairs as no violation and offered later rebooking rather than guaranteed seating on the purchased trip. That left the motion looking like price control that hides allocation costs instead of a coherent safety duty. Side B's overclaim of a concession and two clipped speeches further weakened its path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's pressure that "no rule is violated" when no pair exists breaks the safety-hostage claim and forces an unresolved choice between a strict no-sale rule and an unnamed transfer of scarce seats.
