### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a competitive debate by resolving the clash it most needed to win. CON's strongest route was a sharp dilemma: either contacts must consent, which seems to bring back the coordination problem, or they need not, which exports non-users' data. CON's opening was clipped, which cost it some development, but its rebuttals were pointed and its "hallway" imagery was memorable. PRO answered the dilemma directly in A1 by separating two burdens. Accepting a single cross-service conversation is very different from migrating a whole friend group. CON then partly conceded this point, and PRO used that concession effectively in its closing. CON's fallback was that the benefit is modest while the security exposure is structural. That weighing has real force, but it lost some bite once PRO showed that cross-service threads require informed acceptance. That undercut CON's repeated "people who never chose" framing. CON also never fully engaged PRO's point that its own alternatives (archives and antitrust policing) cannot let someone reach the people who stay behind. PRO's final framing, "who controls the barrier," left the cleaner reason to vote. Still, CON kept a credible residual-risk argument alive, so the margin is moderate rather than large.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2. It distinguished consenting to a conversation from relocating to a new service. CON's reply accepted that this is "not wholesale migration" and could only argue that the remaining gain is narrower. That shifted CON from a dilemma that could defeat PRO's case to a weighing contest, and PRO then won that contest by noting that CON's alternatives cannot reach people who stay on the incumbent platform.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a close but real victory. The debate turned on CON's consent dilemma: either contacts must opt in (recreating coordinated migration) or they don't (privacy export to non-users). PRO resolved it cleanly in Rebuttal 2 — accepting a cross-service conversation while keeping one's existing app is a categorically lighter burden than group migration, and consent means no silent data export — and then secured a major concession: CON admitted archives "cannot preserve tomorrow's messages" and that its regulatory alternatives cannot deliver reaching people who stay. CON's final weighing ("optional, gradual benefit vs structural, compulsory exposure") was rhetorically sharp but rested on a framing PRO had already defused, since exposure only reaches users who accepted cross-service contact. CON's clipped opening was a minor execution cost. Both sides used proper Q/A formats, argued the motion as written, and avoided fake sourcing. PRO's direct answers under pressure plus the archive concession give it the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1–B4.3: PRO's answer that "acceptance is not migration" — family keeps their app, account, and history while merely permitting communication — dissolved CON's central dilemma and forced CON to shift from a structural objection to a narrower weighing contest it could no longer decisively win.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins a high-level, closely contested debate by resolving the central clash over consent and lock-in while exposing the limits of Side A's alternatives and risk framing. Side A (CON) posed a sharp dilemma arguing that opt-in consent recreates coordinated migration while non-consensual access exposes uninvolved users, and landed real mitigation regarding group-chat holdouts and widened endpoint attack surfaces (alongside a minor execution cost from a clipped opening). However, Side B decisively showed that clicking accept while keeping one's existing app and network is vastly easier than full group migration, forced Side A to admit archives cannot preserve future communication, and demonstrated that informed per-contact acceptance prevents silent exposure of refusing users—leaving Side A's claim of compulsory exposure for non-participants reliant on metaphor rather than mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling in [B3.1] and [B4.1]-[B4.3] (crystallized in [B5.3]) of Side A's consent dilemma from [A2.3] and [A3.1]: Side B separated ordinary interpersonal consent to accept a chat on an existing app from collective platform relocation, simultaneously preserving PRO's exit benefit and neutralizing CON's claim of unconsented data and message exposure.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): CON (A) wins. PRO opened strongly: network effects as a moat, plus analogies to email and number portability. Its first rebuttal also landed real hits. The incumbent already holds the data, and an "everyone consents" standard looks like a veto on exit. CON then narrowed the debate effectively. Interoperability adds a recipient rather than substituting one, and leaving with your own account differs from carrying other people's associations elsewhere. That forced a dilemma: either consent limits portability, or nonconsenting users bear new exposure. PRO's answers conceded both horns. It agreed a refusing member's data need not route to the new client. It also adopted "continuity is the default" under inconclusive evidence, and CON turned that into a clean weighing point: harm from disclosure is irreversible, and liability only compensates after the fact. PRO also paid execution costs: - Its second rebuttal was clipped. - It claimed CON's questions went unanswered, though CON answered them directly. - Its closing misdescribed CON as requiring group consent for a user's own account to leave, which CON had explicitly disclaimed. - Its closing cited nonexistent reference IDs. PRO's competition argument stayed alive, but CON's comparison of partial competitive gain against irreversible third-party risk was cleaner. It went more squarely unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.1/A5.4 point that interoperability "adds another provider" rather than "substitutes" one directly dismantled PRO's central reply that the incumbent already holds the data. PRO never squarely answered it. PRO's closing simply repeated that exposure "cuts against whoever holds the data now."
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but Side A (CON) did the more decisive comparative work in the later rounds. A's pressure questions extracted two costly concessions from B: that a refusing member's identity and messages need not route to the third party (making the promised "community portability" conditional), and that under B's own suspension rule "continuity is the default" when evidence is inconclusive. A hammered both in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, framing the vote as partial lock-in relief versus irreversible privacy risk borne by non-consenting users. B's central rhetorical frame — consent rules as "the moat wearing a human face" — was effectively neutralized by A's distinction between controlling one's own disclosures and vetoing another's departure. B's strengths were vivid framing and the live point that retention limits and antitrust never let a user reach friends from a rival client, but B's Rebuttal 2 was clipped for exceeding the word limit, mischaracterized A's answers as "unanswered" before A had spoken, and the Closing cited nonexistent reference IDs [C1.2, C4.1]. These execution costs, plus A's cleaner resolution of the decisive clash, give CON a narrow but real edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.4–A4.6): by locking in B's concession that refusals are honored and then attacking B's "continuity is the default" security rule as resolving uncertainty in favor of access with users bearing irreversible harm, A converted B's own answers into the deciding comparison — partial competitive gain versus uncompensable privacy risk.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side A won by reframing the social graph as shared relational data rather than individual property and systematically winning the resulting benefit-risk comparison. Side A showed that when one member ports a group conversation while others stay, interoperability adds a new data recipient rather than substituting for the incumbent—a point Side B never answered. Using sharp Pressure Questions, Side A forced Side B both to concede that non-consenting peers can block their messages from routing across services (making lock-in relief partial) and to adopt a "continuity is the default" rule under inconclusive security evidence (exposing users to irreversible disclosures for that partial gain). Side B also incurred noticeable execution costs in the second half: getting clipped at the word limit in Rebuttal 2, prematurely claiming Side A left questions unanswered before Side A's turn to respond, and citing nonexistent IDs ([C1.2, C4.1]) while misstating Side A's position on account exit in Closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A4.4]-[A4.6], Side A turned both of Side B's Pressure Question answers ([B4.1]-[B4.2]) into a decisive pincer: honoring a peer's refusal makes community portability conditional rather than seamless, while defaulting to continued access when security evidence is inconclusive forces users to bear irreversible privacy exposure that after-the-fact corporate liability cannot undo.
