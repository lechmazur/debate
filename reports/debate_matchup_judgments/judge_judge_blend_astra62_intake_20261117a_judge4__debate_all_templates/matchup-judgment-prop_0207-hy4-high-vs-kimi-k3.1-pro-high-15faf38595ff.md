### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a clear but competitive debate. PRO built its case on a clean structural point: universal subsidies compensate litres, not people, so they are regressive by design. It then turned CON's admission of "generosity to the affluent" into pressure. CON's repair was to tax affluent consumption. PRO argued this either collapses into targeting or relies on state capacity that CON elsewhere doubts. CON's strongest line was administrative fragility. It pressed on exclusion of undocumented and near-poor households, the timing of payments, uneven local price shocks, and political durability. Its closing point, that a coverage gate cannot see people who never register, was genuinely sharp. However, PRO met that pressure directly: - **Sequencing:** pay cash first, then raise prices in small steps. - **Adjustment:** a base transfer with a buffer, topped up from observed local prices. - **Gaps:** bridge payments for unresolved claims. - **Durability:** a visible, legal entitlement that can be audited and challenged if cut. PRO's best move was its Q2 on double standards. It showed that CON never subjected "imperfect automatic protection" to the same tests of coverage, adequacy, and durability it demanded of transfers. CON's answer conceded that controls are not crisis-proof. It also never squarely showed that subsidies reach the poorest who buy little fuel, or that they protect anyone through arrears and shortages. CON's "keep the floor, narrow the waste" alternative partly concedes PRO's diagnosis. CON also paid an execution cost: two of its turns were clipped for exceeding the word limit. PRO answered the pressure questions cleanly and in the required A1:/A2: format. Its closing framed the comparison memorably: visible, front-loaded risks under cash versus hidden failure under controls.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came in PRO's Rebuttal 2. Answering CON's durability question, PRO argued that a cash-strapped state fails under controls too, through arrears, rationing, and queues ("A cheap price printed at an empty pump is not security"). Combined with its earlier double-standard question, this exposed that CON demanded proof of coverage, adequacy, and durability only from the replacement. CON's reply that cash also fails at an empty pump was partial: it neutralized shortages as a reason to prefer cash but left the regressive-leakage and hidden-fiscal-cost points standing.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a high-level, closely contested debate by establishing a stronger comparative institutional case and exposing Side A's asymmetric standard of proof. Side A landed sharp tactical rebuttals—rightly separating formal elite taxation from prospective poverty enrollment [A4.1] and showing that payment-clearance gates cannot detect unregistered outsiders [A4.5, A5.1]. However, Side A held cash replacement to a zero-exclusion perfection standard while defending universal price controls with an untenable caveat ("while supply operates" [A4.3, A5.2]) and a partial tax patch that still subsidizes every non-poor household litre at the pump [B2.5] and rewards fuel waste. Side B persuasively showed that in fiscally stressed states, universal price controls fail by design—routing most money to heavy fuel users while hiding costs in supplier arrears, shortages, black markets, and cuts to clinics and schools—whereas phased, buffered, low-barrier cash transfers mitigate transition errors before prices move. Side A also incurred a minor execution cost from having two turns clipped at the word limit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive rebuttal exchange came in [B4.1-B4.5] answering Side A's pressure questions [A3.1-A3.2]. Side B dismantled the charge that cash requires impossible inflation forecasting by proposing walk-in cash, presumptive eligibility, buffered base transfers, small price steps, and observed-price feedback ("feedback, not clairvoyance"), then flipped Side A's austerity-freeze objection by showing that cash-strapped states keeping below-cost price controls destroy protection quietly through supplier arrears, rationing, queues, and clinic cuts ("A cheap price printed at an empty pump is not security")—a causal link Side A's exogenous treatment of supply collapse [A4.3] failed to neutralize.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side A powerfully showed universal subsidies reward litres and the affluent, but Side B sustained the decisive burden: replacement must identify, adequately compensate, pay, and keep funding every vulnerable household. B showed PRO's tax-equivalence, cash-first timing, gate, and visibility safeguards assume the disputed administrative capacity and mainly test people already found, leaving missed informal, undocumented, and near-poor households unprotected. That live chain-of-failure argument survived PRO's replies despite B's clipped turns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was B's Rebuttal 2 and closing attack on the payment gate: cleared payments and bridge claims cannot reveal households that never register, so PRO's safeguard is blind to its most serious exclusion risk.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Both sides were disciplined and well grounded, so this is a close call. PRO (B) built the more conventional structural case: universal subsidies leak upward, strain public budgets, and can produce shortages. PRO also landed a fair symmetry point: a subsidy does not guarantee pass-through to fares or food either. CON (A) did more to shape the decisive clash, and three moves made the difference. First, CON reframed the harm as an economy-wide input shock rather than an income gap. Second, CON posed a sharp dilemma on "targeting": broaden eligibility and you rebuild universality with a slower front end; keep it narrow and false exclusion returns. Third, CON pressed timing hard, showing that prices move before cash arrives. CON also seized on PRO's own concession that "No system can pay someone it has never encountered before a shock" and made it the structural boundary of targeting. CON had weaknesses. Its answer to the fiscal question was partly concessionary ("trimming the universal bill"). Its "cash buys nothing at an empty pump" line sidestepped the fact that controls cause the shortages. PRO's answer that exclusion can be corrected while leakage persists by design was solid. Still, CON's asymmetry framing — that waste is less harmful than exposure — stayed live through the closing. CON narrowed the debate more effectively, giving it a slight but real edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange came in A4.5 and A4.6. PRO's A1 answer on sequencing included the admission that it cannot pay someone never encountered before a shock. CON turned this into proof that sequencing protects only the "mapped." CON then paired it with the dilemma that PRO's broadened targeting line is either near-universal or exclusionary. PRO's closing reply, that pass-through also misses people, was apt but came late and did not fully neutralize the timing point.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins a clear but competitive debate by exposing the fiscal and supply-side contradictions in Side A's defense of universal price controls while offering a credible, sequenced cash replacement. Side A made a sharp case that fuel is an upstream input embedded in bus fares, freight, and generators, leaving unmapped informal households exposed if compensation lags. However, Side B proved that weak states unable to administer transfers cannot sustainably finance open-ended below-cost fuel plus top-ups without arrears, empty pumps, queues, and cuts to public services that poor and unlisted households rely on. Side B also cleanly broke Side A's targeting dilemma by showing that broad household or categorical coverage mitigates exclusion without reverting to regressive per-litre consumption subsidies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's state-capacity symmetry attack across [B2.1], [B3.1], and [B4.5]—asking how weak states can finance below-cost fuel indefinitely, prevent shortages, and fund extra top-ups without cutting pro-poor services, while catching Side A's [A2.2] slip about empty pumps when price controls are what cause supply shortages in the first place.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): PRO won the central comparison: universal subsidies guarantee regressive, consumption-based leakage and fiscal drain, while cash exclusion is a sequenced, testable risk. CON's timing and unmapped-household pressure was strong, but PRO answered that subsidies likewise guarantee neither supply nor fare/food pass-through, and CON never explained how weak states sustain the universal floor plus top-ups.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and closing turn on guarantees was decisive: a subsidy guarantees only a discount per litre, not availability or lower downstream prices, whereas a missed transfer can be identified and corrected.
