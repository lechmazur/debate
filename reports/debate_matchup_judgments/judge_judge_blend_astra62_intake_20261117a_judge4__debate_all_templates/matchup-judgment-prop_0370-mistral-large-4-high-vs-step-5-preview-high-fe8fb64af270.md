### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a clear but competitive debate. PRO built a coherent and durable route to victory: a personal phone cannot be separated from the worker's private life, while employer hardware creates an "off switch." Scope controls on a personal device are therefore only policy promises running on private property. CON (Side A) raised real pressure on three fronts: - **Drug-test and certification analogies.** PRO answered these reasonably: those conditions are episodic, whereas an installed app is continuous and ambient. - **"Second slab of glass."** CON pointed out that work phones also travel on commutes. This was CON's best line, but it never fully dislodged the separability point. - **Data scope over device ownership.** CON's A1 answer in Rebuttal 2 conceded that leaving a personal phone behind disables the worker's private life. That effectively granted PRO's asymmetry. - **Opt-in and widening.** CON's claim that a "revocable opt-in" is compatible with a routine mandate was a stretch, and its list of controls was asserted rather than shown to prevent widening. PRO's argument in B4.5 also had bite. It contended that if the safeguards CON describes are standard, then a personal-phone mandate is unnecessary. CON's reply, "an alternative is not a requirement," answered whether the mandate is permissible, but not PRO's claim that it is unnecessary and therefore disproportionate, which was the comparative point PRO pressed. Both sides paid execution costs: - Each had one rebuttal clipped for length. - PRO's closing cited nonexistent reference IDs ([C3.2, P4.3]), which is a real cost, though the substance it attributed to CON was roughly accurate. - CON's closing leaned on rhetoric ("hardware mysticism," "PRO fears the phone") more than on resolving the off-switch clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The locker and off-switch exchange decided the debate. PRO's Q1 asked whether the nurse can leave her personal phone at home. CON's A1 answer conceded that leaving the personal phone behind disables private life. PRO then used this in B4.3–B4.4 and the closing to show that only employer hardware provides a separable sphere. CON's counter that work phones also travel on commutes was clever, but it did not neutralize this asymmetry.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): B wins by separating mandatory installation from continuous surveillance and explaining how technical safeguards can constrain access. A establishes a genuine advantage of separable employer hardware, but repeatedly assumes that installed software necessarily creates permanent employer access, without adequately answering permission controls and work-hour scoping. B overstates the motion as a categorical ban, limiting its margin, but still supplies the stronger justification for allowing routine, bounded requirements. A’s unsupported technical generalizations and nonexistent closing reference IDs further weaken its case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B directly challenges A’s claim that contact harvesting and continuous GPS follow inevitably from installation: these are configurable permissions and functions. A’s physical-off-switch response remains relevant, but treating every safeguard as merely an employer promise fails to answer B’s distinction between technical restrictions and discretionary policy.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side B made the motion’s narrower default-rule framing decisive: employers can coordinate and monitor work, but large employers should supply separable equipment rather than routinely commandeer an indispensable personal phone. Side A strongly exposed B’s worst-case surveillance assumptions and established that data scope matters, but “manageable, therefore permissible” did not fully justify routine cost-and-risk shifting or neutralize the off-switch comparison. Both sides had clipped turns, and B’s closing used nonexistent reference IDs, keeping the margin competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s scope-not-hardware challenge seriously damaged B’s continuous-surveillance framing, but B’s answer that a work phone can be left behind, powered down, or handed over—whereas abandoning a personal phone sacrifices private life—best preserved a live reason to support the proposition.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins because it neutralized CON's central comparison and CON never repaired it. CON's strongest route was that a containerized personal phone is less intrusive than a company handset. PRO's B4.3 answered this directly: the same sealed profile can run on employer-owned hardware. Control over a device that holds only work data is not an intrusion on the worker's private life. CON's closing simply restated "strictly less intrusive" without engaging that point. On the dead-zone pressure, PRO offered rugged company phones or standalone panic devices. It also noted that connectivity failures hit personal phones too. CON then attacked web tools but never addressed the dedicated-device alternative. CON had some real wins. The steel-toe-boot answer to Q1 on consent was clean and honest. Its consent framing landed. Its point that tracking tools can help workers through wage and safety records was real, and PRO conceded it. However, CON's A4.4 conceded that boilerplate requirements across unrelated jobs are overbroad. CON then defended mainly role-necessary requirements, which drifts away from defending the motion's "routine condition." PRO also turned CON's cost-pass-through point into an admission that employers want to offload costs. PRO's closing slightly regressed by leaning on web tools again. Its consent-as-leverage argument stayed somewhat generic. On grounding, CON's claims that the host "cannot read container contents" and that the partition is "inert outside shifts" were overconfident technical assertions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B4.3: "The decisive comparison is not sealed work profile versus no work; it is the same sealed profile on employer hardware versus mandatory installation on a personal device." This dismantled CON's claim that company phones are more intrusive, and CON never answered it.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): A wins by distinguishing necessary work tools from necessary installation on personal property. Its employer-provided alternative preserves safety and coordination, while its explicit opt-in allowance defeats B’s repeated claim that the proposition eliminates workers’ preferred single-device option. B effectively challenged A’s overly broad account of coercion, but never established why employer control over separate work hardware is more invasive of personal privacy. B also overstated containerization’s guarantees. A therefore supplied the cleaner comparison, despite some exaggerated privacy and consent claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal answered the rural-safety challenge with employer-provided equipment and explained that dead zones can defeat personal phones too. It then exposed B’s central comparison error: needing reliable safety tools does not establish a need to mandate personal-phone installation. B largely repeated its company-phone privacy claim rather than answering that distinction.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): B kept the debate on routine, broadly imposed requirements and showed that messaging, safety, and recordkeeping benefits do not require installation on worker-owned hardware. A answered the consent pressure well, but increasingly defended narrow, role-necessary apps rather than the proposition’s routine condition. A also repeatedly compared a work container with total employer control of a company phone without answering why employer control of separate work hardware threatens personal privacy, and overlooked B’s express allowance for voluntary opt-in. B’s ownership-versus-robustness distinction and employer-funded alternatives left the cleaner route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 directly answered the rural-aide challenge with employer-owned offline-capable equipment, then exposed A’s central comparison: the same separated work environment could reside on employer hardware, so containerization did not establish any need to use a personal phone.
