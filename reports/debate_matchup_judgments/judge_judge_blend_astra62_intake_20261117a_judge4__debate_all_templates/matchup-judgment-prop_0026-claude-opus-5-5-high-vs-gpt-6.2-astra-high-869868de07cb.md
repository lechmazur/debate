### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): In an exceptionally sharp philosophical exchange, Side A built the fuller and more decisive case for the proposition as written. Side B cleverly avoided defending medical paternalism by distinguishing interpersonal authority (what binds the surgeon) from first-personal deliberative reasons (what guides the patient), and won narrow sub-clashes on lifelong incapacity [B2.6] and the Jehovah's Witness faith analogy [B4.5-B4.6]. However, Side A decisively exposed the cost of Side B's strategy. First, across every collision on the table, Side B conceded that bodily autonomy governs without exception—granting patients "the right to be wrong without being overruled" [B4.9]. Second, by narrowing dignity to "equal human standing," Side B abandoned real-world bedside appeals to dignity (which lose to autonomy or fail on their merits) in favor of a principle that is completely silent across nearly all standard medical choices, such as ventilators and transfusions [A4.3-A4.5, B4.3]. Even in Side B's lone edge case of a woman deferring surgery out of internalized sexism, dignity defeats a sexist conviction rather than bodily autonomy itself, while autonomy remains the overriding moral constraint.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Q2 [A3.2] and Rebuttal 2 dilemma [A4.3-A4.5] proved decisive: Side A showed that real bedside appeals to dignity consistently lose to bodily autonomy, while Side B's redefined "equal human standing" gives no guidance at all in typical medical choices (forcing Side B's admission in [B4.3] that it "prescribes neither blood nor ventilation") and still yields to the patient's veto when tested.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by sustaining a precise distinction between third-party authority and the moral weight of reasons inside a personal medical choice. A showed that honoring a veto does not make autonomy outweigh dignity for the patient, and that B’s “only through consent, therefore lesser” test fails for values the patient herself finds decisive. B never neutralized that metric dispute and largely restated that non-interference always wins.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2: answering that cancellation must be honored yet dignity still weighs more on whether she should refuse care for a sexist reason, then using the Witness case to show a non-enforceable value need not sit beneath bare choice.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the decisive metric clear: in personal medical choices, the value that settles what happens to a competent patient's body carries the relevant moral weight. It forced Side A into repeated concessions that dignity can only advise, persuade, or be rejected without reversal, then framed that as autonomy prevailing in every live conflict. Side A's authority-versus-reasons distinction was elegant and kept the debate competitive, but it never produced a personal medical choice where dignity should override bodily autonomy, and its best example still left the patient's veto intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal built the cleanest trap: actual dignity appeals are contested and lose to consent, while Side A's redefined dignity is either silent or merely advisory. Side A answered well, but by honoring the cancellation it conceded the practical outcome that decided the debate.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins a high-level debate by staying anchored to the proposition as written—comparing bodily autonomy against appeals to dignity in personal medical choices—and dismantling Side B's central philosophical move. Side B argued that because equal worth (dignity) grounds consent, certifies voluntariness, and protects non-choosing patients, autonomy cannot outweigh its source. Side A cleanly refuted that inference ([A4.1], [A5.4]): equal worth can justify granting an individual final authority over their body without giving competing appeals to dignity power to override that authority, and even winning the argument about dignity confers zero permission to operate ([A4.3]). Side A also persuasively separated dignity's reach over incapacitated wards from its priority where choices exist ([A4.2]) and showed that checking for pressure verifies only that a choice is autonomous, not worthy ([A5.1]). Side B landed real pressure in [B4.3] and [B5.2] with willing-clinician healthy-limb amputation and lethal-prescription cases, forcing Side A to invoke clinical harm beyond consent ([A5.2]), but Side A's core asymmetry held firmer overall.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's [A4.1] (reinforced by [A5.1]) broke the spine of Side B's "derivative value cannot outweigh its source" thesis by showing that a principle can justify giving someone the final say over their body without giving everyone who invokes that principle an equal vote to override them—a distinction Side B largely repeated past in [B5.1] rather than refuting, though Side B's [B4.3] counterexample to Q2 was a close runner-up.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B better defended the motion’s actual comparison: once equal worth assigns a patient authority over their body, a competing appeal to what a dignified life requires still cannot authorize an unwanted incision. Side A’s repeated claim that every pro-autonomy reason is really dignity, and therefore dignity is heavier, blurred “dignity as the basis of authority” with “appeals to dignity” that try to override a choice. B’s distinction between grounding a final say and letting others vote on its content survived A’s pressure, while A’s closing overread B’s last sentence as a concession it did not make.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 separation of a dignity-based reason for giving the patient the final say from a competing dignity appeal that would set that answer aside, which directly blocked A’s “you conceded it’s dignity” route.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the central clash on the practical authority question: dignity may explain why persons deserve respect, but it cannot replace the patient’s consent or allow another person’s dignity account to override a competent refusal. Side A’s dignity-as-source and incapacity arguments were strong, but B’s grounding-versus-priority distinction preserved the live route: equal worth can justify giving the patient final say without giving every competing dignity appeal an equal veto. B’s infected-limb asymmetry—where the surgeon can win the dignity argument yet still lack permission—was the cleaner reason to vote PRO. A’s healthy-limb and lethal-prescription examples showed autonomy is not absolute, but they did not defeat the core claim that autonomy carries greater weight in the decisive refusal cases.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to A’s “dignity supplies autonomy’s justification” pressure: a principle can justify giving someone final authority without letting others invoke that same principle to override the decision, and the surgeon may still lack permission after winning the dignity argument.
