### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): PRO won by building its case around the parent's dual role as guardian and paid publisher, and by keeping that frame central through every round. It argued that a legal "request" functions as a veto for risk-averse parents. It raised the futility of de-identification and the tension between distrusting parents as judges while trusting them as archivists. PRO answered each of these. It separated custodial storage from adjudicating one's own revenue. It also flipped the permanence argument, since private retention is reversible while continued exposure is not. CON's A2 answer, that the teenager argues her own side at the family table, did not meet PRO's point about power asymmetry. CON's closing dilemma, "procedure without protection or state-supervised editing," was sharp. However, PRO had already pre-empted it: a parent can still win, but only by giving reasons to a neutral third party. That is a real gain short of a veto. Both sides kept good evidentiary discipline.

PRO answered in A5.3: storing a file is custodial and costs the parent nothing, while ruling on one's own revenue is adjudication under a conflict of interest. This neutralized CON's best structural attack and reinforced PRO's core framing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B was the sharper rhetorician. Lines like "it is an invoice" and "the veto, now wearing a hearing" landed, and B made real pressure points: the dependence and filing-burden problem, the vagueness of "embarrassing," and the risk that the standard swallows a parent's own story. A answered B's pressure questions directly and concretely. A1 named a decision-maker (a platform reviewer with appeal to a youth-privacy regulator) and a test (identifiability, sensitivity, objection). A2 defined what counts as removal. That defused the "unassignable veto" and "unlisting isn't removal" attacks reasonably well. The decisive problem for B came in its own A2. To answer the case of a parent who refuses, B proposed imposing a legal duty on platforms to act on a featured minor's complaint. That is essentially a stronger legal right for the adolescent to request removal, which is what the motion asks for. A's closing exploited this cleanly. B's response was to recast the motion as a right held specifically against the mother, with a tribunal attached. That rests on B's own narrowed reading of the motion as written. As a result, B's alternative ends up conceding the mechanism. What survives is the narrower worry about parental speech and how wide the "sensitive context" net reaches, which matters but does not outweigh the core relief both sides now accept.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's Q2 ("what in your alternatives actually removes it when the parent refuses?"). B answered by proposing a platform duty triggered by the minor's complaint. A's closing then showed that this duty concedes the motion's core: the adolescent's objection must be able to override the uploader's refusal. B's turn on "Call it a duty or a right" was clever but could not fully undo the concession.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): B won because it survived the hardest pressure and turned CON's strongest alternative into a version of the right itself. A's opening and rebuttals were sharp, especially on the dependent teenager and the mother's speech, but B answered the "who decides and under what test" challenge concretely and satisfied a workable identifiability-sensitivity-objection standard. B also maintained that removing the searchable monetized source remains meaningful despite reuploads. A's fallback platform duty was effectively a conceded removal right, weakening its opposition to the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's A1/A2 response was the decisive exchange: it named a platform reviewer and youth-privacy regulator, clarified that unlisting alone does not count, and then closed by showing that a platform duty triggered by a minor's complaint only works if that complaint carries legal weight.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Both sides debated at a high level, but Side A built the cleaner path to victory. A answered B's sharpest pressure — who decides and under what test — with a concrete, workable standard (platform reviewer, appeal, identifiability + sensitivity + objection) and a clear de-identification remedy, rather than dodging. B's strongest remaining route was the "platform duty" alternative, but A's closing exposed that a duty triggered by a featured minor's complaint only functions if the objection can defeat the uploader's refusal — i.e., it collapses into the very right the motion proposes — gutting B's core distinction. B's "veto over the family record" and "mother can't name her daughter" lines were rhetorically potent and landed real damage on breadth, but A had consistently reframed the clash as distribution of a nonconsenting person's identity versus private preservation of memory, and B never pressed the unstable-embarrassment point after the opening. Both sides handled pressure questions well with proper A1/A2 format; B's rhetoric was punchier, but A did more decisive argumentative work in the late rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Q2 ("what in your alternatives actually removes content when the parent refuses?") forced B to concede a platform takedown duty, and A's closing ("Call it a duty or a right, the adolescent's objection must be able to defeat the uploader's refusal") neutralized B's main alternative, showing it delivers the same mechanism B claimed to oppose.
