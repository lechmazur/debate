### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Both sides argued well. A built a strong conflict-of-interest case: one vertically integrated firm runs the order book, holds custody, and operates a house desk, and that structure invites front-running and misuse of client funds. A carried that case through to the end. Its sharpest late move was arguing that B's own guardrails (audit trails, firewalls, insider disclosures) concede the very risks securities law exists to address. B kept two routes alive that A never fully neutralized. - **Structural incompatibility leading to mass delisting.** A's answer that disclosures can be tailored, citing commodity trusts and royalty pools, was met with B's point that those structures rely on trustees holding cash-flowing assets. - **Offshore migration.** A's replies ("regulatory blackmail," "a few determined gamblers") were weak. B also turned A's own concession on personal-wallet withdrawals against it, showing users could move offshore easily. B answered the pressure questions directly with concrete conduct rules: banning front-running, ring-fencing affiliates, and verifying 1-to-1 reserves. That made its alternative regime concrete rather than merely "light." B's weak spot was the claim that separating functions exists mainly because of credit settlement. That is contestable, and A correctly pointed to conflict prevention instead. A also had a fair point that B's "light" regime looked heavier than the label suggested. Neither point fully overcame B's comparative case on outcomes. Net: a narrow edge to B for better adaptation and turning A's own answers against it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5: B combined A's acceptance of mass delistings (A4.2) with A's admission that personal-wallet withdrawals continue (A4.1). Together, these showed that delisting cannot sever access and instead pushes retail users offshore, which undercut A's claim that delisting protects savers.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B built the stronger comparative case. A's conflict-of-interest story was vivid and well-grounded, but B answered it with a plausible distinction between pre-funded spot crypto and credit-settled stock markets, showing targeted guardrails could address predation without forced broker-dealer fragmentation. More decisively, B turned A's own remedy into an offshore risk: if domestic venues delist most tokens, users can still buy commodity crypto onshore, withdraw to personal wallets, and reach unregulated foreign venues. A never fully resolved that route to harm. B also handled pressure directly and reframed securities registration as structural mismatch rather than investor protection, while A's closing leaned on asserting that B's proposed safeguards were concessions without defeating B's onshore/offshore comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive exchange was the B4.5 collision: A's delisting solution plus personal wallet self-custody undercuts the claim that domestic users are protected by severing access to unregistered tokens, since ordinary crypto withdrawals can still bridge them to unregulated offshore venues.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined, well-grounded, and followed format cleanly, making this a high-quality exchange. Side A built a strong principled case (same risk, same standard; vertical integration breeds predation) and landed sharp pressure questions, especially the "umpire betting against the players" framing. But Side B consistently answered with concrete mechanisms A never fully neutralized: pre-funded spot settlement removes the credit-era rationale for forced fragmentation, registration demands corporate issuers that autonomous protocols and foreign developers cannot supply, and the resulting delisting cascade plus personal-wallet withdrawals empties domestic venues while pushing retail to offshore black boxes. Side A's counter-responses ("regulatory blackmail," "severing banking rails," tailored disclosure forms) were more assertion than mechanism, and B turned the wallet-withdrawal point against A's own case. Side A's closing concession-trap was effective, but B had preempted it by framing targeted guardrails as stopping predation upfront without mismatched plumbing. B's endgame weighing—regulation judged by who it actually protects—gave the cleaner reason to vote, while A's endgame rested largely on a values label. Narrow but consistent edge to B across the decisive clashes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal collision argument (B4.5): it took Side A's own concessions—that foreign and autonomous issuers will not file registrations, forcing mass delistings—and combined them with A's defense of personal wallet withdrawals to show the securities mandate empties regulated domestic venues while herding retail into offshore markets, turning A's investor-protection frame against itself. Side A's best counter was the closing concession-trap (A5.1) arguing B's guardrail list admits the very conflicts securities law exists to solve, but B's preemption in B5.1 blunted it.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides were sharp and well-structured, but PRO kept the decisive clash anchored to the motion's actual comparison: securities-style regulation versus a "lightly supervised" category. CON's best routes were the compliance mismatch, the risk of delisting and offshore migration, and the claim that relying on exemptive authority admits securities law does not fit. Those routes were real but only partly sustained. PRO answered the mismatch point by noting that securities law already tailors disclosure forms across bonds, funds, and partnerships. PRO answered the delisting point by separating leaderless commodities from active promoters who refuse to disclose. PRO's Q2 then exposed a tension in CON's case: CON kept adding strict segregation, proprietary-trading bans, and debt audits, drifting away from "light" supervision. CON's answer that the regime is "light relative to securities law" was serviceable, and its argument that one-to-one reserves make net capital unnecessary was a good counter. PRO's closing tied the case together effectively. It argued that platform-run listing disclosures embed the same conflict of interest PRO had attacked throughout, and that promised reserves within a bundled firm resemble FTX's own promises. CON's offshore-migration warning remained partially live but was asserted more than demonstrated. Overall, PRO gave the cleaner reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 and A4.3–A4.4 did the most damage. It answered CON's "exemptive authority equals concession" argument by citing tailored disclosure across non-equity securities and electronic custody. It also turned CON's increasingly strict tailored regime into an admission that light supervision is inadequate, while still identifying gaps in issuer disclosure compulsion and firm structure.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side A built the cleaner path to victory by keeping the debate focused on what most platforms actually do: centralized intermediaries listing venture-backed tokens and bundling exchange, brokerage, and custody functions. A effectively neutralized B's strongest pressure on delistings and wallet custody, and exposed the lighter category's reliance on platform self-policing and after-the-fact audits. B's misfit and offshore-migration arguments were real but less decisive after A's exemptive-authority and issuer-disclosure responses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 was the most decisive exchange: it distinguished vanished developers from active opaque issuers, showed delisting does not freeze the underlying blockchain, and explained how qualified custody can release tokens to personal wallets, turning B's strongest pressure into a reason securities regulation adapts rather than fails.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases, but A controlled the decisive clash slightly better. A's live route—that only securities law compels issuer-level disclosure and function separation, whereas B's "distinct category" delegates policing to volume-paid platforms—survived the exchange: B4.1's platform listing standards left the conflict-of-interest problem standing, and A's closing landed it cleanly. B's two main routes (delisting forces offshore migration; exemptive authority concedes misfit) were each answered squarely by A (blockchains still run without retail mass-marketing; securities law already tailors forms across bonds/funds/partnerships and adapted paper-to-electronic custody), and B mostly repeated the concession frame rather than dismantling those counters. B's "strict light supervision" tension, exposed by A's pressure questions, was answered plausibly but left the label stretched. A also narrowed wisely, conceding leaderless commodities to defend the "most platforms" wording of the motion. B was rhetorically sharper and never collapsed, keeping this competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 (Rebuttal 2): A's direct A1/A2 answers turned B's pressure back—distinguishing vanished developers from refusing promoters, explaining custodians release tokens on withdrawal, and reframing exemptive authority as ordinary tailoring rather than failure—then recombined them into the argument that B's regime still leaves the issuer-disclosure gap and the bundled-firm crash risk unaddressed.
