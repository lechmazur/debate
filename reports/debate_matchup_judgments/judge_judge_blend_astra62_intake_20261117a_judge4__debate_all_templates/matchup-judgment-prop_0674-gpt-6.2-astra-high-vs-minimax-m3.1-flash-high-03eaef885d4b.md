### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO wins because its central asymmetry survived the whole exchange. Moscow can veto a settlement's terms, but it cannot veto reliable aid. PRO also argued that a Western push for an early signature shifts time pressure onto Kyiv and teaches Moscow that holding out pays. CON's case rested on a conditional: an "enforceable regional settlement" with "credible guarantees." PRO repeatedly pressed on how those guarantees would actually be enforced: - Verification does not compel governments to act. - Governments that are already fatigued would have to act while also fearing they could unravel a ceasefire. CON never answered who enforces the guarantees. In its closing it asserted "automatic consequences" without a mechanism. CON's case was also blurred by several moves: - It conceded that aid should continue as leverage. - It claimed that Ukraine's stronger bargaining power is a cost. - It made a muddled "paradoxically" line in Rebuttal 1. CON's best point was that coercion of Moldova need not require diverting troops. It arrived late, and PRO had already partially addressed it through the Moldovan government's calculus. PRO's A1/A2 answers were direct and clear. CON's answers partially conceded weak punishment under fatigue and then pivoted to settlement design.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Pressure Question Q2 and its Rebuttal 2 follow-up: governments reluctant to sustain aid today would have to enforce consequences tomorrow while also fearing they would unravel a ceasefire. Combined with the closing point that "Moscow gets a vote" on the settlement but not on aid, this exposed that CON's direct-deterrence settlement depended on the same Western resolve it claimed to replace.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won the central comparative clash: B's deterrent depended on obtaining and enforcing credible regional guarantees, while A showed a push to conclude quickly weakens leverage to get them and that governments hesitant to sustain aid would be even less reliable enforcers once they had a ceasefire to preserve. A's aid mechanism did not require Moscow's signature and answered the Moldova pressure with force-diversion and demonstrated endurance, whereas B's fallback that aid continues if Moscow rejects conceded that aid, not the push for settlement, supplies the backup deterrent.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A's pressure Q2 followed by Rebuttal 2: if Western fatigue undermines sustained aid, what specific consequence would those same governments enforce after a ceasefire? B answered only with vague consultations and responses, leaving its enforceable-settlement route without a credible enforcement mechanism.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by defining the live distinction between talks now and a Western push for an immediate signature, then showing that the latter risks signaling fatigue and pressuring Kyiv while leaving Moscow expecting better terms through delay. Side B’s direct-versus-indirect deterrence frame was sharp, but it relied too heavily on an idealized enforceable settlement without adequately solving Russian consent, Western enforcement credibility, or the fatigue problem exposed in pressure questions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to “credible guarantees” and Western fatigue: verification alone does not compel enforcement, and governments anxious to preserve a ceasefire may be less willing to punish later coercion, whereas sustained aid removes Moscow’s expectation that waiting will produce softer Western pressure on Kyiv.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built a concrete, persistent causal mechanism. The cost of conquest is not the same as the cost of coercion. During active combat, disrupting Western supplies has a high immediate payoff, which gives Moscow a reason to pressure Poland and other transit states. A monitored halt removes that payoff even when sabotage is deniable. CON paired this with conditional, reversible sanctions relief that gives Russia something to lose through restraint. It also answered pressure honestly, admitting that if Moscow refuses, no new deterrent appears but nothing is lost. PRO (Side B) made reasonable points on sequencing and enforcement: a pause can allow reconstitution, and aid supplies enforcement capacity. However, its rebuttals stayed generic and leaned on assertions about "showing coercion fails." It cited a nonexistent reference (C1.2). PRO also conceded that Moscow "might still prefer vulnerable targets," which weakened its claim of wider deterrence. CON then turned PRO's own line that "Ukraine can negotiate while support continues" into an admission that credible refusal comes from weapons, not from postponing talks. PRO never squarely rebutted the "smaller prize" mechanism. CON's framing arguably blurs the motion by keeping Ukraine armed alongside the settlement push. PRO only partially exploited that, so CON holds the clearer live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.5 decided the debate. CON seized on PRO's "Ukraine can negotiate while support continues" to argue that credible refusal depends on arms rather than postponing talks. That undercut PRO's sequencing argument. CON added that Moscow need not be compelled to accept monitoring, because relief is simply withheld without verification. PRO's closing never answered this directly.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by distinguishing costly conquest from wider coercion and sabotage: NATO risk stays constant, but a monitored settlement reduces the immediate payoff from disrupting supplies while conditional, reversible relief gives Moscow something to lose. A argued aid raises invasion costs and enables enforcement, but never squarely neutralized that payoff-reduction mechanism or B's no-disarmament, no-recognition, refusal-costs-nothing settlement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A1 was decisive: a delayed shipment can decide a battle during combat but may arrive in time during a halt, so sabotage becomes less valuable even when attribution and reprisals are uncertain.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B built the clearer live route by defining wider aggression to include coercion and sabotage against supply states, then arguing that a monitored halt reduces the wartime payoff for such coercion while conditional sanctions relief preserves leverage. B also preserved optionality: if Moscow refuses, Kyiv keeps arms and no relief is granted. Side A’s deterrence-through-strength case was coherent, but it was less effective against B’s target-selection and sabotage-payoff arguments and sometimes treated negotiation as capitulation despite B’s no-ultimatum framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal answered A’s pressure questions directly: during active combat, delaying a shipment can change a battle, while during a monitored halt the same delay is less valuable; if Moscow refuses talks, Kyiv keeps its defenses and no relief is granted. This narrowed the clash and gave B the cleaner weighing.
