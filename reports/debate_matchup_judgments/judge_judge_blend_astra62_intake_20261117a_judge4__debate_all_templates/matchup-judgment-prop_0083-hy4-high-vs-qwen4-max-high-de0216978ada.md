### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): This was a close, high-quality debate, and PRO edged it. PRO built a coherent case on three pillars: an asymmetry of costs, a provenance test ("was there ever a shutter?"), and self-report enforcement ("ask the sender"). In Rebuttal 1, PRO turned CON's enforceability objection against CON's own narrower rule: proving a photo is "materially false" requires the very forensics CON warned about. PRO also answered both pressure questions candidly. It conceded that no rule catches a determined liar at upload, then explained how the rule turns ambiguous silence into a sanctionable false statement. CON scored real hits on three points. First, it argued that a false "No" is process noncompliance rather than proof of a false person. Second, it exposed PRO's uneven scope, since friend-drafted lines are "honest too" yet excluded from the badge. Third, its closing reframed the motion as "labels process, not betrayal." However, CON's answer on chat attribution ("approval makes it an accountable act") felt less intuitive than PRO's point that daters infer a particular mind behind the words. CON's material-correspondence test also left open who judges resemblance before the date. On the motion as written, which asks platforms to treat undisclosed AI as deceptive, PRO's policy rationale and weighing of residual errors gave the slightly cleaner reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2.1–A2.3 was the most decisive exchange. It showed that CON's alternative rule, punishing materially false photos and bait-and-switch appearances, depends on the same detection capacity CON called impossible, so the unenforceability objection hurts CON too. CON's best counter was B5.1, which argued that "silence becomes a claim" manufactures deception rather than identifying it. That counter kept the margin narrow.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): B built the clearer affirmative case: dating profiles create expectations about both appearance and personal attribution, disclosure preserves the recipient’s choice at low cost, and self-reporting makes previously ambiguous silence an actionable representation. A offered a serious material-correspondence alternative and exposed real weaknesses in B’s treatment of dishonest uploaders and non-AI ghostwriting, but its narrower anti-fraud rule left the pre-disclosure impression and attribution concerns less convincingly addressed. B’s comparisons and memorable framing made the decision clearer without relying on unsupported evidence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to the enforcement pressure was decisive: disclosure cannot stop every liar, but it converts silence into a discrete false statement and shifts the inquiry from subjective resemblance to sender-known provenance. A’s response that this merely creates process noncompliance was sharp, but did not fully defeat B’s argument that platform disclosure duties can themselves define the expectations on which users reasonably rely.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): PRO wins on the cheap-disclosure asymmetry and the provenance line: a generated face or drafted persona replaces the signal daters rely on, while disclosure asks the one party who knows. CON's material-correspondence test leaves attribution and recognizability to be judged after the date, and its endorsement answer does not fully explain ghostwritten charm. A's process-versus-person objection is strong, but B's self-report/default mechanism and error weighing give the cleaner reason to vote PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was B's first rebuttal turning enforceability: disclosure is self-report by the uploader who knows, whereas CON's material-falsity rule requires forensic comparison after harm, undercutting CON's surveillance objection.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): CON wins because it gave the cleaner path to a decision. It defined deception as misrepresenting who you are and what you want, and two concrete contrasts made that test stick: an accurate AI-relit photo versus an authentic but outdated photo of a married man. CON also turned PRO's own concession that a secret human ghostwriter "would mislead too" into a lasting structural attack. If human help misleads and carries no label, the badge tracks software rather than deception. PRO's strongest route was timing. It argued that synthetic wit and vulnerability harvest trust before intent can ever be checked, and that endorsement cannot cure this. That argument stayed partly live and was well stated in the closing. PRO's other answers were weaker: - Its A2 reply to the accurate-likeness question fell back on the "wax model" claim that a lens was involved. CON credibly said that is not a belief users swipe on. - PRO's answer to the ghostwriter question leaned on administrability, which CON correctly said is not the same as deception. CON answered PRO's Q1 directly with concrete examples: a lie about intent, a fabricated dead sister, and a bot running unread chats. That showed its truth test is not toothless. CON's points that scammers won't self-label and that honest translators and accessibility users will carry the stigma also went largely unanswered beyond PRO's assertion that a neutral label need not shame anyone. Overall, CON's rebuttals narrowed the clash more sharply and its rhetoric was more memorable ("census of the honest," "provenance can only be confessed").
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's B2.1 and B3.1 seizing on PRO's concession that a secret human ghostwriter misleads too. CON argued this means the motion's label tracks the tool, not the deception. PRO's A1 reply fell back on a "scalable, repeatable source class," and CON in B4.6 and the closing recast that as administrability rather than deception, leaving PRO's principle and policy visibly apart.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): A won a close, high-quality debate by more effectively exposing the gap between deception and AI provenance. Its accurate-likeness example and use of B’s human-ghostwriter concession showed that the rule can brand honest users while leaving equivalent human assistance unlabeled. B’s strongest answer—that attraction is formed before later sincerity checks, using apparent personality as evidence—remained a live and substantial route, but its final defense leaned heavily on an asserted interface default, camera provenance, and administrative scalability. A’s enforcement objections were sometimes overstated after B denied that labels certify safety, preventing a larger margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal turned B’s ghostwriting concession and wax-model analogy into a sharp dilemma: the label either tracks substantive misrepresentation, including unlabeled human ghostwriting, or merely tracks software and lens provenance. B’s timing-based closing answered part of this but did not fully repair the over- and under-inclusiveness.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing the label tracks provenance rather than deception: it brands accurate, endorsed AI assistance and vulnerable users while missing secret human ghostwriting, old misleading photos, and non-compliant scammers. PRO's timing and source-substitution argument powerfully challenged CON's truth test for conversation, but its answer on accurate likenesses reduced the false belief to camera provenance, and administrability did not establish deception.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it directly answered the pressure questions with intent and fabrication examples, exposed the accurate-photo retreat to the wax-model point, and reframed disclosure as a census of the honest rather than detection of the dangerous.
