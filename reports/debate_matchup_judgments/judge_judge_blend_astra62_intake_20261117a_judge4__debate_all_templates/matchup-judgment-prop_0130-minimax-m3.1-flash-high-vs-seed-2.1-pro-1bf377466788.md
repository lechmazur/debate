### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON wins narrowly because its case stayed coherent across all rounds. Its core point was that irreversible loss makes title transfer hollow when foreign custody is demonstrably safer. It also argued that once title passes, no external enforcer remains. PRO never squarely neutralized this "no global sheriff" point. PRO's answers leaned on institutional reform, intervention, and emergency relocation, and CON credibly called these toothless after a sovereign transfer. PRO built a solid legitimacy frame. Its Q1 pressure on CON's "final authority" concession was sharp. However, PRO hurt itself in several ways: - Its first rebuttal was clipped, which is a real execution cost. - In A1 it conceded that continued foreign custody "at least preserves the object." - Its closing was muddled. It said the dispute "concerns who bears risk, not who has authority," which is CON's framing. Its "circularity" argument was hard to follow. CON has its own weaknesses: - It generalized without support that "many returned objects" were lost or re-looted. - It framed the motion as immediate, unconditional transfer, which somewhat strawmans PRO's safeguarded-return model. - Its phased-repatriation model partly converges with PRO's position. Even so, CON gave clearer weighing (irreversibility versus symbolic title) and delivered it more memorably.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 and B4.3 argued that CON's shared governance already grants "final authority," so the museum is left with mere custody. CON answered in A4.1–A4.2 that the real difference is who bears the risk of irreversible loss. It added that nothing can enforce conservation once full title passes to a sovereign state. That answer largely defused the concession charge and kept CON's preservation route alive.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): A built the stronger path by treating preservation as manageable rather than a permanent veto: return could restore title while retaining foreign custodianship, loans, and conservation partnerships. B’s irreversible-loss concern was forceful, but it largely assumed unsafe physical relocation and never fully neutralized A’s conditional-return model. A also exploited B’s shared-governance concession and showed the preservation veto could become circular.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s pressure on B’s concession that origin communities would hold final authority over phased repatriation crystallized the clash: if that authority is genuine, B’s position leaves only custody/timing, and using preservation to deny title becomes self-justifying.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by keeping the debate on the proposition as written—whether superior preservation can defeat restitution—while Side A gradually conceded the ground that mattered. B's pressure question Q1 exposed that A's "shared governance" model already grants origin communities final authority over interpretation, display, access, and phased repatriation, which is functionally return minus title. A's A4.1 answer (risk of irreversible loss) was honest and its strongest ground, and its use of the motion's "even when" clause was clever, but B4.3/B5.1 pressed the concession effectively and B's closing circularity point (the object is endangered by the return its rightful owner could prevent, so the incumbent keeps it forever) crystallized the clash memorably. A's A4.2 enforcement answer leaned on existing museum governance structures, which B had already criticized as leaving the foreign institution as rule-setter, making it somewhat circular. A performed well—plausible mechanisms, no fake sourcing, responsive pressure answers—but B made the debate easier to decide and answered more pressure squarely. Both sides argued the motion rather than replacing it; B simply had the cleaner path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q1 (B3.1) asking how A's "shared governance with final authority" differs from returning title except in timing, followed by B4.3/B5.1 pressing the concession, was the decisive exchange—it forced A into defending conditional phased repatriation, which concedes the legitimacy principle at the heart of the motion.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): CON (A) wins a clear but competitive decision. PRO (B) opened with the more vivid case. Its strongest points were that preservation capacity was built partly from colonial plunder, and that "survival" means more than polished metal in a London basement. After the opening, though, PRO kept repeating the same museum-failings list (uncatalogued backlogs, damage, deaccession sales) almost word for word. CON adapted more effectively. It disowned the museum-veto position PRO attacked and put the burden of proof on the holding museum. It specified independent assessment with fixed deadlines and immediate ownership transfer, and it answered both pressure questions directly. CON also used PRO's A2 concession that phased return is acceptable for concrete, verifiable risks. That concession undercut PRO's categorical framing. PRO's closing then argued for immediate, unconditional return because no neutral mechanism can exist. This sat in tension with its own earlier concession, and the closing never squarely engaged CON's object-specific framework. CON's closing did have a muddled passage that nearly argued PRO's side, saying preservation cannot decide title. It also leaned on a narrow reading of the motion that converged with PRO. Overall, though, CON did more to narrow and resolve the clash, while PRO relied on structural-power assertions and repetition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A4.1–A4.5 answering B3.1/B3.2. CON offered a concrete check: the museum bears the burden, assessment is independent and community-accountable, ownership transfers immediately, and deadlines are fixed. CON then pointed out that PRO's own A2 answer (B4.2) accepts phased return when risks are verified. This exposed the tension in PRO's categorical stance, which PRO's closing tried to escape by declaring all safeguards inherently co-opted rather than answering it.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): B wins by narrowing the dispute to whether preservation can ever condition transfer, not whether colonial theft is wrong. B's framework of immediate legal return, museum/independent community-accountable risk assessment, fixed deadlines, and phased custody answered A's main structural objection workably, and B used A's own admission that concrete risks permit phased return to undermine the categorical proposition. A's no-neutral-mechanism critique was forceful but too totalizing, and B's title-versus-stewardship distinction left the cleaner route to decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's use of A4.2 was decisive: PRO conceded that phased, time-bound return is permissible for concrete risks, which is much closer to B's conditional restitution than to an unconditional rule; B then reframed preservation as governing manner/timing rather than ownership.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by conceding the moral core of restitution and forcing the debate onto narrower, decidable ground: preservation as a stewardship claim governing manner and timing of transfer, not a title claim sustaining the wrongdoer's ownership. A's proposed architecture—independent community-accountable assessors, burden of proof on the holding museum, immediate ownership transfer with custody safeguards, fixed deadlines—gave concrete answers to B's pressure questions, and A's closing turned B's strongest structural argument ("no neutral mechanism exists") against B: if that were true, preservation could never legitimately decide who owns a looted object. A also caught a real tension when B accepted phased return for concrete, verifiable risks [B4.2] while defending a categorical proposition, and leveraged it in [A4.5] and [A5.3]. Side B (PRO) argued vividly—the circularity of plunder-funded stewardship, the historical use of "conditions" as delay tactics, and the double standard on documented museum negligence—but later speeches largely restated these points rather than resolving A's design answers, and the closing leaned on a sweeping, unfalsifiable cooption claim instead of engaging A's title/stewardship distinction. Both sides were grounded without fake sourcing; A made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal and closing: A4.5 exposed that B's own framework [B4.2] accepts independent assessment and phased return, and A5.1 converted B's "no neutral referee" argument into the decisive title-vs-stewardship distinction—preservation can shape restitution but cannot complete the theft.
