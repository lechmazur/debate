### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): PRO's single strongest route was that novelty bias is structural and therefore needs a structural budget rule. It pressed this consistently, backed by a sensible maintenance-budget analogy and a credible point about year-long horizontal waste across a subfield. CON neutralized much of that route. It conceded the bias problem and answered it with a concrete mechanism: independent commissioning authority, a costed slate given first claim before novelty panels make awards, and a public justification requirement for any deferral. Its central argument was the targeting objection. A floor measures spending, not decision-relevant checks, and where it binds it can force a postponable check ahead of an irrecoverable discovery. PRO never fully answered this. PRO's reply that urgent wetland-type projects will rank highly was rebutted by the point that rank among novel proposals does not measure the cost of delay. PRO's closing claim that justifications are effortless was undercut by CON's sharp line that a board meeting its percentage can decline the same check with no reason at all. CON answered both pressure questions directly and adapted across rounds, including a candid concession on Q2. PRO repeated its bias claim in later speeches and cited nonexistent reference IDs ([C1.1], [C1.3], [C1.2], [C2.4]), which is an execution and grounding cost. PRO kept a live structural-bias argument, so the win is clear but competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.1–B4.2 and B5.3 answered PRO's 'first claim is a fiction / executives will throttle it' pressure with a ceiling-and-slate mechanism. CON then turned it: a percentage floor lets a board decline any specific consequential check without explanation once the target is met. That blunted PRO's main structural-bias argument.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing that a fixed share forces the wrong marginal tradeoff and can meet its target while ignoring the checks that actually matter. Its first-claim design, with a public duty to name and defend any deferred consequential replication, answered the bias objection more cleanly than Side A’s claim that justification is mere cheap talk. Side A diagnosed crowding-out well but never neutralized the point that a percentage, once met, permits silent neglect of the highest-value check.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s comparison that a percentage lets officials decline the same important check with no reason at all, while first claim forces them to name the check, the displacing project, and why delay is cheaper.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side A won by separating the need for timely, consequential replication from a fixed spending share. Its independent first-claim mechanism answered the bias objection while exposing the reserve's marginal rigidity, especially through the wetland and delay-cost comparisons. Side B effectively showed discretionary systems underfund replication, but never showed why a percentage guarantees the right checks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing point that a funder can meet its percentage floor while declining the same consequential check without any reason, whereas its proposal requires publicly naming and justifying that deferral.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): PRO wins because it built one coherent mechanism and kept refining it across rounds. Original work earns career credit for its author, while checking a premise benefits everyone who relies on it. A standing reserve therefore changes which proposals get written at all, not just how panels rank the pile in front of them. PRO answered CON's main alternatives with care. On embedded baseline arms, PRO conceded they work when the adaptation is worth testing regardless of the original result, and argued for waiting when the adaptation is only worth funding if the premise holds. On upfront power standards, PRO noted that a better-powered first trial still cannot show what happens in a second set of classrooms. On the risk of a quiet year, PRO answered that the share is set across the whole portfolio, not field by field. CON's strongest moment was seizing PRO's admission that panels may lose a novel project they would have preferred. PRO's closing answered this directly. The best rule for choosing between two finished proposals need not be the best rule to announce before either is written. CON's other answers were weaker. Its reply that novel researchers also bear the risk of rejection was met by the credit-asymmetry point and never restored. Its charge that PRO had retreated from reshaping career incentives was answerable, since PRO only needed enough teams to test pivotal premises. CON's closing timing argument misread PRO's case: a standing reserve is meant to bring replications forward earlier, not to create them after the fact. CON also cited nonexistent reference IDs ([P3.2], [P4.5], [P4.2], [P5.1], [C1.1], [C4.5]), which costs it on discipline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over CON's B4.4 "panels forced to fund the weaker study" point. PRO answered in A4.2 and A5.1 that the reserve changes the set of proposals that exist, not just the ranking of an unchanged pile. Paired with A5.2's point that novel investigators get discovery credit while replicators do not, this neutralized both CON's merit-preference attack and its "everyone bears risk" reply.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): A wins by showing that funding rules must shape which replication studies are prepared before panels choose among finished proposals. B sharply exposes the cost of displacing preferred novel work and offers floating or targeted alternatives, but never fully solves the pre-application recruitment problem that justifies a standing reserve.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal distinguishing a fair floating hearing from a predictable competition worth recruiting partners for, then carrying that proposal-generation point through the closing, was the decisive exchange.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO wins the central fixed-versus-floating clash. CON shows a fixed reserve can displace a preferred novel project, but PRO answers that the reserve changes which proposals are prepared in the first place: teams will not recruit partners for a competition that may fund no replications. PRO also credibly limits embedded replication to cases where simultaneous testing is worthwhile, explains why portfolio-wide demand mitigates quiet years, and distinguishes generalizable replication from local procurement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 distinction between a fair hearing and a predictable opportunity most decisively damaged CON's floating-share alternative, because panels cannot fund strong replication proposals that were never developed.
