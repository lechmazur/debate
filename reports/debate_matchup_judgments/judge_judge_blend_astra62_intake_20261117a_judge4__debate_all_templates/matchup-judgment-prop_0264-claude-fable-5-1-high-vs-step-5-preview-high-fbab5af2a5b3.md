### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): CON (B) anchored the debate to the motion's own criterion—whether misconduct costs stop falling "mainly" on taxpayers—and never let go. B's opening structural case (indemnity, intentional-acts exclusions, city-paid excess, department-subsidized base premium) forced PRO into a series of concessions: the city may still pay catastrophic excess, intentional acts go to decertification, and the base premium is department-funded. When PRO pivoted to deterrence and experience-rating, B correctly flagged this as abandoning the proposition as written, and PRO's closing ("who prices the next failure") confirmed the substitution rather than repairing it. PRO never quantified what fraction of a settlement actually leaves the taxpayer despite being asked twice. PRO's best surviving route—the personal experience-rated surcharge as a condition of insurability—was contested but not fully destroyed, and PRO's pressure questions (especially the exclusions contradiction) were genuinely sharp and answered honestly by B. But B's honest concession that no plan satisfies the criterion, framed as a reason the motion fails, was a coherent negation strategy PRO could not dislodge. Both sides argued cleanly without invented sourcing; B was simply more disciplined about the decision rule and landed the more damaging comparative weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 [B2.1-B2.2] was the decisive turn: catching PRO's shift from "who pays" to deterrence as a retreat from the motion's own purpose clause, then showing that PRO's subsidy concession, excess-layer concession, and exclusion concession together leave the main share of costs exactly where they started—plus an insurer's margin. PRO spent the rest of the debate defending the reframed version, and B's closing [B5.1-B5.3] crystallized that substitution and exposed the internal tension in PRO's claim that rate increases "return to the individual" when the base premium is department-funded.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B (CON) wins solidly by anchoring the debate in the motion's explicit purpose clause—that misconduct costs not fall "mainly" on municipal taxpayers—and proving through insurance and labor economics that PRO's own architecture leaves taxpayers bearing the main cost plus an insurer's margin. Once Side A allowed departmental base-premium subsidies (or union wage pass-through), city payment of excess verdicts, and intentional-act exclusions, Side B demonstrated that individual officer surcharges and deductibles cover only a sliver of seven-figure settlements while insurers pass paid losses back to municipalities at renewal. Side A mounted a polished case for ex-ante risk pricing and asked sharp pressure questions, but reframing the motion around "who prices the next failure" could not overcome Side B's proof that the promised financial transfer fails on its own terms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B4.2]-[B4.3] and [B5.3], Side B turned Side A's claim that carrier payouts shift liability onto private balance sheets by showing that insurers are pass-throughs: because base premiums are department-funded or bargained into wages, paid losses return at renewal as higher municipal rates plus carrier margin rather than landing mainly on an individual officer's surcharge.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by anchoring the debate to the motion’s explicit “mainly” criterion and using Side B’s own concessions—department-subsidized base premiums, city-paid excess, intentional-act exclusions, and premium pass-through—to argue that most misconduct costs remain with taxpayers. Side B offered a compelling risk-pricing and deterrence case, but never credibly showed that the main financial burden shifts off municipal taxpayers, and its shift toward “who prices the next failure” was less faithful to the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal directly answered the pressure questions and turned Side B’s seven-figure limit, city-paid excess, and department-funded base premium into proof that taxpayers still bear the main share, while Side B never answered the fraction question.
