### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO's opening was a serious liability. It read as a neutral encyclopedia entry rather than advocacy, it summarized critics' views, it leaned on a stack of named scholars and journals as authority, and it was clipped for length. CON's opening was cleaner and more focused, framing the debate as coerced presence versus voluntary consent and offering barrier-removal as an alternative. From the first rebuttal onward, however, PRO did more damaging comparative work. It argued that abstention does not withhold authorization, since non-voters are governed and taxed anyway. It argued that blank ballots preserve conscience. It also built a clear incentive mechanism: parties ignore habitual non-voters because ignoring them carries no electoral cost. PRO's Q2 asked what in a voluntary system makes parties court low-income non-voters, and it was the sharpest pressure of the debate. CON answered that competition under easier access would make those voters worth reaching. PRO then turned that answer in its closing: by CON's own logic, parties serve voters who can tip the margin, and reliable affluent voters remain the cheaper target. CON's liberty and consent framing stayed rhetorically strong. Its means-testing and paperwork burden argument was reasonable, but PRO's reply about tax-indexed fines and self-declared exemptions blunted it. CON's claim that blank votes can simply be ignored was asserted rather than developed. On balance, PRO's mechanism-driven case outweighed CON's more principled but largely asserted consent framing, though PRO's opening costs it margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Q2 and CON's A2. CON conceded that parties serve only voters who can tip the margin and relied on access reforms to make low-income citizens reachable. PRO's closing turned this into proof that voluntary systems rationally write off habitual non-voters even when access is easy, which left CON's alternative mechanism weakened.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): A built the clearer path to victory: persistent turnout inequality gives parties little reason to court habitual non-voters, while mandatory ballots change that electoral incentive. A also answered both pressure questions with concrete mechanisms for responsiveness and proportionate enforcement. B offered a forceful consent-based objection and memorable rhetoric, but its voluntary-access alternative never adequately explained why parties would invest in persuading unreliable non-voters. A’s clipped, source-heavy opening and some speculative assumptions keep the win from being dominant.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal and closing turned B’s close-election argument against it: even with easier access, parties still favor reliable voters because reaching habitual non-voters requires extra turnout effort. B’s response asserted renewed persuasion without overcoming that incentive problem.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO built the cleaner causal path: voluntary systems let parties ignore habitual low-income non-voters, while near-universal ballot return lowers the cost of voting preferences and makes reluctant or blank voters a pool rivals must compete for. CON's liberty and enforcement-burden arguments were forceful, but its voluntary alternative never answered why parties would invest in non-voters when winning does not require them, a concession PRO exploited decisively. PRO's clipped, citation-heavy opening hurt grounding, but later rebuttals answered both pressure questions directly with tax-indexed penalties, exemptions, and access obligations.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was PRO turning CON's claim that parties serve voters who can tip margins into proof of voluntary failure: campaigns will still chase reliable affluent voters and write off communities whose turnout is costly and uncertain.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): PRO framed the stakes cleanly: who chooses the government, a self-selected affluent subset or the whole public. Its line that "absence is permission to ignore" remained live and was effective. However, PRO argued almost entirely through asserted mechanism. It never offered a concrete example of compulsory turnout actually shifting party behavior, and it mostly restated "parties must face the whole electorate." CON adapted more across rounds. It developed a distinction between conditional support and mere presence. It also argued that blank ballots change no seats and that high turnout lends winners inflated mandates. Most damaging was the enforcement dilemma: either collection hits displaced citizens, or exemptions become unverified and therefore optional. PRO's A2 conceded that exemptions would be unverified. CON turned that concession into a regressive-fairness argument: secure citizens opt out effortlessly while precarious ones miss notices. PRO never answered this turn beyond repeating "easy waivers." CON's claim that abstention gives non-voters leverage is debatable, and PRO's rebuttal of it partly landed. Even so, CON left PRO's representativeness mechanism contested while cleanly winning the enforcement clash, so CON wins by a clear but competitive margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2 in Rebuttal 2 (A4.3–A4.4) was the decisive exchange. It took PRO's concession of "self-attested," unverified hardship waivers and showed that they make the mandate optional for stably housed citizens. Meanwhile, agencies cannot tell displacement-driven silence apart from "avoidable refusal," so penalties still land on the precarious. That inverts PRO's fairness claim.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B built the cleaner challenge to the motion’s central promise: compulsory attendance does not itself produce accountability, and blank or reluctant ballots can leave organized insiders dominant while removing parties’ incentive to earn participation through mobilization. A persuasively established voluntary-turnout bias and offered humane enforcement safeguards, but increasingly asserted that guaranteed attendance would make parties address neglected interests without answering B’s policy-monitoring and conditional-support argument. B also exposed the waiver dilemma more effectively, though A’s tolerant-enforcement answer kept the debate competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s distinction between physical presence and political leverage was decisive: parties respond to monitored, conditional support, not merely a demographically complete polling line. Its follow-up contrasting automatic tax withholding with crisis-specific voting waivers then sharply answered A’s administrative comparison.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A won the central mechanism clash: B asserted that compelled presence forces parties to serve the whole public, but A showed blank ballots change no seats and reluctant, non-monitoring voters can be won with branding while attentive insiders retain policy leverage. A also trapped the penalty design between harming displaced, hard-to-notify citizens and unverified waivers that let secure citizens opt out easily. B's biased-electorate case remained plausible but its link from attendance to representative outcomes was never fully repaired.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it answered the tax-administration pressure by distinguishing automatic withholding from sudden hardship and outdated addresses, then turned B's self-attested waiver into an upside-down fairness problem.
