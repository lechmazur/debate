### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but PRO (B) kept the debate anchored to the motion's actual wording—"most wasteful" items and "even if" transition costs—and used that framing to force a decisive concession from CON in Rebuttal 2 (A4.2: a narrow phase-out "can be justified" given proof of a workable alternative). PRO's closing capitalized on this cleanly, arguing that "most wasteful" plus "workable alternative" simply is the motion, and that fees merely price waste while a phase-out removes it at the point of disposal—a sharp, memorable instrument-comparison that CON never fully neutralized. CON's strongest material (equity concerns, substitution harms, the demand for proof before removing options) remained live but was increasingly recast as a demand for a certainty threshold the motion's own "even if costs" clause seems to waive, making CON's final retreat ("our concession was never a blank cheque") feel like a narrowing defense rather than an affirmative knockout. PRO's answers to pressure questions, while relying on "the enacting law" as the binding mechanism, were still more responsive and consistent with real policy practice than CON's position, which risked drifting from "ban is premature" toward "no instrument can ever satisfy my evidentiary bar." Rhetorically, PRO's closing crystallized the clash better than CON's, which largely restated prior points.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response to CON's conditional concession (A4.2) in the Rebuttal 2/Closing sequence—turning "if proven, a narrow phase-out can be justified" into "that is exactly the motion"—was the most decisive exchange, since CON never fully escaped this reframing in its closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by establishing a cleaner comparative framework—bounded, policy-manageable transition costs versus open-ended environmental externalities—that directly leveraged the motion’s “even if” language. While CON landed strong blows on substitution risks and implementation uncertainty, PRO successfully showed that CON’s evidentiary threshold would give the status quo a permanent veto, and crystallized CON’s conditional concession on narrow phase-outs as effectively endorsing the motion’s core principle. PRO’s rebuttals were sharper on the asymmetry of proof burdens and the behavioral difference between pricing waste and removing the default.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s second rebuttal (Turn 7), where it answered CON’s pressure questions on evidentiary thresholds and binding protections, turned the “guarantee” challenge back onto CON’s own fee-based alternative, and crystallized the decisive comparison between a fee that monetizes harm and a phase-out that removes the wasteful default.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON (A) built the more durable structural case: that the externality (litter, drain-clogging, cleanup) is best addressed by adjustable, targeted instruments (material-specific EPR fees, deposits, litter-abatement charges) rather than by banning categories of product whose harm depends on disposal location, not mere existence. CON's "foam cup in a bin vs. foam cup in a drain" framing exposed a definitional gap in PRO's "obvious bottom tier" test that PRO never fully closed—PRO's criteria (commonly littered, not typically recycled) remain statistical generalizations about categories, not a precise test distinguishing properly disposed items from leaking ones. The pressure-question exchange was pivotal: PRO's Q1 forced CON to specify a fee mechanism, which CON answered credibly (material-specific, adjustable, with deposits/exemptions); CON's Q1 forced PRO to define its "bottom tier," and PRO's answer (plus conceding that transition funding would come from "producer responsibility fees, small packaging levies") inadvertently showed PRO's model converges on CON's pricing apparatus, undermining the claim that an outright ban is a distinct, necessary addition. PRO's closing attempt to reassert "if the fee is high enough it's just a ban with more paperwork" did not engage CON's prior, specific rebuttal that a high fee preserves availability, allows deposits/refunds to lower the charge, and permits accessibility carve-outs—leaving that counter live and unanswered. CON's closing crystallized the clash cleanly ("harm is a location and a cost"), while PRO's closing mostly restated its "floor, not perfect ranking" framing without landing a fresh blow. Both sides argued with discipline and no fabricated evidence, but CON's rebuttals did more cumulative damage to PRO's distinctive policy claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.1/A4.3 response to the Pressure Questions, which both answered the "fee vs. ban" dilemma (showing a high fee need not equal a ban because availability, deposits, and exemptions remain) and pointed out that PRO's own transition-funding answer (producer levies, EPR) collapses into CON's pricing model—undercutting PRO's rationale for needing an outright phase-out.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON (Side A) persuaded that the proposition mistakenly targets the wrapper rather than the waste. By consistently distinguishing a foam cup in a sealed bin from one in a storm drain, CON showed that leakage and disposal costs can be priced directly through EPR, deposits, and litter fees without the overbreadth of a ban. In Rebuttal 2, CON effectively turned PRO's concessions—transition funds drawn from producer levies and exemption regimes for failing substitutes—into evidence that a ban requires the same administrative complexity as pricing while being less adaptable. PRO's 'hidden subsidy' framing was powerful but never overcame the conflation of the object with the harm, leaving CON's comparative case cleaner after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 [A4.3], where CON demonstrated that PRO's transition financing and substitute exemptions effectively adopt CON's pricing model while adding an unnecessary prohibition, collapsing PRO's claimed administrative simplicity and sharpening the 'target the waste, not the wrapper' distinction.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): PRO kept a live path: defines most wasteful by observable traits (single-use, not curbside recyclable, persists/litters), frames phase-out as a floor correcting externalized cleanup costs, and forces concession that CON's pricing needs EPR/fees too. PRO's decisive comparison - if fee is low it fails on diffuse foam/cutlery litter, if high enough to work it is a ban with more admin - was never squarely defused, while CON's vagueness and regressivity charges were met with phased timeline, exemptions for accessibility, and producer-funded hardship help. Motion explicitly tolerates some inconvenience.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4 push that a calibrated fee either fails to stop drain-clogging litter or must be set so high it replicates a phase-out, plus who pays collection even for safely disposed items, directly undercuts CON's "price leakage, don't ban" route.
