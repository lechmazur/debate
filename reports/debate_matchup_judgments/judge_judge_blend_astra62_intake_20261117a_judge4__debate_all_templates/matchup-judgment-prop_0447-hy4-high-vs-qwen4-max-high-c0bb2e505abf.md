### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): This was an exceptionally tight, high-level exchange. Side A (CON) built a formidable case: the credited-vs-general-revenue dilemma, the "creditor" principle, and joint liability as a surrogate that reaches the make-or-buy moment via expected cost (A5.3 powerfully blunted B's "broom, not a price" dichotomy). A also landed a clean unanswered hit on B's safe-harbour mechanism (A4.6: remittance receipts on short contracts do not establish continuity). However, B's decisive surviving route was the lawful-fragmentation gap: joint liability is capped at euros legally owed, and lawful discontinuity lawfully generates fewer owed euros than direct employment — a liability regime cannot price that sourcing choice. A's best reply ("thresholds are eligibility rules, change benefit rules") addressed the coverage mechanics but not the incentive comparison at the heart of the motion. B answered pressure questions directly (A1/A2 format, clean design clarifications) and set the floor above direct-employment firm cost, which answers A's closing "paying must be worse than covering" objection. B defended the proposition as written — pricing the sourcing choice — with a coherent mechanism that A narrowed but never neutralized. Margin reflects that both sides left their best points partially standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The A4.6 strike — that B's receipt-based safe harbour tests remittance, not continuity, since an agency can remit in full on three-week contracts and still leave qualifying gaps — was the sharpest single blow and went unanswered in B's closing. But B's B4.5–B4.6 sequence, arguing joint liability is backward-looking and capped at a wage-based euro that fragmentation can shrink, while a floor tied to hours bought cannot be erased by re-tendering, was the decisive comparative weighing that A never fully closed out.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side B more convincingly showed that the surcharge either collapses into collecting contributions already owed—better done by joint liability with a named creditor—or becomes a disconnected levy the firm can pay while the worker's record and eligibility gaps stay unchanged. Side A's incentive story never stably explained where the extra euro goes or why paying is worse for the firm than covering the worker.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal and closing, which locked A's "same record, extra share" and "or pay" formulations into a licence rather than coverage, while keeping joint liability as the instrument that both deters and credits the worker.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B wins because its central dilemma survives: PRO's extra euro is either the contribution already owed, a worker credit above ordinary entitlement, or uncredited revenue that leaves the worker uncovered. PRO's hour-based floor answers wage pass-through only by conceding that divergence. B also shows joint and several liability supplies the same ex-ante make-or-buy deterrent while ensuring every euro collected is capped by and credited to a named worker.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 decisively turns PRO's 'same record per hour, extra share from the buyer' formulation into a concession that the surcharge is revenue rather than coverage, and notes that thresholds and waiting periods are eligibility problems a levy does not itself fix.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued at a high level with clean format compliance and no fake sourcing. Side B (PRO) built the stronger path to victory: the compliance-credit design neutralized the double-payment objection, met A's own stated test ("payment linked to risk"), and reframed the dispute as "chase or a gate" — enforcement against a dissolved shell versus a default rate at the only solvent door. B's pressure question forcing A to name the solvent party produced the concession that social insurance honors the claim, which B's closing weaponized as the public fund socializing the discount. A's best line — that a rate falling to zero for compliant chains is a charge for missing paperwork, not the proposition as written — was a sharp meta-attack, but B parried it credibly with "a rate with an exemption is still a rate" and the rebuttable-default logic. A also landed real hits on incidence (exclusion of small suppliers over format mismatches) and the VAT disanalogy, keeping the margin from widening, but B made the debate easier to decide and closed more decisively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5-B4.6/B5.1: B's "chase or a gate" crystallization, followed by the closing blow that the solvent party A finally named is the public fund, converted A's enforcement-based alternative into a concession that the discount is socialized — the decisive exchange of the debate.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by adapting the higher-share rule into a rebuttable default with a credit for verified downstream contributions, which fairly answers double-counting while still attaching the cost of uncollectable gaps to the solvent firm that chooses the chain. Side B lands strong hits on verification burdens, incidence, and fidelity to a pure “when they use” charge, but never fully neutralizes A’s core point—reinforced by B’s own recovery concession—that dissolved shells leave the public fund holding the risk. A’s gate-at-payment framing and closing use of that concession make the comparative choice clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal and closing, which treat B’s “social insurance honors the claim and seeks recovery” as the decisive concession that the solvent party is ultimately the public fund, then weigh a payment-gate presumption against a chase of disappearing debtors.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): PRO's credit answer avoids double-charging but concedes that a lawful, fully insured chain owes nothing extra, shifting the motion from a higher share for using platforms or subcontractors to a charge for missing proof. PRO's dissolving-shell and public-fund arguments were powerful, but did not restore the proposition as written or defeat CON's targeted-liability comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and closing use PRO's own A2 admission—that the buyer owes nothing extra in a lawful, fully insured chain—to show the credit turns the alleged risk levy into a paperwork penalty, while also defeating the VAT analogy.
