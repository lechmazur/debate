### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO wins a competitive debate. Its causal story stayed coherent from opening to closing: an ecosystem treated as an object cannot claim harm in its own right, so the costs of irreversible damage go uncounted. Personhood adds a represented party, and that party constrains infrastructure before harm is locked in. PRO directly neutralized CON's main harm claim, the fear of a local sovereign and an unaccountable guardian. It argued that appointment, conflict rules and review can be legislated in the same grant, and CON's Rebuttal 2 effectively conceded that safeguards can keep a guardian accountable. CON's best line was the additionality and sequencing argument: statutes can create ecosystem standing and duties without personhood, so why grant it automatically on request? This was a real burden on the motion's 'when requested' trigger, and PRO's reply ('communities would not be asking otherwise') was thin. However, CON's position carried a tension PRO's case exposed implicitly: if personhood adds little beyond existing law, its harms are also small, while PRO's irreversibility impact stayed live. CON's opening was also clipped, which cost execution. CON's later speeches shifted ground rather than defending the original exclusion harm. PRO was repetitive, leaning on the 'residue' and 'statutory box' phrases, but it answered both pressure questions directly and framed the stakes more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 answer in Rebuttal 2 settled the representation clash. It held that the guardian speaks only for defined ecological interests (flow, habitat, freedom from unlawful harm), while downstream communities keep their claims and recourse through statutory review and appeal. This forced CON to retreat from the 'local sovereign' harm toward a narrower sequencing and institutional-fit argument.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins a clear, competitive debate by dismantling the mechanism and trigger of the proposition rather than merely defending fast infrastructure. Although Side B (PRO) wrote with stronger rhetorical punch, avoided Side A's minor opening clip, and effectively forced Side A to concede that statutory safeguards can keep guardians accountable, that move exposed a deeper flaw in Side B's case. Once Side B admitted that substantive duties, defined ecological interests, and unlawful-harm standards must still be legislated, Side A showed that direct statutory protections and public-interest standing can impose the same ecological constraints without inventing a separate legal party, while Side B mostly repeated its "object/residue/statutory box" phrasing. Side A also won the clash on sequencing and basin-wide governance: a single local community's petition shows concern, not that existing remedies fail or that granting a new institutional litigant fits a shared river affecting downstream cities, farms, and other Indigenous communities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A2.1]-[A2.4] and [A4.1]-[A4.3], Side A turned Side B's concession that duties and safeguards must still be legislated ([B2.3], [B4.1]) into a decisive additionality and sequencing dilemma, while dismantling [B3.2] by showing that a community's request for recognition proves neither an unfixable statutory gap nor that full personhood fits a shared river.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): A won narrowly by turning CON’s representation objection into a design point and by using CON’s concession that safeguards can be legislated. CON’s additionality and sequencing pressure remained strong, but it never fully negated PRO’s core distinction between statutory protection of an object and giving the ecosystem its own legal party status. PRO’s framing of irreversible harm and the request trigger gave the cleaner affirmative reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal directly answered the shared-river pressure by limiting the guardian to ecological interests and keeping downstream claims intact, which blunted CON’s sovereignty concern while preserving PRO’s burden-shifting advantage.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): CON wins on the motion as written. From Rebuttal 1 onward, CON held PRO to the actual grant: personhood conferred whenever a community requests it, with infrastructure complication accepted in advance. PRO's case leaned more and more on safeguards that are not in the motion, such as transparent appointments, deadlines, downstream representation and balancing. CON's pressure questions made this explicit. PRO answered honestly, conceding that the grant is still required even without those limits and that nothing in the motion prevents a veto. CON turned those admissions into its central argument. CON also offered a coherent alternative: cumulative basin limits, enforced by existing actors such as agencies, cities, landowners and communities that hold standing. This directly answered PRO's best route, the claim that no actor can challenge cumulative harm. PRO's closing nonetheless repeated that CON "identifies no actor," which ignores that answer. PRO's strongest point was that project-by-project approval leaves cumulative harm without a counterparty. It stayed live but was never shown to require personhood specifically. CON's fork argument and its accountability contrast between a river that "cannot testify, settle, or be removed" and humans who can were the cleaner, more memorable framing. Both sides argued with sound grounding and no fake precision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's fork in Rebuttal 2 and the closing proved decisive: "If later design can bind the guardian, it can bind an agency. If it cannot, levees survive only as hope." Paired with PRO's A1/A2 concession that the grant is required regardless and that the motion contains no anti-veto rule, this left PRO's safeguards argument either redundant or insufficient. PRO's closing restated the fork in its own favor but never escaped it.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by proving that legal personhood is either redundant or dangerously unaccountable compared to statutory cumulative basin limits enforced by affected human communities. While Side B smartly defended the motion as an enabling principle rather than a complete statute and highlighted cumulative harm from piecemeal permitting, Side A showed that a river cannot instruct counsel, settle tradeoffs, or be held accountable when flood walls, power lines, and fisheries conflict. Once Side A paired statutory basin caps with standing for affected cities, landowners, and communities, Side B could not show why inventing an uninstructable rights-holder beat enforceable duties that legislatures can still revise.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's two-horned rebuttal in [A2.5] and [A5.4] against Side B's claim that injunctions and vetoes are avoidable design choices ([B2.4], [B5.3]): if statutory deadlines and basin limits can bind actors, they can enforce cumulative protection without inventing a river plaintiff; if they cannot bind an open-ended integrity right, critical infrastructure like flood walls survives only on hope—so PRO cannot use the first branch to prove personhood is safe and the second to prove it is necessary.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B won by keeping the focus on the motion’s actual command: grant legal personhood when requested and accept infrastructure complication. Side A’s guardianship model was principled and plausible, but it increasingly relied on implementation safeguards that Side B successfully treated as additions not supplied by the motion. Side B also extracted damaging concessions in the later rounds: that the request still triggers the grant even if the requester rejects limits, and that nothing in the motion itself stops an open-ended integrity claim from becoming a veto. Side B’s alternative—cumulative standards plus standing for accountable actors—was not perfect, but it was cleaner, more amendable, and gave the reader a clearer reason to reject personhood as the required remedy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s rebuttal and closing fork after Side A admitted the motion contains no rule preventing a veto: if safeguards can bind agencies, personhood is unnecessary; if they cannot bind an open-ended integrity claim, personhood risks blocking necessary infrastructure such as flood walls.
