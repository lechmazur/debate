### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): The first half was competitive. PRO built a structural case: privacy enables dissent and accountability, and surveillance powers expand once granted. CON countered with real force: security is a precondition for exercising rights, and the vulnerable need protection from private violence. CON's targeted-warrant distinction in Rebuttal 1 was a reasonable answer to PRO's mission-creep argument. The debate then collapsed for CON. Its own pressure questions attacked encryption backdoors and security-first framings, which are effectively PRO arguments. In Rebuttal 2, CON answered PRO's questions by conceding them outright. It offered Section 702 as an example of narrow powers expanding to target activists. It admitted that no durable check exists once security is prioritized, since courts defer to executive security claims. It also said domestic-violence and school-shooting harms can be handled without subordinating privacy. This gutted CON's own case. PRO's closing cleanly cashed in these concessions. CON's closing reverted to its opening themes without addressing what it had given away. PRO stayed coherent throughout, while CON contradicted itself on the decisive clash. PRO's own claims, such as surveillance tools being "overwhelmingly" and "routinely" misused, were broad and unsupported. That weakness was not exploited.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 asked for any narrow, warrant-bound surveillance power that was never later expanded. CON's A1 named Section 702 as exactly such an expansion, and its A2 conceded that no enforceable check exists under a security-first framework. PRO's closing turned these concessions into the decisive weighing, and CON never walked them back.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): A wins because B's rebuttal effectively conceded the two decisive mechanisms A needed: real narrow surveillance powers expand beyond their original justification, and security-first frameworks lack a durable check against abuse. A sharpened those concessions in closing, while B's pressure questions misattributed A's premises and B's final reframe could not restore a controlled-security case. A gave the cleaner comparative reason why privacy must hold formal priority.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's pressure questions followed by B's Section 702 answer and admission that no enforceable check remains once security is prioritized—A converted B's own answers into proof that security-first powers expand and become unaccountable.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side B won by holding a clear burden: in a free society, privacy is the structural check that keeps security from becoming unaccountable control. Side A’s pressure questions attacked premises Side B never held, and Side B turned that misattribution into a direct hit. In the second rebuttal Side A then confirmed mission creep, admitted no durable check if security is prioritized, and said the cited harms need not subordinate privacy. The closing tried to restore “security is the prerequisite,” but it never neutralized those concessions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal, which answered the misaimed questions by showing they restated Side A’s own premises, then weighed Side A’s failure to name any narrow surveillance power that stayed narrow.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): B wins by a clear but competitive margin because it ran the more consistent comparative frame and capitalized on A's own answer. A opened strongly. Its historical record of surveillance abuse (COINTELPRO, the NSA metadata program, the near-total FISA approval rate) was its best material and stayed live throughout. Its default-rule argument in the closing was a reasonable reply. But A's support grew increasingly overstated and loosely sourced: - that counterterrorism programs monitored domestic violence support networks; - that the metadata program was "repeatedly abused" against journalists and religious minorities; - new foreign examples (Egypt, Hungary) added in rebuttal, which widened the debate rather than answering it. A's A1 then conceded that narrow, warranted access to encrypted communications is acceptable when there is an imminent threat. That blurred A's own line. A's closing also garbled the concession, attributing it to CON, which was a sloppy misstatement. B consistently distinguished unaccountable dragnets, which it rejected, from bounded and targeted security, which it defended. This let B absorb most of A's historical examples as failures of bad security rather than proof that privacy ranks higher. B also answered both pressure questions directly, giving concrete guardrails in A1. B's closing weighing argument was not airtight: rights can yield in extremes without being secondary. Even so, B turned the concession into a crisp decision rule. A's only answer was its "paper guardrails" point, and that was asserted rather than developed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B4.3/B5.1 against A4.1. B seized on A's admission that narrow, warranted access to stop imminent threats is justified, and argued that a value which yields when lives are at stake cannot be society's primary ordering principle. A's response, that the motion concerns which value is the default, was sensible. But A delivered it late and backed it mainly by repeating the FISA statistic, so it never fully neutralized B's framing.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): B wins because A explicitly conceded that narrow, judicially warranted security can override privacy for imminent threats, which weakened privacy's status as the primary value. B distinguished unaccountable dragnets from targeted accountable security and argued persuasively that security is the precondition for exercising any private rights. A's historical surveillance examples were strong but did not fully neutralize that distinction or the concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3 turned A's admission that targeted warranted access is justified into proof that security can rightly override privacy in high-stakes cases, undermining privacy as the foundational ordering value.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by keeping a cleaner comparison: accountable, targeted security is the floor that makes privacy usable, while the cited abuses are failed dragnets rather than proof that privacy outranks security. Side B’s mission-creep and rubber-stamp critique stayed live, but B conceded warranted access for imminent threats and never fully repaired that tension with its claim that privacy is the higher ordering value. A’s closing made that concession decisive for a third-party reader.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal, which answered the guardrails question with concrete accountability limits and then used B’s own concession that narrow warranted access is justified to undercut privacy as the always-prior value.
