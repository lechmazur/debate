### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins clearly. Its core mechanism held up through the whole exchange: nearshoring depends on low landed cost, Latin American plants import mostly inputs, so protection raises the cost of exactly the exports the policy is meant to attract. CON also turned PRO's evidence against it. It showed that the maquiladora upgrade and USMCA rules of origin were reciprocal market-access bargains, not unilateral walls. It then pinned PRO's infrastructure reply as a concession, since grids and ports are non-excludable public goods that need neither tariffs nor targeted rents. On institutions, CON used PRO's own admission that the region lacks Chile's institutions to argue that conditional subsidies get captured and extended, with Manaus as the example. PRO answered the pressure questions directly by proposing a five-year component tariff plus export credits, which was to its credit. CON then dismantled it: the treasury pays twice, dependence moves upstream, and protection undermines the very export benchmarks PRO relied on for discipline. PRO's 'no alternative' charge was met with Costa Rica and the NAFTA path. PRO's closing largely repeated the Chile and Venezuela framing that CON had already rebutted, and its dismissal of Costa Rica as 'niche' came late and thinly. PRO also relied on some shaky specifics, such as treating USMCA review clauses as a way to enforce subsidy sunsets, and CON correctly flagged this.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.2–A2.3, reinforced in A4.3 and A5.1, did the most damage. When PRO answered the infrastructure gap by saying 'build the grid, that's fiscal spending too,' CON accepted the investment but showed it needs no protection. That left the motion's distinctive cost, input friction and allocable rents, undefended. CON then argued that input tariffs undermine the export-performance discipline PRO relied on to keep subsidies honest, which collapsed PRO's central safeguard.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side B (CON) won the decisive clash. The motion's "even if" clause — accepting protection and fiscal burden as the price — was the live dispute, and B hammered it relentlessly: every time A defended the policy, A's answers reduced to public goods, treaties, and duty-free inputs, which are CON's tools, not the protection the motion demands. B's pressure question (name a tariff you'd defend even when it raises landed costs) forced A to finally name a five-year component tariff, and B's rebuttal trace — tax the assembler's inputs, then pay export credits to offset the damage, while the Ohio buyer sees a higher quote — was the sharpest exchange of the debate. A's best route was export-performance conditionality as external discipline, but B answered it squarely: protection makes exporting dearer, disabling the very discipline mechanism, and USMCA review clauses bind tariffs, not subsidy sunsets. A answered both pressure questions with proper A1/A2 structure, but B's closing ("Manaus with an export clause stapled to it") crystallized why A's hinge fails. Both sides used plausible background knowledge without fake citations; B was more disciplined and more damaging throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3's dismantling of A's five-year component tariff: it exposes the double cost of taxing inputs then subsidizing exports to undo the damage, and notes that "domestic capacity displaces imports" merely moves import dependence upstream — the direct hit that broke A's answer to B's pressure question.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A won decisively by holding Side B to the motion's core burden—increased protectionism and fiscal outlays—and proving that taxing imported inputs directly undermines the cost and speed advantages nearshoring requires. Whenever Side B defended industrial policy via electrical grids, water infrastructure, or USMCA market access, Side A cleanly separated non-excludable public goods and reciprocal trade treaties from unilateral tariffs and firm subsidies. Side A also showed far stronger command of regional political economy, dismantling Side B's mischaracterizations of Chile, Venezuela, USMCA rules, and US IRA/CHIPS subsidies, before exposing the fatal contradiction in Side B's export-conditioned tariff model.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side A's pressure questions forced Side B to propose a concrete five-year auto-component tariff paired with export-benchmark credits and USMCA review clauses ([B4.1]-[B4.3]), Side A dismantled the mechanism in [A4.3]-[A4.5] and [A5.1]-[A5.2]: taxing an assembler's inputs and paying export credits charges a strained treasury twice, raises quotes against Asian competitors immediately, misstates how USMCA polices tariffs rather than domestic subsidy sunsets, and causes firms to miss the very export benchmarks meant to discipline weak ministries ("Manaus with an export clause stapled to it").

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built the cleaner comparative case. It first conceded that both paths carry risk and fiscal cost. It then argued that its path leaves durable capability behind: ports, grids, trained workers and a formal tax base. CON's alternative, by contrast, leaves the region exposed to the same commodity cycle. PRO's rebuttals turned CON's own examples. Brazil's auto sector became evidence against protection without an export test. The maquiladoras became evidence of liberal treatment with no ecosystem-building. Its pressure questions also exposed a real contradiction. In Rebuttal 1, CON claimed that "every import-substitution regime began with export disciplines and sunset clauses." In Rebuttal 2, CON admitted that none did. That self-inflicted reversal gutted CON's main historical route. CON's late pivot had some force: conditionality that relies on USMCA enforcement amounts to outsourced sovereignty. But it came late and only partly addressed PRO's point about statutory sunset clauses. CON also conceded that its own diversified path carries fiscal burden and depends on foreign demand. That concession largely collapsed its "safer" claim into the "familiar risk" framing PRO had already set up.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 asked CON to name one Latin American import-substitution regime that began with export-performance requirements and sunset clauses. CON had to answer "none," which contradicted its own earlier "they all calcified" claim. PRO then used that admission in its second rebuttal and closing to argue that CON's history condemns a design never tried. This was the decisive turn.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won the decisive exchanges. Its pressure question forced B to concede that no Latin American import-substitution regime ever used binding export tests with automatic expiry, gutting B's core historical analogy; A then converted that concession into "untried is not disproven" while noting B's default produced the stagnation being debated. A's reversal of the maquiladora example (it ran on duty-free inputs, i.e., liberal treatment, not protectionism) was the sharpest single move of the debate. A also landed the symmetry argument that Chile's copper/renewables path depends on Chinese demand, European subsidies, and public infrastructure spending too, blunting B's "Washington's permission" and fiscal-burden lines. B fought back well — the political-economy of rents, stranded specialized infrastructure, and outsourcing sovereignty to USMCA enforcement were genuinely live routes, and B's honest A1 concession was good epistemics. But B never fully answered why diversified commodity exposure is "safer" rather than merely familiar, and its closing leaned on the enforcement point A had already partially blunted. A made the debate easier to decide with cleaner weighing: both paths borrow and depend on foreign demand, so choose the one that leaves capability behind.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure Q1 demanding a named ISI regime with export requirements and sunset clauses. B conceded none existed, and A exploited the concession in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, converting B's main historical case into a verdict on a design never tried while pairing it with the maquiladora reversal showing B's other example was a failure of liberalism, not protectionism.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side B built the stronger comparative path to victory by directly defending conditional, export-linked scaffolding and ecosystem investment while dismantling Side A's core historical objections. Side B cleanly flipped Mexico's maquiladoras into an indictment of passive liberalized assembly, forced Side A to retract its claim that past Latin American ISI regimes began with binding export tests and sunset clauses, and extracted a concession that Side A's commodity-and-renewables alternative also requires debt-financed public infrastructure and foreign policy-sensitive demand. Side A recovered impressively late in the debate by attacking Side B's reliance on USMCA enforcement as vulnerable to Washington's political shifts (and Side B made a minor procedural error in Turn 7 by accusing Side A of dropping questions before Side A's answer turn), but Side A never answered automatic statutory expiry, Costa Rica's cluster success, or how capital-intensive commodities and renewables absorb half the workforce out of informality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B2.2]-[B2.3] and [B3.1], Side B neutralized Side A's historical case on two fronts: turning Mexico's maquiladoras against CON by noting they ran on duty-free imported inputs without domestic supplier ecosystems, and catching Side A's false claim in [A2.1] that prior import-substitution regimes began with export disciplines and sunset clauses, forcing an explicit retraction in [A4.1].
