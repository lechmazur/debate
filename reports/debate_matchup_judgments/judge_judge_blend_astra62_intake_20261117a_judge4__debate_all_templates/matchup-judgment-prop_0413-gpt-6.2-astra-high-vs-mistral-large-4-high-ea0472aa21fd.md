### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B built a far more complete, responsive, and principled case. B neutralized Side A's core contract argument by showing that the state and a birth parent cannot legitimately bargain away a non-signatory child's lifelong access to a shared fact about their own birth, and exposed via Q1 (which A sidestepped in A4.1) that A had no defense against adult access when secrecy was never promised. B also dismantled A's proposed alternatives—showing medical summaries ignore identity and mutual-consent registries treat a parent's death identically to refusal—while honestly conceding that advance no-contact directives cannot preserve anonymity vis-a-vis the adoptee and weighing that trade-off cleanly. Side A had a strong point on the irreversibility of disclosure, but dropped its opening foster-care argument, grew repetitive in later rounds, and repeatedly made the muddled claim that releasing a paper record makes a name travel through genetic databases.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: When Side A argued in A2.1 that a confidentiality promise binds the state regardless of a "third party" adoptee who never signed it, Side B cleanly dismantled the framing across B2.1-B2.2 and B4.4 ("Her son did not pledge his ignorance as the price of his adoption" and "CON calls the applicant a 'third party.' To the surrender agreement, yes; to her own birth, no"), exposing why a bilateral surrender agreement cannot confer exclusive ownership over a shared relational fact.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by meeting the motion's full scope: an adult's right to the fact of her own birth, with contact separately controlled. It damaged Side A's foster-care causal chain, showed registries collapse death into refusal, and answered the prospective case Side A largely evaded. Side A's retroactivity and irreversibility points stayed live, but they did not defeat a forward-looking right or the cleaner choice between reunion consent and reading one's own record.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal: the child never pledged ignorance, hesitating over disclosure is not abandonment, and a registry treats a dead or unregistered parent as a veto.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): B built the stronger comparative case: confidentiality promises matter, but do not automatically give one party lifelong control over another adult’s birth history. B exposed the gaps in A’s surrender/foster-care causal chain, showed why medical updates and consent registries do not answer the identity claim, and supplied concrete contact safeguards while candidly conceding that anonymity is lost. A pressed retrospective reliance and irreversibility effectively, but increasingly repeated those points without answering B’s hardest cases or justifying parental consent as decisive in every placement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions by distinguishing access from contact, specifying advance no-contact directives, and then turning irreversibility back on A: state-enforced delay can permanently destroy an opportunity for knowledge or contact. The closing sharpened this by distinguishing a mother’s natural death from the clerk’s policy-driven refusal.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) built a much stronger comparative case by neutralizing Side B's practical arguments and cleanly reframing the core rights conflict. On medical need and DNA testing, Side A demonstrated that knowing a name confers no access to updated medical charts, confidential intermediaries can relay ongoing clinical updates without forced identification, and mandatory release compels exposure precisely in cases where consumer DNA would not reveal a parent—points Side B largely left unanswered while repeating earlier lines. On the central philosophical clash, Side A showed that adulthood ends paternalistic withholding for the adoptee's good without erasing an institution's duty of confidence to a third party, and used Pressure Questions to expose the cost of Side B's absolutism, under which nothing a sexual assault survivor could show would ever prevent state disclosure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side B declared in [B4.1] that "nothing" a birth mother who relied on confidentiality after an assault could show would justify withholding her name—comparing birth files to psychiatric or medical records—while claiming in [B4.5] to seek a "default of openness," Side A in [A4.5] exposed both flaws cleanly: a real default allows exceptions whereas "nothing" renders the mother's voice meaningless, and accessing painful facts about oneself differs fundamentally from compelling the state to identify someone else.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that the motion demands an unconditional right, then making that absoluteness the live weakness. A conceded the adoptee's real loss and identity stake, but answered the medical and DNA claims more carefully: an intermediary can keep a living link and relay later diagnoses, DNA does not always reveal a parent, and compelled naming forces disclosure even where none would otherwise occur. A's pressure answers were decisive: knowing a name is not contact, yet the harm is still the agency disclosing a promised confidence, and under the proposed right the mother can show nothing that changes the outcome. B's consent-asymmetry and "perpetual minor" frame stayed emotionally strong, but never fully neutralized that distinction or the irrevocability of release. The cleaner reason to reject an absolute right therefore remained with A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer that agency disclosure itself breaches the mother's confidence even if the adoptee never contacts her, paired with forcing B to admit that nothing she could show would justify withholding under the proposed right.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a close, high-quality debate by keeping the motion’s unconditional identifying-information right at the center and answering both pressure questions with precise distinctions: disclosure is not contact, and the confidence binds the agency rather than the adoptee. Side B’s nonconsent and identity arguments remained powerful, but its answer that nothing could justify withholding largely asserted the conclusion, while its medical-updating and DNA points were substantially answered. A therefore left the cleaner case for review rather than an outcome fixed in advance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal directly answered that even silent record-reading discloses the mother’s pregnancy, then used B’s answer of “nothing” to expose the proposed right’s inflexibility; its explanation that an intermediary can relay later diagnoses also weakened B’s strongest practical objection.
