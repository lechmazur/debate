### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins clearly. It built a coherent case that treats transit as shared infrastructure, matches it to broad-based finance, and reframes modest initial ridership as the wrong metric for a permanent default. CON's strongest route was fiscal risk: lost fare revenue is certain, the replacement is uncertain, and that is compounded by capacity costs and political irreversibility. That route stayed partly live. However, PRO answered CON's pressure questions directly with a concrete replacement-levy structure, a reconciliation formula, and a crowding-funded service standard, which blunted the "just a promise" and "death spiral" charges. CON's best later point was that payroll and property levies are passed on to workers and renters. That point came late and was never weighed against the plainly regressive flat fare PRO had already highlighted. CON also leaned on repeated, unsupported precise figures ("30-50%," "under 10%"). Its targeted-subsidy alternative never squarely answered the costs PRO raised: exclusion, cutoff errors, and administration at the door. Its closing was clipped before it could complete the comparison, which is an execution cost. PRO's later speeches narrowed the clash more effectively, especially by calling CON's cost comparison "selective accounting" and by framing fares as the volatile revenue source.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1/A2 answers in Rebuttal 2, followed by [B4.3]-[B4.5], were decisive. They converted CON's "unfunded promise" attack into a concrete earmarked mechanism with a service floor and crowding fund. They then turned CON's "uncertain benefits" test into selective accounting that prices bureaucracy, stigma, and delay at zero. CON's reply on the incidence of property and payroll taxes was its best counter but did not fully restore the fiscal-risk argument.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins a solid decision by engaging every clause of the proposition and winning the decisive structural comparisons. Side A (CON) raised real policy objections—that fares cover 30 to 50 percent of operating budgets while collection costs under 10 percent, that payroll and property taxes carry regressive incidence on wages and renters, and that targeted subsidies cost less. However, Side A leaned too heavily on calling the motion an unfunded mandate lacking political viability rather than fully engaging a normative mandate to fund transit through broad-based taxation, and incurred an execution cost when its closing was clipped mid-sentence. Side B decisively neutralized Side A's attacks: it turned fiscal stability by showing farebox reliance triggers ridership-shock cuts (unanswered by A), resolved the crowding objection by noting modest initial gains mean modest fleet needs while all-door boarding immediately frees capacity (also dropped by A), and showed that targeted subsidies keep gatekeeping hardware, policing friction, and income-cutoff exclusions intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side A dismissed service protection as an unviable promise given municipal income-tax limits [A2.1-A2.2] and demanded exact tax rates and bus funding in Pressure Questions [A3.1-A3.2], Side B's [B3.1] and [B4.1-B4.3] cleanly dismantled the trap—specifying an earmarked multi-base levy tied by formula to audited fare revenue, a service floor, and crowding standards, rightly rejecting a single universal rate across cities as false precision, and pairing fleet funding with capacity released by all-door boarding.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by judging the motion on its own terms: modest early ridership does not defeat a permanent civic default funded by an earmarked, ability-to-pay package with a service floor. Side A kept a real fiscal and tax-incidence objection alive, but never showed that replacement taxes are worse than flat fares or that targeted subsidies avoid stigma, delay, and gatekeeping. Its closing mostly restated the farebox loss and was clipped.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal answered both funding questions with a dedicated levy and crowding standard, then turned A’s “uncertain benefits” test into selective accounting that prices bureaucracy and exclusion at zero.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins solidly. It used the motion's own concession of modest gains as its central lever: if the fare is not the binding barrier, then frequency and coverage matter more. It then built a clear alternative mechanism of targeted affordability, fare caps, retained revenue, and money for service. Its rebuttals were precise. It showed that PRO's own 10-15% collection-cost figure means most fare revenue disappears under fare-free transit. It caught the double-spending of "savings" on new capacity. It noted that broad taxes are not automatically progressive. It also pressed the unfalsifiability of permanence well. PRO (B) leaned on aspirational framing about a right to mobility and a virtuous cycle. It used brittle unsupported figures, such as 20-30% increases among low-income riders and 10-15% collection costs, and cited nonexistent IDs [C1.1, C1.5]. Its first rebuttal was clipped. Its pressure questions were thin. In its second rebuttal, the A1:/A2: labels did not match the order of A's questions, and its answer to the falsifiability question essentially conceded that no failure would count against permanence. PRO's closing restated assertions rather than resolving the mechanism comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer to PRO's "funding failures, not policy failures" response ([A4.3]) was decisive. A showed that redefining crowding, unchanged car use, and transit-desert burdens as reasons to spend more means no evidence could ever count against permanence. That turned PRO's central permanence mechanism into "insulation from evidence."
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won solidly by engaging every clause of the motion—major-city scale, permanence, broad taxation, and modest initial ridership gains—while out-executing Side B on fiscal arithmetic and comparative policy design. Side A demolished Side B's claim that eliminating fare-collection costs frees money for capacity (since abolishing fares forfeits the remaining 85-90% of farebox revenue), presented a superior equity mechanism (automatic enrollment, anonymous reduced-fare cards, and fare caps that preserve revenue from affluent riders and tourists to fund frequency and night service), and exposed Side B's fallback claim of an "unbreakable political mandate" as an unfalsifiable IOU on future budgets. Side B offered a readable universalist critique of means-testing stigma, but suffered a clipped first rebuttal, dropped the net farebox revenue hole, and never showed how removing turnstiles fixes infrequent service when ridership gains are modest.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After [A2.2]-[A2.3] arithmetically dismantled [B2.2]-[B2.3]'s claim that saving 10-15% in collection costs could fund capacity while deleting 100% of fare revenue, Side A used [A3.1] to ask what observable failure could ever count against permanence; when [B4.1] dismissed crowding and deferred repairs as mere "funding failures" that build political leverage, [A4.3] showed that permanently deleting dedicated revenue makes funding the exact failure mode while insulating the policy from evidence.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by making the motion’s own terms—permanent free fares even with modest ridership gains—the decisive burden. A showed that universal free rides waste revenue better spent on frequency, coverage, and targeted relief, and that B’s “virtuous cycle” converts crowding, weak mode shift, and underfunding into reasons for more commitment rather than evidence against the policy. B’s equity and public-good framing never escaped that insulation problem, and was weakened by clipped rebuttal, nonexistent references, and brittle percentage claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal and closing most decisively turned B’s claim that crowding and deferred repairs are mere funding failures into proof that permanence makes the policy unfalsifiable.
