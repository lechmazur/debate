B's dual-use insight that "safeguard and threat are made of the same material: state discretion" went unanswered, and B's counter that super-majority protection makes wrongful classifications harder to correct turned PRO's own remedy into a liability. PRO's best line—that charity volatility is certain while electoral volatility is manageable—was blunted once B replaced the charity strawman with legislated plural instruments, and A's claim that Ireland "reversed decades of decline" plus the stretched statutory-veto characterization of Te Taura Whiri slightly weakened grounding. Both sides followed format cleanly (proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 structure), and A's pressure questions were genuinely sharp, but B's answers were more direct and B's late speeches consistently narrowed rather than widened the dispute. A's closing was largely recap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1–B2.2: the observation that PRO's fund-design concessions quietly adopt CON's plural-custody position, leaving "standing" as the only distinguishing feature—which B then showed is a liability, not a benefit. A's best exchange was A3.2/A4.4 forcing B to admit its alternative is also state-administered, but B's reply that diffused discretion is contestable while concentrated discretion is entrenched largely held.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): B wins narrowly by separating stable public financing from state custody: standing funds can support the community-controlled repositories A favors. A never establishes that permanent funding necessarily monopolizes cultural authority, while its alternative retains substantial state dependence. However, A effectively challenges B’s promises of electoral immunity. B’s unsupported claims about Te Taura Whiri’s statutory powers and overstated funding guarantees substantially narrow the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response that standing funds could finance tribal colleges and community-controlled archives directly undercut A’s central custody objection. A’s reply that community governance abandons the motion fails: the proposition requires standing public funding, not government ownership or centralized cultural authority.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins a high-level debate by arguing the proposition as written and trapping Side A (CON) in a decisive comparative dilemma. Side B established that preserving endangered languages and archives requires continuous infrastructure—salaries, servers, climate-controlled storage—and that volatile charity and short grant cycles cause irreversible loss when fluent speakers pass away between funding rounds. Side A offered an incisive institutional critique of bureaucratic standardization, oral-history consent obligations, and legislative discretion, including a sharp counter-turn in [A4.4] that supermajority protection can entrench flawed classifications (and Side B slightly overreached factually in [B4.1] by attributing Te Matawai's iwi-appointed board structure to Te Taura Whiri). Side B capitalized cleanly, showing that standing funds can finance community-controlled repositories without seizing custody and that Side A could only solve volatility by adopting durable state programs that still depend on state-defined eligibility.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's multi-turn squeeze on Side A's "plural custody" counter-model—initiated in [B2.1] and [B2.4], sharpened in Pressure Questions [B3.1]-[B3.2], and crystallized in [B4.4]-[B4.6]—decided the debate. Forced in [A4.1]-[A4.3] to propose legislated multi-year appropriations tied to state-"recognised communities," Side A effectively conceded the need for durable public money while reproducing state classification power.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A (PRO) built and defended a coherent path to victory: cultural memory as infrastructure, continuity over donor discretion, standardization of formats rather than content, and statutory accountability mechanisms. A answered B's politicization pressure directly in A1 with concrete statutory design (community-selected boards, measurable endangerment criteria, audits), which B never fully neutralized. B's strongest moments were the dependency and opportunity-cost framings and the Welsh counter-reading, but B's pivotal concession in A1 ("nothing stops a foundation board") undercuts its own reliability critique, and the "irreversible budget commitments" equivalence was glib against A's asymmetry that a budget line can be corrected while an extinct language cannot. Both sides followed format and used background knowledge responsibly, but A made the decisive clash clearer and closed with the cleaner weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over B3.1/B4.1: B forced A to defend who controls public funds, and A's A1 answered with fixed-term community boards, published criteria, and audit mechanisms; B's reply conceded that nothing stops a private board from cutting support, effectively surrendering the reliability comparison that anchored B's case. A's closing crystallized this: "A budget line can be corrected; an extinct language cannot."
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): B built the stronger comparative case: standing public support can combine continuity with community governance and reviewable safeguards, whereas A never adequately explained how private alternatives would serve communities lacking resources. B answered the governance pressure concretely; A conceded public transparency advantages, then largely repeated claims of inevitable dependency and lost agency. A’s opportunity-cost objection remained relevant, and B overstated guaranteed continuity and used nonexistent reference IDs. Nevertheless, A’s contradictory treatment of funding as both easily defunded and irreversible substantially weakened its final comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s distinction between revisable appropriations and irrecoverable language loss decisively answered A’s attempted equivalence between irreversible budget commitments and cultural extinction. Its community-selected boards and accountability safeguards also challenged A’s assumption that public funding necessarily entails state ownership, without receiving a comparably specific response.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built a far more coherent and resilient comparative case across all five rounds. Side B persuasively established that preserving endangered languages and community archives requires multi-decade continuity and technical infrastructure that episodic private charity rarely provides to marginalized communities, while directly answering Side A's politicization and opportunity-cost objections through community-governed statutory design and evidentiary insurance value. Although Side A (CON) raised smart points in Rebuttal 1 on Welsh bureaucratization and archival metadata assumptions, its case unraveled late: Side A conceded in [A4.1] that statutes invite transparency and appeal rights that private boards lack, never showed how tiny speaker communities could build endowments before elders die, and relied on a self-contradictory claim that standing funds are both easily cut by one vote and as irreversible as language extinction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's accountability pressure in [B3.1] through its closing crystallization in [B5.2]-[B5.4]: [B3.1] forced Side A ([A4.1]) to admit private funders can walk away at will and that statutes offer superior transparency and appeals, and when Side A claimed in [A4.4] that budget lines are as irreversible as language death, Side B decisively exposed the false equivalence ("A budget line can be corrected; an extinct language cannot").
