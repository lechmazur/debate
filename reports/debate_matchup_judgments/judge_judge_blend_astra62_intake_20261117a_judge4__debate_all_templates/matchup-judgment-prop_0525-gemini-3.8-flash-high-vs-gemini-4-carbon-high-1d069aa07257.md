### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by turning the motion's word "only" into a decisive burden. CON offered concrete alternative models that can secure safety, subsistence, and family unity without all three enforceable entitlements sitting in the destination state. These included group protection, funded transit hubs pending resettlement, sending-state backstops and readmission clauses, and pre-transfer family exemptions. Because "only" makes the trio a strict necessary condition, PRO had to show that none of these models could ever justify the rules. PRO mostly reasserted that anything short of domestic enforceability is an "empty legal fiction." PRO did press real points: aid is fickle, executive decrees can be rescinded, and sending states lack extraterritorial jurisdiction. Its pressure questions were sharp. However, CON answered them directly with treaty review boards, automatic stays, multi-year funding, and binding timelines. CON then turned PRO's "midnight deportation" image: a regime lawless enough to break binding treaties will ignore domestic courts too. CON's counterfactual also went largely unrebutted. Rejecting funded regional partnerships does not open wealthy borders; it leads to smugglers and raw pushbacks. PRO's reply, that substandard deals also fuel smuggling, was thin. PRO's closing was largely a rhetorical recap and did not engage the necessity burden. CON's mechanisms are somewhat idealized and convenient, which keeps the margin from being larger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.1 answer to PRO's "deportation flight at midnight" pressure was the most decisive exchange. It supplied a non-court mechanism (a treaty review board triggering a stay and return to the sending state). It also turned PRO's own example: a state lawless enough for secret expulsions would disregard domestic court rights as well. This undercut PRO's central claim that enforceability must sit in the destination state's own legal system.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B won by constructing plausible treaty, funding, monitoring, and sending-state alternatives that secure non-refoulement, subsistence, and family integrity without destination-court entitlements to work, appeal, and reunification, then showing that the word "only" condemns those partnerships. Side A never neutralized those mechanisms or the comparative harm of raw pushbacks, relying instead on repeated assertions that missing destination standing equals a legal void.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 answers undercut the midnight-deportation argument by pairing joint review stays and readmission with the symmetry that a regime willing to shred treaties would ignore domestic filings too.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B won by making the word “only” the decisive clash and offering plausible functional substitutes—treaty-backed stays, funded transit hubs, phased work permits, readmission backstops, and family protections through sending-state mechanisms. It answered pressure questions directly and kept the practical cost of A’s rule—lost burden-sharing, smuggler routes, and raw pushbacks—central. Side A’s enforceable-rights/agency narrative was rhetorically powerful, but it never fully neutralized B’s countermodels or explained why destination-state litigation is the sole sufficient safeguard.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 answer to A’s appeal question was decisive: it offered an automatic treaty-board stay/readmission mechanism and argued that domestic courts also fail in lawless regimes, blunting A’s claim that only destination-court appeals can prevent refoulement.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins because it fixed the mechanism of the debate early and CON never contested that framing. A safe-third-country rule does not rescue anyone from a warzone. It ships a claimant already at a state's door to another country. That reframe turned CON's main impacts against it: "fragile economies" and "regional cooperation" became evidence of burden-dumping. PRO also showed that transfers send claimants back into transit states, so refusing defective transfers relieves those states rather than pushing them to seal borders. CON never answered this. CON's best weapon was PRO's "categorically unjustified" answer on work rights, and CON pressed it hard. However, PRO had already justified the answer: aid is revocable, and a labor market too fragile for refugees should not receive offloaded claimants. CON also made an equally costly admission of its own. It said a transfer stays justified even when aid falls below subsistence and a parent is separated from a minor child for years. PRO used that admission effectively in its closing. CON's claims about UNHCR mandates and "automatic suspensive effect" were overstated and asserted rather than substantiated. CON's "bombs and death squads" framing kept repeating the mechanism error that PRO had already exposed. PRO adapted across rounds, while CON largely repeated its absolutism charge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B2.3/B4.4 sequence was the most decisive exchange. It argued that monitoring and UNHCR oversight "cannot stop a deportation flight; observers only write reports after refoulement." When CON then shifted to binding administrative stays, PRO treated this as a concession that safety requires a binding halt to removal. It added that intergovernmental protocols fail when both governments prefer rapid removal, so only a claimant-enforceable suspension right is reliable. This neutralized CON's substitute-safeguards line.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by tying the necessity test to what safe-third-country rules actually do: hand off people already at the door, not rescue them from a warzone. A showed that revocable aid, monitoring, and diplomacy predictably fail after transfer, and answered B’s work-permit hypothetical directly. B’s purity-test close never fully repaired that mechanism confusion or the post-handoff durability gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal, which accepted B’s safety-plus-subsistence hypothetical and still showed why revocable aid and non-claimant remedies do not justify the transfer.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A built the clearer winning path by framing the motion around durable, claimant-enforceable protection rather than safety only at the moment of transfer. Its answers to pressure were direct, and it effectively showed that monitoring, diplomatic coordination, and aid-dependent subsistence are weaker substitutes for enforceable appeal, work, and family unity. Side B’s pragmatism and the work-permit concession were strong, but A neutralized them well enough by arguing that revocable aid and work bans can coerce return and undermine real safety.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to Side B’s suspension-protocol claim was decisive: it distinguished intergovernmental monitoring from a claimant’s own enforceable right to halt removal, exposing why B’s appeal substitute was less reliable.
