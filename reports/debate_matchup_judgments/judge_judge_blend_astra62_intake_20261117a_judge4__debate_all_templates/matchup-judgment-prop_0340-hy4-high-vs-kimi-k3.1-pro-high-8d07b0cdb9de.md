### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a competitive debate by holding the cleaner frame. A single optimized feed still embeds a value choice, usually an engagement proxy, and user-stated goals create a benchmark that feed can be judged against. CON's best attacks were real. The platform still translates labels into ranking. Presets can be engagement "wearing a badge." Amplification causes shared harms. CON also landed a sharp point in Rebuttal 2: PRO's chronological fallback abandons the chosen goal rather than enforcing it. But PRO answered each pressure point directly: - **Translation:** every objective needs translation, so the real question is which stated purpose disciplines optimization. - **Externalities:** safety floors constrain how content is promoted; they do not set what the feed is for. - **Verifiability:** PRO gave a concrete ranking contract with failure triggers and remedies. CON's own A2 then conceded that friends, local, learning and calm remain legitimate bounded user goals. That substantially grants "far more control" relative to a one-feed status quo. CON then shifted the dispute to "sovereignty" and a "sweeping transfer," which is a narrower motion than the one written. PRO's closing question, "purpose for whom?", plus its point that a systemwide safety duty cannot tell whether a feed failed as a learning or close-friends feed, went largely unrebutted except by restating platform authorship. That left PRO with the more live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was Q2/A2 on ownership. CON asked in what sense users own the feed's purpose if platforms define and override goals. PRO answered that ownership means the authority to set, inspect, revoke and enforce a target within safety floors. CON's reply conceded that friends, learning and calm remain meaningful bounded purposes. That concession made CON's position look like a limited version of PRO's motion rather than a rejection of it, though CON's point that the contract falls back to chronological ranking was its strongest counter.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side A built the cleaner, more decisive case on the proposition as written. It persuasively argued that behavioral traces like clicks and watch time flatten diverse user purposes into one engagement proxy, and it won the central framing clash in closing: systemwide safety duties can block public harms at the edges, but they cannot decide what a feed is positively for across different users and contexts (such as close friends, chronological recency, local relevance, or learning). Side B stayed highly competitive by exposing the principal-agent limits of qualitative ML labels, dismantling A's map analogy and showing that platform-defined "ranking contracts" do not make presets self-verifying to ordinary users. Still, Side A won the institutional comparison because Side B's own "declared common recommendation duties" depend on the same measurement machinery, concrete structural presets remain viable, and Side B was ultimately forced under pressure to concede bounded controls for friends, local, learning, and calm—retreating from defending one universal feed into disputing whether bounded goal choice counts as "ownership."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's pressure questions [A3.1]-[A3.2] and closing follow-through [A5.1]-[A5.2] formed the most decisive exchange: after Side B argued that harm overrides negate user goal control and demanded goal-measurement machinery, Side A asked why that machinery should not serve user-chosen goals and which constructive aims (friends, local, learning, calm) violate public safety baselines. Side B had to concede both systemwide measurement duties and bounded controls for those exact aims [B4.1]-[B4.3], allowing Side A to show that safety floors police means without supplying a positive purpose for one universal feed.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by framing the one-feed model as a hidden engagement proxy and giving users’ chosen goals a concrete accountability role: an external benchmark the platform could visibly fail. CON’s implementation and externality objections were strong, but PRO answered the key pressure with a plausible ranking-contract/remedy mechanism and kept safety floors as constraints rather than a full platform-owned purpose. CON never fully showed why bounded, testable user-goal control could not coexist with platform responsibility, so PRO retained the cleaner route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over the user-accessible test and remedy. PRO’s ranking-contract answer gave a way to distinguish genuine goal pursuit from engagement relabeling, while CON’s reply that the platform still defines and enforces the contract was sharp but did not fully erase the advantage of having an explicit benchmark.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a competitive debate. Its strongest structural move was separating two questions: what a feed may promote, governed by shared safety and eligibility rules, and what the feed should accomplish for this user now. CON's best weapon was a dilemma: either user goals are mere feedback, or they bind and must be neutralized whenever they cause harm. That dilemma was clever and well pressed. PRO blunted it by arguing that it proves too much, since no meaningful control is absolute and interpreting or bounding a goal does not make the goal unreal. PRO's A2 also gave a concrete answer to "what decision moves to the user": top positions among eligible items, how much exploration or viewpoint diversity, and when recommendations stop. CON never squarely rebutted that answer. It reasserted that defining terms like "gossip" or "reliable" is itself the objective stack. PRO also neutralized CON's spillover framing by noting that centralized engagement optimization redistributes attention and invites gaming too. CON's fallback to an "accountable common objective stack" is still one objective for everyone, and PRO's point that common rules cannot reveal a person's present purpose stood unrefuted. PRO took a modest execution cost because two turns were clipped. Only closing sentences were lost, so the cost was limited.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Q2/A2 exchange decided the debate. CON asked what concrete ranking decision actually moves to the user once defaults, screens, and expirations are in place. PRO answered with specifics: which eligible items get top slots, how much exploration they receive, and when the feed stops, with bicycle-repair and family examples. CON's reply, that these all depend on platform definitions, risked proving too much, and PRO exploited that in its closing.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side A wins a clear, high-level debate because its model of bounded goal control squarely satisfies the proposition while defeating Side B's central all-or-nothing dilemma. Side A proved why one feed—even Side B's idealized "common objective stack"—cannot infer shifting session purposes from ambiguous clicks or protect users against their own momentary impulses. Side B pressed a smart public-infrastructure and civic-spillover case and had cleaner length discipline (Side A had the tail end of two turns clipped), but B overreached by claiming that safety screens, alert slots, and platform classifiers reduce user control to zero. Side A persuasively showed that reordering top eligible slots, setting diversity, ignoring impulsive clicks, and enforcing session stops transfer real power within shared guardrails.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's turnaround on inconsistent behavior ([A2.3]-[A2.5], sharpened in [A3.1] and [A4.2]) was pivotal: when Side B argued that clicking gossip under a "sober news" setting proves user goals fail or must be overridden, Side A showed that clicks reflect impulse rather than consent—so honoring the stated goal over the gossip click is precisely what makes goal control valuable rather than a contradiction.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): B narrowly won because it gave a concrete, defensible model of user-set goals inside enforceable safety rules, and answered A’s dilemma with specific transferred decisions such as ordering among eligible items, exploration, stopping, and session limits. A’s accountability/objective-stack framing was rhetorically sharp, but it too often treated any platform implementation or guardrail as proof that no meaningful control remained, which did not defeat the proposition’s demand for far more, not absolute, control. B’s clipped turns kept the margin narrow.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 answers to A’s pressure: the alert/public-interest-slot response to the urgent-alert problem and the concrete ranking decisions blunted A’s claim that user goals were either empty labels or dangerous overrides.
