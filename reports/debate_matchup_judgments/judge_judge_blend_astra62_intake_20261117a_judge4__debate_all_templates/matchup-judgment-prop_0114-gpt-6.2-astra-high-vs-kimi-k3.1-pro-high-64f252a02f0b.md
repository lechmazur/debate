### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): This was a high-quality, closely matched debate, and PRO edges it. PRO built a coherent social-insurance rationale around the aide whose morning care counts toward her pension while her afternoon care does not. That framing stayed tightly tied to the motion's demand to make unpaid care visible. PRO also adapted well across the rounds: - **Incentives:** It reframed the incentive objection by arguing the status quo already penalizes unpaid care. - **Retirement floor:** It explained that a general floor addresses poverty but not differential accrual above that floor. - **Pressure questions:** It answered both questions directly, with clear A1:/A2: labels and an honest admission that attribution will miss some care. CON delivered sharp, disciplined critiques: the benefit is deferred, attribution is intrusive, the policy privileges informal care over purchased care, and PRO's flat basic rate abandons any "repair of lost accrual" logic. That last point is CON's best, and its closing pressed it well. However, PRO largely absorbed that point rather than being damaged by it. PRO openly embraced the idea that the credit rewards the work performed, which fits the motion's language about invisible labor. CON's main alternative, immediate support plus an adequate floor, is not mutually exclusive with the credit. CON also had to concede that denying the credit preserves no employment choice. CON's defense that "the morning counts because accrual is attached to paid employment" partly assumes the very status quo the motion challenges. Overall, both sides argued well, but PRO made the decisive comparison easier to grasp.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange began with CON's repeated charge that a pension credit "supplies no care today," a charge PRO's Q1 had already pressed by getting CON to concede that denying the credit preserves no employment choice. PRO's closing then turned the charge: the pension right earned on the paid morning shift buys nothing at the bedside either, yet it counts. That exposed CON's immediate-usefulness test as applied only to unpaid care. CON's reply, that accrual attaches to employment, was sharp but leaned on the very status quo under challenge.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO won the central clash: unpaid care creates a distinct pension-accrual loss that neither immediate income support nor a general retirement floor repairs. CON's strongest attack, that a public-funded flat credit is not true loss restoration, was answered by treating the credit as basic social-insurance accrual for care work rather than salary replacement. PRO's no-paid-aide question forced a damaging concession that denial preserves no employment choice, and PRO answered the attribution challenge more concretely than CON answered why the future pension loss should be excluded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO rebutted CON's "not real insurance" attack by explaining that the credit insures a basic rate of pension accrual for verified care, not the caregiver's previous salary. This kept PRO's case coherent while CON's alternatives did not address the pension record itself.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): CON won by forcing PRO’s rationale to shift from repairing interrupted pension accrual to a flat, tax-funded basic-rate award for care time, then showing that shift fails to justify deferred timing, inequity versus purchased care, imperfect attribution, or superiority to immediate support plus a retirement floor. PRO’s bedside analogy and “count both halves” close were rhetorically sharp but did not restore a coherent insurance logic or neutralize those design objections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2, which used PRO’s own A1/A2 answers to show the credit neither restores actual losses nor creates present care options, converting the proposal into selective deferred compensation rather than pension repair.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined, avoided fake precision, and engaged each other directly. PRO built a clean principled case: unpaid care is socially necessary labor, and treating it as pension-invisible shifts its retirement cost onto caregivers. Its strongest line was the closing turn, "only paid contributions count — that is the injustice." CON was sharper analytically and drove the clash. The two-sisters example exposed a real tension between repairing lost contributions and rewarding care itself. CON's point that "the same dollar cannot be both a saving and an extra dollar in her pocket" damaged PRO's fiscal-savings claim, and PRO's later answer (A4.7) mostly retreated from it. CON then pressed a clear dilemma on the daughter with no work history: either presume a loss, so the credit goes beyond gap repair, or require evidence that she cannot supply. PRO answered with a standardized credit, which is workable but effectively concedes the presumption horn. PRO also conceded in A2 that an aide could take budget priority over a credit. That let CON close on opportunity cost: the next public dollar should buy cover for care, not top up a secure retiree. PRO's reply that the motion requires recognition rather than ranking every expenditure kept it competitive. On balance, CON narrowed the debate more effectively and left PRO's credit looking less necessary given a floor plus services.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.2–B4.4 was the decisive exchange. It accepted PRO's gap-repair rule for the sisters, then turned it on the never-employed daughter: a standard rate tells you how much to pay, not whether there was a loss. It also forced PRO's budget-priority concession into a direct contest between a capped credit and an aide preventing another family's loss. PRO's closing reframe that "only paid contributions count" was strong but did not fully neutralize this.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the core injustice in focus: pension systems count paid contributions and erase verified care years, leaving many caregivers with permanently smaller old-age security. Side A raised strong challenges about targeting, opportunity cost, and whether a retirement floor plus services could do the job better. But B answered the hardest pressure by shifting to a prospective, standardized, capped credit: it need not reconstruct counterfactual wages or reward caregivers with no contribution gap. A floor prevents destitution and an aide can prevent future loss, but neither repairs a record already damaged. A's limited-budget objection was real but was softened by B's willingness to cap, target, and sequence spending rather than insist on unlimited supplements. The debate was close and well-clashed, but B gave the cleaner path to granting care pension significance without making the policy infinitely expensive or administratively impossible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's answer to the daughter with no prior work history was the decisive exchange: documented loss need not mean proving a hypothetical salary; verified sustained care can generate a standardized contribution for that year. This neutralized A's strongest measurement and targeting pressure while preserving the gap-repair rationale.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B more convincingly showed that pension credits are not the warranted response to caregiver disadvantage. The sisters example and later pressure exposed a real clash between gap-repair and care-reward designs, including unprovable counterfactuals for people with no prior record. Side A’s standardized-credit and “sequence both” replies conceded priority to aides in tight budgets without explaining why an above-floor imputation should outrank preventing another loss. That left the proposition’s specific instrument under-justified against a floor plus services.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 was decisive: after A redefined “documented loss” as a standard care-year credit and declined to rank a comfortable retiree’s supplement over an aide, B showed that even a capped credit still spends the next dollar on an extra payment rather than cover that would preserve wages and contributions, and that presuming a loss is not the same as proving one.
