### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won a competitive debate by showing that a staged, compensated mandate with independent extensions differs meaningfully from voluntary licensing: transfer is the default, delays require public justification, and technical assistance is enforceable. Side B raised strong scarcity, tacit-knowledge, and incentive objections, but repeatedly treated compensation as forfeiture and assumed any extension pauses all transfer activity. Its pre-crisis contracting alternative also failed to cover unexpected or uncontracted winners, the precise gap the motion addresses. Side B's repeated clipped turns were an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's deadline dilemma was the strongest pressure, but Side A answered it directly: published milestones, regulator-approved extensions, continued training duties, and remedies preserve enforceability without forcing unsafe production. Side B's reply that this becomes voluntary did not overcome the distinction between manufacturer discretion and publicly supervised delay.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by trapping Side B in a dilemma regarding the fixed window. When Side B conceded that an independent regulator would grant extensions during early manufacturing struggles, Side A decisively pointed out that Side B's mechanism pauses exactly during the most critical shortage, neutralizing the affirmative's core benefit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A5.1, which flawlessly weaponized Side B's A1 concession to prove the affirmative mechanism fails to solve the peak shortage.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing a persistent dilemma in PRO's case: a binding transfer deadline either disrupts peak-scarcity manufacturing or bends until it adds little beyond voluntary arrangements, while imposing certain ex-ante innovation costs. CON's contractual alternative provided a cleaner binding mechanism without those costs, and the "litigation, not doses" framing on tacit knowledge undercut PRO's claim that the mandate would actually deliver more doses. PRO never fully resolved why a statutory trigger after emergency authorization beats pre-crisis contracts, and CON's closing weighing—that the benefit arrives late and small while the cost arrives early and certain—crystallized the debate in their favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal demonstrating that PRO's deadline must either slip during the decisive early months (making it little better than voluntary arrangements) or bind and pull scarce engineers from working lines, paired with the tacit-knowledge point that the law would guarantee litigation over "teaching well enough" rather than actual doses.
