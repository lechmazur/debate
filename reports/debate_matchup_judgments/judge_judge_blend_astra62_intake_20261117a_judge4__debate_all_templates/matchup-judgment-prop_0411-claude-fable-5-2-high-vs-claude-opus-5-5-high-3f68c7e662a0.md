### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won because PRO never neutralized the core feasibility objection. PRO's main fix—accessible vans carrying ordinary passengers—was turned by CON: an occupied van cannot answer a wheelchair request, and near-parity still requires far greater accessible-vehicle density than a reliable 20-minute floor. CON's geometry argument made that cost concrete, while PRO's answers became vague ("whatever local demand requires") and looked like a blank check. On transit, CON's pressure questions forced PRO to accept same-day, door-to-door parity, but PRO twice avoided saying what fixed-budget service would be cut. PRO's closing reframe treated CON's eventual goal agreement as a concession, but CON plausibly distinguished gradual improvement from legally prioritizing the costliest stopwatch gap over rides that do not currently exist.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's occupied-van/geometry rebuttal was decisive: it showed that using accessible vans for ordinary trips does not solve availability, and that shortening waits from 20 minutes to near-standard requires multiplying accessible-vehicle density rather than merely extending coverage. This, combined with the repeated fixed-budget transit cut question, exposed the weakest part of PRO's mandate case.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B wins the comparison between two enforceable standards. A establishes the harms of unequal access and identifies plausible funding mechanisms, but does not adequately resolve the additional fleet requirements or transit-budget tradeoffs. B explains why a reliable service floor can require less vehicle density than near-parity, while still addressing rides that never arrive. A's closing then mischaracterizes B's mandate as a voluntary promise and conditional improvement as agreement on parity. B's cost magnitudes remain illustrative rather than established, but its mechanism, direct answers, and comparative weighing are stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest response answers A's challenge that a twenty-minute guarantee requires the same coverage as near-parity: geographic coverage is not the same as vehicle density. Combined with the point that occupied shared-fleet vans cannot immediately answer requests, this defeats A's claim that pooling largely dissolves the marginal-cost problem.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B won by isolating the motion’s distinctive burden: not guaranteeing accessible rides, but legally prioritizing near-parity over other mobility investments. B directly answered A’s strongest affordability question with the coverage-versus-density distinction, then used A’s answers to show that A accepted same-day paratransit without addressing fixed-budget tradeoffs and rejected even a guaranteed twenty-minute ride. A’s equality argument, pooled-funding mechanism, and closing frame about legally committing to improvement were compelling, but they did not neutralize B’s marginal-cost and priority-order comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both pressure questions directly, explained why a twenty-minute guarantee need not require parity-level fleet density, and converted A’s answers on paratransit and twenty-minute service into a clear indictment of the motion’s costliest distinctive requirement.
