### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built and sustained the stronger case. The core clash was B's "foresight-free safeguard" frame versus A's "quota yields noise, not evidence" objection. B's hindsight argument—that mechanism-targeted testing only finds what you already suspect, as with zolpidem and carbamazepine—went largely unanswered in substance; A's response that broad data collection can also be untargeted was blunted by B's point that such data comes from the same too-few participants. B answered both pressure questions directly and honestly, even conceding "yes, the target must be met," and then mitigated the scarce-patient hypothetical by defining proportionality relative to the disease population. A's strongest live route—the contradiction that regulators judge adequacy while targets still bind—was met with a workable building-code floor/inspector analogy, though A's closing counter (code compliance certifies; proportionality doesn't) kept it partially alive. B's consistent weighing—bounded, visible, shared delay versus hidden, open-ended, unevenly distributed harm—was the clearer reason to vote. A was disciplined and sharp, but B did more to narrow and resolve the decisive disputes. B's repeated word-limit clipping is a real execution cost that keeps this from a wider margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answer to A's scarce-patient question: conceding the target must be met, then reframing proportionality as relative to the disease population so requirements shrink with scarcity, and turning A's "harmless shortfall" premise back with the zolpidem point that nobody can know a gap is harmless without the data. A's sharpest counter was exposing in [A4.4] that if regulators judge adequacy, adequacy—not proportionality—does the real work, which B answered with the building-code floor analogy.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins a high-level debate by proving that mandatory proportionality across every age, sex, and ethnic category imposes a certain, potentially irreversible approval veto for an unreliable evidentiary payoff. Side B (PRO) made a strong case that discretionary, mechanism-based testing fails when subgroup differences are unsuspected in advance, grounding this effectively in zolpidem, carbamazepine, and clopidogrel. However, once pressed on statistical power, Side B conceded that proportionate cells need not power subgroup conclusions and instead offer preliminary signals to pool and chase later. Side A decisively exploited that retreat: small proportional strata—especially in scarce disease populations—produce statistical noise rather than reliable pre-approval validation while still letting the hardest cross-demographic target delay life-saving therapy for progressive-disease patients (including underrepresented patients) whose existing care is failing. Side A also executed cleanly within every limit, whereas Side B incurred a repeated execution cost by getting clipped in all four substantive speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side B defended underpowered quotas in [B2.5]-[B2.6] and [B4.1]-[B4.3] by claiming they create an honest overall average, detectable signals to pool later, and proportionally smaller targets when patients are scarce, Side A dismantled all three claims ([A2.3], [A4.5]-[A4.6]): weighted averages can mask subgroup harm, scarce populations shrink quotas into tiny noise-prone cells while still bottlenecking approval timing on the slowest cross-category, and needing subsequent confirmation means PRO delays everyone's access without eliminating post-approval uncertainty.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B won by keeping the vote on the motion as written: an inflexible proportionate veto even when it materially slows approval without decision-relevant evidence. B damaged the claim that headcounts equal discovery, showed small cells can be noise, and exposed the contradiction between "the regulator judges adequacy" and "every target must still be met." Side A's foresight argument and consent frame were strong and not fully erased, but A never supplied a workable exception for scarce or life-saving cases and several speeches were clipped before the weighing finished. B answered both pressure questions directly and left the cleaner reason to reject demographic compliance as the approval rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal and closing: once A said regulators judge adequacy and that subgroup signals often need later pooling, the building-code analogy failed, because meeting a demographic floor is not the same as having evidence that should control approval timing.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): A (CON) controlled the decisive framing: the motion's only distinctive contribution is the hard approval gate, and everything else is common ground. A's statistical pressure (underpowered slices produce noise, not findings) forced B into repeated concessions — "not a magical guarantee," "no single percentage point is magic" — and A's three-condition chain made the benefit look speculative against a stipulated certain delay. A's pressure questions were sharper, especially the yes-or-no rare-disease trap, and A answered B's pressure directly with the A1/A2 structure. B fought back well: the "fines create no randomized evidence / patients supply the experiment" comparison was B's best material, and B's reframe to an enforceable floor was a legitimate defense of the requirement rather than evasion. But B never fully restored the evidentiary value of proportionality itself after the statistical attack, and B's honest concession that "approval waits" handed A the emotionally decisive image of a dying patient sanctioned for a sponsor's failure. A's closing ("the dead cannot be retroactively treated") cleanly mirrored and answered B's best line, and "make the sponsor pay, not the patient" crystallized a surviving route to victory. Close, competitive debate; A edges it on sharper clash-narrowing and weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.4-A4.6): after B conceded "approval waits" for an unfilled cell, A pinned B's position as penalizing the one actor with no control over trial design, while answering B's pressure question with the sponsor-pays alternative (penalties, post-approval commitments, label disclosure). B's counter in closing — that fines create no evidence and patients become the experiment — was strong but came too late to fully reverse the exchange.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a high-level clash by proving that a binding pre-approval demographic quota is the wrong statistical and regulatory tool for protecting diverse patients. While Side B (PRO) persuasively argued that ex post fines and labels cannot generate randomized pre-approval evidence and cleanly tied proportionality to the intended patient population, Side B never overcame the core biostatistical problem: proportionate subgroup slices in standard efficacy trials are too small to reliably establish differential effects, whereas targeted pharmacokinetic, renal, and oversampling studies address real biological risks without a blanket veto. Side A decisively won the comparative weighing by contrasting the motion's stipulated, population-wide delay of proven therapies—including in rare and rapidly fatal diseases—against a speculative subgroup signal, showing that halting approval punishes sick patients rather than failing sponsors.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A3.2], Side A asked whether approval of a working rare-disease or aggressive-cancer drug must wait if a single demographic cell remains unfilled. After Side B bit the bullet in [B4.3] ("approval waits"), Side A leveraged that concession in [A4.5-A4.6] and [A5.2-A5.5] to flip Side B's "who bears the cost" framing—showing that a hard gate sanctions dying patients for sponsor recruitment failures while delivering an underpowered subgroup sample that even Side B admitted cannot guarantee certainty, capped by directly countering [B5.5] with "if the injured patient cannot be retroactively enrolled, the dead one cannot be retroactively treated."
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B won by narrowing the motion to its hard approval gate and showing that proportionate slices are often too small to deliver the subgroup protection Side A invoked. Side A answered the rare-disease question honestly, but admitting that approval waits, while conceding proportionality is no guarantee, left a stipulated delay buying an underpowered signal. Side B’s penalties, labels, and post-approval commitments answered the enforcement objection without withholding a working drug, and its closing weighed that choice more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s question forcing a yes on waiting for an unfilled cell, then its later use of Side A’s “no magic” concession to argue that the wait sanctions patients for an evidentiary gain too weak to justify it.
