### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): PRO wins solidly. PRO built a tight frame tied to the motion's own condition. If no safe alternative exists, fines and tows create no bed and only strip away stability, while concrete harms remain regulable. PRO then held that line throughout. PRO's A1/A2 answers were direct and damaging. Noncoercive welfare checks, outreach, and safe-parking offers are not barred, and a recorded refusal falls outside the motion. That neutralized CON's carbon monoxide and incentive questions. CON's case drifted. It began by defending fines and towing as "leverage," then retreated to "leverage is not about fines—it is structured contact." That concession largely ceded the ground the motion actually contests. CON's later chilling-effect argument has some plausibility. However, it rested on misdescribing the motion as a "categorical bar on routine contact" when the motion bans only fines, towing, and harassment. CON's scattering claim also cut both ways. PRO correctly noted that punishment itself disperses people away from outreach. CON's verification point ("no officer can verify at 2 a.m.") was effectively turned by PRO. Uncertainty is a reason for restraint before coercion, not a license to punish first.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2 was the decisive exchange. PRO answered CON's carbon monoxide and incentive questions by separating noncoercive welfare checks and recorded safe-parking offers from fines and tows. It also noted that a roving patrol is no carbon monoxide detector either. This forced CON to redefine "leverage" as mere contact, which conceded that punishment itself was not needed.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won solidly by anchoring every exchange to the proposition's exact scope while exposing Side A's steady retreat from defending routine fines and towing. Side B established severe, unrefuted harms from fines, impoundment, and 2 a.m. move-alongs—debt, lost medicine and work tools, fractured sleep, and lost mobility—while preserving targeted enforcement against visible hazards like waste or blocked sightlines. Side A pivoted to arguing that banning harassment would chill noncoercive window-taps, carbon monoxide checks, and safe-parking outreach, but Side B cleanly dismantled that equivocation: civilian outreach and noncoercive welfare checks are not barred, offering an actual safe-parking slot removes refusers from the motion's protection, roving patrols cannot spot invisible gas anyway, and punitive enforcement is what drives sleepers into isolated hiding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's "leverage" and "2 a.m. uncertainty" claims across [B2.1], [B2.5], [B3.2], and [B4.2]: Side B showed that when no safe alternative exists there is nothing for punitive "leverage" to attach to, when a safe-parking slot does exist cities can simply offer and record it rather than issuing tickets, and street-level uncertainty at night is a reason for restraint before seizing someone's vehicle rather than a license to punish first.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): PRO kept the closer reading of the motion: no routine fines, towing, or harassment when no safe alternative exists, while still allowing outreach, noncoercive checks, and responses to proven harm or a refused real offer. CON’s live case depended on collapsing “harassment” into all routine contact and then treating speculative departmental chilling as fatal. PRO answered that pressure directly, showed punishment is certain harm that neither detects hidden danger nor creates a bed, and left the cleaner reason to vote yes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s second rebuttal and closing, answering that nothing in the motion bars a genuine welfare check or a recorded safe-parking offer, then exposing CON’s shift from defending fines and tows to redefining all contact as the banned “leverage.”

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): PRO began with a sympathetic and coherent case. Its strongest pressure was that fining or towing someone with no alternative serves no purpose, and that towing pushes people into worse, isolated places. CON, however, controlled the decisive clash on workability. It pointed out that PRO's safeguards (outreach, shelter checks, hazard-only towing) appear nowhere in the motion and were borrowed from CON's own managed model. It also distinguished CON's positive check ("is there a bed to offer?") from the negative PRO's exemption requires ("prove no alternative exists"). Finally, it exposed a live contradiction: PRO said posted time limits stay enforceable but also said to "punish only when there is a choice to comply." Its point that the "given night" renews indefinitely also went unanswered. PRO's second rebuttal claimed protection ends once an alternative is offered, but it never handled the case where no bed opens for a week. PRO's closing largely restated the cruelty frame without engaging the curb-duty and reset points. PRO's humane frame remained live, so the result is clear but competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's B4.2–B4.4. It answered PRO's A3.2 "same check" question by separating a positive offer check from disproving a negative. It then turned PRO's own A4.1 and A4.3 against each other to show that time limits become suggestions whenever no bed exists. PRO never resolved this in its closing.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins a clear but competitive debate by proving that routine fines, towing, and move-along orders serve no valid purpose—and actively worsen homelessness—when no safe shelter or parking option exists. Side A (CON) argued sharply on administrative friction, effectively distinguishing positive bed checks from disproving private alternatives [A4.2] and catching Side B's tension between keeping posted time limits [B4.1] and punishing only when compliance is possible [B4.3, A4.4]. Even if PRO restricts multi-night time-limit enforcement when shelters stay full, however, Side B won the decisive comparative clash: whenever a verified bed or safe-parking spot exists, offering it ends the motion's exemption, and when none exists, Side A never explained how fining or towing solves curb impacts rather than displacing cars to unlit streets, dumping stripped vehicle-sleepers onto sidewalks, and trapping them in warrant debt [B1.2-B1.3, B2.2, B5.1-B5.2].
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's "leverage into services" counter-model across [B2.1], [B3.1], [B4.3], and [B5.1-B5.2] decided the debate: after forcing Side A to admit in [A4.1] that fining or towing advances no public goal on a single shelterless night, Side B showed that "leverage" remains just as hollow over multiple nights because you cannot manage someone into a shelter bed or safe-parking spot that does not exist.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by judging the motion as written. It showed Side B was borrowing a managed procedure the text does not require, distinguished a positive shelter offer from proving “no safe alternative,” and left unanswered the contradiction between enforceable time limits and “punish only when there is a choice.” Side B’s cruelty and displacement case stayed live, but it never supplied an enforceable off-ramp once a one-night emergency renews.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it separated “is there a bed to offer?” from disproving a negative, then showed Side B’s own choice-to-comply test turns posted limits, move orders, and refusal consequences into unenforceable suggestions.
