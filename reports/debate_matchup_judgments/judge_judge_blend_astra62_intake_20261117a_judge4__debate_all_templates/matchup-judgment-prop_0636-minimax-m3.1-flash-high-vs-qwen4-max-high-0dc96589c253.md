### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won the decisive clash. A's opening dilemma — if absent provenance is neutral the mandate has no bite on deceivers, if suspicious it penalizes anonymous speech — framed the whole debate, and A pressed it consistently. B offered a genuine middle course ("origin unavailable" as neutral, production history not authenticity badge) and a real weighing point (opponents demand perfection while every disclosure law fails against determined evasion), but A's closing landed the sharpest distinction: ordinary disclosure rules work because evasion is itself visible and punishable, whereas B's own neutrality rule makes stripping indistinguishable from a screenshot or source protection. B's flagship catch — the compliant tool plus lying caption — was conceded by A to be real, but A plausibly showed voluntary provenance already reads that signal and the mandate's marginal contribution is uniform display, which standardizes a backwards sorting ("chain verified" for cooperating fabricators, "origin unavailable" for witness footage). On the identity-verification carve-out, B's pseudonymous/organizational credential answer was adequate, though A's "franchised gatekeeper" point retained some force. B never fully neutralized the "burden lands on candor" charge; B's fallback to incremental, uniform protection was coherent but outweighed by A's asymmetry weighing. B's clipped rebuttal was a minor execution cost. A made the debate easier to decide with sharper, more memorable rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing (A5.1-A5.2): B argued every disclosure law fails against deliberate evaders, and A answered that those laws work because evasion is visible and punishable — while B's "origin unavailable, no invented conclusion" rule deletes the very inference that would make signal-stripping a violation. This crystallized the central dilemma and left B's incremental-protection route visibly weakened.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B made the stronger mandate-versus-voluntary comparison. A established useful preservation and display benefits, but insufficiently weighed them against easy evasion and potentially misleading provenance distinctions. A also repeatedly attacked identity checks that B explicitly rejected, while its first pressure question oddly challenged its own policy. B overstated the signer-identity connection and understated explicit synthetic labels, so this remained competitive rather than decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s stripped-file walkthrough exposed the limits of A’s inconsistency checks: without a marker or provenance claim, there is nothing to compare. A’s still-stamped candidate video demonstrated a genuine but narrower benefit. B more effectively pressed whether that benefit justified mandatory sorting and its effects on authentic, provenance-free footage.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B built the clearer path to victory by showing that PRO’s neutrality safeguard also neutralizes the mandate against determined deceivers: stripped provenance becomes indistinguishable from ordinary metadata loss, while cooperative creators bear the new labeling burden. A offered a plausible incremental case for uniform display and preservation, but its flagship compliant-deceiver example did not establish meaningful detection beyond voluntary provenance. A’s clipped rebuttal and partly self-directed pressure questions also weakened execution. B consistently shifted the comparison to the mandate versus voluntary provenance and fraud enforcement, rather than merely attacking identity verification.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal followed the deceiver through A’s “origin unavailable” rule and exposed the lack of any mismatch to detect. A’s compliant-generator example in Rebuttal 2 was then answered directly: it catches a liar who preserves evidence against himself. B’s closing disclosure analogy crystallized the same clash—ordinary disclosure regimes punish visible evasion, whereas A had made signal absence expressly inconclusive.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built and defended a clean causal story — authenticate the artifact, not the person — and kept sharpening the same decisive asymmetry: CON's signal must be an oracle while CON's alternative merely has to exist. CON's best pressure (the label authenticates neither sender nor consent; the parent's safety came from calling back, not the label) was directly answered with the smoke-alarm reframe: the label's job is to trigger out-of-band verification, and the blanket no-transfer rule CON proposed refuses genuine emergencies too. More damaging, CON's final remedy — provenance plus targeted duties on platforms — is essentially the motion's implementing framework, a point B landed in closing and A never really escaped, retreating to "those conditions should be explicit first," which is a drafting critique rather than a refutation. A's bearer-key and stripping objections were real but applied asymmetrically, and B exploited that repeatedly. Both sides had solid grounding with no fake citations; both handled Q/A format correctly. B's progression across rounds was tighter, and B made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1-B4.6): the "hang up and call the real number" answer plus exposing that CON concedes identity verification fails while proposing provider-side authentication — "our design" — collapsed CON's core objection and set up the closing kill-shot that CON's alternative is the motion restated.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): A established the stronger practical mechanism: synthetic-origin disclosure can prompt independent verification without identifying the poster, while mandatory interoperability can improve preservation over voluntary badges. B repeatedly demanded authentication or truth guarantees that this benefit does not require, and treated reasonable implementation provisions as unavailable unless explicitly stated in the motion. B’s stripping, false-reassurance, and compliance objections nevertheless limit the margin, especially because A overstated coverage and incorrectly equated targeted verification duties with its broader mandate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s smoke-alarm response crystallized its earlier answer: the callback resolving the fraud does not negate the label’s value in triggering that callback. B’s closing conceded that disclosure can prompt verification, weakening its central objection.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): A kept the debate focused on whether provenance improves consequential decisions without an identity gate. Its distinction between attesting synthetic origin and proving truth was decisive: a label can trigger a callback or escalate an unattested file without identifying the speaker. B raised genuine preservation, overtrust, and governance burdens, but often judged A against authentication or perfect fraud prevention rather than the status quo. B’s independent-verification alternative also depended on the useful signal A was mandating, leaving its comparative route less clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s answer to the bearer-key and stripping objections was most decisive: compliant misuse produces an honest synthetic disclosure, while stripping produces absence rather than authenticity. A then explained why mandatory downstream handling makes absence more informative than under voluntary labels. B’s response that an external callback—not the label—authenticated the caller did not defeat A’s narrower claim that the label prompts verification.
