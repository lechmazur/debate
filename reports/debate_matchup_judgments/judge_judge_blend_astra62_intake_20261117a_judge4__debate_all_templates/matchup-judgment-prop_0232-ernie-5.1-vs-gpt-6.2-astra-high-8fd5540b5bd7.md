### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON (A) wins with a disciplined distinction between taking cannabis sales off the black market and taking coercive power from armed groups. It pressed that distinction consistently through every round. PRO (B) opened with brittle, overconfident claims: illegal market share in Montevideo "cut by roughly half" and a "measurable drop in cartel-controlled territory" from Colombian pilots. It then rested almost its entire later case on seed-to-sale data as intelligence infrastructure. CON dismantled that pillar with the auditor and identical-ledger illustrations. An extortion payment made out of a grower's own pocket leaves no discrepancy in a compliant ledger. PRO never directly answered this. Its closing simply reasserted "turning a black box into a ledger." CON answered both pressure questions concretely. It offered confidential association reporting to an outside prosecutor, immunity, and relocation, plus a redirection of enforcement away from possession. PRO's A1 leaned on unverifiable "state intelligence at the time" claims. Its A2 conceded selection bias in the Colombian pilots, which CON then exploited. PRO also largely dropped CON's points about export corridors and foreign markets, and about legal share concentrating where armed groups are already weak. CON's comparative framing was cleaner and better grounded: the security gain comes when growers can refuse to pay, not when they can account for the crop.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.1 auditor scenario, extended in the closing's identical-ledgers image, was decisive. It showed that a fully compliant licensed supply chain reveals nothing about extortion paid out of a grower's proceeds. That directly neutralized PRO's central claim that regulation supplies the data needed to fight organized crime, and PRO never answered it.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by targeting the weakest part of A's causal chain: legal sales can shift without revealing or cutting the criminal rents that sustain armed groups. A's strongest late move was that regulation creates necessary visibility, but B's concrete, non-registry enforcement alternative and its identical-ledger example showed that extortion remains invisible in a compliant cannabis ledger. B also exposed the Uruguay and Colombia examples as uncertain about whose revenue was lost and whether security gains preceded licensing. A's closing repeated the visibility claim instead of answering B's protection-without-registry mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's identical-ledger argument was decisive: two licensed growers can have identical records while one pays extortion, so seed-to-sale tracking does not reveal the difference. B then answered A's pressure question concretely with anonymous farmers' association reports and outside prosecutors, undercutting A's claim that legalization supplies the necessary intelligence infrastructure.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B systematically dismantled both of Side A's routes to victory while maintaining superior epistemic grounding. On revenue, Side B showed that shifting domestic retail sales does not reliably impair armed groups funded by export corridors, cocaine, and territorial extortion, noted that legal retail concentrates where cartels are already weakest, and exposed selection bias in Side A's Colombian pilot example. Side A eventually conceded in closing that protecting witnesses and tracing extortion matter more than retail shifts, staking the entire debate on the claim that seed-to-sale tracking creates the visibility needed for anti-extortion policing. Yet Side A never answered Side B's central rebuttal: a licensed grower who pays cash extortion out of post-sale proceeds produces a clean commercial ledger indistinguishable from an unextorted grower's, whereas targeted tools like outside prosecutors, pre-testimony relocation, and cultivation immunity directly address grower silence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B2.1] and [B5.1], Side B demolished Side A's core "black box to ledger" pivot by pointing out that when a licensed farmer sells every gram legally on invoice and then pays a roadblock extortionist out of pocket, the commercial ledger matches perfectly and reveals nothing about the extortionist—a fatal flaw Side A never answered despite staking its closing win condition on regulatory visibility.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won with a clear causal mechanism: customers displaced week after week, and national rules letting supply routes bypass cartel-held territory. PRO also made a comparative point about state capacity: weak states are no better at policing a secret trade than a licensed one. It held the motion's actual test, "weaken," against CON's drift toward "cripple" or "dismantle." CON (Side A) raised legitimate concerns about co-option and state capacity, but its case had three serious weaknesses: - **Weak grounding.** Its opening relied on shaky examples, such as cartels undercutting Mexican legal dispensaries and assassinating regulators. PRO exposed these as describing a system that does not exist. - **Misdescribed concessions.** CON claimed PRO conceded that gang-controlled towns "define vast stretches" of the region, and that PRO had previously said governments lack capacity. Neither accurately reflects PRO's speeches. - **A self-defeating answer.** In its A2 response, CON admitted cartels may not replace cannabis income "dollar for dollar" and merely "absorb the hit." That concedes the weakening the motion asks about. PRO answered CON's pressure questions concretely. On demand, it argued that competing alongside dealers still erodes their sales and margins. On enforcement, it proposed ownership disclosure, outside auditors, and invoice reconciliation. CON then recast those safeguards as an admission of fragility rather than engaging them as design. PRO's closing crystallized the debate cleanly: a cartel surviving the loss is no reason to spare it the loss.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came on diversification. CON argued that losing cannabis "weakens nothing structural." PRO's Q2 forced CON to concede that other rackets cushion the loss rather than replace it. PRO's closing then turned this into the line "That a cartel can survive the loss is no reason to spare it the loss," which directly ties the concession to the motion's "weaken" standard.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): A wins on the comparative mechanism: PRO built a live route to weakening cartel income through lawful consumer and grower alternatives, while CON's strongest structural critique depended on co-option and state weakness. A materially damaged B's opening empirics by noting Mexico lacks a nationwide legal recreational market, and A answered the enforcement pressure with concrete safeguards rather than evasion. B's capture argument was serious, but A's response that state incapacity also afflicts prohibition, plus national sourcing outside cartel-controlled territory, left enough of PRO's route intact. A's closing effectively crystallized that a cartel surviving lost cannabis sales is still weakened, not reimbursed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal that Mexico has no nationwide system of licensed recreational dispensaries undercut CON's claim that cartels were already outcompeting legal shops, turning B's central failure example into an unsupported prediction.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A built a much cleaner comparative path to victory anchored to the motion's actual standard of weakening, rather than single-handedly destroying, organized crime. Side A immediately caught Side B's factual overreach regarding Mexican dispensaries, distinguished supplying a market from physically governing territory, and showed how national legalization lets urban retailers source outside cartel-controlled farming regions. After Side A's pressure questions forced Side B to concede that cartels would absorb an unreplaced revenue hit, Side B relied on shifting the burden toward total structural collapse and treating standard anti-capture audits as proof of failure, whereas Side A persuasively showed that imperfect regulation of auditable competitors beats a prohibition regime where the state enforces a monopoly for criminals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's territorial and diversification defenses across Rebuttals 1-2 and Closing: separating supplying a town from governing it [A2.3], showing national legalization lets city shops bypass cartel-held growing zones [A4.5], using Q2 [A3.2] to force Side B into conceding an unreplaced revenue hit [B4.3], and sealing the comparison by noting that under prohibition the state itself keeps law-abiding shopkeepers out of the market [A5.3].
