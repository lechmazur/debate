### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A (CON) built the more decisive case and defended it consistently. A's core attack—that building domestic clusters does not achieve real sovereignty because hardware depreciates fast, supply chains remain foreign-concentrated, and crises demand burst scale that fixed public capacity cannot provide—was never squarely neutralized. B progressively retreated from "massive compute clusters" to a "modest sovereign floor" whose crisis role still depends on commercial burst contracts, which A exploited repeatedly as a concession of the motion's core. A's Ukraine reversal, the equity arithmetic on poorer nations, and the multi-jurisdiction failover answer to B's pressure questions were direct and damaging. B's competence/negotiation-from-knowledge fallback was plausible and rhetorically framed well ("who controls the switch," "floor beneath the contract"), but A showed competence can be built on rented clouds without owning depreciating silicon, and B's regional-hub answer for poor nations was undercut by the foreign-soil and diplomatic-gridlock points. B's closings crystallized the clash well but rested on claims A had already contested. A won the decisive exchanges while maintaining strong epistemic discipline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal 1 [A2.1-A2.5], which pinned B's retreat from massive clusters to a modest non-cutting-edge cluster, flipped the Ukraine example against B, and dismantled the equity argument with simple arithmetic—forcing B onto the defensive for the rest of the debate.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B exposed the central gap: A never explained how its modest sovereign floor would sustain the crisis workloads used to justify reducing heavy cloud dependence. B also directly challenged A’s affordability and institutional-competence arguments, while A repeatedly claimed concessions B had expressly denied. A retained a legitimate controlled-fallback argument, but did not establish its operational sufficiency or comparative value. B’s overconfident instant-failover claims and assertion that replacement-chip bans immediately freeze existing clusters limit the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange paired two responses to A’s modest-cluster defense: emergency burst capacity still depended on commercial providers, and engineers could evaluate vendors and develop expertise using rented infrastructure. A answered neither adequately, instead falsely describing the competence argument as conceded.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B won by forcing and then exploiting Side A's retreat from large sovereign clusters to a modest floor that still depends on private burst capacity precisely for the crises the motion treats as decisive. B consistently showed that ownership neither solves chip-supply exposure nor matches elasticity, multi-vendor failover, or equity, while A misstated B's concessions and never neutralized that heavy-dependence problem. B's mobility-over-metal comparison therefore remained the cleaner voting path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal was decisive: it answered the export-ban and equity questions with portable multi-cloud and joint procurement, then used A's own admission that emergencies still ride commercial burst capacity to collapse the "rather than depend heavily" claim.

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) consistently controlled the decisive clash: crisis-time operational control versus contractual remedies. Side A's best counter—that diversified, multi-jurisdictional contracts and allied datacentres can secure public-interest AI—was effectively gutted when A conceded the need for dedicated, air-gapped, isolated racks, which let B show taxpayers would pay owner-level depreciation plus oligopoly markup while retaining tenant vulnerability.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response to B's arbitration and reputational penalties crystallizes the decisive clash: a breach-of-contract remedy cannot keep critical services operating during a foreign cutoff. Side B’s obsolescence and relocated-dependence points were real but never neutralized the core risk that a provider must obey its home government.
