### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): PRO wins a clear but competitive debate. PRO set out a bounded duty early: triggers for material changes, deadlines, and no worker veto. It then defended that model consistently, and its A1 answer gave a concrete mechanism: a statutory checklist, the employer bearing the risk of omissions, and expedited adjudication. That blunted CON's hold-up argument. PRO also shifted the expertise clash usefully. Councils judge workplace effects rather than model mathematics. PRO argued that interpretation is unavoidable and should not be monopolized by the party capturing the savings. That was the sharpest comparative point in the debate. CON's strongest route was "threshold control," the idea that the power to force pre-deployment adjudication is itself the tollgate. It was well pressed and partly live. However, CON never squarely answered PRO's expedited-deadline mechanism. CON's alternative of independent regulators also left the deployment-level knowledge gap open. CON's closing reply that councils, like regulators, cannot see shift-level facts was unconvincing, since workers are the people on those shifts. The arguments about patients, investors, and future hires were raised repeatedly but stayed largely assertional.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange that decided the debate was CON's B4.3/B4.4 against PRO's A5.1. CON reframed the clash as "who interprets," answering "accountable regulators, not councils." PRO replied that regulators can set rules like "meaningful human review" but cannot know whether reviewers on a particular shift actually have the time or authority to reverse a score. CON's closing response, that councils cannot see this either, was weak and left PRO's knowledge-gap point standing.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined, format-compliant, and built real clash. B's strongest live route was threshold control: that materiality classification becomes the hold-up itself, and that councils are bargaining actors rather than fact-finders. A answered this more concretely than B pressed it—a statutory checklist, pre-deployment disclosure, expedited labor-board arbitration, and no veto give the design coherent internal limits, and A correctly noted that if everything consequential is "material," then filing with a deadline is simply the process, not an indefinite tollgate. On the second clash (who interprets), B's regulator answer was responsive but left the local-knowledge gap A kept exposing: regulators can set "meaningful human review" but cannot see shift-level rubber-stamping, whereas councils plausibly can. B's asymmetric-accountability point (patients, future workers) was partially blunted by A's concession that external duties persist. A's answers to pressure questions were sharper and more institutional; B's were good but leaned on repeating the threshold claim. B's closing crystallized one clear reason to vote, which was effective, but A's closing resolved both live clashes. Narrow win to A on stronger pressure-question handling and a more decisive resolution of the debate's core comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The B4.1/B4.2 exchange: B's argument that no veto is needed because the power to force pre-deployment adjudication is itself the tollgate was the sharpest attack of the debate, and A's A4.1/A5.3 counter—expedited deadlines and a checklist that makes omission the employer's risk—was the most decisive defensive move, keeping A's bounded-design route alive.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins a high-level, competitive debate by decisively winning the comparative governance clash over who interprets workplace safeguards before harm hardens into job design. Side A (CON) generated serious pressure by showing that Side B's six-factor materiality trigger covers most consequential AI updates and that pre-deployment disputes create procedural leverage even without a veto. However, Side B reasonably bounded that process cost (usable workflow disclosure rather than code audits, expedited threshold review without open-ended stays, and fixed deadlines with no veto) while exposing a critical blind spot in every version of Side A's alternative: post-harm lawsuits ([A2.6]) require workers to absorb injury, surveillance, or discipline first; voluntary consultation lets the cost-capturing purchaser ignore inconvenient warnings; and ex ante regulators ([A4.3]) write general rules without seeing shift-level realities. Side A also overreached in closing by claiming an employer that files still waits for classification and good faith to be litigated before any clock starts ([A5.1]) and that elected worker councils cannot see shift-level facts ([A5.3]).
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side B's Q2 ([B3.2]) exposed Side A's admission in [A2.6] that substantive standards are enforced after concrete harm, Side A pivoted in [A4.3] to ex ante public regulators setting pre-rollout limits. Side B's [B5.1] cleanly dismantled that recovery—arguing it "answers the rule, not the deployment"—by showing that a regulator can require "meaningful human review" in general but cannot know that on a specific shift reviewers lack the time or authority to reverse an AI score.
