### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): B better defended optional delegation: budget and merchant boundaries need not be the agent’s only instructions, and users can retain confirmation for sensitive transactions. A established that financial caps do not bound every harm, but never justified treating advance purchasing instructions as incompatible with the motion. Its staple exception conceded substantial ground, while queuing unavailable users’ decisions left B’s lost-opportunity argument standing. B’s exaggerated claims about harmless errors and its occasional portrayal of A as banning all automation limit the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure questions exposed both the cost of waiting for unavailable users and A’s concession permitting automatic staple purchases. B then directly answered A’s questions with advance booking filters and product instructions. A’s response called these “item-level consent” without establishing why advance authorization requires item-by-item confirmation.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B won by keeping the vote on the motion's actual trigger. Budget and merchant rules can bound who is paid and how much, but they do not identify the right product, clinician, substitution, or refund terms. B demolished the wrong-coffee worst case with allergens, formula, and nonrefundable bookings, then turned A's later SKU, clinician, and refundability filters into an admission that the stated guardrails do not capture intent. A's opt-in and missed-slot points stayed live, but they defended a richer instruction set than the proposition specifies and never justified waiving item-level confirmation for discretionary bookings once only payment rules are set.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal and close: A's added filters and cancellation receipts conceded that payment rails do not supply intent, while auto-approving reversible staples and confirming substitutions and irreversible terms answered fatigue without deleting the last checkpoint.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO's opt-in framing survived: allowing autonomy does not force sensitive automation. CON showed budget and merchant rules cannot capture intent, but conceded auto-approving true staples and never justified banning bounded, reversible delegation for consenting users. PRO better handled time-sensitive losses and reframed caps as a safety floor rather than the agent's only instructions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 directly answered both pressure questions, exposed CON's staples concession as inconsistent with a blanket confirmation rule, and crystallized the asymmetry between a slot lost while asleep and a bounded, cancellable mistake.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): A wins the central comparison: staging retains automated constraint-checking while adding an opportunity to catch contextual mistakes before payment. B repeatedly compares machine rules against human review rather than answering their combination, and its pressure answers never adequately address confidently mistaken execution. B establishes genuine convenience and confirmation-fatigue benefits, but increasingly relies on additional restrictions to contain the risks. A's sweeping legal claims receive little weight; its stronger preventive-safety argument earns a clear, competitive win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's staging rebuttal in A2.3, sharpened in A4.5–A4.6, was decisive. Human review supplements rather than replaces machine safeguards, and confirmation can prevent an irreversible charge rather than recover it afterward. B's response that a human click cannot resurrect money misses that timing distinction.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B won by holding the motion to its actual trigger: budget and merchant rules as a sufficient basis for unconfirmed checkout. Side A never escaped the charge that it kept adding substitution bans, layover limits, uncertainty halts, and hard stops for non-refundable or perishable orders. B also beat the confirmation-theater frame by showing that staging keeps machine checks and adds a concrete pre-checkout look, while A conceded that logs and revocation cannot recover irreversible honest-merchant mistakes and wrongly treated that click as post-hoc recovery rather than prevention. A's fatigue and consistency points stayed live, but they did not justify removing the last consent step under the rules the motion actually names.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal was decisive: it separated exact auto-ship consent from dynamic agent selection, answered that confirmation preserves conspicuous assent and stops the charge before money moves, and pinned A's admission that audit logs and revocation cannot unwind a completed non-refundable or perishable purchase.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): CON won by showing budget and merchant rules do not determine what is bought, that PRO survived only by adding item-level bans and halt-if-uncertain judgment beyond the motion, and that staging preserves all machine checks while adding a cheap pre-payment human check. PRO's confirmation-fatigue argument never answered that both-layers comparison, and PRO conceded audit logs and revocation cannot recover irreversible non-refundable purchases.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it answered the auto-ship and dispute-rights questions with prior item-level consent and conspicuous-assent distinctions, then used PRO's concessions on unenumerable traps and unrecoverable charges to show confirmation prevents errors before checkout rather than trying to reverse them after.
