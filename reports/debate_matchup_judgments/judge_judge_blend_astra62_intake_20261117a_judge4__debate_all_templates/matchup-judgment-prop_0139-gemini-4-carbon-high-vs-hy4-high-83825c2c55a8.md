### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO wins because it kept the decision anchored to the actual comparison: permission versus prohibition. Its core claim was that a ban creates no universal safety and simply removes the one immediate refuge. That claim was never squarely neutralized. CON built a sophisticated case on moral hazard, rationed safety, and displacement, and pressed well on the open-ended permission and on the caregiver exception. But its harms remained largely speculative institutional predictions. PRO repeatedly showed that rejecting the motion does not help the late arrival or the woman with a son either. CON's flat "No" to guardrails let PRO frame CON's principle as "no car may be a refuge until every car is." CON's sharpest move was that "one glance" enforcement still requires staff who could protect the whole train. PRO answered it adequately: excluding a visibly out-of-place adult man is far easier than detecting covert groping in a mixed crush. PRO also turned the bridge analogy and CON's own concession of a "short-term shield." Both sides handled the A1:/A2: format cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The key exchange came after CON's [A4.4] claim that the shield is "enforcement, not segregation" because intruders must be seen and excluded. PRO replied in [B4.1] and [B5.3] that spotting an adult man entering a women-only section is trivial, whereas no guard at a mixed door can identify which man will grope once the doors close. That kept the sex label's distinct mechanism alive and blunted CON's strongest attempt to collapse PRO's remedy into universal policing.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Both sides were strong, but A controlled the decisive clash. A's crush/anonymity mechanism survived B's upstream crowd-control answer, and A's identification-vs-prediction asymmetry (spotting a man entering a women-only car vs. predicting which man in a mixed crowd will grope) directly neutralized B's best point that "the shield is enforcement, not segregation." A also turned B's guardrails attack cleanly: a ban writes no staffing floors either, and B's flat "No" to A2 (rejecting even guarded versions) exposed a categorical principle that sacrifices solo commuters' immediate protection, which A exploited effectively in the closing. B landed real hits on displacement, exclusion of women with sons/caregivers, and moral hazard, and was rhetorically crisp throughout, but never squarely answered why removing the sole available refuge helps the women left in mixed cars beyond the "leveling down" objection A pressed repeatedly. B's "crush is controllable, not weather" was asserted more than demonstrated, and the late-arriving, cash-strapped-system problem A raised went largely unanswered. A made the debate easier to decide on the motion as written (a permission question), while B's strongest material pushed toward a different, stricter motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The B4.4 "shield is enforcement, not segregation" versus A5.3 "one glance vs. prediction" exchange: B's sharpest structural attack was met with the debate's most decisive counter — excluding a visible category at a doorway is categorically easier than predicting an offender inside a packed mixed car — which resolved the mechanism clash in A's favor.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B wins because it better defended permission as written against an ongoing harm. Side A built a sharp case that open-ended sorting creates coercion, residual risk, and an institutional alibi, and correctly refused to rewrite the motion into a guarded alternative. But Side B showed that a ban writes no staffing floors or universal duties either, and that a women-only doorway creates an instantly visible rule breach that mixed-car monitoring cannot match for stealth groping. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won because it anchored the debate in the motion's actual wording: "allowed," voluntary, and openly partial. It then forced CON to defend prohibition as the better rule. Its harm-reduction framing held up, and so did its central comparison: one failed system with an optional refuge versus the same failed system without one. CON (A) produced three serious pressures: - **Alibi:** a women-only car gives a failing agency a cheap way to look responsive while avoiding real fixes. - **Enforcement dilemma:** the boundary either relies on soft self-identification that intruders ignore, or on hard enforcement that becomes appearance policing. - **Externality:** the car could thin female presence in mixed cars and invite victim-blaming of women who ride there. The enforcement dilemma was CON's sharpest line. PRO partially defused it with the priority-seat analogy, self-identification without inspection, and the point that refusing to leave after a request is itself the violation. CON's remaining weight rested on mechanisms it did not establish. The claim that a ban channels political pressure into reform, and the thinning and victim-blaming effects, were asserted rather than shown. PRO's "a ban is also paper" turn exposed that weakness. CON's reply, that a ban costs nothing while conditions require competence, was clever but did not show that a ban produces any reform. PRO honestly conceded "not by itself" on A2. CON exploited that concession well, but it did not overcome PRO's core point: removing an option leaves women more exposed now. The result is a clear but competitive edge for PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over enforcement and paper rules. CON's A2.2–A2.3 built a dilemma: the boundary is either a soft suggestion or body policing. PRO's B4.1–B4.5 answered on two levels. First, refusal to leave after a request is the enforceable act, settled by self-identification rather than appearance checks. Second, it turned CON's "paper decree" critique back on CON, since a ban is equally paper and funds nothing. CON's quiet-car reply ("silence is compliance") was sharp but did not restore a positive mechanism for why prohibition helps.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): A won on the motion as written: "allowed" sets a low bar, and A consistently framed the choice as a failing system with one voluntary refuge versus the same failing system with that refuge confiscated. A conceded honestly ("It does not, by itself") and paired each concession with a comparative, and its closing inversion — failure becoming a reason to leave women more exposed, with "a principle as the alibi" — crystallized the stakes memorably. B landed the sharpest technical exchanges (the quiet-car disanalogy, the asymmetry between a costless ban and budget-dependent conditions, the enforcement dilemma), but B's two live routes to victory — the alibi mechanism and the net-harm-to-the-majority claim — rested on speculative displacement and political-pressure effects that A repeatedly pressed for a mechanism and never received. B's rebuttal work was excellent, but its case for prohibition, the higher burden, never fully discharged.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's quiet-car rejoinder [B4.2] — that quiet cars have no "silent rule-breakers" because silence is compliance, so the analogy cannot rescue sex-based enforcement — was the single most damaging exchange, puncturing A's enforcement model. A's best counter-pressure was [A5.1]-[A5.2]: B cannot call legal conditions impotent "paper decrees" while treating a legal ban as decisive, since a ban funds no train and disciplines no guard.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a close but clear exchange by turning “partial harm reduction” into net harm. A broke the quiet-car and self-identification rescue: spotting a sex-based intruder still depends on who looks female, so the boundary is either appearance policing or not a real refuge. B’s own concession that staff rules do not by themselves stop spillover blame then left the promised whole-train patrols unavailable on the motion’s premise of already failing agencies. B’s “a ban is also paper” point stayed live, but A’s later distinction—that a prohibitory ban is self-executing while affirmative conditions require competence the system lacks—was the cleaner reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s rebuttal of the quiet-car analogy: silence is compliance there, but a women-only boundary requires challenging people by appearance, and B’s admission that staff rules do not themselves stop blame norms left the spillover case unrepaired once imaginary patrols were stripped away.
