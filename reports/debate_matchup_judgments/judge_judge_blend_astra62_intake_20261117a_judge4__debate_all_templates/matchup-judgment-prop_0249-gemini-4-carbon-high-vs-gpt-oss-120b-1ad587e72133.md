- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): PRO built a clear externality-plus-market-failure case and adapted well across rounds. CON's case rested on affordability, startup burden, enforcement difficulty, and better alternatives. However, in Rebuttal 2 CON answered PRO's Q1 by conceding that labels and lawsuits "cannot close the externality gap" and "provide no deterrence." It also conceded in A2 that unpatchable devices become permanent backdoors. Those concessions removed CON's main comparative alternative. PRO's closing exploited this cleanly. CON's closing ignored the concession and recycled the affordability claim, leaning on brittle specifics ("256 KB," "2023 estimate," "majority of botnet traffic") without engaging PRO's safety-baseline analogy. CON's legacy-stock point cuts against both sides equally, and PRO neutralized it. The remaining affordability and SME concerns were live but outweighed, given that CON itself admitted the harm is unaddressed without a binding rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 asked how labels or liability could stop botnet externalities. CON's A1 conceded they cannot, and PRO's closing [A5.1] quoted that concession to show every non-mandatory alternative had collapsed. This was the decisive turn of the debate.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO built the cleaner path to victory. B framed unpatchable cheap IoT as a public-safety externality that voluntary labels and lawsuits cannot solve, then turned A's own hardware constraints into an indictment of selling permanently vulnerable devices. A's strongest affordability and legacy-stock objections were substantially answered: new mandates need not retrofit old stock, enforcement can be exploit-driven, and cross-border floors are workable. Most damagingly, A conceded that labels and tort liability provide no deterrence, collapsing its alternative case.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was B exposing that A's own admission that labels and lawsuits cannot stop botnet externalities destroyed CON's preferred alternative, while B converted A's unpatchable-budget-hardware point into an argument for mandatory memory headroom rather than a defense of the status quo.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): CON (B) wins clearly. B built a coherent case against the mandate: the worst botnet devices come from vendors that dissolve, so fines cannot reach them; penalties instead fall on reachable firms, especially startups; and upstream silicon end-of-support leaves downstream makers unable to patch at all. A responded with patches that never quite repaired the case. Import certification cannot guarantee that a seller survives for years. A "non-updatable" carve-out effectively concedes that the motion's "all consumer IoT devices" requirement is unworkable. A late escrow and third-party patching scheme then opened a new weakness. B exploited it sharply: outside contractors would need proprietary source code and private signing keys, creating a central master-key target. A's grounding was weak, relying on unnamed "studies show" and "reports reveal" claims and an unsourced order-of-magnitude estimate. A's pressure questions were a serious own-goal: both asked B to restate CON's own strongest points, which B answered with ease. A's closing largely repeated its case and never addressed the supply-chain problem or the key-handover risk.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3–B4.4 did the most damage. B showed that A's escrow-funded third-party patching would require handing over source code and signing keys, which creates a new security risk. It also argued that the "non-updatable with retirement schedule" tier abandons the universal mandate and contradicts A's own longevity argument in A1.4.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): Side A won by making the enforcement and feasibility failures decisive. A repeatedly showed that the proposal would not reach the rogue foreign and shell-company sellers driving botnets, while B's fixes—import certification, importer liability, escrow-funded patching—failed against direct-to-consumer imports, dissolved shell importers, closed-source silicon, and the unsafe centralization of proprietary keys. A also turned B's non-updatable tier into a concession that contradicts the motion's "all consumer IoT devices" requirement. B had a clear security rationale, but A's rebuttals left the live route to victory badly damaged.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange was the two-part attack in Rebuttal 2: customs certification cannot guarantee future maintenance obligations, and B's "non-updatable" carve-out surrenders the universal update mandate. A then gutted the escrow remedy by explaining that third-party patchers cannot patch closed-source chips or safely take every maker's private signing keys.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won by showing the mandate misses its main targets and punishes reachable firms. Vanishing direct-to-consumer sellers and shell importers evade customs, fines, and importer liability, closed-source silicon cannot be patched by downstream brands, and A's non-updatable and retirement exceptions concede the requirement for all devices. A's escrow rescue never answered how modest funds or outside contractors could patch proprietary orphan firmware.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: escrow-funded patching would require surrendering source code and private signing keys to a central master-key target, while still failing for vanished importers and upstream chip cutoffs, collapsing A's enforcement mechanism.
