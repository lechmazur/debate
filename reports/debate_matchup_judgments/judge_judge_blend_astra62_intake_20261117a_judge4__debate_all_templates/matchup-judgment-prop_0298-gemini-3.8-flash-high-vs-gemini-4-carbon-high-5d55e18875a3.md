### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins a clear but competitive debate. PRO's moral frame was strong: the state broke these families, so the state owes repair, and no one should pay a toll to learn their own identity. But PRO never developed that frame much past its opening. CON's opening gave the debate its working structure, with three lines that PRO only partly answered. The first was a perpetrator-incentive argument: a government facing costly claims will quietly starve the archive. The second was a privacy and data-sovereignty harm from open public-domain access. The third was a set of limits that states cannot overcome on their own: records held across borders or by non-state bodies, and lineages with no paper trail where only DNA can help. PRO's best counter was that CON still relies on the state to license its vaults. CON turned that point by distinguishing passive suppression through budget starvation from a one-signature, zero-cost license that removes the fiscal excuse. PRO's privacy fix of community trusts and privacy firewalls was reasonable, but CON correctly pointed out that those trusts depend on the same politically vulnerable appropriations. PRO's answers to the pressure questions were thin. Statutory mandates and subpoenas cannot compel legislatures to fund indexing, and the appeal to diplomatic treaties for records held by hostile states was asserted rather than shown. PRO's closing largely recapped its opening. CON has weaknesses of its own: its claim that network economics will make platforms serve minority records is speculative, and its account of licensing safeguards is somewhat optimistic. Still, CON adapted across rounds and left more live routes standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came after the pressure questions. PRO answered with "statutory mandates, judicial enforcement, and permanent trust endowments." CON replied that a family cannot subpoena an entry it does not know exists, and that judges cannot force legislatures to fund transcription that would increase the state's own liability. That reply neutralized PRO's accountability claim and strengthened CON's passive-suppression account.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B won the decisive exchanges. A's strongest route—the "licensing contradiction" (CON still needs the state and adds a paywall)—was directly answered by B's mechanism that states suppress passively via starved indexing budgets, and that zero-cost vendor licenses, contractual free library/tribal access, and distributed copies break that dynamic. B also kept two live routes A never fully neutralized: the transnational/private-custody records problem (A's "diplomatic treaties" answer was thin) and the DNA dead-end for destroyed lineages. A's answers to pressure (statutory mandates, permanent trust endowments) were plausible but hand-wavy, and B's "you cannot subpoena an unindexed box" riposte landed. A additionally incurred a real execution cost by repeatedly citing nonexistent reference IDs (C1.1, P2.2, etc.), while B's citations were mostly accurate. Both sides were substantively strong; B made the debate easier to decide and resolved more of the opponent's pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The B4.1/B4.2 answers to A's pressure questions were the turning point: instead of dodging, B specified enforceable licensing contracts, free digital masters, and the passive-starvation mechanism for state suppression, then B4.3 dismantled A's subpoena/endowment answers with "a family cannot subpoena an entry it does not know exists."
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A built the cleaner comparative path: licensing contracts returning free masters and guaranteeing library/tribal access answered the paywall pressure, distributed copies and private capital answered passive state suppression, and cross-border plus DNA dead-ends remained largely unanswered. Side B's reparative-duty and market-neglect case was strong, but its closing repeated paywall and state-guarantee claims without neutralizing A's contract and distributed-copy mechanisms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: families cannot subpoena unindexed records they do not know exist and courts cannot compel legislatures to fund transcription, undercutting B's legal-enforcement answer to state self-interest.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins on the core comparison the motion requires: public funding versus leaving recovery mostly to private markets. PRO built a clear causal story. The state already holds the sealed originals. Markets skip fragmented, low-margin records and put indexes behind paywalls. "Publicly funded" decides who pays, not who sees. PRO also neutralized CON's privacy and sovereignty attacks with credentialed access, tribal-controlled grants, and mirrored copies. CON's strongest line was "perpetrator state as gatekeeper," and it had real rhetorical force. However, CON's A1 answer conceded that private platforms only index voluntary or already-public material. That effectively admits markets cannot recover the sealed records at stake. CON's A2 answer then pivoted to philanthropy, academic consortia, and repatriation. This drifted toward a different motion, "decentralized community stewardship," rather than defending private markets as the motion frames them. CON claimed PRO surrendered the motion by allowing tribes to keep records sealed. That overreaches: the motion does not demand universal public exposure, and PRO explained why. Both sides cited nonexistent reference IDs (C-/P- labels), which is a minor execution cost to each. Overall, PRO adapted better across rounds and gave the cleaner reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing turned CON's A1 answer into a concession. If commercial platforms only index voluntary or existing public-domain archives, then markets cannot break state suppression of sealed files. CON's own alternatives (philanthropy, repatriation, consortia) also abandon reliance on markets. That reframes the debate as public action versus records staying buried, which CON never squarely answered.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A (PRO) won by keeping the debate on the motion as written: publicly funded digitization versus leaving recovery mostly to private genealogy markets. A's decisive route — the state already holds the sole originals in closed vaults, private firms cannot scan them without state permission, and markets skip low-margin emancipation and wartime registries — was never neutralized. B's answer to A's pressure questions (private platforms only index "voluntary submissions and existing public-domain archives") effectively conceded that markets cannot break state suppression, and B's pivot to philanthropy and paper repatriation abandoned the market model the motion asks B to defend. A also turned B's own points: praising commercial digitization revived B's privacy objections against corporations, and the library argument conceded taxpayer funding while routing it to private monopolies. B's best line was the "perpetrator state gatekeeper" paradox and the claim that A's tribal-sealing concession abandoned the motion; but A's concession is a coherent carve-out — public funding with sovereign refusal rights still contrasts with private-market reliance — so B's "PRO surrendered the motion" framing overstated what happened. A answered both pressure questions directly and with substance (statutory access rules, mirrored copies, unconditional tribal grants). B loses some grounding for repeated nonexistent reference IDs ([C1.3], [P2.3], etc.) and for the overreaching concession claim; A had one similar stray reference. Both sides were rhetorically sharp, but A made the decisive clash clearer and resolved more of the pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The pivotal exchange was A's second pressure question (how do tribes and non-profits recover state-held originals without public funding?) meeting B's answer that private firms only index voluntary and public-domain archives. A's closing seized this: markets concede they cannot break state suppression, reducing B's alternative to charity and paper repatriation — the collapse of the market-defense burden the motion assigned to CON.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO sustained a coherent distinction between publicly funded, tiered access and open exposure, answering privacy and sovereignty with credentialing, redactions, mirrored copies, and unconditional tribal control. CON's gatekeeper critique was forceful, but its market alternative collapsed into admitting private firms only handle voluntary or public-domain records, leaving sealed and low-margin files inaccessible—the central market failure PRO identified. The library answer also never overcame PRO's point that it rents public money to private paywalls while omitting those records.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was B's first pressure question: A answered that private platforms cannot unseal adoption or tribal records, conceding they protect privacy only by providing no recovery, while B's closing used that admission to show inaction preserves state vaults and market exclusion.
