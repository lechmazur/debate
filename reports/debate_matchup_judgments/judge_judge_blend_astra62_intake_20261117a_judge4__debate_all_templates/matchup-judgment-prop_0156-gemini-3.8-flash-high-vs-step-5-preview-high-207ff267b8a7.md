### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins a clear but competitive debate. CON set the comparative frame: conditional support versus disorderly default and Grexit. It defended that frame consistently and engaged each of PRO's pillars. - **AfD:** It countered PRO's populism point by arguing that AfD's breakthrough came with the migration crisis. - **Southern party collapse:** It attributed this partly to local causes. - **Banking union:** It noted that common supervision and resolution were actually built. - **NextGenerationEU:** It cited NGEU as later evidence of shared liability, which was its most effective point. - **Coercion charge:** Its "impossible standard" argument pressed PRO to say what conditionality-free solidarity would look like. PRO never answered that. - **Concession:** CON honestly conceded that distrust rose, then weighed this against the evidence of what members actually did. PRO had a strong, coherent thesis: financial survival is not political solidarity, and the coercive treatment of Greece in 2015 overrode democracy. However, PRO largely restated that thesis across rounds rather than adapting it. Its weak spots were: - **NGEU:** The reply that the "Frugal Four fought viciously" does not explain why NGEU passed anyway. - **Bank-recycling claim:** CON's point about haircuts on private creditors went largely unanswered. - **Greek choices:** PRO's account of Greek "capitulation" never fully answered why Greece's government signed the deal and voters re-elected it. PRO's answer to the populism question was good, and so was its framing of the deposit insurance scheme (EDIS). Even so, CON did more to narrow the dispute and give a decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over whether post-crisis trust was repaired. PRO argued that the stalled European deposit insurance scheme (EDIS) shows durable northern distrust. CON answered that the same northern governments later approved joint borrowing and grants through NextGenerationEU. That is shared liability for southern recovery, and it undercuts PRO's claim of structural, permanent distrust. PRO's reply, that the Frugal Four resisted, conceded that the agreement ultimately happened, so the point stayed with CON.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): B won by making the comparison B had to win and winning it. B's core move—defining solidarity as mutual risk-sharing under rules rather than unconditional charity, with the counterfactual of disorderly Grexit—was introduced in the opening, defended under direct pressure, and crystallized in the closing ("solidarity is measured by what members do when leaving is possible"). B answered both pressure questions with required A1:/A2: labels, turned the Greek referendum from coercion evidence into proof of preserved membership, and used NextGenerationEU as narrow new support that directly answered A's "distrust is structural" claim, reframing EDIS delay as ordinary federal caution. A's case had real strengths—the creditor-debtor hierarchy frame and the liquidity-threat coercion story were coherent and rhetorically sharp—but A recycled the same three examples (Greek referendum, AfD, EDIS) in nearly every speech with limited progression, and twice cited nonexistent reference IDs ([C1.1], [C1.6], [C2.6] instead of B-labels), an execution cost. A never adequately answered B's central challenge: why Greeks and northern taxpayers kept choosing the union if trust was destroyed. B made the debate easier to decide; A's late speeches mostly restated rather than resolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to A's Greek-referendum pressure question (B4.1-B4.2): conceding the humiliation while reframing it—Tsipras signed the deal, Greece retained elections and the exit option it declined to use—converted A's strongest evidence into proof of preserved mutual commitment. B's follow-up that "coercion is politics without exits; Greece retained that option" was the decisive reframing of the debate.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): B won by reframing the comparison as conditional solidarity versus disorderly collapse, not versus unconditional harmony. B answered the referendum and deposit-insurance pressure with retained exit/elections and risk-control paired with later joint borrowing, and weakened A's populism attribution, while A never explained how solidarity could require accountability-free support.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it turned Greece 2015 into preserved choice under rules and EDIS delay into federal caution outweighed by NextGenerationEU, directly neutralizing A's two pressure questions.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a competitive debate by holding the motion's key terms in place. The motion asks about political distrust, and PRO kept the debate on that ground. Its central distinction was that survival and continued membership under threat of ruin measure fear more than consent. PRO pressed this from Rebuttal 1 through the closing, and CON never fully neutralized it. CON had real assets: - Breaking the no-bailout taboo. - Building the ESM, Banking Union and ECB backstops. - The September 2015 Greek election. - A reasonable falsifiability answer pointing to the absence of exits. However, CON mostly proved that institutional solidarity existed. It conceded "friction and anger" without showing that this distrust was smaller than the solidarity preserved. CON's speeches also grew repetitive: the closing largely restated the opening. PRO's answer to the conditionality question was somewhat vague. It never directly rebutted the significance of the September 2015 mandate beyond the "binary" framing. Its "distrust migrated into the system" turn is clever but hard to falsify. On balance, PRO made the comparison clearer and turned CON's evidence more effectively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's A2 and closing response to CON's "every electorate stayed and euroskeptics dropped exit" pressure. PRO argued that staying when exit means ruin measures fear rather than trust, and that euroskeptics governing inside the system shows distrust migrated rather than vanished. This reframed CON's strongest evidence as consistent with the motion. CON's reply, that averting ruin is itself solidarity, did not address the distrust half of the comparison.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined and responsive, but A controlled the decisive metric clash. The motion asks whether distrust deepened more than solidarity was preserved, and A relentlessly exposed that B's evidence (no exits, continued membership, later euro support) measures regime survival rather than trust. B's falsifiability answer in B4.2 ("my claim fails if electorates dismantled integration") effectively conceded the proxy problem: distrust can persist without exit, which A had already argued and crystallized in the closing ("a union you cannot afford to leave is not a union you trust"). B's strongest line—conditionality was the only politically feasible solidarity in a union of sovereign parliaments—was met by A's asymmetry reframe (creditor checks vs. debtor loss of policy control), which B answered only by repeating the "no open tab" point. B's NGEU groundwork argument was a good original contribution but A blunted it by noting capability is not repair. Both sides followed the A1/A2 format honestly and grounded claims in plausible background knowledge without fake precision. A's pressure questions were sharper and B's answers, while direct, ended up reinforcing A's framing. B closed well rhetorically ("forged, not outlasted"), but A made the debate easier to decide on the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.2): A dismantled both pressure questions directly, reframed conditionality as asymmetry rather than accountability, and turned B's own "euroskeptics governed within the framework" evidence into proof that distrust migrated into the system rather than dissipating—a move B never fully neutralized.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won the central metric clash: institutional survival and staying in the euro do not by themselves prove political trust. A consistently showed conditionality, the 2015 Greek override, and bilateral resentment deepened distrust, while neutralizing B's no-exit and euro-support evidence as choices made under fear of ruin. B's institutional solidarity case was strong, but it repeatedly reasserted survival as solidarity and mischaracterized A's asymmetry objection as a demand for unconditional, painless transfers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A's Rebuttal 2 and closing response to B's membership argument: continued membership under an accept-or-collapse binary measures fear of exit, while euroskeptic parties abandoning exit but governing as internal opponents shows distrust migrated into the system rather than disappearing. B never squarely repaired that distinction.
