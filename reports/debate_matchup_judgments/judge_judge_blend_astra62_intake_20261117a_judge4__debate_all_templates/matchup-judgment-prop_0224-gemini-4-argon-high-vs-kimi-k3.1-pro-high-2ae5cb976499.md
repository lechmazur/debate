### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides built plausible causal stories, but B maintained sharper analytical discipline throughout. B's core move—refusing to let strong life-extension economics "launder" uncertain new-build spending, and insisting the motion is about a bundled program's speed-per-dollar, not the value of its best component—was never squarely defeated. When A conceded in A4.2 that life-extension credit should be restricted to genuinely closure-bound reactors with full refurbishment costs deducted, B exploited this cleanly in rebuttal 2, pointing out that A could no longer invoke an "enormous wall" of abatement to rescue slipped new-build timelines. B also neutralized A's "contradiction" charge (geographic diversity vs. transmission delay) by showing the asymmetry cuts both ways: partial transmission dependence isn't universal decade-long delay, just as an existing switchyard isn't universal rapid nuclear delivery. A's case remained rhetorically strong and well-organized, with vivid framing ("plugging firm power into existing arteries"), but repeatedly relied on best-case nuclear assumptions against worst-case renewable assumptions, a framing B explicitly called out and A never fully rebutted. B's opening being clipped is a real execution cost, but B recovered the lost point in the next round without losing momentum.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal 2 (B4.4–B4.5), which uses A's own concession on restricted life-extension accounting to undercut A's "enormous wall of abatement" claim, while symmetrically dismantling A's attempted "contradiction" gotcha on transmission versus site-reuse assumptions.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): CON won by keeping the motion on the full life-extension-plus-new-build package and forcing consistent, dated accounting. PRO’s strongest assets—cheap immediate life-extension abatement and renewable system/transmission costs—stayed live, but after PRO accepted strict closure counterfactuals and full refurbishment costs, the “enormous wall” of LE savings could no longer automatically bail out uncertain new-build delivery inside 2026–2036. CON’s asymmetric-assumptions critique (best-case nuclear sites/schedules vs worst-case renewable saturation/backup) remained the cleaner comparative frame, and PRO never made the late-delivery risk disappear under that frame. CON’s clipped opening was an execution ding, but later rebuttals and closing still made the vote easier: portfolio win requires dependable abatement per public dollar across the whole program, not valuable nuclear assets plus optimistic switchyard assumptions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON A2.6/A4.5–A4.6: strip best-case nuclear delivery and worst-case renewable integration; properly restricted LE credits cannot rescue slipped new builds under consistent 2026–2036 portfolio accounting.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively dissecting the proposition's bundling of life-extensions and new-builds. A successfully argued that Side B was using the relatively cheap and fast life-extensions to mask the severe schedule and cost risks of new nuclear builds within the 2026-2036 window. A's handling of the pressure questions was precise, establishing strict counterfactuals for life-extensions that neutralized B's inflated baseline, while realistically defending phased renewable deployment against B's saturation and transmission critiques. B's case relied too heavily on optimistic assumptions about nuclear construction timelines and conflated existing switchyards with completed reactors.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it answered the pressure questions by accepting the counterfactual for life-extensions but restricting it to genuine closure risks, thereby preventing PRO from using avoided backsliding to launder the poor performance of late-arriving new builds. A also cleanly resolved the alleged contradiction regarding renewable transmission and saturation.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides accepted the "emissions avoided per public dollar before 2036" framing, but B built a tighter, harder-to-escape arithmetic trap: since the pool of reactors with credible retirement risk is small, an "equivalent scale-up" to match a decade-long renewable program necessarily pushes most nuclear dollars into new build, which cannot generate electricity inside the 2026–2036 window. A's pressure-question answer (A4.1) effectively conceded this ("equivalent public commitment... does not repeal arithmetic"), and B exploited that concession decisively in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, quoting it directly. A's fallback—selective "advanced-stage completions" and "repeat units"—was never grounded in a plausible real-world pool, and B's observation that such ready-to-complete projects "barely exist" went largely unanswered. A's strongest material (immediate abatement from life extension, symmetric system-cost accounting for renewables) remained live and partly effective, keeping the debate competitive, but it wasn't enough to neutralize B's core calendar argument, which became the debate's decisive axis. A's Rebuttal 1 being clipped before the judge saw its conclusion is also a real execution cost, cutting off development of its strongest counter-framing at a key moment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's capture of A's own concession in A4.1 ("equivalent scale-up does not repeal arithmetic") and its repeated use in Rebuttal 2 and Closing to show the bulk of nuclear funding is structurally locked out of the 2026–2036 window was the single most decisive exchange.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON won on the motion’s actual metric: 2026–2036 abatement per public dollar under an equivalent-scale nuclear portfolio. Life extension is PRO’s best lane, but CON showed the finite at-risk pool cannot absorb “equivalent” public spend, so arithmetic forces most dollars into new build that delivers near-zero kWh inside the window (or burns money then cancels). PRO’s selective/milestone framing and system-cost complaints were real, but never restored a portfolio-average edge once that clock and budget split were fixed. PRO R1 clipping and weaker handling of pay-on-delivery vs upfront capital risk also cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2/closing lock on B4.1’s arithmetic concession: after scarce credible extensions, matching scale forces majority new-build dollars that produce virtually nothing before 2036 (or zero if cancelled post-milestone).
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A won by strictly enforcing the 2026–2036 timeframe and the "equivalent scale-up" constraint. A successfully trapped B into admitting that because the pool of at-risk life-extensions is finite, matching the scale of a renewable build-out forces the bulk of nuclear funding into new builds. Since new nuclear takes over a decade, this yields zero abatement by 2036. A also decisively won the risk allocation clash by pointing out that renewable incentives pay upon delivery (shielding taxpayers from development failures), whereas nuclear milestone funding still burns upfront public money if a project is cancelled. B's concession on "portfolio arithmetic" in Rebuttal 2 was fatal, and A capitalized on it flawlessly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they exploited Side B's concession on "portfolio arithmetic" to prove that matching the renewable scale-up forces nuclear funding into 10+ year new builds that produce zero abatement within the target window, while also dismantling B's system-cost argument by distinguishing between private ratebase grid upgrades and direct public tax expenditures.
