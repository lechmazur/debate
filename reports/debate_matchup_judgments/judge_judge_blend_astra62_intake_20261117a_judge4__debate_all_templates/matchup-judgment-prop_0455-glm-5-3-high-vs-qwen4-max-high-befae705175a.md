### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins by holding the debate to the motion as written. Its most important move was to anchor on the "even if" clause and argue that PRO's showcase example, North Dakota, is one where capture infrastructure arrived alongside phase-in targets. On CON's account, the supply-reduction clause never fired, so PRO never actually defended the case the motion requires it to defend. CON also built a clear mechanism contrast. A price ranks wells by damage and funds abatement. A threshold-cliff sorts arbitrarily and collects no revenue. CON then tied the substitution and relocation point to the specific features of a globally traded fuel, which answered PRO's charge that the relocation logic "would invalidate every environmental rule." PRO had strong framing, especially "pure waste," the one-way-door idea, and the clever pressure questions arguing that CON's own mandate ends in shutdown and its own pricing closes wells. However, CON answered those questions directly. It distinguished enforcement against a refusing defaulter from pre-authorized supply loss, and it explained the ranking and revenue difference between a price and a threshold. PRO's own answers were weaker: - On the substitute question, PRO said "same molecule, different well," which leaned on phase-ins that effectively dissolve the clause. It then conceded LNG and relied on buyer preferences rather than any enforceable rule. - On the trickle question, PRO answered with a definition, "strict means serious." CON exposed this as circular. - PRO's closing mostly restated the waste framing and its claim that CON had conceded the "teeth," without closing the net-emissions gap CON kept pressing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 turned North Dakota against PRO. CON argued it showed capture mandates working because infrastructure arrived with the rule, so the "even if it reduces supply" clause never fired. PRO's case would then rest on an example where the motion's defining cost was zero. PRO never squarely recovered from this, and only reasserted that thresholds "forced" capture.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a high-level, competitive debate by better understanding oilfield mechanics, global fuel substitution, and regulatory instrument design. Side B built a strong case around methane's 20-year warming potency, North Dakota's flaring reductions, and the political-economy insight that the "even if" clause prevents lobbying escape hatches, while landing sharp pressure questions exposing that Side A's preferred policies also close hopeless or non-compliant wells. However, Side A decisively dismantled Side B's core claim that banning flared gas costs no usable energy by explaining that shutting associated-gas wells before pipelines exist cuts oil and co-produced gas output—a point Side B never refuted and largely ignored in closing. Side A also turned North Dakota by showing that phased targets arrived alongside pipelines so short-term supply never fell, proved that domestic shut-ins summon dirtier marginal substitutes like imported LNG or coal (which Side B weakly answered with buyer preferences after conceding LNG's leakage chain), and cleanly distinguished methane pricing—which ranks abatement costs, drives emissions below any cliff, and funds takeaway infrastructure—from blunt threshold bans.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A2.1]-[A2.7], Side A simultaneously turned Side B's flagship North Dakota example—showing infrastructure arrived alongside phased targets so the motion's supply-loss clause never fired—and broke Side B's seatbelt and scrubber analogies by noting those rules let the underlying activity continue, whereas shutting wells shifts globally traded fuel demand to LNG, coal, or laxer foreign producers, setting up the pressure question that forced Side B's damaging LNG concession in [B4.1].
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B won a competitive debate by keeping the motion’s explicit supply-loss clause central and exposing A’s reliance on North Dakota, where infrastructure arrived without that clause firing. A’s pressure questions sharply challenged B’s enforcement and pricing alternatives, but B answered both directly: last-resort shutdown differs from pre-authorized supply loss, while pricing selects efficient abatement and preserves revenue and marginal incentives. A never fully established that replacement LNG, coal, or foreign production would leave global emissions lower.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal neutralized A’s strongest pressure exchange by distinguishing enforcement against a defaulter from statutorily accepted scarcity, then explaining why a price and a shutdown threshold can lose the same barrel while producing different selection, revenue, and incentives.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Both sides were sharp, but PRO (B) built the cleaner path to victory. It reframed flared gas as supply that is already lost, so the "short-term supply" cost shrinks to the oil from marginal wells. It then set up a durable weighing: deferrable barrels against methane that can never be recovered once released. CON (A) ran a strong case. It pressed the "even if" clause, used the Niger Delta to show where capture is uneconomic, and turned PRO's "few sites, most of the waste" into an argument for targeted enforcement rather than a ban. It also claimed that cure periods and exemptions dissolve strictness. Two things tipped the debate to PRO. First, PRO's pressure questions exposed that CON's scalpel still needs a defining line and a binding shutdown power. Second, PRO answered the dissolution charge with the closed list of emergency exemptions and the speed-limit analogy. CON's replies were inventive: relative ranking, license revocation, and the idea that fees fund the enforcer. Still, license revocation for noncompliance implicitly needs a threshold. PRO's closing also turned the fee argument back on CON: a state funded by fees profits from continued venting. CON's late claim that deferred barrels are "just as irreversible" was asserted rather than established. It also slightly widened the debate in the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 and its closing follow-up decided the debate. CON's targeted-enforcement scalpel needs something to define "worst" and something to make a shutdown order binding, which is effectively a threshold. A ranking-based standard also never reaches zero. Together these neutralized CON's best turn, the "few sites, most of the waste" point.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): In a high-level debate from both sides, Side B (PRO) won a clear but competitive decision by winning the institutional comparison and bounding the energy-supply tradeoff. Side A scored real points by exposing Side B's opening overstatement that the supply tradeoff is a mirage, forcing Side B to concede that where non-pipeline options fail, remote oil wells must wait, which carries real revenue and financing costs. However, Side B decisively dismantled both halves of Side A's counter-case. First, Side B refuted the claim that emergency exemptions and verification protocols surrender strictness, showing that non-routine safety blowdowns and cure periods are ordinary due process ending in mandatory compliance, unlike pricing that buys permanent permission to vent. Second, Side B's pressure questions and closing exposed fatal flaws in Side A's targeted-ranking and fee alternative: relative rankings leave a permanently permitted tier of waste, while fee-funded inspectors create perverse fiscal dependence on ongoing plumes unless fees exceed abatement costs and trigger the exact shut-ins Side A opposed. Finally, Side B bounded short-term supply losses via phase-ins, reinjection, and on-site power while persuasively weighing temporary oil deferral against irreversible near-term methane warming.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response in [B4.4]-[B4.5] to Side A's strictness dilemma ([A2.5], [A3.2])—explaining via the speed-limit and ambulance analogy that statutory emergency exemptions and cure periods end in illegality whereas Side A's fees buy permanent permission to keep venting—cleanly neutralized Side A's central critique and went unanswered in Side A's final speeches, reinforced by [B5.1] turning Side A's fee-funded inspector argument ([A4.3]) into a perverse incentive to keep waste flowing.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): A won a close, high-quality debate by answering B’s strongest objections without conceding the policy away: verification, cure periods, and emergency exceptions need not make routine emissions negotiable, while shut-in supply is potentially deferrable in a way emitted methane is not. B’s remote-well, revenue, and weak-governance pressures were serious, and its lease-and-fee alternative was substantive. However, its repeated claim that safeguards necessarily dissolve strictness was less convincing, and its targeted approach still left a permitted emissions tier. A’s capture options and bounded-cost claims remained somewhat optimistic, keeping the margin competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal most decisively separated strict limits on routine emissions from emergency exemptions and due process, then turned B’s weak-governance objection against discretionary, negotiable fees.
