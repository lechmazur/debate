### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) narrowly wins. Both sides were disciplined and well grounded. PRO's strongest route was the distinction between preserving a record and manufacturing a new first-person "I." The line "who authorized that I?" is memorable, and PRO answered the drafting question cleanly. CON, however, made the debate easier to decide against the motion. It did three things well: - **Concrete test case.** The widow case is fully specified: lawfully held data, unanimous relatives, labeling, source limits, and shutdown rights. It isolates exactly what the prohibition adds, and PRO only answered it by asserting that consent is the only permission that matters. - **Executor analogy.** CON pressed that executors already license likenesses and approve performances in which actors speak new first-person lines. In closing, PRO conceded that executors can do these things. That concession weakens PRO's bright line, since a labeled chatbot is as plainly a simulation as an actor. - **Silence and foreseeability.** CON's point that silence usually reflects inertia rather than refusal went largely unrebutted. So did its point that consent is either too vague to be informed or too specific to survive model updates. PRO's reply that "impossibility is not an entitlement" was crisp, but it accepted CON's charge that the rule forecloses remembrance for nearly everyone already dead.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A3.2 into A4.4 against B4.3. PRO said publishing controls a finite record while a chatbot ventriloquizes the dead. CON countered that editors shape a voice and actors supply new first-person lines, so the moral work is done by fidelity, disclosure, and source limits rather than by whether software recombines words. PRO's closing then conceded that executors may license likenesses and approve performances, which narrowed its distinction to the medium alone.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A won because it gave the cleaner reason to require pre-death opt-in: a mimic chatbot manufactures new first-person speech attributed to the dead, and later family or executor authorization cannot supply the dead person's consent to speak. Side B's stewardship safeguards and widow example raised a strong overbreadth concern, but A answered them by distinguishing publishing finite records from creating an interactive "I" and by showing silence is missing authorization, not an invented refusal. A also handled the pressure on drafting consent and unforeseeable uses more directly with a workable bounded-consent account.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's response to the executor/publication analogy: diaries and recordings disclose actual words, while a chatbot creates new first-person answers on demand; labeling and family approval may reduce deception but do not authorize the dead to speak.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): A narrowly wins by testing the blanket prohibition against a labeled, source-limited memorial authorized by family stewards. B persuasively explains why labeling does not itself supply consent, but insufficiently justifies why stewardship can authorize invented first-person performances yet never a bounded chatbot. A therefore leaves a substantial overbreadth objection standing. B's clear answers on consent and retrospective application, alongside A's exaggerated claim that consent would expire with every update, keep the margin small.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's comparison between actors supplying invented first-person lines and chatbots constrained to sourced recollections most directly weakened B's preservation-versus-mimicry distinction. B's description of a chatbot as queryable first-person agency sharpened the difference, but did not fully establish why it requires categorically nondelegable permission.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a close debate because its decisive frame held up through the whole exchange. Its core question was whose identity is being performed. Keeping and quoting someone's words is one thing; generating new first-person speech in their name is another. PRO kept that distinction consistent from the opening through the closing. CON pressed hard and often well. It exposed what it called PRO's asymmetry: imperfect foresight is excused for a pre-death click but treated as disqualifying for executors. It also forced PRO into a candid concession that people who died before opt-in settings existed have "no lawful route." CON offered a concrete regime of accountable proxy consent as the alternative. However, CON's defense of its ideal case changed under pressure. The bot became "labeled, archive-grounded," saying "the archive doesn't say" beyond the record. That drifts toward the search-and-quote tool PRO explicitly allows under its rule, which shrinks CON's claimed harm. It also partly concedes PRO's line between archive and mimic. PRO had an answer to the asymmetry charge. Self-consent and proxy consent differ in kind when the act is impersonation, not merely in how much the consenter can foresee. CON contested this rather than defeating it. PRO's concession on pre-existing dead was owned as the intended consequence rather than dodged. CON delivered sharper rebuttals. PRO delivered the cleaner, more memorable decision rule. On balance, PRO's route to victory remained more intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's A4.7 against PRO's B4.1–B4.2. CON charged that PRO accepts imperfect foresight for a pre-death click but rejects it for executors. PRO's answer was that an executor authorizes someone else's unknowable first-person statements, which is a categorical difference rather than a matter of degree. That answer, together with PRO's closing point that "a label can warn the listener; it cannot authorize the speaker," largely contained CON's strongest attack.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side A wins because it maintained the decisive line that generative mimicry is not merely using a deceased person's records but manufacturing new first-person speech, and that only the person being performed can authorize that risk. Side B offered serious practical objections, especially the near-impossibility of pre-death opt-in for people who died before the technology existed, but A answered them by distinguishing legal archival access from banned generative impersonation. B's estate-control alternative remained proxy consent by the living, and B could not fully resolve A's pressure about revocation after a model has already learned and spoken from intimate data.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was A's rebuttal that executor or widow consent is proxy authorization of someone else's open-ended voice, while opt-in is the person accepting their own performative risk. That sharply weakened B's central alternative of accountable estate permission.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): B wins by more convincingly distinguishing ownership of shared memories from authority to generate speech in another person's identity. A's private, archive-grounded bot presented a serious overbreadth challenge, but B explained why labeling, family unanimity, and revocation address management rather than the missing personal permission. Both sides answered the pressure questions directly. A weakened its comparison by repeatedly portraying opt-in as an unaccountable old checkbox, even after B specified authorization of the core use; the motion also does not make opt-in a substitute for continuing safeguards.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest response was that uncertainty about future outputs does not make self-authorization equivalent to proxy authorization: someone can accept an uncertain performance of their own identity without giving relatives authority to make that choice for them. A's subsequent repetition of the foresight objection did not squarely defeat this distinction.
