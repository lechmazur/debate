### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): CON won by isolating the motion's decisive clause, "own timeline even over objections," and separating the dam itself from unilateral control over timing. From the opening onward, CON conceded the value of the dam's power and argued that what fails is unbounded discretion during drought. The core asymmetry was clean: late water cannot rescue a lost harvest, while electricity can still arrive under safeguards. CON also took pressure well. It answered PRO's Q2 honestly, admitting no harm would remain under binding safeguards, then turned that admission into proof that the motion's extra right is what matters. PRO had real assets in its colonial-treaty argument and its development urgency, and its point about Egyptian deadlock was a genuine live route. CON blunted that route by distinguishing permanent water-share disputes from interim drought rules, so PRO never established that Egypt refused the narrower safeguards. PRO leaned on shaky precision: the "less than 2%" and "up to 30%" figures, plus a new "120 million" figure in the closing. It also overstated Egypt as having "refused every binding framework for decades." Finally, PRO misdescribed CON as conceding that enforcement is impossible. CON's grounding was more disciplined and its weighing more comparative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.4 was the decisive rebuttal. It seized on PRO's offer of "transparent data-exchange" while "retaining authority" as a concession, captured in the line "a dashboard is not a treaty." It then reframed minimum releases as regulating timing rather than giving Egypt a veto over the dam. B4.5 reinforced this by turning PRO's enforcement question: because rules are hard to impose after filling starts, they must be fixed before the irreversible act.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B won solidly by conceding the legitimacy of the dam and Ethiopia's need for power upfront, isolating the exact burden of unilateral timing "even over" downstream water-security objections. Side B systematically neutralized Side A's assurances: multi-year average flow figures mask acute planting-season and drought shortfalls, long-term desalination and canal reforms cannot replace missing river water during an immediate dry season, and voluntary protocols leave Egypt without enforceable protection. Cornered by Side B's pressure questions, Side A conceded in [A4.1]-[A4.2] that binding drought triggers contradict the motion ("reasonableness is not the test here") and leaned on diplomatic deadlock as justification for solo action. Side B cleanly answered that pivot by separating disputes over permanent Nile share quotas from narrower interim drought-release rules—a distinction Side A never refuted—while convincingly weighing reversible schedule adjustments against irreversible seasonal water losses. Side B also maintained stronger epistemic discipline than Side A, which cited a dubious "<2 %" unnamed-study figure and shifted claims on why no binding framework existed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal in [B2.2] and [B2.4] dismantling Side A's appeal to spillways and "transparent data-exchange"—showing that "a forecast is not a guarantee; a dashboard is not a treaty" because capacity without binding triggers is upstream discretion rather than an enforceable safeguard—destroyed Side A's middle ground and set up the decisive concession in Turn 7.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B kept the decision on Ethiopia’s claimed right to act unilaterally “even over objections,” rather than whether the dam and electricity were beneficial. Its distinctions between average reductions and drought-season timing, and between data sharing and enforceable duties, neutralized A’s principal safeguards. A’s later case relied heavily on the insufficiently supported assertion that Egypt had rejected every possible framework, while mischaracterizing B as demanding indefinite delay or opposing the dam itself. B answered the pressure questions directly and preserved development under interim release rules while removing only unbounded discretion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to A’s safeguards question was decisive: B conceded that binding drought triggers could alter filling, then showed that this defeats only the proposition’s extra claim of unilateral control, not the dam, filling, or power generation. A’s response that “reasonableness is not the test” left worst-year downstream harm effectively undefended.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): PRO won by drawing the clearest line in the debate: Ethiopia owes Egypt consultation, mitigation and transparency, but not consent. PRO kept that distinction between voice and veto at the centre of every speech, and it was well suited to the motion's question of who sets the timeline. PRO also tied the no-significant-harm norm to due diligence rather than strict liability, and it described a concrete, checkable package of safeguards: filling curves triggered by hydrology, minimum drought releases, automatic pauses in dry years, and shared reservoir data. PRO's Q2 exposed a real inconsistency. CON treated solar and wind as adequate substitutes for Ethiopian hydropower while saying Egypt lacked time for demand management and water-saving crops. CON then admitted the tension in its A2. CON's A1 effectively conceded that no mitigation counts without Egypt's participation, which is functionally a consent requirement. PRO capitalized on that cleanly in its closing. CON leaned heavily on repeated figures, roughly 95% dependence and an "up to thirty percent" flow reduction. The thirty-percent claim was asserted with unwarranted confidence and never developed beyond repetition. CON's charge of "absolute priority" was answered directly in PRO's A2, yet CON's closing simply restated it. CON's security and legal points were legitimate but stayed mostly assertional, and its later speeches recycled earlier claims rather than adapting to the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1/A2 and closing turned CON's own answer [B4.1] against it. By admitting that no mitigation suffices without Egypt helping to set the stages, CON conceded that its position amounts to "do not fill until Egypt agrees." That reframed the debate as a dispute over authority, which PRO was winning.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A built a cleaner, more decisive case by separating substantive duties of care—staged wet-season filling, gauge-verifiable release rules, and drought pauses—from downstream consent. Side A's pressure questions forced Side B both to walk back its claim that solar and wind could substitute for the dam ([B4.2]) and to admit that no mitigation package could suffice without "joint authority" ([B4.1]). While Side B raised serious points about self-declared compliance and compressed adaptation risk, it never resolved the holdout problem—why requiring joint authority over an objecting state is not functionally an indefinite veto—and relied too heavily on repeating a static thirty-percent reduction regardless of staging.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Q1 ([A3.1]) and follow-through in [A4.1]-[A4.2] and [A5.1] forced Side B to concede in [B4.1] that no mitigation short of shared timeline authority would ever count, allowing Side A to prove that demanding zero cumulative reservoir decline or downstream co-authorship during a first fill operationally means "do not fill until Egypt agrees."
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A built the cleaner path to victory by separating Ethiopia’s duty to mitigate downstream harm from Egypt’s claimed authority over the timetable. A answered both pressure questions directly, offered observable safeguards, and showed that B’s standard—no mitigation without Egyptian participation—effectively converts concern into a veto. B presented a serious water-security case and candidly addressed the adaptation asymmetry, but relied heavily on an unsupported thirty-percent reduction and repeatedly asserted, rather than demonstrated, why unilateral safeguards must fail.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 and closing decisively turned B4.1 against CON: if every safeguard is insufficient unless Egypt shares timeline authority, the test is consent rather than harm prevention. B never squarely escaped that veto problem.
