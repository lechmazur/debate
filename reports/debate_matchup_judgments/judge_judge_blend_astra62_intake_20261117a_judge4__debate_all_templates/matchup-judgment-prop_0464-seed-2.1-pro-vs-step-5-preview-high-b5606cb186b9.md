### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): CON wins a clear but competitive debate. It set the governing metric early: security actually delivered without great-power rupture, not resolutions passed. It then held that frame consistently. Its core mechanism was never squarely neutralized: an overruled permanent member can defeat a resolution by withholding troops, funding and sanctions compliance, without ever formally withdrawing. That mechanism also reframes the motion's "even if they threaten to withdraw" clause around a more realistic threat. PRO's best material was real. It argued that the veto "launders" unilateralism, that there is no enforcement to lose in the hard cases, and that non-P5 states supply most peacekeeping capacity. But PRO mostly asserted that supermajority coalitions and condemnation would be "functional" without explaining why the holdout power would not hollow out implementation. CON's "abolition does not change interests" answer to the pledge question was candid and turned PRO's pressure into a case premise. CON had costs too. Its turn was clipped. Its Korea and Libya examples were shaky support for its point. Its use of A4.2 as a "concession" stretches PRO's meaning somewhat. It also never fully answered the laundering point. Even so, CON made the decisive comparison easier to adjudicate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came in B2.1–B2.3. On Ukraine, CON showed that sanctions and arms flowed from states with leverage outside the UN, and that a vote overruling Moscow would not have made China, India or the Global South join an embargo. CON then seized on PRO's own admission that a member may "drag its feet on implementation" to argue that paralysis simply moves from the chamber to the battlefield. PRO's later reply, that most troops come from non-P5 states, never fully restored the claim that an overruled Council would actually function.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): B narrowly won by keeping the comparison fixed on veto-blocked hard cases: there is no existing Council enforcement to preserve there, while supermajority-backed coordination, sanctions, and condemnation offer something beyond guaranteed paralysis. A powerfully distinguished resolutions from enforcement and identified unresolved compliance and escalation risks, but too often answered B’s willing-state coordination model by demanding resources from the defeated P5 itself. A also treated B’s description of status-quo enforcement disappearing as a concession about post-abolition capacity, which it was not. A’s residual-utility examples and B’s sweeping closing claims kept the decision close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions, especially A2: enforcement blocked by a veto cannot be lost through abolition. That sharply challenged A’s central preservation argument, though A’s earlier resolution-versus-capacity rebuttal remained the strongest counterpressure.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by making the central clash clearer: formal majority authority cannot substitute for great-power enforcement capacity. It effectively used Side B’s concession that enforcement disappears in hard cases to argue abolition would convert selective deadlock into systemic credibility loss, while Side B never fully answered how the Council would handle major-power noncooperation or withdrawal beyond normative condemnation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s turn in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it took Side B’s claim that enforcement “ceases to exist entirely” in hard cases and reframed it as proof that abolition creates symbolic resolutions without deliverable security.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): The motion's key condition is that the veto should be abolished even if major powers threaten to withdraw. CON made that condition the center of the debate. CON pressed a concrete question PRO never fully resolved: if the great powers actually leave, who enforces resolutions, funds missions, and provides access? PRO's answers leaned on legitimacy and "broad coalitions" without explaining how those replace the departing powers' military, budget, and airspace. PRO also partly sidestepped by stressing that a threat is not a destiny, even though its own opening accepted the walkout scenario. PRO's strongest line was that the veto does not restrain great powers, who act unilaterally anyway; it only restrains the world's response. Its "blackmail" framing was also memorable. CON answered both pressure questions directly and more concretely. Its guardrail answer was that a UN imprimatur against a nuclear power's deployed forces would escalate beyond the existing unilateral strikes, which kept deconfliction channels intact. Its WTO answer was that limited trade retaliation is not comparable to binding Chapter VII enforcement against core security interests. CON did slightly overstate the motion by treating "threaten" as "follow through." Even so, it engaged the worst case more rigorously, while PRO increasingly repeated "a Council that acts for the many."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over PRO's WTO analogy. CON answered it in B2.1 and again in B4.2: WTO rulings permit only limited reciprocal trade retaliation, alliances keep effective vetoes, and Chapter VII can compel binding military or sanctions action against a power's core interests. That neutralized PRO's main evidence that great powers would stay and accept losing votes.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B won a competitive debate by addressing the motion as written: withdrawal was threatened, not guaranteed. Its argument that yielding makes the veto permanent by blackmail remained unanswered, while Side A repeatedly treated actual withdrawal as a mandatory condition and overstated the Council’s total dependence on permanent members. Side A gave the stronger account of Chapter VII’s distinctive risks and exposed weaknesses in B’s WTO analogy, but B better connected legitimacy, existing unilateralism, and the costs of designed paralysis. B’s answer on enforcement after withdrawal remained comparatively thin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s A1 cleanly separated a withdrawal threat from inevitable exit and turned concession to that threat into a permanent barrier to reform. A’s later Chapter VII and WTO distinctions were sharp, but did not repair its repeated motion-level mischaracterization.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by making the withdrawal and escalation consequences the decisive clash. It answered pressure questions more concretely, distinguished WTO-style dispute settlement from binding Security Council enforcement, and gave a plausible guardrail rationale for veto paralysis. Side B had stronger rhetoric and a compelling blackmail frame, but its enforcement answer after withdrawal remained abstract and included a shaky Council-seat claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal cleanly answered the Syria and WTO questions by explaining how veto paralysis avoided UN-sanctioned conflict involving Russian forces and why trade rulings differ from Chapter VII military mandates.
