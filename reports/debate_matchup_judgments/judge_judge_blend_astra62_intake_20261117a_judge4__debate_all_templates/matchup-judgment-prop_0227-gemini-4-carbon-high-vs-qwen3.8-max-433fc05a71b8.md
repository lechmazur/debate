### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO won because it set up a clear causal story: ransom payments fund future attacks, and insured, institutional payers are the main revenue source. PRO then defended that story against each CON attack. Several PRO rebuttals did real damage: - **Leaked data:** CON said public aid "cannot undo leaked records." PRO showed this point cuts against paying too, since a ransom never deletes the stolen copies. - **Pivot to leak or DDoS extortion:** PRO noted the ban would cover those payments as well. - **Reporting:** PRO separated being hacked from paying, then added safe harbors so early reporting carries no liability. - **CON's own policy:** CON endorsed sanctions enforcement, which already bans some payments. PRO used this to show CON half-accepts the principle. CON raised real concerns: hidden payments routed through intermediaries, time-critical recovery where only the key works, and attacks spreading to softer targets. CON was also somewhat more epistemically careful. PRO overreached at times, for example claiming decryptors "routinely corrupt" data and pointing to "seized-decryptor repositories." However, CON's case did not progress much past its opening. Its closing largely recapped earlier points. It also never fully answered PRO's point that ransoms buy no leak assurance. CON's "either way" framing was clever. But it sat uneasily with its own description of what success looks like, and it did not overcome PRO's comparison of starving the attackers' revenue versus subsidizing them. Overall, PRO made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The key exchange was PRO's A4.3 and CON's answer in B4.6. In A4.3, PRO argued CON could not have it both ways. If victims keep paying covertly, syndicates have no reason to move to weaker states. If major payouts truly dry up, small targets cannot replace that revenue. In B4.6, CON replied that partial evasion and market adaptation can happen together. That answer is plausible, but it conceded that institutional payouts would fall. It also relied on unsupported claims that many small extortions could sustain the ecosystem.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side A (PRO) built the cleaner path to victory. Its institutional argument—that ransomware's profitability runs through audited insurers, boards, and compliance machinery that cannot easily launder uninsurable millions—was pressed consistently and sharpened by effective pressure questions exposing the tension between CON's "evasion" and "displacement" stories. A's sharpest move was catching that CON's own alternative ("sanctions enforcement") already criminalizes some payments, a concession B never answered. A also directly answered B's pressure questions with proper A1/A2 format and safe-harbor logic, and repeatedly turned B's "aid can't undo leaks" point back on paying ransoms. Side B fought well: the "book payments as consulting" evasion story and the ransomware-as-a-service "lower the floor" adaptation model were coherent, and the closing's "visible payments are intelligence points" was B's best late framing. But B's core harm story remained speculative and B let the sanctions concession and the contradiction point stand, while its closing was largely recap. A controlled the decisive clashes and made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3: Side A exposed the tension between CON's claim that audited firms would evade the ban covertly and CON's claim that gangs would pivot to smaller, weaker victims—then reinforced it by noting CON's own platform already bans sanctioned payments. B's response (partial evasion plus market adaptation are compatible) was reasonable but left A's institutional-dry-up logic largely intact.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO built the cleaner comparative case: payment finances future attacks, keys still require rebuilding and never guarantee leaked data stays hidden, and funded reporting-linked aid plus insurer/board liability makes large institutional payouts hard to hide. CON's underground and displacement warnings remained plausible but were weakened by its own endorsement of mandatory reporting and sanctions bans, and by PRO's weighing of one victim against many future victims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A's Rebuttal 2: safe-harbor reporting, uninsurable off-books payments versus free legal rescue, and the point that CON's sanctions enforcement already concedes payment bans can coexist with reporting.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): PRO built a coherent incentive story: payment funds the industry, so a ban plus victim aid starves it. Its pressure questions on reporting incentives and moral hazard were pointed, and its A1/A2 answers were honest. PRO bit the bullet on the hardest hospital case and did not abandon non-compliant victims. CON, however, did more comparative damage across the debate. It exposed a tension in PRO's case: PRO calls ransomware an industrialized business, yet also claims gangs often fail to deliver working decryptors. PRO never resolved this tension. CON also forced PRO into an explicit admission that payment stays banned even when both live systems and backups are encrypted across a hospital network. CON then weighed that certain, immediate harm against a deterrent payoff that leaks through non-participating jurisdictions and covert payers. On the moral-hazard dilemma, PRO's answer in A2 (aid regardless of posture, plus penalties and delayed grants) let CON argue that the penalties recreate the desperation that drives covert offshore payment. CON's answers to PRO's questions were plausible enough to blunt them. PRO's closing largely restated the bounty framing and partly conceded leakage abroad, which CON exploited. CON's alternative, using public funds for pre-breach hardening, mandated reporting and crypto seizure, also offered a cleaner path to the same goals. PRO's deterrence point stayed live, but CON gave the clearer reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1–B2.3 did the most damage. It argued that PRO cannot claim both that ransomware is an industrialized business and that gangs routinely cheat payers. It then showed that conditioning aid on security standards either abandons the most desperate victims or subsidizes weak defenses. That dilemma forced PRO's A2 concession, which CON turned into the covert-payment argument in later speeches.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but Side B did more decisive comparative work. Side B's opening constructed a genuine dilemma out of Side A's own victim-support language (generous aid = moral hazard; targeted aid = desperation and covert offshore payment), and its first rebuttal landed a sharp internal-contradiction point: if ransomware is a rational business, attackers must generally deliver working keys, undercutting Side A's "payment often fails" escape hatch. Side A's pressure answers in Rebuttal 2 were honest and principled (no waivers, since waivers magnetize hospital attacks; rescue-with-accountability rather than abandonment), but Side B's Rebuttal 2 turned both answers effectively—showing that lawful reporting aligns with survival and wallet tracing while a ban makes disclosure self-destructive—and Side A never returned to the B2.1 contradiction or the B4.1 tracing argument. Side A's closing frame ("stop paying the predator") was memorable, and its core causal story (payments fund the industry) was never disproven, only shown to leak. But Side B consistently made the tradeoff clearer—certain present harm versus speculative global deterrence—and offered a workable alternative policy. Side B wins on sharper rebuttals, more original dilemma construction, and better handling of pressure questions, with Side A retaining a live but partially blunted incentive argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 [B2.1]-[B2.3]: the observation that Side A's "industrialized business model" premise contradicts its claim that attackers routinely cheat payers, combined with the trap that conditioning aid on security standards abandons exactly the underfunded hospitals most likely to be locked out, was the most damaging exchange of the debate and was never fully answered.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): CON wins the weighing: certain, immediate hospital paralysis and data-leak harm versus PRO's speculative, leaky global deterrence. PRO never convincingly restored encrypted systems, solved double extortion, or escaped the conditioned-aid dilemma, while CON offered reporting, upfront hardening, and crypto disruption without victim bans.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's exposure of PRO's contradiction—that an industrial ransomware business would regularly deliver faulty keys or leak anyway—and its hospital-waiver pressure, which forced PRO to accept forbidding payment even when backups and ICU systems were destroyed.
