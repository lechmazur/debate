### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Both sides were strong, but PRO (B) progressively narrowed the dispute and made it easier to decide. B's key moves: the reductio that CON's endorsed alternatives (donor eggs, embryo testing) also involve third-party mitochondria and conception-routing, shrinking the safety objection to carryover/matching; the honest concession that nothing beats not conceiving, reframing the comparator to what families actually do; and the licensing dilemma — if MRT is safe enough to permit, withholding public funding rations prevention by wealth. CON's "permission is not procurement" answer (A4.1) was coherent and well-defended, and CON's opportunity-cost and reconstruction-vs-donor-egg distinctions were genuinely sharp, but B blunted the opportunity-cost point by showing it applies to every funded service and by observing that prevented cases never draw on the palliative services CON prioritizes. B's closing exploited CON's own licensed-last-resort language to land the equity hammer ("risk tolerable enough to license does not become intolerable when the mother is poor"), which CON's closing answered more abstractly. Execution costs for B: three clipped turns and a stray reference to nonexistent IDs [C1.5, C1.7], which slightly dents grounding, but the visible argumentation carried the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B's Q1/B4.7 licensing dilemma against A's A4.1 "permission is not procurement" defense, culminating in B's closing that CON's own licensed-last-resort concession means the children will be born either way and the only remaining question is who can pay — the strongest comparative moment of the debate.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): A wins by distinguishing permission to offer MRT from sufficient justification to fund it. B's IVF analogy effectively established that genetic relatedness can deserve public support, but did not establish that this particular technique's residual risks and costs were acceptably small. A answered the licensing and donor-egg challenges directly, while challenging B's assumed savings against established alternatives. B's closing sharpened the equity appeal but largely repeated the inference that lawful access entails public entitlement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B successfully challenged A's initial existing-patient distinction by invoking publicly funded embryo testing. The more decisive response, however, was A's point that licensing, reporting and registries need not depend on reimbursement. This undermined B's funding-as-safety mechanism and answered its later permit-or-ban dilemma.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B wins a clear but competitive debate by prevailing on both the comparative baseline and the regulatory-equity clash. Against safe, fundable alternatives like donor eggs, Side B showed that public funding of MRT does not buy disease prevention per se, but rather maternal nuclear genetic relatedness at the cost of underwriting unresolved heritable carryover risk. When Side A smartly pivoted to argue that licensing private MRT while withholding public funds rations access by wealth, Side B decisively separated regulatory harm reduction from public procurement, showing that equal-access claims attach only after an intervention meets the public safety and effectiveness standard. Side A wrote vividly and narrowed the biological dispute well, but conceded MRT cannot beat non-gambling alternatives on safety, deferred cost-effectiveness to ordinary appraisal, cited nonexistent reference IDs once ([C1.5, C1.7]), and was clipped three times for exceeding word limits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's answer in [B4.1] and [B5.1]-[B5.2] to Side A's private-licensing equity wedge: Side B distinguished a regulatory "leash on private choices" from a public "purchase order," explaining that equal access applies after a therapy meets the public standard, not before—otherwise any lawful, risky private add-on the wealthy buy would automatically become a public entitlement.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) built the cleaner path to victory. Its rebuttal strategy was disciplined and adaptive: after forcing Side B to concede that donor-egg IVF "avoids transmission," CON committed to publicly funding donor eggs for every high-risk carrier, which dismantled PRO's equity and "genetic surrender" cost arguments and reduced MRT's unique benefit to relatedness alone. CON then paired this with a sharp risk asymmetry (an adult's hard choice vs. a novel heritable risk imposed on a child who cannot consent) and a substantive trial-versus-service distinction when pressed. PRO's best surviving route — the "penalty" frame and coverage-with-evidence-development — was genuinely strong and memorably argued, and PRO answered pressure questions honestly (the "None" concession was candid but costly). But PRO never fully neutralized CON's point that the public's marginal purchase is relatedness plus novel risk rather than health. Execution cost for A: three turns were clipped for exceeding word limits, which keeps the margin from being wider.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's pressure question Q1 (will CON fund donor-egg IVF for high-risk carriers?) and CON's answer "Yes" in Rebuttal 2, which eliminated PRO's equity route, exposed PRO's subsequent admission that MRT adds "None" in disease prevention over donor eggs, and crystallized the debate into whether relatedness alone justifies public funding of novel heritable risk.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): A wins on the incremental comparison: publicly funded donor-egg IVF prevents transmission, so MRT's additional expense and novel risk require justification beyond avoided disease costs. A answered the affordability pressure directly and sharply contrasted parents' relatedness benefit with the child's additional exposure. B defended relatedness but insufficiently justified that trade. However, B's conditional-funding defense was strong: the motion does not require routine entitlement. A's overdrawn research/service distinction and clipped turns keep the margin competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A showed that avoided lifetime disease costs cannot uniquely justify MRT when donor eggs also avoid those costs. Its subsequent commitment to fund donor-egg IVF answered B's strongest affordability question and removed the claim that this alternative necessarily leaves poorer families paying privately for prevention.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B won by controlling the comparative baseline and winning all three decisive clashes by the end of the debate. By explicitly committing to publicly fund donor-egg IVF for every high-risk carrier, B neutralized A's downstream cost-savings and disease-prevention claims, isolating MRT's marginal benefit to nuclear genetic relatedness. While A made a serious, well-grounded case that forcing poorer carriers to give up genetic parenthood is an unfair penalty, B answered persuasively that MRT does not remove that burden so much as shift novel, unquantified mitochondrial carryover risk onto a non-consenting child. Finally, when A adopted capped cohorts and stopping rules to manage safety concerns, B exposed that capping enrollment turns away eligible patients and effectively concedes MRT belongs in research trials rather than need-based service coverage. Side B's margin is tempered slightly by A's strong equity framing and B's repeated end-of-turn word-limit clippings.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B4.3]-[B4.5], answering [A4.3], Side B dismantled Side A's attempt to defend public funding as "coverage with evidence development" with "capped cohorts and stopping rules." Side B showed that a capped cohort turns away eligible carriers once the cap is reached, directly contradicting A's promise of need-based service entitlement and proving A was describing a research trial under a service label.
