### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a competitive debate. CON conceded the problem is serious and fought only the remedy, so the debate turned on whether parity adds anything beyond direct conduct bans. CON raised real concerns: entry and exit are different transactions, the standard can be gamed, and legitimate verification is needed. PRO absorbed most of these early by defining parity as same channel, comparable steps, and proportionate authentication. It then framed parity as a complement to direct bans, not a replacement. That framing defused CON's "parity is redundant for fake buttons" point. PRO's obstacle-course example also gave a concrete case that sits between CON's named prohibitions. CON's reply was that a direct ban reaches any screen without a legitimate function. That reply quietly relies on the same functional judgment PRO argued parity helps anchor. CON's gaming objection, that firms could make sign-up cumbersome, was answered: firms lose sign-ups, and unnecessary barriers stay independently prohibited. CON's claim that parity "codifies acquisition tactics" never explained why easy exit harms consumers. CON's closing also partly misread PRO's example as being about billing failure. CON was disciplined and coherent, but PRO gave the cleaner reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B4.3–B4.6 against A4.5. PRO conceded that parity need not uniquely cure subscription fatigue and recast it as a ceiling on manufactured friction. Its example was a visible, non-negotiation cancellation flow filled with deterrent screens, which escapes CON's named bans. CON answered that a direct ban would reach such screens. That answer leaned on a case-by-case functional judgment without a benchmark, which was PRO's point, and so left PRO's comparator advantage standing.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): B wins by defending practical parity, not identical clicks, and explaining how enrollment supplies an additional benchmark against evolving cancellation barriers. A’s strongest objection—that functional prohibitions could already catch unnecessary screens—keeps the debate competitive. But B explains why the comparison complements those prohibitions, while substantially answering the security and gaming objections. A repeatedly returns to transaction differences already accommodated by proportionate exceptions and never convincingly establishes that easier cancellation rewards manipulative enrollment. B provides the clearer connection between cumulative consumer harm and the proposed remedy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B distinguishes cancellation’s contractual consequences from obstacles to requesting it. A subsequently concedes that prepaid value, disputes, and shared accounts do not inherently require retention calls or repeated confirmations. That concession weakens A’s transaction-difference objection, while B’s security exceptions preserve legitimate verification.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by showing parity is either gameable through equally cumbersome sign-up or dependent on the same functional test for wrongful friction that direct conduct rules already provide. Side B establishes real cumulative harm and a useful comparator, but never proves parity is necessary beyond conspicuous renewal, visible cancellation, no mandatory negotiation, verification limits, and prompt cessation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it turned Side B's answers on verification and retention calls into concessions that purpose, necessity, and repetition can identify obstruction without importing sign-up design as the legal benchmark.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON wins because the motion's second clause, mandating that cancellation be "as easy as sign-up," became the real point of clash. CON conceded that deceptive renewals and post-cancellation charges are genuine harms. It then argued that the distinctive parity benchmark is either redundant with direct exit duties or actively harmful. The harms CON identified were weaker safeguards for insurance and shared work data, slower sign-up as an evasion, and disputes over which enrollment route counts as the benchmark. PRO's clarifications were easy for CON to turn. Outcome-based tests, proportional safeguards, and "mixed enrollment against the easiest route" each moved PRO toward CON's direct-duty standard. CON repeatedly made this point land: "if parity becomes perverse, abandon parity." PRO's best route was that parity supplies an anchor that makes "accessible" administrable. It pressed this in Rebuttal 2 and the closing. CON's answer was squarely aimed and persuasive: PRO's own closing still relied on judgments like "genuine risk" and "materially harder," so the benchmark adds a dispute rather than removing one. PRO also paid an execution cost when its first rebuttal was clipped. Its reply to the slow-sign-up incentive was thin. CON's answers to the A1/A2 pressure questions were direct and narrowed the debate cleanly. That progression made CON the easier side to vote for.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A2.1–A2.2 against B2.1–B2.2. CON showed that PRO's own clarifications abandon symmetry for outcome duties. CON then reinforced this in A4.4 by noting that "mixed enrollment against the easiest route" is not symmetry at all but a new accessibility rule. PRO's later anchor-for-administrability response never fully recovered the distinct work parity was supposed to do.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): A wins the policy-design clash. B establishes meaningful consumer harm, but A more convincingly distinguishes effective cancellation duties from benchmarking cancellation against enrollment. B’s examples already breach A’s accessibility requirements, while its parity rule retains proportionality judgments and adds disputes over comparable enrollment routes. A answers the pressure directly and sharpens this distinction through closing; B largely repeats its administrability claim. A’s broader compliance-cost and safety predictions remain speculative, keeping the win competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s response to B4.1 is decisive: readily accessible cancellation cannot reasonably include hidden links, mandatory retention sequences, or a multi-call runaround. This undermines B’s principal examples of protection that parity supposedly adds beyond direct cancellation duties.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A convincingly showed parity is either redundant—because direct duties against hidden routes, obstruction, and continued billing already cover the harms—or harmful, adding benchmark disputes and pressure against proportionate safeguards. Side B's administrability defense never escaped still needing judgments about genuine risk and materially harder exit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: B's mixed-channel 'easiest route' standard abandoned actual symmetry while rebranding A's direct-accessibility rule, leaving parity with no distinct protective work.
