### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): PRO won because its case stayed clear and targeted throughout. It argued that stronger nuisance rules are not rental bans, that they penalize only disruptive operators, and that only consequences that threaten profit change the incentives of absentee investors. CON's case weakened under pressure in several ways: - **Ungrounded numbers:** it relied on brittle, unsourced precision, such as "a quarter of the municipal budget," a "60% drop" in complaints, "fifteen to twenty percent" listing declines in Portland and Barcelona, and "30-40%" of bookings. - **Self-contradiction:** it first touted decibel alerts, then conceded that compliance evaporated once fines were reduced. - **Inverted economics:** it claimed that returning units to long-term leases or building denser housing tightens supply, which PRO cleanly refuted. - **Misaimed questioning:** its Q1 wrongly attributed "modest penalties" to PRO. - **Unacknowledged concession:** its final "graduated framework" of registration, suspensions, and mandatory insurance quietly concedes stronger enforcement while calling it something else. - **Execution costs:** three of its turns were clipped, and it misdescribed the listing-decline figures as "PRO's own data." PRO had minor flaws. It cited nonexistent reference IDs [P1.2, P2.2], and its claim that the Barcelona and Portland declines came from licensing restrictions was also unsupported. Even so, PRO answered both pressure questions directly and closed by explaining why warnings plus modest tickets simply become a business cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 and A1 exchange was decisive. PRO seized on CON's admission in [B2.4] that soft tools fail once fines drop, and used it to show that CON's own evidence proves stiff penalties are indispensable. CON had no clean escape. Its eventual tiered alternative effectively accepted stronger rules, and PRO's closing then exposed that a warning and a modest ticket just buy more weekends of disruption.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B won the core clash. B successfully distinguished targeted nuisance enforcement from blanket rental bans and turned Side A’s own concession into the decisive issue: A touted decibel/soft enforcement, then admitted compliance evaporates when fines drop. B showed strong owner liability and escalating fines are therefore necessary, while A’s fallback graduated warnings and modest penalties would simply be a cost of doing business for party houses. B also neutralized A’s tourism/housing harms by explaining that peaceful listings, compliant rentals, and hotels can absorb demand, and that returning units to long-term housing expands supply. Side A relied on unsupported exact figures and named-city effects, repeatedly clipped its speeches, and its pressure question mischaracterized B’s position. The economic harms case was plausible but overbroad, and B left the cleaner route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s pressure question and Rebuttal 2 exposed that Side A had conceded soft monitoring fails without stiff fines; B then turned this into proof that escalating fines and permit revocation are the only working mechanism, and later showed A’s modest penalties would merely buy multiple weekends of party-house chaos.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B (CON) wins clearly. A's case leaned on brittle fake precision: Barcelona's 40% complaint rise and 28% post-2021 drop, a 15% New Orleans voter-turnout decline, a 20% San Francisco rent rise, and a New York 2022 "30-day cap." None of these is verifiable in a closed-book debate, and they read as fabricated authority. A also lost material when both of its rebuttals were clipped. B built a coherent alternative: enforce existing disorder laws against actual offenders. B also pressed three distinctions A never neutralized. First, rogue party houses differ from ordinary hosts. Second, mega-destinations differ from regional and seasonal towns. Third, occupied homes cannot become long-term rentals. B then exposed tensions in A's mechanism. Police verification conflicts with the claim that police arrive too late, and A shifted to giving complainants their own decibel meters. B also showed that a 30-day annual cap cannot yield "dozens" of stays, and that A's "event permits" concede that local tourism needs flexible hosting. A's closing mostly restated the same claims without answering these points. B's closing tightly crystallized them.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answered A's attempt to rescue the 30-day cap. B showed by simple arithmetic that the cap allows only about ten three-night weekends, not "dozens" of stays. B then argued that A's "event permits" concede local tourism needs flexible hosting and suspend the rules on exactly the peak weekends when party risk is highest. In the same speech, B showed that A had retreated from police-verified readings to complainant-held meters, which reopens the abuse risk A claimed to have solved.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): Side A won by dismantling Side B's core enforcement story. B claimed police-recorded verification would prevent abuse, then retreated to city sound monitors and complainant-held decibel meters under pressure; A showed this either makes existing disorder laws sufficient or invites false fines against innocent hosts. A also exposed the 30-day cap arithmetic and the event-permit concession as self-defeating for seasonal and college-town tourism. B's case relied heavily on unsupported exact statistics and was clipped twice, compounding the credibility gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was A's pressure on B's noise-verification contradiction: if police can arrive to measure noise, existing laws already suffice; if they cannot, B's tip-line verification collapses into either ineffective enforcement or neighbor-operated decibel abuse.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won by exposing A's enforcement contradiction and showing caps harm ordinary hosts and seasonal surge capacity without creating long-term housing. A relied on unsupported precise statistics and shifting verification schemes, while B consistently targeted actual offenders under existing laws.
