### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): This was a close, well-matched debate. PRO built a coherent ownership and market-failure case and defended "fair terms" as non-discriminatory parity. It also gave a reasonable answer that "tools" can cover a usable calibration pathway without banning pairing outright. CON, however, did more to shape the decisive clash. It argued from the opening that price, not permission, is the binding constraint for India's repair economy. It then used the fair-terms dilemma and pressure questions to extract PRO's admission that parity does not lower the authorised price. That let CON frame PRO's real gain as the gap between two prices, accrued in a market where labour competition is already intense, while compliance costs fall across every budget SKU. CON's calibration "contradiction" was overstated, and its claim that existing remedies already suffice was thin. Its own concession that the gain is "real" also weakened it somewhat, and PRO fairly called CON's costs speculative. Even so, CON's rebuttals forced PRO onto narrower ground, and its weighing was more memorable and better targeted at PRO's chosen moral centre, the budget buyer. The edge is narrow but real.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The pressure-question exchange on "fair, reasonable terms" decided the debate. CON's Q1 forced PRO to define fair terms as parity with authorised repairers, and PRO's A1 conceded that parity "does not guarantee a cheap screen." CON's Rebuttal 2 then turned that concession into its weighing frame: the law's benefit is only the price gap, and it is thinnest where PRO placed its moral centre. PRO's answer about component-level repair helped, but it did not fully neutralise this frame.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): A established the stronger comparison: enforceable access removes manufacturer-controlled barriers without needing to make every repair affordable. B raised legitimate concerns about discontinued parts, enforcement and compliance costs, but repeatedly reduced access benefits to price differences and treated expensive screens as representative of repair generally. A directly answered the calibration challenge: providing usable tools need not prohibit pairing. B’s insistence on unlimited inventory obligations was less justified than A’s ordinary implementation safeguards. Discontinuation remained underdeveloped by A, but B did not show that its costs outweighed the broader access benefits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A broke B’s affordability dilemma by distinguishing non-discriminatory access from administered pricing. Its contrast between expensive screens and potentially economical battery, port or board repairs exposed why persistent high prices do not make an access right worthless.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side A wins a high-level, fiercely contested debate through stronger argumentative durability and more complete rebuttal coverage. Side B reframed the topic memorably around India's improvisational repair lanes and budget-phone part prices, pressing hard on parity pricing and software pairing. However, Side A cleanly escaped Side B's price-control dilemma without overpromising: defining fair terms as non-discriminatory parity still ends unilateral manufacturer refusals and unlocks existing technician competition across batteries, ports, laptops, rural repairs, and schematic-enabled component-level work—not just expensive budget display assemblies. Side A also grounded calibration utilities naturally in the motion's "tools and documentation" language. Meanwhile, Side B backpedaled on whether status quo channels already provide access and contradicted itself on discontinued models, claiming in Rebuttal 2 both that firms can self-excuse unavailability and that the law forces costly perpetual stocking across every discontinued SKU.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in [A4.1] and [A4.4]-[A4.5] (crystallized in [A5.2]) to Side B's parity-pricing trap ([B2.3], [B3.1]): conceding that parity does not guarantee cheap budget screens while showing that non-discriminatory access to parts, schematics, and diagnostic tools still enables component-level board work, battery/port/laptop repairs, and fierce local labour competition that manufacturer locks otherwise block.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined and responsive, but PRO built the cleaner path to victory. It turned CON's own alternative into a concession: CON accepts that deceptive warranties, abusive tying, and selective battery refusals should be stopped. That narrowed the dispute to the default rule. PRO then argued persuasively that CON's targeted scrutiny requires the same regulatory determinations, only made product by product and initiated by owners or shops. That places the cost of delay on the weaker party. PRO's "every hazard describes the unregulated present" asymmetry was memorable and largely unanswered on substance. CON's reply that informal shops stay outside a certified system has some force, but it does not show the status quo handles scavenged-cell repair any better. CON had real strengths. It pressed breadth across device types, solvency and liability gaps, and the tension between tiered certification and universal access. It also gave a crisp closing line that "the default is the motion." However, CON's concrete answer to Q1 conceded PRO's core example. Its alternative never explained how it reaches the grey market it criticizes. PRO's answer on the tool-versus-credential distinction was somewhat hand-wavy, and its liability answer leaned on existing consumer-law machinery. Even so, PRO's comparative weighing was clearer and more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The key sequence ran from PRO's Q1 to its closing. PRO's Q1 asked CON to name one pairing restriction it would strike down. CON answered concretely that it would prohibit refusing a genuine battery solely because an independent shop installed it. PRO's closing then reframed that answer as conceding the entitlement, leaving only the default in dispute. PRO argued the default favors a statute, because CON's model-by-model process still needs every determination PRO's regulator would make, just slower and owner-initiated. CON's counter that "the default is the motion" was sharp but did not neutralize the delay and burden-allocation point.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): B wins narrowly. It answered the concrete-access challenge with regulator-enforced prohibitions and targeted supply orders, rather than defending an unchecked manufacturer veto. A’s default-and-delay comparison remained persuasive, but its closing overstated B’s concession and left recurring inventory and compliance costs insufficiently answered. B also successfully challenged A’s unsupported leap from access to traceable, accountable repair.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal separated supplying repair inputs from ensuring competent, accountable workmanship: certification, registration, and insurance are additional requirements, not automatic consequences of access. Combined with its concrete targeted-access alternative, this weakened A’s claim that only its proposal could improve today’s unsafe repair market.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side A won a sharp, disciplined debate by anchoring the clash in India's existing grey-market repair reality and proving why an ex-ante statutory default beats Side B's reactive alternative. Side A neutralized Side B's safety, fire, and insolvency warnings by showing that informal shops already repair devices daily using scavenged parts and guesswork, so commercial access to genuine parts, manuals, and tiered training reduces existing hazards rather than creating new ones. Side B kept the round competitive by pressing unanswered recurring inventory costs on small assemblers of budget electronics and probing the tension between certifying lithium-battery repairs and empowering informal shops. Ultimately, however, Side A showed that Side B's complaint-and-investigation model places the burden of delay and litigation on small shops and owners with broken devices against manufacturers that profit from replacement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Turn 5 pressure questions [A3.1-A3.2] and Turn 9 follow-through [A5.1-A5.4]: after forcing Side B in [B4.1-B4.2] to endorse striking down battery-pairing refusals and ordering manuals supplied upon complaint and investigation, Side A cleanly demonstrated that Side B needed the same regulatory line-drawing while turning consumer repair into a model-by-model litigation queue.
