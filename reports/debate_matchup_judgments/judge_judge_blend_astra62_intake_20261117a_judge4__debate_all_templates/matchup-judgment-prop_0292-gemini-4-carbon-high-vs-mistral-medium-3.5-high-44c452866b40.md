### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): PRO (A) built a coherent causal story and defended it across every round. Its core claims were that legacy torts fail against labeled "AI parody" deepfakes, that speech doctrine shields such parody, and that a narrow statute built on anatomical definitions closes the gap with little cost to legitimate speech. It also answered CON's overbreadth, abuse, and offshore objections directly with specific mechanisms: only the depicted person can sue, pseudonymity does not shield plaintiffs from the court or defendant, and subpoenas and injunctions work even against hidden uploaders. CON (B) relied heavily on assertion: "existing torts cover it," definitions are "inherently subjective," platforms will over-censor. It also cited examples like non-explicit situations and historical reenactments that PRO showed fall outside an explicit-content statute. CON's first rebuttal was clipped. Its pressure questions were thin. In its later speeches, CON shifted to a mischaracterization that PRO's statute rests on "emotional harm alone," although PRO had explicitly grounded it in an objective anatomical rule. CON also contradicted itself by claiming that emotional-distress torts suffice while arguing that emotional harm cannot justify liability. PRO exploited that tension cleanly in its closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing (A5.1) turned CON's own A1 answer against it. CON said intentional infliction of emotional distress already covers labeled deepfakes, then argued that liability for subjective emotional harm erodes free speech. PRO showed these two claims cannot both hold. That left CON's "no remedy gap" position self-defeating, while PRO's objective-definition statute stood unrefuted.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built the stronger, more coherent path to victory. A's opening established concrete tort gaps (the "AI-generated" label defeating defamation/false light, misappropriation's commercial requirement) and framed the speech-narrowing as a feature, not a bug. A's rebuttals engaged every B reference point directly, answered pressure questions cleanly in A1/A2 format, and landed two decisive blows: (1) B's internal contradiction between claiming IIED covers labeled deepfakes and claiming subjective emotional harm can never justify speech restrictions — a tension B never resolved through closing; and (2) B's citation of revenge porn statutes actually proving legislatures had to act because common law failed. A also effectively neutralized B's overbreadth story with bright-line anatomical definitions and the observation that no legitimate satire or education requires non-consensual explicit imagery of an identifiable person. B's case was competent but repetitive, leaned on conclusory assertions ("remedy gap is a fiction"), suffered a clipped first rebuttal, and closed with a recap that restated the First Amendment principle rather than answering A's parody loophole clash. B's own pressure Q1 awkwardly conceded the conditional premise.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3-A4.4 and A5.1: A exposed B's fatal inconsistency — B argued IIED already remedies labeled "AI parody" deepfakes while simultaneously arguing that First Amendment principles bar speech liability premised on subjective emotional harm, since courts reject vague "decency's bounds" standards for parodic speech. B's rebuttal 2 and closing walked into this trap and never escaped it.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO showed labeled synthetic pornography defeats defamation and parody-based speech defenses bar emotional-distress claims, leaving a real remedy gap. Its bright anatomical definition answered overbreadth claims about non-explicit satire, education, and art, while CON repeated those examples, conceded some restrictions were justified, and contradicted itself on whether IIED both works and is barred by speech protections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing exposed CON's contradiction: claiming IIED covers 'AI parody' deepfakes while arguing offensive speech is protected because emotional harm is subjective, showing why an explicit objective carve-out is needed.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): CON (B) wins clearly. PRO established real harm, but its route to victory was the claim that a private right uniquely delivers compensation and deterrence, and it never secured that claim against CON's attacks. CON pressed a contradiction that stayed alive through the whole debate: notice-based safe harbors let compliant platforms escape damages, creators remain anonymous, and so the only reachable defendants are risk-averse intermediaries. Those intermediaries will over-remove content rather than litigate. PRO's answers made the problem worse. Naming CDNs, payment processors and ad networks as defendants let CON argue that site-wide deplatforming would follow. Proposing "digital signatures" and "creator attestations" let CON argue that unattested authentic leaks and satire would become presumptively removable. PRO kept repeating "narrow," "surgical," and "no legitimate value" without engaging the mechanism CON described. PRO's grounding was also weaker: a vague South Korea reference and a "well-documented" claim with nothing behind it. CON answered both of PRO's pressure questions directly, offering crime-victim funds and restitution for compensation and hash-based staydown for re-uploads. It then turned PRO's own concessions, including the need for criminal referrals, into its closing weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B2.1–B2.2, followed by B4.3–B4.6. CON showed that PRO's notice-based safe harbor removes the solvent defendants that PRO's compensation story depends on. When PRO then pivoted to suing CDNs and payment processors, CON turned that pivot into evidence of site-wide over-censorship. This answered PRO's core compensation claim and also strengthened CON's speech-harm case.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B won by building and sustaining a single decisive contradiction: PRO's plan needs notice-based safe harbors to avoid censorship, but those same safe harbors leave no solvent defendant to fund the promised compensation, while stripping them forces intermediaries into blunt over-censorship. Side A's attempts to escape (class actions, fee-shifting, then CDNs/payment processors) each got turned — most damagingly when B showed that suing site-wide infrastructure produces deplatforming, not abuser accountability. B answered both pressure questions directly (victim funds, restitution, hash-matched staydowns), while A's answers (A4) opened new vulnerabilities B exploited (attestation requirements making unmarked authentic speech presumptively unlawful). A's best live route — certain victim harm versus speculative censorship risk — was squarely met by B's closing inversion: for anonymous-abuser victims, compensation is the illusion and censorship the certainty. Both sides used clean background knowledge without fake precision, but B's clash-narrowing and turn-heavy progression made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1–B4.5: B's second rebuttal took A's A4 answers (suing CDNs/processors; watermark/attestation consent verification) and converted them into B's strongest material — infrastructure defendants can't adjudicate single uploads and will sever whole platforms, and attestation requirements let powerful figures get authentic leaks and satire deleted as presumed fakes.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by exposing and sustaining a central contradiction: with notice safe harbors PRO collapses into takedown with no compensation, while without them hosts and infrastructure providers will delete or deplatform rather than litigate. PRO's pivot to CDNs, payment processors, watermarks and attestations strengthened that censorship concern and never identified a plausible solvent abuser-defendant, while A answered compensation and re-upload pressure with victim funds, restitution and hash-based staydown.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A's Rebuttal 1 safe-harbor trap, reinforced in Rebuttal 2: compliant platforms pay nothing, anonymous creators stay unreachable, so PRO must target neutral intermediaries who will over-remove rather than risk statutory damages.
