### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A won by consistently converting the clash into concrete comparisons: the child/spousal support analogy grounded the duty in familiar legal machinery, and A repeatedly showed that B's harms (one-size payments, lower-income earners with affluent parents, trust dodges) hit scenarios a means-tested duty explicitly excludes. A answered both pressure questions directly with the required A1/A2 format and used B's own concession that budgets are strained and prioritizing family-less seniors "is a matter of equity" as a pivot in the closing. Side B's autonomy argument was asserted more than developed against the support-law analogy, its administrative-burden case grew repetitive across rebuttals, its A2 answer conceded the equity framing without strong counter-weighing, and its closing was largely recap of already-contested points rather than engagement with A's taxpayer-subsidy framing. B was competent and coherent but made the debate harder to decide, not easier; A made the decisive clash cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 dismantling B's "lower-income earners with modestly affluent parents" scenario [B2.4] by showing it fails twice (no duty arises if parents can self-support, and low earners owe nothing under ability-to-pay rules), then extending the same logic to trusts and cross-border enforcement — this gutted B's distributional and administrative objections in one pass.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A built the clearer path by locking the duty to means-tested, exempted contribution and then using that limit to neutralize Side B’s one-size, anti-poor, and wealth-shielding objections. Existing child- and spousal-support practice supplied a workable analogy, and A turned B’s own concession that budgets are strained and childless seniors deserve priority into a direct reason someone must pay. Side B pressed real autonomy and complexity concerns, but never fully answered why capable adults may externalize an indigent parent’s costs, and its closing largely restated those harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both pressure questions, showed the lower-income/affluent-parent scenario is excluded by the duty’s own limits, and tied private contribution to public dollars freed for seniors with no family.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): A built the cleaner comparative case: means-tested contribution targets capable children, protects indigent parents, and frees scarce public funds for seniors without family support. A directly exposed B’s recurring mischaracterizations of the duty as uniform, applicable to affluent parents, or burdensome to low-income children. B’s administrative-complexity objection remained plausible but was repeatedly asserted rather than weighed against recoveries, while B’s autonomy and universal-care arguments never neutralized A’s subsidy-to-wealthy-shirkers comparison. B’s later speeches largely restated earlier claims and answered pressure less directly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal answered both pressure questions upfront, then dismantled B’s distributional scenario on two independent grounds and gave the simple public-savings comparison. B’s corresponding response reiterated complexity and cost-shifting without squarely answering why a capable child’s contribution would not free public funds.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B (CON) built the stronger path to victory. B's opening reframed the duty as an inherited "birth debt" and raised three durable harms: abuse survivors forced to fund abusers, regressive incidence on children of the poor, and enforcement backfiring on proud seniors. A's rebuttals largely restated calibration ("modest percentage of disposable income") rather than resolving the core dilemmas. B's pressure question on the abuse-verification standard exposed a genuine no-win trap: either uncorroborated affidavits gut revenue or survivors without paper trails pay their abusers—and A's A1 answer (burden on claimant, sworn affidavits) actually deepened the trap, which B capitalized on in Rebuttal 2. B also turned A's own Q2 against A by noting the verification quagmire exists only under a filial mandate, and the "rates adjusted annually" concession was used effectively to show rate hikes on stretched families are inevitable. The "two identically paid nurses" comparison went essentially unanswered through closings, as did seniors refusing care to spare their children. A's fiscal argument was further undercut by B's point that childless seniors and seniors with destitute children yield nothing, so public coverage is needed regardless. A answered A1/A2 in format and was coherent, but relied on assertion and repetition while B narrowed and won the live clashes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3: B seized on A's own safe-harbor mechanism—burden of proof on the claimant plus decades-old evidence requirements—to show it contradicts A's promised cheap, automated child-support-style enforcement, and then flagged the two unanswered harms (seniors refusing care; identical-salary unfairness), which A never squarely addressed.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B wins because it left the decisive clashes standing: a filial levy taxes the accident of birth, cannot be both modest and fiscally meaningful, and cannot combine fast garnishment with a real abuse exemption. Side A’s reciprocity and budget story was coherent at the start, but later answers muddled the burden of proof, conceded annual rate hikes, and never repaired the same-salary, different-parents inequity or the risk that seniors refuse care. B also answered the funding question with progressive social insurance and showed why that system must exist anyway for childless and unsupported seniors.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it used A’s own safe-harbor and adjustable-rate answers to show a no-win between revenue, speed, and protecting survivors of undocumented abuse, while the unanswered birth-lottery comparison collapsed A’s claim of progressive equity.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B built the stronger comparative case: filial contributions fall arbitrarily on equally situated workers, cannot cover seniors without solvent children, and require exactly the costly abuse adjudication that undermines claims of rapid, cheap enforcement. B also supplied a coherent progressive social-insurance alternative. A answered every prompt formally but relied on unsupported assurances about minimal costs, substantial revenue, and workable exemptions, while leaving the birth-lottery injustice and seniors’ avoidance of care largely unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal decisively turned A’s own safeguards against the proposal: either lax affidavit standards gut enforcement, or demanding decades-old documentation traps abuse survivors, while either route defeats automated, low-cost collection. B then exposed the unresolved dilemma between modest deductions yielding little revenue and higher rates squeezing working families.
