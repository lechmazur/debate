Even on purely deleted content, Side A proved that lawful cross-platform fraud archives fill a real gap left by short platform purge windows while protecting citizens through content bans, licensing, and a single-request registry. Side B argued memorably and resolved the preservation-order "theatre" dilemma well, but lost ground via unwritten exemptions, a premature claim in [B4.5] before Side A's answer turn, and a weak closing deflection on fake profiles.

When Side B replied that a fake profile is a "fraud instrument" rather than a citizen's trace [B5.4], Side A [A5.2-A5.4] sealed the exchange: nobody knows a deleted profile was fraudulent without an archive to compare it against, proving that regulated purpose—not deletion status—must do the sorting.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): A wins the scope-and-verification clash. B repeatedly treated “non-public” as “unauthorised,” although authorising disclosure to a lender does not make a record public. This left A’s cross-institution fraud-check objection substantially unanswered. B convincingly distinguished readily purchasable broker dossiers from harder-to-access first-party copies, exposing A’s exaggerated claim that the ban provides no protection. Nevertheless, B never justified its consent exception under the proposition or adequately weighed the legitimate verification activity its prohibition could capture.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s response that an impostor can sign the authorisation form directly undermined B’s claim that verification costs amount only to obtaining a signature. B’s closing assumed the real subject had already authorised the relevant sharing and again focused on posts and location trails, rather than resolving A’s objection about non-public verification records.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side B made a compelling case against open-market broker sales, extracted a candid concession that current protections are insufficient, and sharply distinguished criminal first-party breaches from credit-card broker purchases. However, Side A won clearly by holding Side B to the proposition as written. Side B tried to rescue third-party identity verification by reading "non-public" as "not released" when an applicant authorizes a credit check. Side A dismantled both the textual stretch and the fraud mechanism, showing that in identity-theft and synthetic-identity schemes at a new bank, the impostor signs the form rather than the real citizen, while burner-device fraud relies on deleted account trails. Combined with Side A's pivot to strict buyer licensing and Side B's closing retreat that "rules can adjust" the market, Side A proved targeted regulation beats blanket prohibition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A4.1]-[A4.2], Side A shattered Side B's central defense that legitimate identity checks survive the ban at the mere cost of "a signature on a verification form" ([B4.1], [B4.7]) by pointing out that when an impostor applies in your name, the fraudster signs the form rather than you—leaving your non-public history unauthorized for release under PRO's own test and banning the exact check that catches the thief.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): A presented the more vivid harm case and drew a strong distinction between data available for purchase and data obtainable only through a breach. But A repeatedly narrowed “non-public” into “unauthorised,” an exemption the motion does not contain. B exposed that shift, supplied a plausible fraud-check example where the impostor—not the data subject—provides authorisation, and answered both pressure questions directly, including an honest concession about current safeguards. A never squarely repaired the textual overbreadth problem, leaving B with the stronger case against prohibition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s A1 in Rebuttal 2 was decisive: the fraudster signs the verification form, while the victim’s non-public history remains subject-unauthorised. This turned A’s consent test against itself and exposed why “non-public” could not simply be read as “non-consented.”
