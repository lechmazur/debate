- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) controlled the decisive clash throughout. B also won the failure-mode comparison (gradual single-function switch failure vs. total sudden screen blackout) and the "floor" argument that buttons stay safe even when automakers cut corners. A fought well on unfamiliar-car fumbling, accessibility (arthritis, mittens), and OTA fixes, but those points were largely blunted by B's rental-car red-triangle answer and the point that sensor automation and steering-wheel shortcuts are not screen-dependent. B's execution costs—two clipped turns (opening and rebuttal 1) and misreferenced "[C2.1, C2.3]" IDs—plus A's disciplined word management keep this from being a blowout, but B still made the debate far easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Pressure Questions and Rebuttal 2: B forced A to defend a screen propped up by "raised, tactile bezels" and "small hardwired backups," then crystallized in the closing that admitting emergencies need hard keys concedes the primary control for emergency functions—while A's best counter (slim backups under a screen-primary model) still left B's floor-comparison and "nuisance vs. blackout" asymmetry standing.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A built the cleaner safety case: eyes-free tactile location, failure of one switch versus total screen blackout, and a higher floor when design is poor. Side B's accessibility, consistency, and OTA points were competitive, but its reliance on tactile bezels and hardwired backups let A show the safe touchscreen must borrow physical controls, while B repeatedly attacked sprawling button banks rather than A's narrow handful of core functions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2 concession trap was decisive: haptics only confirm after finding a target, automation and steering shortcuts do not require touchscreens, and B's hardwired worst-case backups concede the principle for hazard and defrost controls that are worst-case by definition.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the decisive clash about worst-case, eyes-off control: hazards, defrost, and urgent climate needs are precisely the moments where a tactile, independent switch has the highest floor. B effectively turned A’s tactile bezel and hardwired-backup concessions into proof that safe screen-led design still borrows physical-button properties, and it compared failure modes cleanly: a bad button usually fails locally, while a bad screen can hide, lag, or black out multiple functions. A made strong points about unfamiliar cars, accessibility, and over-the-air fixes, but never fully answered why a merely backup physical key is enough for functions that are themselves emergency controls. B’s clipped turns and occasional bad reference IDs kept the margin modest, but its route to victory remained live and clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure question and Rebuttal 2 exposing that A’s “safe” touchscreen model needs tactile edges and hardwired backups, then arguing that because hazards and defrost are worst-case functions, those physical controls should be primary rather than merely redundant.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) built the cleaner path to victory. It conceded that protected-controller architecture works with any interface, which neutralized PRO's reliability argument ("PRO is crediting the button for work the wiring does"), reframed distraction as frequency times duration where automation wins, and trapped PRO into an unfalsifiable position when PRO answered the "matched performance" question by insisting no test could ever show screen parity. A's closing crystallized this sharply: a hardware mandate only does work in the case where buttons lose. Side B's tactile-feedback mechanism and vivid emergency scenarios were genuinely strong, and B's pressure Q1 (why can't protected architecture support buttons?) forced a useful concession, but B's answers relied on assertion ("lab tests systematically miss it"), a weak stuck-heater climate example, and a brake-pedal analogy A dismantled (primary control vs. compound-failure backup). A's three clipped turns are a real execution cost, and B's emergency scenario was never fully destroyed—hence a clear but competitive, not solid, margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal: answering B's pressure question with "It can. That is the point," converting B's own reliability argument into architecture-neutral ground, then exposing B4.1's unfalsifiability ("If calm-pavement tests are too easy, test on rough roads under time pressure... a hardware mandate only does extra work in the case where buttons lose")—the decisive exchange of the debate.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B neutralized reliability by showing protected architecture, not plastic, does the work, reduced tactile advantage to a brief-versus-slightly-briefer glance, and used automation to cut interaction frequency. Side A powerfully stressed emergency failures but left its zero-glance claim effectively unfalsifiable and did not answer testing buttons and screens on rough roads under time pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: if buttons truly outperform screens under stressful real-road tests, a performance standard would deliver buttons; a hardware mandate adds value only if buttons lose that test, a case Side A declined to defend.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A won narrowly by turning Side B’s strongest emergency scenarios into an argument for automation, neutralizing the reliability point through protected architecture, and exposing that B’s remaining tactile claim became hard to test or falsify. A’s pressure questions and closing made the decisive clash clearer: if buttons truly outperform under stress, a demanding performance standard would select them; if not, a hardware rule locks in the worse design. A’s repeated clipping was a real execution cost and kept the margin small, but B never fully answered the “cannot spare a glance, cannot spare a hand” rebuttal or the claim that routine climate interactions are better reduced by automation than shortened by buttons.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s response to B’s high-stress backup argument: if the driver cannot afford even a glance, they also cannot afford to reach for a button, so pre-emptive automation and protected architecture matter more than a manual last resort.
