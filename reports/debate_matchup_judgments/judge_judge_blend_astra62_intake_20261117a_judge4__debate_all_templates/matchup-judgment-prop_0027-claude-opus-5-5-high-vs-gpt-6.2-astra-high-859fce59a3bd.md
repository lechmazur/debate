Side B clinched the debate in [B5.1]-[B5.4] by showing no calm interval existed across insurgency, sectarian war, ISIS, and militias seizing Kirkuk, memorably noting that a building surviving a fire its owners set is not safer than one that never burned.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a competitive, high-caliber debate by constructing a compelling 2011 Arab Spring counterfactual and systematically dismantling Side B's defenses against it. Side B proved substantial pre-2011 insurgency costs and cleanly won the sub-clash showing that post-2003 Iraqi insurgent networks enabled ISIS to operate across borders and seize Mosul. However, Side B staked its net comparative ledger on the premise that an undissolved Baathist army would have met 2011 protests with a contained 1991-style crackdown rather than a prolonged territorial war. Side A decisively refuted that premise—pointing to pre-existing armed Kurdish autonomy in the north, Iranian-backed Shia militia ties, Iran-Saudi border geography, and the Hama 1982-to-Syria 2011 parallel—leaving Side A's "two Syrias side by side" weighing standing at the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: When Side B asked [B3.1] why Saddam's loyal officers would not simply crush a 2011 uprising as they did in 1991, Side A [A4.1-A4.2] showed both that 1991's permissive conditions had reversed (an armed Kurdish region, a sanctions-hollowed army, and Iranian militia ties) and that Assad's loyal officers had likewise crushed Hama in 1982, yet that exact minority-clique loyalty prevented defection in 2011 and produced a decade-long civil war—an answer Side B never neutralized and walked back into in closing [B5.1-B5.2, A5.1-A5.4].

B’s pressure questions exposed the gap between loyal repression and a decade of lost territory, and between willing foreign fighters and a cross-border organization. A’s answers were sharp but incomplete, especially the claim that Baathist officers or scratch-built networks would have produced an equivalent Mosul-capable force. B more honestly conceded crackdown costs, then weighed scale and duration without overclaiming the counterfactual.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: even granting a serious 2011 Iraqi uprising and an independent Syrian jihadist influx, A still had to prove a catastrophe larger than both the pre-2011 insurgency and the later spillback, and had shown repression more clearly than that greater scale.
