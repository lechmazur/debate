### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): This was a close debate, and CON wins it narrowly. PRO's strengths: - It had the more motion-friendly framing, since "more often" and "steer toward the most expensive" set a modest burden. - Its inertia-and-defaults mechanism was intuitive. - Its sharpest move was the alleged contradiction between CON's "symmetrical friction" claim and its "amputate order volume" claim. - Its answer to "who decides" was also decent: the platform's algorithm already decides on every order. Why CON edged ahead: - **More granular engagement.** CON built a coherent alternative remedy (itemized, all-in disclosure plus effortless switching) and kept pressing three live routes: revealed preference for door fulfillment, hidden non-dollar costs of pickup, and order volume for delivery-dependent kitchens. - **Direct answers to pressure.** CON met PRO's contradiction head-on. It argued that a default changes the service category before costs are weighed, which can cause abandonment. It then turned the point into a dilemma: if switching is easy, PRO's change does little; if switching is hard, disclosure is the better fix. - **Dropped points on PRO's side.** PRO never meaningfully answered the volume-vs-commission distinction. Its closing largely restated earlier lines instead of resolving that clash. Execution costs on both sides: - Both used nonexistent reference IDs (PRO cited C1.x; CON cited P2.x and C2.x). - CON misattributed the opacity concession as "PRO admits [A1.5]," when A1.5 was CON's own claim. These errors roughly cancel. CON's more adaptive rebuttal work gives it a thin but real edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's "symmetrical friction vs. amputate order volume" contradiction (B4.3) against CON's A4.1 reply. CON explained that a different service-category default causes abandonment before users weigh their options, then reframed the point as a dilemma: either switching is easy and PRO's change is marginal, or it is hard and disclosure is the better fix. That answer largely neutralized PRO's best pressure point.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): A’s modest reading of “more often,” its inertia argument, and its answer that platforms already choose defaults kept the proposition intact. Its pressure questions exposed a real tension between B’s effortless-switching and severe order-volume claims. B offered strong total-cost, disclosure, and restaurant-demand critiques and partly resolved that tension, but too often treated a conditional, reversible default as categorical paternalism without showing why disclosure alone neutralizes default steering. A’s weaker restaurant and congestion weighing keeps the margin competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Q1 and A4.3–A4.4 forced B to reconcile one-tap switching with predicted order-volume collapse. B4.1 gave a credible service-category and abandonment distinction, but it did not fully restore a reason to preserve high-fee default steering.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO won by narrowing the proposal to more frequent, context-sensible lower-fee defaults while preserving one-tap delivery, and by showing that platforms already choose defaults for profit. CON's disclosure alternative and total-cost warnings were strong, but did not overcome the inertia argument or explain why an intentionally chosen premium upgrade harms autonomy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's pressure question and Rebuttal 2 exposed the tension between CON calling switching symmetrical trivial friction while warning lower-fee defaults would amputate order volume; CON's distinction about abandonment still conceded that defaults powerfully shape outcomes.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): This was close. CON (A) had the most memorable weapon: the "asymmetry of error" argument, together with the airline-versus-bus analogy. A delivery default that a walker didn't need still succeeds. A pickup default for a homebound person fails outright. PRO (B) ultimately had the case that fit the motion better. The motion asks for lower-fee or pickup defaults "more often," not always. PRO's second rebuttal tailored the defaults, keeping delivery for repeat, late-night, bad-weather, accessibility and distant orders. That largely defused the exhausted-parent and mobility-impaired scenarios CON kept citing. It also blunted the courier-density harm by keeping volume where delivery is actually valued. CON branded this tailoring a fatal concession, but that misreads the "more often" proposition rather than defeating it. PRO's closing correctly reclaimed it as the core principle, illustrated by the $12 lunch two blocks away. CON also leaned heavily on the same repeated congealing-dinner imagery, adding little each round. PRO's claims also had gaps: - PRO's assertion that platforms "resist" visible pickup was never substantiated. - PRO never used the "lower-fee options" route (cheaper delivery tiers), which would have sidestepped CON's error asymmetry entirely. The edge goes to PRO for keeping a live, motion-consistent route to victory that CON never fully neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came after the pressure questions. PRO's A2 answer [B4.3] proposed context-sensitive defaults, keeping delivery for accessibility, late-night and distant orders. That directly answered CON's homebound-consumer and courier-density pressure. CON's reply [A4.4] tried to turn it into a "massive retreat," but under a "more often" motion the tailoring strengthens PRO rather than conceding the debate.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A made the cleaner case that checkout transparency does not neutralize default-driven anchoring and that delivery can remain readily available without always being preselected. Its contextual exceptions answered pressure without abandoning the motion’s “more often” standard. B’s error-asymmetry and courier-density arguments remained substantial, but B repeatedly framed the proposal as universal inversion and overclaimed that A’s exceptions conceded the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first pressure question exposed the tension between trusting users to notice itemized fees and expecting them to miss a pickup default. B gave a credible mental-models answer, but its later “massive retreat” characterization did not defeat A’s narrower, context-sensitive case.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A wins because the motion only requires cheaper defaults more often, and A convincingly frames current defaults as monetized status-quo bias while answering CON's harms with need-sensitive exceptions and one-tap delivery. B's asymmetry-of-error argument is powerful, but A turns distance, weather, late-night, and accessibility cases into smart-default exceptions rather than a defense of always steering users to the priciest path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 is decisive: genuine delivery need still self-selects delivery, while artificial low-value trips need not be preserved to sustain courier liquidity. B calls this a retreat, but A's closing correctly recasts it as the conditional default the motion actually proposes.
