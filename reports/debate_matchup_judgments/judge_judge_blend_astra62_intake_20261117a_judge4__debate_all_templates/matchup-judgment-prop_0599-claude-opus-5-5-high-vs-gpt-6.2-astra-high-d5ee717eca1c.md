B's core route — certain daily exclusion versus a marginal, design-governable risk — survived the debate, and B progressively narrowed CON's case to a single surviving mechanism (cross-border matching of a portable health identifier), then argued that mechanism requires violating the very rules the framework mandates. B answered both pressure questions directly and well, especially turning CON's anonymous eligibility check into a design rule only a continental framework can make universal, and caught CON's concession that the bank account is "a stronger case for portability." CON's best surviving material was genuinely strong: the "what a lapse leaves behind" asymmetry (local trail vs. cross-border matchable entry) and the limiting principle to stop recognition where its benefit stops. But CON's enforcement answer ("nothing guarantees enforcement across fifty-five states") was successfully mirrored back by B against CON's own preferred AU privacy rules, and CON never fully answered how its alternative achieves scale without the common standards it kept conceding were useful. B did more to resolve the clash and gave the cleaner weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3-B4.4: B answered CON's clinic-disclosure question by adopting anonymous eligibility checks as a framework design rule, then noted that under CON's model the government ordering the handover is the same government designing the clinic system — converting CON's best safeguard into an argument for the framework. CON's counter-asymmetry in A4.4 (a wrongly logged portable number creates a trail another country can pick up) was sharp but B's closing symmetry argument on unenforceable national rules largely blunted it.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by holding the debate to portability's marginal effects. B showed a portable identifier creates cross-border matching that national IDs and SIM registers do not, that a later sanction cannot unmake that match, and that clinics, licenses, and transcripts can be served without making the person recognizable everywhere. A's safeguards were serious, but where they worked they made continental ID unnecessary for the sensitive services, and calling misuse a "violation" never answered the patient who must trust a single lapse.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's A1 in the second rebuttal was decisive: portability adds a match, not a list, so two clinic registers become linkable across a border only once they share an identifier. A's "make logging it unlawful" close did not erase what one noncompliant entry leaves behind.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a close, high-quality exchange. B's strategy of designing a minimal framework — offline signed attestations, no central database, no lookup facility, revocation via downloaded lists — did real argumentative work, blunting A's surveillance case at the design level rather than through unenforceable promises. A's strongest surviving lines were the enforcement gap (B honestly conceded no AU precedent) and the revocation-as-weapon point, but B's closing answer (a state can revoke its own credential but not a host clerk's attestation, so the refugee is not "cut off everywhere") materially neutralized A's sharpest scenario. On the motion's own terms — service gains vs. conceded surveillance risk — B won the weighing: livelihood stakes and recurring fragmentation costs versus a residual, mitigated risk, with the pointed counter that bilateral pacts carry the same sighting danger wherever they actually work. A's bilateral alternative was well-argued but met with coverage and timing gaps, and A's assertion that "the motion assumes the lookup gets built" overstated the concession. Both sides complied with format, answered pressure directly, and avoided fake sourcing; B's case was slightly more original and its design-level answers held up better under A's repeated pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The revocation clash: A4.1 argued the issuer keeps the power to put names on the revocation list, giving abusive governments continent-wide reach, and B5.1 answered that a government can revoke only its own credential — not a host-country clerk's attestation — so the "cut off everywhere at once" scenario requires the home credential to be the only acceptable proof, which the framework's design contradicts.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): B won by showing the service gains do not require a continent-wide default-trust layer, while that layer uniquely extends revocation and recognition to governments the AU cannot discipline. A built a careful offline-attestation model and honestly conceded there is no enforcement precedent, but those answers fed B's comparison: protections still depend on state restraint, removal comes after harm, and pairwise or selective recognition can deliver the pension proof without making abusive issuers trusted by default. A's closing narrowed the affirmative toward opt-out of hostile issuers, which undercut the case for the motion as written. B's irreversible-harm weighing stayed cleaner than A's rent-delay frame once ratification and accreditation delay were put on the same scale.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B granted that a downloaded revocation list does not reveal a presentation, then turned that concession into continent-wide cutoff power and contrasted advance bilateral refusal of a hostile issuer with PRO's post-audit withdrawal.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): B won a close, substantive debate by consistently separating credential verification from issuer surveillance and by showing how locally issued attestations could remain useful even when a home credential was revoked. A’s enforcement critique and irreversible-harm framing were forceful, but its strongest scenarios often depended on lookup functionality, exclusive reliance on home credentials, or compulsory trust that B’s framework did not require. B also answered pressure more directly and gave the clearer comparison with bilateral agreements.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The revocation exchange was decisive: B’s bulk-list question forced A to concede that revocation checks need not reveal presentations; A pivoted to continent-wide exclusion through revocation, but B answered that host-clerk attestations and selective issuer recognition prevent the home state from automatically cutting someone off everywhere.
