### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins because it kept a consistent line throughout the debate. It distinguished three things: emergency alerts, scheduled educational reports, and continuous behavioral feeds. It then argued that only the third is the target of the motion. CON (A) began with real routes to victory: - safety in emergencies, - equity for parents who cannot take midday calls or attend conferences, - early detection of slow-burn harms like bullying and withdrawal. But CON's answers in Rebuttal 2 undercut its own case. In A4.1–A4.3 it proposed that conduct data "stays with designated staff" unless a published threshold is crossed. It also said that equal access does not require "raw behavioral exhaust." That is essentially the bounded model the motion endorses. PRO's closing pointed this out directly. CON's closing then tried to reassert real-time parental access to conduct data, which contradicted its own Rebuttal 2. CON's slow-burn harm argument stayed partly live. However, PRO answered it with human-review escalation and a concealment feedback loop, and CON never showed why a parent-facing live feed specifically was needed. The equity exchange was roughly a wash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing (B5.1, B5.4) turned CON's own A1/A2 answers against it. CON had described emergency alerts, scheduled updates, and staff-held conduct data with threshold-triggered outreach. PRO showed that this matches the limited model the motion asks for. CON's closing reversal then looked like an inconsistency rather than a successful answer.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) consistently controlled the framing: the motion limits continuous behavioral surveillance, not communication, and every CON safety example (emergencies, missed classes, slow-burn patterns) was answered with a bounded alternative—urgent alerts, scheduled reports, human review of patterns. Side A's second rebuttal drifted into defending exactly that bounded model (conduct data staying with staff until a published threshold triggers "contextualized outreach," plus "equal access does not require equal exposure to raw behavioral exhaust"), which B's closing caught as a concession of the motion as written. A's closing tried to reassert the real-time feed ("triage governs how the dashboard responds, not whether parents see conduct data"), but this sits uneasily with A's own A4 model where parents do not see conduct data in real time—an unresolved internal tension. A's strongest live routes (slow-burn pattern detection and equity of access) were seriously narrowed by A4.3's own admission that raw feeds standardize micromanagement for the least-resourced. Both sides followed format (proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 structure), avoided fake precision, and argued the motion as written, but B made the debate easier to decide and forced the concessions that mattered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B5.1: B's closing identifying that A4.1–A4.3 had conceded the motion—"CON's final case has conceded the motion... To justify a real-time behavior dashboard, CON must show why live behavioral visibility is necessary, not merely why alerts and trend review are valuable"—which A's A5.1 response ("triage categories govern how the dashboard responds") never fully neutralized, since A's own model withholds real-time conduct data from parents.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): B convincingly distinguishes urgent alerts and pattern detection from continuous parental surveillance, preserving A's strongest safety and access benefits without the contested feed. Its case also repeatedly treats limits as a blanket ban. Most damagingly, A proposes restricting conduct data to staff until escalation thresholds are met, then contradicts that proposal in closing. B identifies and exploits this central inconsistency.

A's response that these rules do not restrict parental visibility contradicts its own explicit wording rather than rescuing its position.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): The motion asks only whether schools should limit messaging platforms from becoming real-time behavior dashboards. It does not require weekly batching. CON's own advocacy affirms that limit. CON called for "no routine behavior stream" and "avoid routine behavioral tracking," and A4.5 says the "best policy limits dashboards." CON was sharper in the mid-debate exchanges. It showed that batching is neither necessary nor sufficient, since a weekly digest can still be a behavioral dossier, and it argued that content-based rules beat delay. However, those points only attack PRO's chosen mechanism, not the proposition. PRO stated early (B2.1) that its proposal is the policy design that prevents the failure mode CON admits exists. PRO also pressed category creep and the redundancy of a live channel once emergencies use phones. CON's closing even argues against a live routine feed, which concedes PRO's core harm. PRO had real weaknesses. Its "structural guarantee" claim was overstated, it was vulnerable on the dossier point, and in B4.1 it cited its own speech as a concession. On the motion as written, though, PRO holds the cleaner path to the ballot, while CON spent its best material defeating a narrower version of the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's A2.5/A4.4 point that batching merely lowers the refresh rate and a digest can still be a dossier. It was CON's strongest material, but it ended up reinforcing that content limits on dashboards are needed. That is PRO's motion, and PRO leaned on it in B4.4 and B5.2 by distinguishing limiting the dashboard from limiting record-keeping.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): B (PRO) won a close, well-matched debate. The decisive clash was whether the remedy should be categorical batching (B) or content-based limits with immediacy (A). B's strongest route: once emergencies are conceded to phones and front-office protocols (a concession B extracted in B2.3 and A4.1 confirmed), the live platform's only unique function is speed for non-urgent matters, which is precisely the micromanagement harm. A never fully restored a residual use case for platform immediacy beyond emergencies. A's best counter—the "content control vs delay" reframe and the point that a weekly digest can still be a behavioral dossier—was a genuinely sharp turn, but B answered it (B4.4): the motion limits the platform from becoming a real-time dashboard, and batching severs the real-time loop and caps data volume. B's category-creep pressure (B3.2) exposed a real enforcement burden on A's counterplan, and while A offered audits and fixed categories, B's "structural guarantee vs policy promise" weighing in the closing gave the cleaner voting issue. Both sides obeyed format, answered pressure questions directly with A1/A2, and used only plausible unsourced reasoning. A was competitive throughout and landed the more original reframe, but B made the debate easier to decide and controlled the decisive comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1's answer to A's pressure question ("No essential function remains"): by carving emergencies out to existing phone protocols, B stripped A's case of any unique benefit for live platform alerts, reducing A's defense to the residual capacity for routine real-time behavior pushes—the very dashboard the motion targets. A's A4.1/A4.2 response partly conceded routine alerts are useless, leaving the counterplan's immediacy advantage largely unexplained.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): B wins on the proposition as written. A’s alternative—prohibiting routine behavior streams and enforcing narrow alert categories—itself limits platforms from becoming micromanagement dashboards. B also preserves urgent communication, undermining A’s repeated warnings about delayed care. A persuasively challenges batching as insufficient, while B overstates inevitable category creep and guaranteed volume reduction. Those weaknesses keep this competitive, but A mainly disputes implementation rather than defeating the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest rebuttal distinguishes content control from delay: weekly summaries can still contain behavioral dossiers. This materially weakens B’s claimed structural guarantee. However, B’s distinction between emergency contact and routine live reporting survives, and A’s preferred content restrictions ultimately reinforce the broader case for limits.
