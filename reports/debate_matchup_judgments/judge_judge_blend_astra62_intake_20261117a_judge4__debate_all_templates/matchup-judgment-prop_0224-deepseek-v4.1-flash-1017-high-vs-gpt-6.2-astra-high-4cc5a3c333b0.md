### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins on the motion as written: an equivalent-scale nuclear program that includes new builds, judged on tonnes avoided per public dollar within 2026–2036. CON established three linked points that PRO never fully neutralized: - **Timing:** a reactor opening late in the window banks few in-window tonnes, while staged renewable connections accumulate savings as each project comes online. - **Additionality:** this discipline applies to extensions and to construction support, not only to renewables. - **Dollar weighting:** cheap closure rescues cannot lend their return to construction dollars. PRO's strongest material was its emphasis on system costs and firm output. However, it stayed at the level of assertion ("renewable dollars often need another dollar"), and CON answered it with a fair accounting method. That method counts incremental wires, storage and firming, but not backup capacity that would be retained under either plan. It credits only fuel actually saved after curtailment and losses. Under pressure, PRO conceded that new builds might contribute nothing inside the window and that it could not quantify the at-risk fleet. CON's threshold arithmetic then showed what that concession costs PRO. PRO's closing moved to a "marginal dollar, stop after rescues" framing. CON correctly noted that this abandons the equivalent-scale comparison and the new-build half of the proposition. CON also answered its pressure questions more directly with A1:/A2: methodology. PRO's answers largely deflected by saying it would not invent precision. CON's opening contained a stray fragment, and its 8-year-versus-2-year hypothetical is stylized. Neither flaw undermined CON's case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A4.3 against B4.5, extended through A5.1. PRO claimed that extensions and uprates alone could carry the comparison even if new builds contributed nothing. CON replied with threshold arithmetic: if construction delivers zero tonnes by 2036, more than half the nuclear budget must earn a doubled return. Calling the rescue tranche "material" does not show it clears that bar. CON then added that stopping after the best rescues is sound budgeting but is not a win at equivalent scale. This exposed PRO's closing reframe as a retreat from the proposition.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a high-level exchange by anchoring every argument strictly to the proposition's cumulative 2026–2036 window and program-scale comparison. While Side B made a strong case on firm generation versus renewable integration costs and inframarginality, Side A systematically dismantled B's routes to victory: distinguishing fuel displacement from capacity retirement, showing that existing gas backup needed while reactors are under construction is a common baseline cost rather than a wind-only penalty, directing renewable public funds to grid-connection bottlenecks to solve both queues and inframarginality, and proving mathematically that sequencing finite reactor rescues first cannot carry a scale-up budget whose new-build tranche barely operates before 2036.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A4.3] and [A5.1], Side A exposed Side B's claim that sequencing rescues and uprates first carries the nuclear program even if new builds deliver zero by 2036—showing that order of spending cannot change dollar-weighted budget arithmetic, and that Side B's closing pivot to simply stop spending after good rescues concedes nuclear loses at an equivalent scale-up.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the focus on cumulative emissions avoided per public dollar across 2026–2036 and showing that A’s strongest life-extension bargains are finite and cannot automatically lift the average return of an equivalent-scale nuclear budget. B’s timing and additionality accounting put real pressure on new nuclear inside the window, while A’s shift to buying only the best marginal tranches did not fully answer the scale-up burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttal 2 arithmetic: even if rescues and uprates perform twice as well as renewables, a nuclear program with zero new-build output by 2036 would need more than half its budget to earn that double return, exposing that A’s best tranches may not carry the whole program.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO wins narrowly by making the per-dollar mechanism clearer. Its first strength was symmetric additionality: count only the generation that public money actually changes, for both technologies. Its second was the refurbishment-gap logic: taxpayers fund only the uncovered share of a backfit, yet the whole unit's output is preserved on an existing connection. Its third was the declining marginal value of midday solar once storage and transmission costs are counted. PRO also answered the pressure questions directly. On the transfer-to-owner worry, it offered a buyer test, milestone payments, and clawbacks. On new-build, it gave an honest minority share with a worked example rather than invented precision. CON kept a live and important route: extensions are lumpy and limited, new-build is back-loaded, and an equal budget carries opportunity cost. CON's closing used PRO's concession that new reactors "need not carry the comparison" effectively. However, CON leaned repeatedly on the "delivered electrons vs promises" slogan, and its additionality answer was thin, resting on the claim that competition reveals whether the subsidy was decisive. CON never showed that pay-on-delivery renewables avoid more tonnes per dollar, as opposed to merely shifting risk. CON's scale argument keeps this close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing gave CON its best-case auction and then separated delivery risk from abatement. Paying only when power flows settles who bears the risk of non-delivery. It does not settle how much evening gas is displaced per dollar, especially once the public share of batteries is counted. This exposed the conflation at the core of CON's "buy delivered electrons" framing.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a competitive, high-level debate by holding the clash firmly to cumulative 2026–2036 emissions avoided per public dollar across an entire scale-up budget. Side B (PRO) argued persuasively that closing partial refurbishment shortfalls on retiring reactors unlocks full firm output on existing grid connections, and cleanly explained why negative-NPV safety backfits would not be solved by a zero-price private sale. However, Side A showed that such marginal extension candidates are lumpy, finite, and exposed to upfront outage risk, while the new-build half of the proposition commits front-loaded public dollars whose output mostly lands near or after 2036. By forcing Side B to concede that new-build delivers only a small minority of pre-2036 tonnes and catching that Side B's acceleration math ignored the public-dollar denominator, Side A established that competitively procured, pay-for-delivery wind, solar, and batteries compound abatement more reliably and scalably within the window.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A3.2] and [A4.3–A4.4], Side A forced Side B to quantify new-build's share of pre-2036 abatement, prompting Side B to concede new-build is a minority [B4.3] that "need not carry the comparison" [B5.4]; Side A then decisively noted that Side B's three-million-tonne acceleration example was gross rather than per public dollar, exposing how front-loaded new-build costs dilute the combined nuclear program's denominator before 2036.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the focus on the motion’s exact metric: cumulative emissions avoided per public dollar by 2036. It forced A to concede that new nuclear would be a minority, back-loaded contributor, then argued that the remaining nuclear case rests on lumpy, administratively uncertain refurbishment gaps. A’s life-extension case was concrete and attractive, especially on existing connections and evening firmness, but B’s delivered-abatement, pay-for-output, modular renewables frame gave the cleaner comparative reason to vote CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered A’s additionality pressure with a plausible auction/screening/pay-on-delivery mechanism, then used A’s own new-build example to show that PRO’s remaining advantage depended on narrow extensions and uncertain acceleration rather than a full-program per-dollar edge.
