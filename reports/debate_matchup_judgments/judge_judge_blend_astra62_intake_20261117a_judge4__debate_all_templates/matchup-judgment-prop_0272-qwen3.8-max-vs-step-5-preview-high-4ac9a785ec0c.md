### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a close debate because it held the clearer reading of the motion. It framed the question as which scarce after-school uses are hardest to replace, and it kept a consistent division of labor: digital readiness is infrastructure that belongs in the core day, while embodied play, sports, and arts are what the enrichment hour uniquely provides. CON (Side A) had real strengths: - It turned PRO's equity argument effectively. An on-campus lab removes the home-broadband dependency, so PRO's repeated reliance on that point weakened over the rounds. - Its "the regular day is already overcommitted" answer was a good one. CON also had costs: - It gradually redefined "enrichment" as homework-completion infrastructure, which drifts from the motion's "purely digital enrichment programs." - Its claim that movement can be protected through PE and recess largely mirrors the move it criticized PRO for making with digital skills. - It cited opponent claims with mislabeled IDs (P2.1, P2.4, and so on). PRO answered both pressure questions directly. It conceded real costs, addressed transport with the late-bus point, and closed with crisper weighing around what children most lack. On balance, PRO left the cleaner decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The strongest exchange was CON's A2 against PRO's equity claim. CON argued that a school lab supplies the devices, bandwidth, and troubleshooting itself, so the home-resource objection fails. PRO only partially recovered by saying a late bus serves both tracks and that a lab "can supply a login, not a childhood." That exchange narrowed the debate to which experience the after-school hour is uniquely suited to provide, a framing that favored PRO.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): CON more convincingly showed that PRO’s sequencing fails under real constraints: the regular day cannot absorb repeated digital practice without displacing core instruction, and a campus lab can equalize devices, connectivity, and supervision more reliably than physical programs overcome transport, gear, and space barriers. PRO’s developmental case for embodied priority remained appealing but did not fully neutralize those access and urgency pressures.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2, answering that an overcommitted school day cannot supply needed digital repetitions and that an on-campus lab supplies every input more cleanly than physical expansions clear their own external barriers.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON wins because its crowded-core-day and campus-lab arguments directly answer PRO's sequencing case: digital fluency needs repeated supervised practice that cannot simply be absorbed in the day or sent home, while an on-campus lab supplies devices, bandwidth, and troubleshooting. PRO's embodied-development case remains strong, but its equity claim is weakened by repeatedly assuming digital access depends on the home despite that rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: the regular day is already overcommitted, homework shifts digital practice to unequal homes, and a school lab can supply every digital input on site, undercutting PRO's main equity and placement arguments.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): This was a close, well-matched debate on who actually reaches the excluded child. CON pressed real points: asynchronous digital programs offer flexibility, physical programs carry hidden costs (staff, transport, weather), and some disabled or rural students may be poorly served by fields and gyms. Its "fund exclusion first" and "locked door" framings were effective. PRO, however, built the cleaner comparative mechanism and kept it intact. Asynchronous home use still depends on private household conditions: a device, bandwidth, and a quiet room. The school-lab fallback reintroduces the same location and schedule barriers CON attributed to fields. PRO's closing distinction is the most decisive weighing in the debate: physical programs' prerequisites are "public and fixable," while digital access depends on conditions repeated privately in every home. PRO also turned CON's own rule ("whatever reaches the excluded child first") back in its favor. PRO answered both pressure questions honestly, conceding asynchrony exists while denying it solves access, and reframing "always" as a priority rule. CON drifted somewhat toward academic remediation (math recovery) rather than enrichment as the motion frames it. PRO's developmental argument went largely unanswered. CON's answers were also solid, which keeps the margin thin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 to CON's asynchrony question was the most decisive exchange. It accepted that digital work can be asynchronous but argued that a "school-issued device" still presupposes bandwidth, quiet, and a safe place, so flexibility is "access promised later." PRO then sharpened this in closing by contrasting public, fixable prerequisites with private household ones. CON's best counter was "a field is only a floor if the child can reach it," which landed but never resolved the point that a school lab faces the same reachability limits.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): B won by showing that PRO’s universal “physical first” priority fails the students it claims to protect. PRO’s floor argument—that sports, arts, and play need no home device, bandwidth, or quiet room—was met by B’s clearer account of physical barriers (transport, schedule, disability, facilities, weather) and of school-supported digital options (loaners, hotspots, labs, offline/async work) that can reach excluded children now. PRO answered pressure directly but never fully neutralized the locked-door cases, while B weighed urgency and actual reach more cleanly against a blanket sequence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 and closing most damaged PRO’s live equity claim: a field or studio is only a floor if the child can reach and use it, whereas school-supported digital can be a present bridge for students physical programs exclude.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won the equity clash by showing physical programs are not a universal floor: they require presence, transport, mobility, schedule, facilities, and staffing. Its asynchronous, school-supported digital alternative directly answered PRO's access objections with loaner devices, offline work, hotspots, and labs, while PRO's developmental and floor-versus-ceiling framing never justified a fixed priority for students physical programs exclude first.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 asynchronous distinction broke PRO's claim that barriers merely change address, forcing PRO into home-access objections that A then answered concretely in Rebuttal 2.
