### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): A razor-thin win for PRO. Both sides argued cleanly with no fake sourcing and complied with the Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 formats. The decisive clash was whether unchanged output freezes the headquarters band. A's pressure Q1 (why are market raises for unchanged work legitimate?) forced B onto a retention standard, and A capitalized by noting B's "pay what retention requires" answer is a market standard that cannot uniquely entrench the HQ band. B's closing response (retention is a floor against leaving, not a license to cut) was good but left B's benchmark for the "correct" salary essentially as the higher number. B's strongest live route—the "appropriation of private savings / tax on personal choice" framing and the sharp asymmetry answer that upward repricing corrects undervaluation while downward repricing monetizes geography—was genuinely damaging and memorable, but A's prospective-disclosure, market-band, contract-honoring safeguards progressively narrowed B's abuse scenarios to conceded exceptions while keeping the general default intact. A made the terms of the general bargain clearer; B made the moral objection vivid but never resolved why the HQ band, rather than a regional band, is the fair default.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Q1 asking why market-driven raises for unchanged work are legitimate if market-driven location cuts are not; B's A1 (upward repricing corrects undervaluation, downward repricing monetizes geography) was the sharpest single answer, but A's follow-through in Closing—that B's own retention standard concedes alternatives, not output, set pay—landed the more consequential blow.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by dismantling Side B's core premise that headquarters pay purely measures output rather than partly reflecting a high-cost local labor market. Side A defended a coherent general rule—transparent, prospective regional bands that preserve real purchasing-power gains while honoring contracts and retention-based exceptions—and exposed the circularity in Side B's claim that lower-paid local peers merely prove everyone should receive headquarters rates. While Side B argued memorably against penalizing private relocation choices, it could not convincingly explain why market conditions legitimately justify raises for unchanged output yet never allow prospective adjustments to geographic premiums, and its late turns relied too heavily on attacking retroactive ambush cuts that Side A had already ruled out.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's multi-turn rebuttal and questioning on market pricing and horizontal equity ([A2.2], [A3.1]-[A3.2], [A4.5], [A5.1]): after Side B argued that local colleagues are underpaid and that employers should stick to an already-set price for unchanged output, Side A forced Side B to explain market-driven raises for unchanged work and unequal pay in the same town, prompting Side B to concede in [B4.3] that fair pay depends partly on what is needed to retain the worker—a market standard that undercut Side B's pure-output theory.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by defending a general, prospective regional-band rule with real safeguards while forcing Side A to explain why a headquarters premium should permanently follow the worker. B’s pressure on one-way market pricing and on what makes the higher salary uniquely fair landed, and B fairly used A’s own “needed to retain” language. A’s moral case against appropriating a private move was strong but never fully displaced the labor-market baseline or the consistency problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s closing use of A’s retention language, after the pressure questions on asymmetric market pricing, most clearly showed why unchanged output does not freeze headquarters pay.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won the decisive clash. B's core pressure — what new economic fact does the move create when the role was already remote and cheaper candidates were already reachable — was never squarely answered. A's replacement-pool response (that pre-move hiring from the cheap region meant "asking someone to leave a high-cost market") was logically incoherent for an already-remote role, and B exposed this directly in Rebuttal 2. Worse for A, its own A2 answer conceded that unchanged remote offers defeat a cut and that the employer must "show a job-related market change" — effectively adopting B's evidentiary default — and B capitalized on the concession. A's remaining case rested on the assertion that location is a "proxy" for labor-market change, which B distinguished cleanly (rent measures private living costs, not accessible competing offers). A's aphorisms (ratchet, eating the surplus twice) were memorable, but the underlying mechanism stayed contested while B's default-plus-exceptions framework survived intact. A also cited nonexistent reference IDs ([C1.1], [C2.1], [C2.2]) in its pressure round, a small execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 [B4.3-B4.5]: B dismantled A's replacement-pool story by noting the role was already remote, so cheaper candidates were always available and the move creates no new replacement option — then captured A's own concession that employers must show a "job-related market change," folding A's framework into B's evidentiary default.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won solidly by separating local housing costs from remote labor markets and systematically dismantling Side A's economic mechanisms. Side B neutralized the windfall and equity arguments early, then proved that an incumbent's move does not create cheaper replacement options for an already remote role or automatically eliminate competing remote offers. Trapped by Side B's pressure questions, Side A gave a logically flawed defense of its replacement-pool claim in [A4.1], conceded in [A4.2] that unchanged remote offers defeat a cut and even wrote that the employer bears the burden to show a job-related market change, cited nonexistent [C...] reference IDs in Turn 5, and failed to answer Side B's decisive rebuttals and concrete labor-market metrics in Closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's takedown of Side A's replacement-cost claim across [B2.2], [B3.1], and [B4.3-B4.4]: showing that if a remote role can be filled from a cheaper region, that recruitment option already existed before the incumbent moved, which exposed Side A's assertion in [A4.1]—that relocation newly unlocks local replacement pricing—as incoherent for an already remote job.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won the decisive mechanism: for an already-remote role, a cheaper address neither creates a new replacement pool nor automatically shrinks competing remote offers. Side B never closed that gap, and its own concession that unchanged offers defeat a cut, and that a job-related market change must be shown, reinforced A's evidentiary default over a general presumption of cuts. B's ratchet and equity points stayed live, but they do not justify treating rent as proof of diminished labor value.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal: the role was already remote, so the move created no new cheaper substitute, and B's concession that unchanged remote offers defeat a cut supports continuity unless the labor market—not housing—actually changed.
