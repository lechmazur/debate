### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO built a clear, workable standard: name the work, the roles, and the attendance pattern, with one explanation able to cover many roles. It then adapted well across rounds. PRO turned CON's own break-room example into a model of a valid justification. It answered the paperwork objection by separating "role-specific reason" from "one memo per role." It also neutralized the mentorship objection by showing the rule can require leaders to attend for teaching reasons. CON mostly repeated its opening claims about thousands of hours and two-tier inequity without engaging those answers. It misdescribed PRO as dismissing the burden as "trivial" and as "admitting" that mentorship is critical to promotion. Its A2 restated the original claim instead of answering why mentorship value wouldn't justify the leader's attendance. Worse, CON's A1 conceded that payroll had no reason to commute, which PRO exploited in closing. CON's closing added a late, brittle calculation (10,000 roles × 15 minutes). It also conceded that waiving justification shifts costs onto workers commuting for no valid reason, which undercut its own position.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing on the payroll point. CON argued that a support/engineering memo gives payroll no reason to commute. PRO reframed this as "not a defect in the memo, a defect in a mandate that covers payroll," turning CON's burden argument into proof of the motion. PRO then paired it with the line that skipping the explanation "takes time from everyone ordered in, every week the rule stands."
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won the central clash: it showed that a role-specific reason can cover multiple roles in one explanation, without requiring individualized memos for every position, which deflated CON's administrative-burden route. A also directly answered the equity concern by explaining that mentors can be required to attend alongside juniors for that teaching reason. CON repeated its harms without resolving those reframings, and A's closing made the decisive point that a support/engineering memo is not meant to justify payroll—the overbroad mandate is the problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was A's reframing of CON's break-room example as itself a workable role-specific justification, and later the closing turn that treated CON's payroll objection as a defect in the mandate rather than in the explanation.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by defending a workable reading of role-specific reasons: one explanation can cover shared needs, mentoring itself justifies the people who must overlap, and unexplained mandates impose recurring commute costs. It kept treating team overlap and mentoring as outside the rule, sidestepped B’s questions, and closed with an invented hours calculation rather than a cleaner comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttals turning the break-room case into a valid shared justification and answering that a senior’s teaching role, not only async core duties, can require attendance when juniors need to learn.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): CON wins clearly by changing what the debate was about. The question became which level of justification actually protects workers: role or policy. CON argued that coordination benefits arise at the team or schedule level. It then showed that role descriptions cannot answer the questions that matter: which days are required, for how long, and who is present together. Its two-engineer example and its Tuesday/Friday example made this concrete. Workers get a testable objection under CON's approach that they would not get from job descriptions. PRO's case had real weaknesses: - **Unverifiable statistics.** The opening relied on survey figures like "three in five" and "one in four" with no grounding. - **A self-defeating example.** The facilities technician maintains on-site systems, so that role plainly needs in-person presence. - **Shifting tests.** PRO's rebuttals moved between "link duties to coordination" and "prove the role requires in-person work." CON caught this explicitly. - **A concession.** PRO's A1 effectively accepted team-level evidence as long as duties are documented, which conceded CON's point that the duty documentation adds nothing. - **A mischaracterized closing.** PRO claimed CON's test applies only "after imposing" the mandate. CON had already said the burden comes before anyone commutes. CON answered both pressure questions directly. It also correctly flagged PRO's overstatement that caregivers have a legal accommodation entitlement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.3 and B4.5 exposed PRO's inconsistency. Under the weak "link duties to coordination" test, an attrition-minded executive can justify five days as easily as one. Under the strong "prove the role requires attendance" test, a team day supported by evidence can be defeated. The identical-duties engineers example then showed that the deciding factor is team assignment and schedule, not role. PRO never answered this.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by exposing a persistent flaw in A's central criterion: role-specific justification either collapses into team-assignment or schedule reasoning, or it blocks coordination that the evidence supports. B's identical-engineers and Tuesday/Friday examples showed job descriptions remain true without distinguishing which people must attend or which days are justified; A never fully resolved that tension. A's accommodation answer deferred to existing law, while B offered a more direct mechanism by letting workers challenge the unnecessary day itself.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's B4.5/B4.6 response was decisive: two engineers with identical duties show the decisive evidence is team assignment and overlap, not role, and requiring proof that engineering duties require attendance can defeat a policy the launch evidence supports.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that role-specific proof either collapses into restating team coordination or blocks useful overlap that no job description can isolate. The Tuesday-versus-Friday test made the live clash concrete: documented duties stay equally true on a justified common day and an unjustified extra commute, while a scope-and-duration burden can fail the extra day before anyone seeks an exception. Side B never resolved that tension, leaned on existing accommodation law rather than its own rule, and misstated A’s burden in closing. B’s worker-protection intuition was real, but it did not establish why the motion’s role-specific test is the accountability that matters.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s two-test distinction, carried through the launch-engineer hypothetical: linking duties to “overlap” is as gameable as culture slogans, while demanding proof that a role requires attendance can defeat a common day the team evidence already supports and still cannot explain Friday.
