### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B built the more decisive path to victory. Its core weighing—the asymmetry of mistakes (a collapsed ceasefire is unrecoverable; delayed truth was actually recovered in Argentina, Chile, and Spain)—survived the exchange and framed the entire debate. B also turned A's own examples: Argentina's collapsed-but-still-dangerous military forced amnesty via mutinies, undercutting A's "perpetrators are weakest" claim, and Chile's confidential commission undermined A's public-testimony requirement. B's militia hypothetical forced a real concession from A ("would not choose bloodshed on principle"), and B pressed it effectively: the disclosure conditions A attached were exactly what the hypothetical militia rejects. A's fatal gap was asserting that undefeated perpetrators are a "rare emergency" without support, while B argued plausibly that most transitions are negotiated with forces that still command armies. B's Uruguay paternalism point also went essentially unanswered. B's execution costs (three clipped speeches) temper the margin, but its arguments were substantively complete and it made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal: catching that A's concessions on an undefeated militia [A4.2] meant A either loses the ceasefire or abandons the motion, since the attached disclosure and accountability terms are precisely what such a militia rejects—and then arguing that this scenario is the normal case, not the exception, making A's "usually" collapse exactly where transitions are most dangerous.
