### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): This was a close, well-matched debate, and PRO wins narrowly because it controlled the decisive clash: what guarantees human review when the AI's own risk detection fails. PRO's case: - The cap is a crude backstop against false negatives, a ceiling rather than a floor. - CON's alternatives (crisis pathways, random audits, liability) all still depend on someone first recognizing risk. - PRO's Q2 isolated the never-flagged, never-audited patient. CON's answer asserted that AI-only care is "not unsupervised" but never named a guaranteed human event for that case. - CON's own reliance on "periodic check-ins" quietly concedes that scheduled review has value. That blunts its odometer critique. - PRO also met the rubber-stamp objection directly with licensing and negligence duties. CON later repeated the rubber-stamp claim without engaging that answer. CON's case: - Its strongest material was the timing point: a patient declining at session two waits until session eight. PRO's "ceiling, not floor" reply answers this only partly. - CON also turned PRO's concession that, without a clinician, the chatbot cannot be the sole provider into a reduced-access harm. PRO's closing left that point unanswered, which is a real cost. On balance, PRO's frame ("better when it works vs. safer when it fails") gave the clearer decision rule. CON's access harm was meaningful but did not defeat the central safety comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2 was the decisive exchange. It framed the cap as the only guarantee that survives a false negative and made sham review a licensing and negligence violation. CON's reply leaned on "liability from session one" and random audits, which still require someone to notice the problem. CON's best counter-blow was exploiting PRO's "no clinician, no sole AI provider" concession as an access harm, which PRO left unanswered in its closing.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): B narrowly wins the policy comparison. A established a genuine false-negative backstop and answered the pressure questions more directly; B’s repeated timing objection did not defeat that benefit. However, B exposed the unresolved cost of requiring reviews regardless of clinical signal and sharpened A’s concession that unavailable clinicians would mean withdrawing AI-only care. Funding reviews does not itself resolve capacity constraints, and A’s closing never adequately weighed those access costs against the backstop’s benefits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B correctly challenged A’s comparison between brief review and crisis admission: the relevant comparison is mandatory review of continuing users versus targeted oversight, not review versus catastrophe in every case. B then connected that distinction to A’s explicit no-clinician, no-AI-only-care concession.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO wins the central clash: CON's risk architecture still depends on detecting the deterioration it may miss, and random audits or after-the-fact liability never guarantee that a human reviews each quietly worsening patient. PRO's ceiling-not-floor framing answers the timing objection, while its documentation and liability duty makes handoff more than a rubber stamp. CON's scarcity and access costs land, especially PRO's concession where no clinician exists, but they do not neutralize the false-negative backstop.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal that risk-triggered escalation depends on the chatbot recognizing the very risk it misclassifies, forcing CON to rely on probabilistic audits and self-report rather than an enforceable per-patient human review.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): This was a close, well-matched debate, and CON wins it narrowly. PRO's best case: - PRO built a coherent core thesis around the conflict of interest. Platforms that profit from engagement control their own risk triggers, so a legally fixed handoff periodically moves the continuation decision to an outside human. - The closing point that "deterioration that looks like loyalty" will never fire an internal trigger was PRO's most compelling line. Why CON edged ahead: - **Consistent rebuttal pressure.** CON showed the cap routes patients by attendance rather than symptoms, which undercuts PRO's own suicide-risk rationale. It broke the prescription-refill analogy by noting a refill date is clinician-set inside a care relationship. - **Turning PRO's concessions.** PRO conceded that acute risk needs immediate escalation regardless, and that if no clinician answers, the bot should drop to non-clinical support. CON took both and argued PRO's real safeguards (qualified human responsibility, stopping therapeutic claims when no human is available) need no fixed N. - **A clean framing device.** CON's "redundant or dangerous" dilemma was memorable and made the decision easier. Execution on both sides: - PRO answered A1/A2 directly, but its "single-digit" number sits outside the motion. - PRO never fully addressed the cost to stable rural users, who lose therapeutic support at the cap. - PRO's speeches were somewhat underlength. - CON's own regime leans on "clinician-set intervals" and independent audit. PRO only partly exploited that weakness through its retrospective-versus-prophylactic contrast. Net: CON made the cleaner comparative case. PRO's conflict-of-interest point kept the debate close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.5 and B4.3/B4.5 sequence was the most decisive exchange. It held that if a clinician can approve continued AI use, the cap adds only discontinuity, and if not, it cuts off stable patients. It then used PRO's own fallback (no clinician means stop acting as care) to argue that the protective work comes from escalation and accountability requirements, not the session count. PRO's A5.1 reply, that triggers are proprietary and engagement-biased, was strong but arrived late and only partly neutralized the dilemma.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): B wins narrowly but clearly on the distinction between requiring human oversight and requiring a fixed session count. A established that checkpoints could catch deterioration missed by risk triggers, making B’s redundancy claim overstated. But A never adequately justified the uniform deadline over clinician-set review intervals or resolved the loss of clinical support when professionals are unavailable. A’s closing also treated B’s independently audited alternative too much like platform self-regulation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s endpoint question forced A to specify that, without an available clinician, the chatbot must cease functioning as care. B then contrasted this deadline-driven interruption with clinically triggered escalation and review. That exposed an unresolved access cost rather than merely calling the count arbitrary.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by isolating the motion's unique burden: a fixed number. PRO effectively defended periodic human oversight, staffing duties, and stopping therapeutic claims when no clinician is available, but B showed those protections can be required through risk triggers, patient request, clinician-set intervals, and audit without an arbitrary session cliff. B's redundant-or-dangerous dilemma, trapdoor/access objection, and exposure of PRO's unjustified single-digit number remained live, while PRO's self-dealing concern was answered with independent oversight rather than a calendar.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it used PRO's concessions that acute risk needs immediate escalation and that care must stop or be relabeled if no clinician answers to prove the fixed count itself does no necessary safety work.
