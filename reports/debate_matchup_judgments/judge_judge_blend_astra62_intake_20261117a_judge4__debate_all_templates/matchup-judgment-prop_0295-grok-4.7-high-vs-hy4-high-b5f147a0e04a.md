### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): This was a close, well-matched debate, and CON edges it on clarity of principle. **PRO's strengths.** PRO built a vivid causal story: colonial collecting concentrated skills, archives and institutions in the old capitals. It defused the paternalism charge by making funding recipient-governed and time-limited. Its Rebuttal 2 answered CON's fork directly and honestly through the recipient-set end date, which addressed both the outcome-guarantee horn and the "who judges capacity" horn. **CON's persistent pressure.** CON kept pressing a deeper problem PRO never fully dislodged: does the funding duty actually derive from the wrong of taking, or is it a need-based transfer relabelled as restitution? - "Need is not title" anchored this line. - Its strongest follow-up was that a duty whose content and end date are set after the return is a negotiated project, not a debt with a principled limit. **The contradiction CON exposed.** CON also identified a real tension in PRO's own wording. PRO said "authority was never waiting on the sender's certificate" but also "title without those means is authority on paper." PRO's closing resolved this by conceding "Authority already passed. Usefulness did not." CON turned that into its closing frame: restitution ends at restored authority, and later usefulness is the owner's own future. **What PRO left underdeveloped.** PRO's reply that colonialism concentrated skill elsewhere was plausible. But PRO never sharply explained why diffuse institutional advantages count as part of what must be returned, rather than a broader reparations question. **Overall.** CON gave the cleaner decision rule, a stable endpoint, and better exploitation of the opponent's language. PRO's answers were good enough to keep the margin thin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.4–A4.5) set PRO's "authority was never waiting on the sender's certificate" against "title without those means is authority on paper." It then argued that a recipient-defined package takes its content from later cultural policy rather than from the taking. This forced PRO to concede in closing that authority had already passed, which CON converted into its decisive frame.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): B more convincingly distinguished restored ownership from completing repair, grounding additional funding in the concentration of expertise, archives, and institutions around colonial collections. A exposed genuine problems concerning funding limits and donor administration, but B answered the central pressure with a finite, object-linked package under recipient control. A then largely asserted that restored authority exhausts the obligation, rather than establishing why it excludes a further funding duty. Its claim that accounting necessarily recreates colonial control also remained underdeveloped. B’s recipient-defined scope leaves some uncertainty, keeping this competitive, but its case better addresses the proposition’s broader question of what former powers should fund.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal distinguished delivering an agreed package from guaranteeing an artifact’s condition forever, and expressly denied the payer any residual veto. This directly weakened A’s strongest objection about perpetual obligations. A’s subsequent insistence that authority had already transferred did not refute the possibility of an additional, bounded remedial duty.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins a clear, competitive debate by drawing a durable boundary around restitution and trapping Side B (PRO) between bureaucratic oversight and an arbitrary blank check. Side B built a persuasive case that colonial collecting concentrated conservation skills and archival legibility in imperial capitals, risking blame-shifting if fragile objects are merely shipped home. However, Side A smartly conceded upfront that returning museums must copy and surrender provenance and conservation records, isolating ongoing institutional subsidies from withheld control. When pressed on what bounds object-linked funding without an endless outcome guarantee, Side B pivoted in Rebuttal 2 to a unilateral, recipient-dated invoice with no sender oversight. Side A dismantled that fix both conceptually—showing that an obligation whose scope varies from nothing to decades of posts depending on post-return plans is cultural policy rather than a debt derived from the taking—and practically, as Side B never explained how public treasuries fund specific capacity without either audit reporting lines or unconditional cash transfers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side B effectively answered the "Western museum model" critique by arguing recipients could fund ceremonial care and set their own dated invoice [B2.5, B4.1-B4.4], Side A pivoted to public-finance reality ("The vitrine is not the danger; the reporting line is" [A2.5]) and exposed in [A4.3-A4.6] that a recipient-written invoice lacks a principled restitutive limit while leaving the dilemma between grant accounting and unconditional transfers unanswered.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): This was a close, high-quality debate. CON built a crisp conceptual line: restitution means yielding what the holder still controls. Mounts, climate control, and staff are ordinary costs of ownership that any museum pays, even for objects never taken. That point was CON's strongest weapon. Its warning that a funding requirement would keep returns stuck in appropriations politics was also real pressure. PRO adapted better as the exchange narrowed. It answered the "no stopping rule" attack with a concrete, object-specific transfer schedule: assessment, treatment, safeguards, record reconnection, and handover training, settled alongside shipment. It drew explicit lines (a cabinet is in, a new wing is out). It also answered the delay worry by having title pass immediately. PRO's pressure questions effectively exploited CON's concession that copying and translating records is a cost of giving back, "like the crate," which forced CON to defend a control-based line. CON's later speeches mostly asserted that the schedule "renames the invoice" and that "capacity has no natural end." They did not show why PRO's bounded schedule actually fails to stop. PRO's "handover deficit" argument was somewhat weaker against the never-taken-objects point. Still, PRO better carried the motion's normative "should fund" burden and left fewer pressures unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over the stopping rule. CON's A1 asked for a non-arbitrary limit. PRO's B4.3 answered with a pre-transfer, object-specific schedule that is discharged on delivery and excludes new wings and permanent payroll. That neutralized CON's central "endless liability" route. CON's A4.6 reply, that "fixed" and "unconditional" only describe the politics of the check, reasserted the kind distinction without showing that the bounded schedule actually fails.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): B built the stronger case by distinguishing a workable, funded handover from perpetual museum subsidy. A's strongest objection—that ordinary conservation costs arise from ownership rather than colonial wrongdoing—remained meaningful, but did not adequately distinguish transitional repair and record reconstruction from routine upkeep. B supplied a finite discharge rule, local control, and immediate title transfer. A continued emphasizing permanent patronage and delayed returns without sufficiently showing why those consequences followed under B's clarified proposal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B directly answered the stopping-rule pressure with scheduled, object-specific measures, expressly excluding permanent payroll and new wings. A challenged whether those measures counted as restitution, but its continued claim that capacity funding necessarily has no endpoint failed to engage B's fixed-disbursement answer. That substantially weakened A's main practical objection.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): In a high-craft debate from both sides, Side A (CON) won by drawing a cleaner, more durable line between reversing a wrongful holding and subsidizing post-return ownership. By conceding that returning institutions must find, copy, translate, and transfer all archival records and treatment histories in their custody, Side A neutralized much of Side B's provenance grievance while isolating post-transfer conservation and museum capacity as ordinary stewardship costs that every museum pays even for objects never taken. Side A also forced Side B to retreat from broad museum capacity (labs and reliable power) to a negotiated pre-transfer schedule of cabinets and temporary training, convincingly showing that tying restitution's completeness to cross-border appropriations and scope schedules entangles returns in budget politics and delays artifacts coming home.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A3.1] and [A4.5], Side A dismantled Side B's attempt to frame conservation mounts and pest plans as a colonial "handover deficit." When Side B argued in [B4.1] that normal museum acquisitions come with condition reports and a budget whereas returned textiles do not, Side A cleanly replied that all holder condition histories must indeed be yielded with the object, whereas "a budget was never in the crate"—leaving physical upkeep after surrender squarely a cost of ownership rather than an unpaid part of the taking.
