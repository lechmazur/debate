### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B wins because A's core justification relied on protecting strangers from unpatched IoT devices, but A conceded that vanished sellers and devices that never install updates receive no patch under the proposal. B exploited those concessions effectively, showing the mandate's real yield is narrow while its costs and market distortions are broad. A retained a plausible partial-harm-reduction route, but B's comparative case for enforceable point-of-sale rules better survived the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 used A's own concessions—"It doesn't" and "no direct benefit"—to shrink the mandate to a thin slice of solvent firms, late flaws, and owners who actually update, then exposed A's automatic-update rescue as depending on a design feature outside the motion.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Both sides executed a disciplined, concession-tracking debate. B's negative strategy was sharp: it extracted A's admissions that a forfeited bond delivers no patch and that never-updating devices gain no direct benefit, then reframed those "extremes" as the core harm categories, and its B4.6 point (auto-update is a design/shelf feature, not part of the motion) was genuinely clever. But A's route to victory survived the exchange more intact. A's honest pivot in A4.1 — the bond works ex ante as deterrence, eroding the price advantage of planned abandonment — recast B's "prepaid exit fee" attack rather than dodging it. A's core logic (only the manufacturer can write a fix; a fix must exist before anyone can install it; some protection beats none) was weighed, not refuted. Decisively, A's pressure Q1 forced B to concede that under its own alternative "where no term was promised, nobody is compelled," meaning abandonment stays legal in B's world and late-discovered flaws go unpatched by anyone — B's unlockability answer ("permission to repair, not a tested fix" for a ten-dollar plug) was never developed. B also conceded the rule changes the thriving firm's decision, and A showed a retired model is thousands of devices, while B's cost case (prices, bonds, entrants, chip suppliers) stayed assertion-level against A's answers. B was marginally more memorable rhetorically, but A made the stronger substantive case for the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure Q1 ("who under your alternative owes its owners a fix for a newly discovered flaw?") forced B's candid B4.1 concession that where no support term was promised, nobody owes anything — gutting B's alternative precisely where the motion's harm lives. Nearly matched by A4.1's pivot turning B's "bond can't patch" concession into an ex-ante deterrence argument, and B4.6's "the mandate's best case borrows my mechanism" steal on auto-update.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): In a high-level, disciplined exchange, Side A built the more complete comparative case. Side B gained real traction on regulatory incidence—arguing that multi-year bonds lock up small entrants' capital, chip-supplier dependencies squeeze small makers, and forfeited bonds cannot write patches after a seller vanishes. However, Side A decisively proved why Side B's alternative of day-one shelf checks, voluntary labels, and end-of-life unlocking fails on the third-party externality both sides agreed matters: day-one checks can be enacted under either policy and cannot fix flaws found later, unlocked firmware gives ordinary households permission rather than a working patch, and voluntary labels still let buyers take upfront discounts for zero-support models at strangers' expense (as Side B conceded in [B4.1] and [B5.2]). Crucially, Side B clipped Side A's honest concessions in [A4.1] and [A4.2] without refuting the mechanisms immediately following them—that folding expected bond forfeiture into retail prices erodes hit-and-run sellers' checkout discounts, and that bargain-hunting buyers who skip paid support at checkout still accept free update prompts or benefit from existing auto-update features once a solvent firm is required to keep patching retired models.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's separation of firm survival from single-model support in [A2.2], pressed through [A3.1] and [A4.2]–[A5.2]: it forced Side B to admit that thriving firms drop older models and that under CON's label-and-shelf-check alternative nobody owes a fix for later flaws on unpromised models, while showing that checkout price sensitivity is not refusal to install free updates and that even auto-updating plugs cannot download a fix nobody wrote.
