Both sides had clean Q/A structure and no fake sourcing.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won a clear but competitive debate. Both sides argued the motion as written and engaged the same clash: whether PRO's price/e-waste gains outweigh added safety/security risk within five years. CON's central Catch-22—strict security gatekeeping kills cheap parts competition, while open calibration fractures hardware authentication and revives parts theft—was never fully dissolved. PRO's best escape, neutral UL-style certification, was sharply rebutted: UL marks certify batch electrical safety, not cryptographic provenance; genuine stolen screens pass safety tests; and building a firmware-auditing certification bureaucracy in five years adds fees that price out the cheap aftermarket PRO's price case depends on. CON also answered PRO's markup argument (quotes track depreciated value; cross-subsidized service margins; integrated OLED assemblies plus ungluing labor set a floor) and kept the cost pass-through point alive with only a weak "even if prices rise modestly" concession from PRO. CON's A1/A2 answers were direct and damaging, and the closing crystallized the existing clash without new material. PRO's strongest surviving material—the opacity-is-not-safety reframe, the "risk is a policy variable, benefit is a market mechanism" weighing, and the recurring nature of savings—kept the debate close, but too much of PRO's benefit side was left uncertain while CON's risk side was concrete and immediate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Turn 8: CON's A1 dismantled PRO's UL certification escape—safety marks cannot cryptographically verify provenance, so genuine stolen parts remain lucrative unless serial-number pairing stays, which reinstates the very gatekeeping PRO's price case needs removed. "Certified parts do not certify amateur hands" effectively closed the safety loop.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): A wins through sharper responses to the decisive implementation and net-benefit questions. B offers a plausible competition mechanism, but repeatedly treats savings as automatic rather than adequately weighing labor costs, upfront cost-shifting, and repair affordability on depreciated devices. Its closing also fails to resolve A’s distinction between safety certification and hardware security. A overstates how inevitably mandates produce hazards and higher retail prices, limiting the margin, but develops the more responsive and comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest rebuttal is that genuine screens stripped from stolen phones can pass safety certification, directly defeating B’s claim that neutral standards make stolen components unmarketable. B subsequently returns to UL-style standards without explaining how they establish lawful provenance or secure touch-controller firmware.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): A built the clearer five-year comparative case around existing integrated hardware, labor and compliance costs, broad price pass-through, and immediate hazards. B offered a strong structural competition argument and a memorable opacity-versus-safety reframe, but its savings and e-waste magnitudes remained largely asserted. Most importantly, B’s closing repeated that neutral certification was ordinary governance and claimed the markup point was unanswered even after A had directly addressed both.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal decisively attacked B’s central certification bridge: electrical-safety marks do not themselves establish firmware trust or parts provenance, stolen genuine components can pass safety tests, and building stronger certification would add delay and cost. A then connected that dilemma to the five-year horizon with the concise point that certified parts do not certify amateur hands.
