### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins the debate. Its causal story held up across every round: physics guarantees that someone sits closest to the matching engine, so a ban relocates the speed race to scarce private land rather than ending it. CON then compared that outcome with cable-equalized, regulated racks and showed the ban would be worse for mid-sized firms and for the brokers serving retail investors. PRO (B) retreated to the conflict-of-interest argument and conceded that off-site latency competition would persist. It never answered the comparative question, and its own Q1 effectively invited CON's answer. CON also directly contested the conflict-of-interest claim on two grounds, and PRO never refuted either: - Rule and fee changes require regulatory approval. - High-speed firms remain the largest volume clients even after a ban. PRO's later speeches were largely repetitive. It offered an unexplained "regulators can impose equal access on off-site providers" claim, which CON dismantled. PRO also leaned on loose figures like "billions extracted" and "tens of millions annually" without any mechanism to support them. CON progressed and narrowed the clash; PRO restated its opening.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's A4.3. It took PRO's fallback that regulators could impose equal access on off-site providers and showed the fallback fails either way. A firm buying the adjacent building for its own servers leaves no third-party provider to regulate. Forcing equalized racks off-site would simply recreate co-location with weaker oversight. That left PRO's concession of off-site competition fatal to its fairness case.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by making the decision rule comparative and then winning it. The ban does not erase proximity; once Side B conceded the speed race moves off-site, A showed that equalized, rentable racks plus broker access are fairer than a finite private land race, and that regulatory approval plus persistent volume clients mean the ban cures no exchange conflict while raising fees and widening spreads. B kept restating the public-utility conflict and never neutralized those mechanisms; its late claim that regulators could equalize off-site providers was speculative and was directly dismantled.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal, answering both pressure questions and showing that a firm buying the building next door leaves no third-party provider for regulators to equalize, so the ban trades regulated parity for a private latency monopoly.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON wins the comparative clash: regulated, cable-equalized co-location gives many brokers and market makers identical distance, while PRO concedes a ban merely moves the race off-site where finite adjacent land creates a private monopoly. PRO never squarely answers regulatory approval, the persistence of volume-based incentives, broker fee amortization, or wider spreads, relying instead on repeating the conflict-of-interest claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 decisively answered both pressure questions and demolished PRO's claim that regulators could impose equal access on off-site providers, noting regulators oversee exchanges not a giant's privately owned building.
