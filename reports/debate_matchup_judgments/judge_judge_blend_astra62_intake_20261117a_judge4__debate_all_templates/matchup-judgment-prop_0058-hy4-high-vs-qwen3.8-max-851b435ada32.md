### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO built a coherent causal story and kept it intact through the exchange. Capacity is a capital stock. Campaign-style speed produces false positives. The errors are asymmetric: leaving duplication costs bounded friction, while cutting a hidden dependency cripples a service, and freezes and buyouts remove the very people who could tell waste from muscle. CON's best counter was the charge of definitional escape, but PRO answered it adequately by drawing the line on method (cut before mapping versus after) rather than on outcome. CON also argued that the status quo causes harm too, but its examples stayed conditional. Phrases like "if pressure targets," "can," and "if a failed contract is cancelled" never established why rough, fast pressure in particular yields net gains. When pressed on who maps dependencies after senior staff leave, CON brought in process owners, legal review, exemptions and phased exits. PRO effectively recast these as the careful-audit safeguards DOGE-style drives displace. Both sides lacked hard evidence, and both stayed suitably cautious. PRO, however, kept narrowing the debate to a clear decision rule, while CON leaned on shifting the burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 asked who maps dependencies once buyouts and freezes remove senior staff. CON's A1 answered with process owners, legal staff, transaction logs, and "exemptions or phased exits." PRO's closing turned this into a concession: CON's safeguards are exactly the careful-audit apparatus that slash-first drives skip. That exchange weakened CON's claim that deadlines alone force good diagnosis.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): B wins the central causal clash: cut-first campaigns can remove the expertise needed to identify dependencies and repair mistakes, making service losses persistent. A establishes real recurring costs of duplication, but relies mainly on safeguarded reforms and does not justify treating capacity damage as temporary transition costs. B also explicitly allows successful drives to count, weakening A's claim that its test excludes successes by definition. However, B's overstatement that savings are one-off and the unresolved boundaries of “DOGE-style” keep this competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's challenge to A's “one-time transition costs” was decisive: rework, appeals, contractor substitution, and lost institutional knowledge can persist. A accepted metrics that would count these harms but never adequately defended its subsequent claim that mistakes are merely bounded, correctable transitions.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side A wins because it kept the motion pinned to slash-first DOGE pressure and showed that Side B could make speed safe only by importing mapping, exemptions, phased exits, and pre-cut review. Side B landed a real status-quo and burden challenge, but its positive examples were ordinary reforms, and its answers never neutralized the false-positive asymmetry or the loss of the people who can tell waste from capacity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal and closing were decisive: after Side B answered that process owners would map dependencies before cuts and protect critical roles, Side A showed that those safeguards are the careful audit Side B had been borrowing, not a defense of campaign-style cuts.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): This was a close debate, and PRO had real strengths. Its incentive argument was its best material: savings-first speed rewards the announced cut rather than the later repair. The asymmetry it drew between visible, reversible savings and diffuse, compounding capacity loss is intuitive. The bait-and-switch charge against CON's safeguarded model also landed partially. CON narrowed the clash more effectively, though. It shifted the burden to "characteristic net damage" and insisted that the no-drive side of the ledger also compounds: queues, duplicate entry, and locked-in bad data. It then argued that central deadlines plus a reversed default ("justify this line or stop paying") remain DOGE-style even with frontline exemptions. PRO's answer to CON's ledger question was thin. It said status-quo waste is "addressable" without explaining why bureaus have not addressed it. That concession left CON's baseline-cost point largely standing. PRO's closing also overstated CON's admission. CON said nothing quickly replaces statutory knowledge, but paired that with a no-departure mechanism. PRO treated it as a concession that the knowledge would be lost. Both sides relied on unsupported assertions about what is typically targeted: payroll for PRO, dormant leases and duplicate systems for CON. CON's line-by-line responses and direct A1/A2 answers made its route cleaner, which gives it a narrow edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.5–B2.6 was the most decisive exchange. CON argued that PRO's irreversibility argument works only if every cancelled line is an expert adjudicator. It pointed out that dormant grants, empty leases and duplicate forms carry no institutional memory ("PRO makes every report a benefits examiner"). That forced PRO to rely on the claim that incentives push cuts toward payroll, a claim PRO asserted but never fully substantiated.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): B provided the stronger causal account: savings-first incentives favor immediately countable cuts while service failures and replacement costs emerge later. A effectively established that recurring waste also damages capacity, but its defense ultimately depended on safeguards it proposed rather than established as characteristic. Showing that centralized speed can coexist with safeguards did not sufficiently answer B’s argument about which behavior the incentives favor. Neither side firmly established magnitudes, keeping this competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s challenge to A’s claim that bad cuts are readily correctable forced an important concession: lost statutory expertise cannot quickly be replaced. A responded directly with prevention measures, but that shifted its defense onto the disputed reliability of safeguards rather than resolving the irreversibility objection.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): A wins because it kept the motion tied to what DOGE-style drives characteristically are: fast, savings-first campaigns that book visible cuts before consequences. B’s recurring-waste ledger and characteristic-versus-abuse distinction stayed live, but B never neutralized the incentive to reach for payroll and contracts, and its growing no-cut lists, signoffs, and rollback rules looked like a different model. A also used B’s concession that statutory knowledge is not quickly replaceable to make capacity loss the harder, less reversible cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s bait-and-switch charge in Rebuttal 1, pressed through the pressure questions: B answered that expertise would not depart, which conceded irreplaceability while assuming the very safeguards that define the drive out of the motion.
