### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined, honest, and free of fake precision, but Side B controlled the decisive clash. B's core structural argument — the asymmetry between pre-connection flexibility (phasing, alternative sites, smaller initial supply) and post-connection lock-in (sunk overhaul costs, installed machinery, payroll pressure making each renewal individually rational even when the whole sequence would never have been bought) — was never fully neutralized by A's procedural answers of "fresh authorization" and "no automatic renewal." A's best moment was extracting B's concession that extensions should be granted whenever expected gains exceed full costs, which A framed in closing as conceding the motion. But B's closing answered squarely: a neutral cost-benefit test decides resolved cases and requires no manufacturing priority; the motion only matters when the comparison is unresolved, and there the sequencing asymmetry favors phasing rather than letting a factory opening dictate a retirement date. A answered B's pressure questions honestly (the blunt "No" on authorizing overhauls without departure evidence) but this narrowed A's case without damaging B's. B's pressure questions were sharper and exposed the ratchet problem A only partially addressed. Both complied with format and used plausible causal reasoning throughout; B made the debate easier to decide and held the cleaner burden story on the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal (B4.3–B4.5): the observation that each fresh extension authorization can look reasonable on its own terms even when India would never have bought the whole sequence at the outset — combined with the point that before connection a second Indian site can compete for the factory, but after connection the obligation to serve it is no longer speculative. This sequencing asymmetry survived A's procedural safeguards and anchored B's closing.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): In a disciplined, high-level exchange, Side A (CON) wins by dismantling both halves of Side B's "temporary coal bridge" case: the size of the lost-manufacturing counterfactual and the controllability of limited coal extensions. Side B smartly seized on Side A's [A4.1] concession to extend when net benefits are proven, but Side A rightly answered that unweighted cost-benefit gives manufacturing no priority and won the decisive battleground: under realistic uncertainty, sequential sunk-cost ratcheting justifies pacing connections rather than delaying coal retirement.

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): This was a tight, sophisticated exchange between two evenly matched sides. Side A built the more vivid case (the machine shop, the evening shift, the buyer who never reopens bidding) and won the stopping-rule clash with the permit cap and hard outer date in Rebuttal 2, which Side B candidly conceded was a serious answer. But Side B won the decisive meta-clash: by extracting Side A's honest concession that extensions should be refused when full social costs exceed the industrial benefit (A's answer to B's Q1), Side B showed that A's position collapses into a net-benefit test plus a "planning instruction," and that the manufacturing-first hierarchy adds bias toward bridges without adding welfare. Side B's B4.6 counter — that a permit's expiry does not reverse illness or emissions, so both choices create lasting losses — effectively neutralized A's core irreversibility asymmetry, and the closing crystallized the cleanest voting reason: "Reject the hierarchy, not manufacturing." - **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A won a high-level debate by forcing Side B's case to collapse from a manufacturing-first priority into a system of capped, welfare-screened exceptions. However, Side A's pressure on uncompensated health and climate harms and late replacement projects forced Side B in Rebuttal 2 to concede both a full social-cost test (rejecting extensions when harms exceed industrial value) and hard permit caps that shut coal units down even if jobs remain threatened. Side A also neutralized Side B's claim that coal bridges are temporary while lost contracts are permanent by noting that illness and cumulative carbon emissions persist after permits expire, and cleanly dismantled Side B's final framing by showing that needing a predictable answer before bidding determines when planners must decide, not that rapid manufacturing should outrank coal retirement.
