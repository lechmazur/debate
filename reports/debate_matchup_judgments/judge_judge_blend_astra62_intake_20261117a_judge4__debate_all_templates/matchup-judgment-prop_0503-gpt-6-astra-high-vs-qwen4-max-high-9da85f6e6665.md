### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins because it built a concrete, layered case and kept adapting it. Its opening offered a costly-signal test: VAT, subsidy cuts and legal reforms create domestic losers and buy no applause. It backed that with real operating exporters such as aluminium, Ma'aden fertiliser, DP World and ACWA Power. In rebuttal it turned CON's own "independent revenue" test against CON by pointing to foreign buyers who owe Riyadh nothing. It also exposed CON's criterion as absorbing every fact. CON (A) was disciplined and honest. It answered the threshold question cleanly and admitted it was not claiming below-cost sales. Its "direction versus motion" and "net of public support" distinctions were genuinely sharp. But CON offered almost no affirmative evidence that diversification is failing or is mostly branding. Its case was entirely burden-shifting. CON also conceded a great deal along the way: - real ports and factories; - a genuine commercial hub; - that some efforts are already substantive; - that unfinished does not mean fake. Those concessions weaken a CON position on a motion framed as "substantive and likely to succeed rather than mostly PR." PRO's trajectory evidence (VAT tripling, corporate tax, the tourism target reached early, rising industrial input prices) was plausible and directly responsive. CON's reply that VAT just taxes oil-funded consumption was clever but only partial. The debate was competitive, but PRO made it easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Rebuttal 1 (B2.1–B2.4). It accepted CON's revenue-engine test and showed that foreign customers in India, Brazil and Uzbekistan already satisfy it. It then charged that CON's criterion was immune to evidence and demanded a falsification threshold. CON's A4.1 threshold answer was respectable. But by then CON had conceded substance in particular sectors, leaving only an unproven-forecast argument.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A wins a high-level debate by pairing rich, multi-sector empirical grounding with stronger causal forecasting across the full proposition. Side B mounted a sharp political-economy critique—distinguishing oil-recycled domestic GDP and subsidized firm profits from net sovereign resilience, and rightly countering A's late claim that disappointing returns fall outside "likely to succeed." However, Side B offered zero concrete examples of failure, conceded extensive real substance (Dubai's hub, global ports, aluminium, fertilisers, pilgrimage, minerals), and relied almost entirely on defensive "does not establish" burden-pushing that would disqualify any ongoing industrial transition. Side A met each filter B erected—pointing to politically painful VAT and corporate taxes, unrigged foreign export markets, rising industrial feedstock prices, and capital reallocation away from NEOM—making a much cleaner affirmative case for probable success over PR.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: When Side B argued [B1.4] that non-oil GDP recycles domestic oil salaries and demanded competitive exports, Side A [A2.1, A2.5] answered with Gulf aluminium, Ma'aden fertilisers, DP World, ACWA Power, and Saudi geology/pilgrimage winning foreign revenue—and after B pivoted to input subsidies [B2.2], A [A4.1] showed states are actively raising industrial feedstock prices to narrow subsidies and test viability.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by separating real sectoral successes from the proposition's economy-wide forecast of likely success. A showed reforms, exporters, and operating hubs, but B repeatedly showed these do not establish competitive earnings at sufficient scale, net of public support, to sustain employment and revenue as oil weakens. B also answered the threshold challenge with a measurable direction-of-dependence test.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal was decisive: VAT and higher administered prices may stretch oil-supported consumption or test industries, but do not yet prove independent non-oil earnings are gaining on public obligations.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins a clear but competitive debate. It turned PRO's own "who pays" test against PRO by tracing ostensibly non-oil demand back to oil-funded state budgets. It then held PRO to its own standard of a "material, durable reduction in dependence." PRO's best points were sound but never produced a trend showing decoupling: - Oil-funded origins do not negate later commercially earned revenue. - Dubai's 2009 rescue reflects a credit crisis rather than a diversification failure. - CON's final test conflates state withdrawal with diversification. CON's pressure questions worked. PRO admitted it could not name a large project cancelled for lack of paying customers. PRO's threshold answer then softened into "credible trajectory," which CON plausibly framed as standard-shifting. CON also landed sharp narrower hits: - Pilgrimage tourism predates oil, so it is not new earning capacity. - Transition spending that raises fiscal breakeven prices makes the economy more oil-contingent during the build-out. - "Addition is not substitution." CON's claim that sovereign ownership equals dependence is contestable, and PRO answered it reasonably in closing. That answer came late, however, and did not supply the missing evidence of a weakening oil link. Grounding was comparable on both sides: CON's NEOM point was hedged with "reportedly," and the Dubai 2009 rescue is ordinary background knowledge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's A2.3 against PRO's B2.3. PRO argued that transition investment raises today's fiscal breakeven price while building tomorrow's earning capacity. CON reframed this as a diversification program that depends on decades of high oil prices and could stall mid-build in a slump: "the progress being contingent on the thing it must escape." PRO's later answer, that operating assets survive price slumps, was reasonable but never showed any trend of reduced dependence. CON's pressure questions then extracted PRO's concession that it could not name a project killed by market discipline.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side B won a high-level debate by proving that adding real non-oil assets is not the same as achieving a material, durable reduction in hydrocarbon dependence. Side A made smart analytical distinctions—separating temporary capex vulnerability from post-build earning capacity and distinguishing sovereign ownership from oil dependence—while displaying admirable epistemic honesty. However, Side B dismantled Side A's Gulf-wide generalization beyond first-mover Dubai, neutralized Side A's UAE tax evidence by showing how VAT and corporate taxes recycle oil-funded spending, exposed the absence of capital-cancellation discipline on state projects, and tracked the steady retreat of Side A's standard from durable fiscal decoupling to mere customer addition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B3.2] and [B4.3], Side B pressed Side A to name any large state-directed project cancelled because customers would not pay, then used Side A's honest concession in [A4.2] to neutralize Side A's external-customer test ([A3.2]) with a razor-sharp distinction: "Customers filter revenue; they do not filter capital," showing that sovereigns keep funding overbuilt capacity and absorbing losses out of oil treasuries even when bookings lag.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side B wins by forcing the decisive distinction between adding non-oil activity and actually weakening oil dependence. Side A established real externally-earning sectors, but never supplied a threshold, trend, or full-cycle test for its promised material, durable reduction. Its concessions on cancelled projects and oil-derived taxation, plus the shift from durable reduction to credible trajectory to other customers mattering, left B's investor-customer-insurer critique and Dubai 2009 example largely intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: customers filter revenue but not sovereign capital, and Side A's inability to name a market-cancelled flagship project exposed the missing discipline behind its market-filter claim.
