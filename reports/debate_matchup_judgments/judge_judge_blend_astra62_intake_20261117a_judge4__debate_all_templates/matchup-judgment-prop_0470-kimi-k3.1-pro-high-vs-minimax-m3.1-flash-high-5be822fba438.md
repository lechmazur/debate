### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): This was a tight, well-matched exchange. Side B (PRO) built a strong case on irreversibility of election-eve counterfeit evidence, the liar's dividend, and a well-bounded enforcement test (fabrication + falsity + knowing pass-off as authentic, ambiguity defeats liability). But Side A (CON) landed the sharpest comparative blow: once PRO's safeguards require proving fabrication, falsity, and knowing deception, the "synthetic" element adds little beyond existing impersonation/forgery/fraud law, while creating a new content category officials and platforms can police. PRO's reply that the ban adds "clarity, not new speech power" was honest but self-undercutting—it concedes marginal benefit while the motion demands justifying the ban "even at some cost." CON also answered the timing clash by arguing that swift notice-based takedowns and prosecution of knowing distributors work regardless of how the content was made. CON's clipped first rebuttal and slightly less polished framing kept this from being a clear win, but CON held the burden game: PRO never showed why the medium, rather than the deception, requires categorical prohibition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange ran from B2.6 through A5.1: PRO turned CON's endorsement of "penalties for impersonation and forgery" into a concession of the ban; CON then reversed it by showing PRO's own liability safeguards collapse the ban into ordinary fraud law, crystallized as "punish the election thief, not the medium."
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): B wins by more successfully defending a bounded prohibition on knowingly distributing counterfeit political evidence, while acknowledging its costs to deception-dependent satire. B directly answered the questions about cropped context, culpability, and malicious complaints. A repeatedly attacked a broader technology ban and treated contextual judgment as disqualifying despite accepting impersonation and forgery laws. A’s strongest surviving objection was that B insufficiently established an enforcement advantage over those alternatives; this keeps the result competitive. A’s clipped first rebuttal adds a small execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest rebuttal exposed the tension between A’s endorsement of forgery penalties and its claim that contextual judgments make deepfake prohibitions unworkable. Its distinction between the original satirist and someone knowingly stripping context also directly weakened A’s cropped-clip objection.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A built a remarkably consistent, legally grounded case from Opening through Closing and weighed the proposition's exact tradeoff more convincingly. ### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a closely fought, high-quality debate. CON's core route—PRO's ban punishes believability itself while PRO's own hardest examples (election-official announcement, boil-water order) fit narrower act-specific liability—survived the exchange. A2.5 and A4.2 repeatedly turned PRO's examples into evidence for CON, and A4.2's observation that realism does not track the harm (a crude fake can panic; flawless satire cannot) was decisive. A4.5's symmetry move—judicial review after removal cannot restore the audience, the same timing objection PRO pressed against corrections—damaged PRO's enforcement story. PRO performed well: honest concession of the currency analogy, a crisp four-element test in B4.1, and direct A1/A2 answers, but the "conspicuous cues" and "ordinary viewer" standards remained vulnerable to the vagueness attack, and the liar's-dividend argument arrived only in the closing as a new angle, too late to be developed (though A5.1 answered it reasonably). PRO's Rebuttal 1 was also clipped for exceeding the word limit. CON's closing crystallized the clash cleanly: "uncertain confidence in authentic evidence at the price of certain discretion over convincing criticism."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The B3.2/A4.2 exchange over unnamed harms: PRO's boil-water emergency example was met by CON's twin points that it fits a narrow false-emergency-directive prohibition and that realism is not the harm marker, converting PRO's best new case into support for act-specific remedies.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): A made the stronger comparative case: B’s concrete examples supported narrow restrictions on election interference and false emergency directives without establishing the additional value of its broader impersonation ban. B clarified its distribution-context test, but did not adequately resolve chilling effects or practical anonymity burdens. A answered the pressure directly and consistently distinguished necessary intervention against wrongful conduct from restrictions on realistic political expression. B’s closing appeal to public trust did not establish that prohibition would deliver that benefit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A turned B’s timing argument against its safeguards: judicial review cannot restore satire’s lost audience, just as corrections cannot undo a timely deepfake’s impact. Together with A’s act-specific answer to the emergency example, this substantially weakened B’s justification for accepting broader speech costs.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B won decisively by isolating and defeating the surplus scope of Side A's proposed ban across every round. Whenever Side A offered concrete urgent harms—a fake election-result announcement [A2.6] or a fabricated boil-water emergency order [A4.3]—Side B showed they were discrete wrongful acts reachable through swift, act-specific prohibitions without licensing officials and platforms to police realism across political speech [B2.5, B4.2]. Side B also turned Side A's timing logic against itself, noting that post-removal judicial review [A4.2] fails to restore a satirist's audience during the decisive moment just as late corrections fail [B4.5], while requiring conspicuous fictional cues directly chills deadpan satire and burdens anonymous speakers during investigations [B2.4, B4.5]. When Side A pivoted in closing to the liar's dividend [A5.1], Side B cleanly noted that plausible deniability stems from public awareness that synthetic tech and foreign actors exist, which a domestic ban cannot erase [B5.1]. Side A also paid execution costs for a clipped first rebuttal and a muddled Q2 [A3.2] that effectively asked CON to argue PRO's burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side B challenged Side A in [B3.2] to name a concrete deepfake harm outside fraud, defamation, threats, or election interference, Side A offered a fabricated official boil-water order [A4.3]. In [B4.2], Side B dismantled this on two levels: false emergency directives already fit a narrow act-specific prohibition on imminent public-safety deception, and hyper-realism is not even the operative harm since a crude fake order can spark panic while flawless satire cannot—turning Side A's best new example into support for targeting wrongful acts rather than realistic artifacts.
