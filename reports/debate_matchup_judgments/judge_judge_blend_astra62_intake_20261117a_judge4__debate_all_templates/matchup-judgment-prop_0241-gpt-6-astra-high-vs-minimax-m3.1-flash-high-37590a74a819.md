### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins a competitive debate by holding the motion's real decision point: what should prevail when the two aims conflict. PRO separated limits on state power from punishment's governing aim, accepted retribution as a genuine reason, and gave a concrete conflict rule. That rule is to keep proportionate accountability but forgo otherwise deserved hardship when it would credibly obstruct rehabilitation, and it is justified by preventing future victims. PRO answered both pressure questions directly. CON (A) had real strengths: the opening dilemma about whether retribution is an aim or a constraint, the warning about net-widening, and the terminally ill prisoner, who shows that retribution can justify punishment where rehabilitation cannot. But CON's A2 answer weakened its own clash by conceding that retribution demands nothing further once a sanction is proportionate and visible. CON then called disrupted addiction treatment mere "convenience," which PRO credibly turned into a public-safety stake. CON's closing dilemma largely restates that PRO waives retribution at the point of conflict, which is exactly what priority means. The terminal-illness case shows retribution is still relevant but does not establish that it outranks rehabilitation when the two collide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B4.2–B4.4 and B5.2 decided the debate. In answer to CON's demand for a rule, PRO stated one in which an otherwise deserved burden can actually be forgone, so retribution yields at the point of conflict rather than receiving treatment as an add-on. PRO then exposed CON's "convenience" framing as dodging the stake of protecting another potential victim. This neutralized CON's claim that PRO's priority was merely retribution with an exception.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B built a clear, persuasive model of comparative priority: preserving proportionate limits and visible accountability while forgoing additional retributive hardship when it credibly undermines rehabilitation and increases the risk of future victims. Side B cleanly dismantled Side A's false dilemma by explaining that genuine moral reasons can be outweighed without vanishing, won a key concession via its due-process analogy and Q1 that justifying who may be punished does not determine punishment's primary aim, and neutralized Side A's terminally ill prisoner example as proving retribution's continuing relevance rather than superiority. Side A offered smart philosophical pushback on universality and net-widening, but misstated the polarity of its own Q1, begged the question on why satisfying a retributive "deficit" matters more than preventing future victimization, and unpersuasively dismissed disrupted addiction treatment and employment as merely the offender's "convenience."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's trap from Q2 [B3.2] through [B4.4] and [B5.2]: when pressed on why extra retributive hardship should override preventing future harm once a sentence visibly acknowledges the wrong, Side A relied on a circular appeal to a retributive "deficit" and called disrupted addiction treatment and work mere offender "convenience" [A4.3], allowing Side B in [B5.2] to expose that Side A had evaded the core public-safety stake.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins clearly. Its paired hypotheticals, the fully reformed assailant and the petty thief with deep behavioral problems, became the axis of the whole debate. A also set a sharp decision rule for "more important": which aim should prevail when the two diverge. PRO (B) had one genuinely good line. Retribution only sets a range, and rehabilitation decides among sentences inside it. But A absorbed it: choosing the better means of delivering accountability does not make accountability secondary. PRO weakened its own case in three ways: - Its second pressure question asked, in effect, why rehabilitation is more important once reform is complete. That invited the answer CON wanted. - In Rebuttal 2 it made real concessions. Retribution "may do most justificatory work" after reform. Deserved punishment for equally culpable offenders "should ordinarily be equal." Retribution is an "indispensable constraint" governing legitimacy. - It cited a nonexistent reference (C2.7). PRO's late reliance on "prospective public protection" folded incapacitation into rehabilitation. CON rightly called that a conflation. CON also answered the frequency argument by separating how often an aim guides choices from how much authority it carries. PRO's closing then called retribution the criterion of legitimacy, which tilts the importance comparison toward CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange is A4.1–A4.5. CON answers both pressure questions directly. It then turns PRO's concession on equal deserved burdens against PRO's claim that rehabilitation determines punishment. It separates rehabilitation from deterrence and incapacitation, which defeats PRO's "public protection" move. Finally, it reframes PRO's frequency argument as confusing how often an aim guides choices with how much authority it has. Together these left PRO's main route, rehabilitation as the design-governing aim, resting on a concession that retribution governs legitimacy.
