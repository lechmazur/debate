### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B won because it kept the decisive burden on PRO: showing that the marginal higher-risk borrower excluded by a cap is worse off with the card than without it. PRO powerfully described debt traps and forced a genuine tension over why high APRs are needed if short bridges yield little interest. But B's pooled-risk answer dissolved that tension: high rates need not require each borrower to revolve; they cover portfolio defaults across balances and time. B also exposed PRO's post-denial alternatives as unreliable, argued persuasively that a missed shift can cascade, and distinguished an extreme 100% APR from the disputed cap line. PRO proved some cards are dangerous, but not that the marginal credit line erased by a cap is a trap rather than a bridge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pooled-risk rebuttal was decisive: it showed A's central contradiction fails because high APRs can price pooled subprime losses without assuming every marginal bridge user will revolve for years, forcing PRO back onto the worst-case revolver rather than the applicant at the cutoff.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): A narrowly wins the proposition as written. No ceiling was specified, and B accepted banning 100% APR cards despite their potential bridge value and resulting exclusion. A explicitly crystallized this concession; B's distinction between that ceiling and “this cap” never established a lower proposed ceiling as the exclusive question. B otherwise argued better on pooled risk and cascading post-denial harms. A's faulty arithmetic, sweeping assumptions, and nonexistent references make this a narrow victory on the surviving principle-level argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's 100% APR question exposed that access loss and imperfect borrower selection cannot categorically defeat a cap. B effectively rebutted A's inference from pooled pricing to inevitable debt traps, but its concession left a legal ceiling justified somewhere—the proposition's unresolved decisive issue.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the decisive burden on the marginal higher-risk applicant: the cap’s access loss is stipulated, while A’s benefit relied too heavily on worst-case revolvers. B’s pooled-risk explanation cleanly answered A’s alleged contradiction and preserved the point that a short bridge can help even if some users spiral.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttal to A’s “brief bridges cannot cover defaults” pressure: B explained risk pooling and APR pricing across the portfolio, blunting A’s claim that uncapped rates must depend on every marginal borrower becoming trapped.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B wins a clear but competitive debate by turning PRO's principled enforceability argument into a practical problem. PRO conceded that denial could leave high-risk borrowers facing job loss, eviction, or title debt that hurt more than a 30% APR, yet still favored the cap. CON used that concession well: since courts also enforce those harsher downstream outcomes, the cap does not keep the sovereign's hands clean. CON further offered a live alternative of structural reforms to stop compounding while preserving need-based access and risk pricing. PRO's limiting principle remained sharp but too abstract when weighed against the concrete rationing harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: after PRO admitted denied borrowers could face worse harms than a 30% APR, CON showed that eviction, utility, and title-seizure enforcement are also state-backed, turning PRO's moral-purity line into a practical failure rather than a clean exit.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B supplied the stronger comparative case: denied credit can produce harms exceeding interest costs, including other state-enforced debts. A answered pressure directly but never adequately justified why refusing enforcement of high-rate debt outweighed those consequences. Its repeated appeal to stipulated rationing confused accepting the tradeoff with resolving it. B also offered targeted protections against A's identified abuses. B's sweeping claims about competition and universal starter-card exclusion weaken its grounding, but its central welfare comparison and rebuttal of A's enforcement distinction remained decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: When A dismissed the $25 example as a one-month advertisement, B extended the comparison to repayment over a year or two and weighed interest against losing employment. B then directly undermined A's distinctive moral rationale: refusing a card creditor can leave the family facing court-enforced eviction rather than freedom from state collection power.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): CON won narrowly by turning PRO’s “rejection does not compound” frame into a welfare clash: unfinanced emergencies and state-enforced eviction, utility, or title harms can outweigh a finite risk-priced bridge. CON also supplied a limiting principle—risk-based pricing plus fee, minimum-payment, garnishment, and bankruptcy rules—which blunted PRO’s exploitation argument. PRO’s sovereignty frame was elegant and direct, but its concession that rejected households could be worse off in immediate liquidity left CON’s practical harm route live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s response to PRO’s clean-hands distinction: refusal does not avoid state coercion, because eviction orders, utility judgments, and title seizures are also court-enforced and can harm more than a bounded card balance.
