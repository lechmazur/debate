### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): This was a close, high-quality exchange. CON built real pressure on three fronts. First, a selection problem: the loneliest students are the least likely to join a club or stay in one. Second, clubs can create hierarchies and hazing of their own. Third, literacy campaigns reach more students at lower cost. CON's sharpest move was saying that PRO's mural example had "funded away every hard part." CON's closing then framed the debate memorably: harm is settled by peers and crowds outside the funded hour. PRO adapted well and narrowed the clash. It designed clubs around access (nonselective, supervised, fee-waived) to blunt the selection and hazing objections. It also kept pressing a distinction CON never fully dissolved: hearing advice is different from having the relationships and trusted adults needed to act on it. PRO's Q1 exposed a real double standard. CON credited a lesson with invitations classmates might make, yet discounted shared activity unless it produced friendship outside the room. CON's answer, that literacy "moves the withholders," was reasonable but optimistic. It also stretched "social-media literacy" into general anti-exclusion norm-setting. The motion's word "alone" helps PRO, since PRO explicitly kept literacy as a complement. CON's answer on staff time (existing teachers and marginal reinforcement) was solid, but it leaned on the assumption that a taught norm sticks without sustained relational infrastructure. PRO's point that an expected weekly presence counts against loneliness now was not fully neutralized; CON's "tolerated, not wanted" reply was speculative. On balance, PRO's causal story was more plausible and better defended, while CON had the slightly better rhetoric and the stronger reach and scale argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 and its follow-up in Rebuttal 2, "Apply the same standard," identified the asymmetry in how CON credited literacy-driven invitations while discounting club-driven contact. CON answered credibly by redirecting to peer reach, but it never fully closed the gap between persuading bystanders and providing a trusted relationship to act through.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B wins a close but real decision. Side A made a vivid case that clubs create recurring contact, trusted adults, and a place to act on good advice. But Side B more effectively exposed the reach problem: the loneliest students are least likely to join or stay, and clubs can harden exclusion unless they are already shaped by adult-enforced norms. Side B also converted Side A’s safeguard into support for its own case: supervision is literacy carried into the room, and that portable norm travels to corridors, chats, and crowds where no club leader stands. Side A answered pressure directly with outreach and nonselective design, but still left the decisive burden that the isolated student must keep returning and be chosen. Side B’s comparative case was cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was Side B’s rebuttal of the mural example: every hard part had been funded away, and adult rules against mocking and cruelty were really literacy carried into the room. That turned Side A’s strongest policy fix into evidence for Side B’s universality and cost objection.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A better carried the comparative burden. Clubs can build recurring contact and trusted adults, but A showed those gains still depend on opt-in, local staffing, and peer choice outside the funded hour, while literacy more directly and cheaply reaches the bystanders and settings that decide exclusion, pile-ons, and escalation. B’s outreach and “fund the missing piece” replies were serious but left the coverage and backfire problems live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal: literacy moves the peers who withhold invitations at a lower threshold and in settings they already occupy, while even proactive club outreach still requires the isolated student to return and be genuinely chosen.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): PRO built an intuitive and well-written causal story: belonging, dosage, supervised after-school hours, and adults who notice withdrawal. Its answers in Rebuttal 2 were solid, especially the claim that a coach's enforced code makes cruelty costly and gives bystanders cover. CON, however, adapted more precisely to the motion's comparative burden. It held the debate to harm prevented per marginal public dollar and pressed two problems PRO never fully closed. First, funded hours may go mainly to teens who would not otherwise be isolated. Second, peer behavior reaches the target even when she attends no club and trusts no adult. CON also recast literacy as bystander behavior change rather than a lecture on information. That neutralized PRO's opening framing of literacy as mere awareness about feeds. CON answered PRO's pressure questions directly and honestly, conceding that "if nobody reaches out, the lesson won't fill his week." In closing, CON turned PRO's own "somebody expects you" theme toward the seat saved at lunch. PRO's closing largely restated its belonging thesis without resolving the additionality and non-attendee reach problems. Because PRO carried the burden to show clubs are the stronger tool, CON's sustained doubt about comparative reach gives it a narrow edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q2 asked what happens when the teammate whose approval matters most is the one circulating the humiliating clip. This forced PRO to concede that teams are not automatically kind and to rest its case on coach-enforced codes. CON then answered in A4.4 that such codes only work if the coach finds out. When the chat stays private and the target fears reporting, prevention still turns on bystanders. That exchange narrowed PRO's main advantage and decided the clash.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B built the cleaner comparative path. A made a strong causal case that clubs create belonging, repeated supervised contact, and adult authority, but B successfully shifted the dispute to harm prevented per public dollar and to the bystanders already deciding whether exclusion or violence spreads. B's access/cost rebuttal was especially damaging: late buses and school-based clubs may not actually reach the isolated teen, while a well-run campaign can shape the peers who circulate a clip or invite someone to lunch even if the target never attends. A's core mechanism remained persuasive on loneliness, but it did not adequately answer B's showings about self-selection, private chats, and next-dollar reach. B also answered A's pressure questions more concretely with low-risk bystander actions and a direct invitation mechanism, leaving the cleaner live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal undercut A's access claim: late buses make attendance plausible but do not show that funded slots reach otherwise isolated youth, and an invitation addressed to a lonely student may matter more than filling a roster with already connected peers.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that funded clubs supply the people, schedules, adult authority, and status costs that loneliness removes and that literacy scripts alone cannot reliably create. B answered the attendance and team-loyalty pressures more directly than A neutralized the empty-evening and missing-relationship problems, especially by tying coach-enforced consequences to bystander cover. A's reach and bystander points stayed live but never outweighed the comparative mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's A2, which answered the circulating-teammate objection by showing that funding buys a coach who can make humiliation costly and give other players institutional cover rather than mere loyalty to the chat.
