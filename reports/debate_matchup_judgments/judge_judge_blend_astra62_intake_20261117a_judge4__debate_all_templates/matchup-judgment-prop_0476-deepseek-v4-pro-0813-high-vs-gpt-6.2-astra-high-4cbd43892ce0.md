### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins a clear but competitive debate. It recast the motion as a comparison between a bond and enforced licensing conditions, not a comparison between a bond and voluntary guidelines. It then built a sharp expected-value critique. At the moment an operator decides whether to cut margins or delay retirement, the bond's added deterrent is only the deposit times the increase in failure probability. That means a bond is either too small to deter or large enough to impose real financing costs on safe operators. CON also offered a concrete alternative: a verified reserve trigger that ends paid service before the gamble is taken. That alternative targets the revenue motive directly. PRO's strongest, and still live, point is that a bond survives bankruptcy and loss of command, when license conditions lose their force. CON honestly conceded that the bond wins on collection. It then limited the value of that concession, arguing that collected money funds tracking, not removal, and that certainty of collection is not certainty of prevention. PRO's answers to the pressure questions were weaker: - "Calibrate to expected harm" did not escape CON's arithmetic. - The claim that forfeit is "near-certain" for abandonment projected certainty backward onto the decision point, and CON exposed this. - Offering exemptions or near-zero bonds partly conceded the cost objection. PRO's closing largely repeated its bankruptcy point without resolving the deterrence math.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.3–A4.4 answer was the decisive exchange. It showed that PRO's "near-certain forfeiture" applies only after abandonment, not when managers choose whether to cut a margin. At that point, the bond's added incentive is the deposit times a small change in probability. This undercut PRO's central deterrence mechanism, and PRO never squarely answered it.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A won solidly by establishing a credible regulatory counterfactual—pre-launch hardware verification plus revoking commercial service authorization when telemetry hits a fuel-reserve trigger—and locking Side B into an inescapable bond-sizing dilemma. Side A proved that because operators recover the entire deposit if a gamble on thinner margins or skipped redundancy still succeeds, a small bond adds negligible expected-value deterrence, while a bond large enough to change that calculus imposes years of non-refundable surety fees on compliant operators. Side A also honestly conceded Side B's strongest point—that bonds collect money after bankruptcy or command loss—and convincingly showed that collecting modest tracking funds in insolvency neither retires dead spacecraft nor justifies making credit access a condition of flight.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's critique across [A2.1], [A3.1], and [A4.3]-[A4.4] dismantling Side B's claim in [B2.3] and [B2.6] that a small bond deters shortcuts because forfeiture is "certain." Side A showed that forfeiture is only certain after abandonment; when deciding whether to skip a backup or dip into fuel reserves for extra revenue, the operator only faces the incremental failure probability times the deposit, whereas cutting off service permission at a reserve trigger removes the entire revenue prize for gambling.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins because it kept a live, decisive route: a posted bond remains enforceable after insolvency, loss of command, or evasion, while license conditions may lose leverage exactly when dereliction risk materializes. CON’s expected-cost and reserve-trigger arguments were sharp, but they never fully neutralized PRO’s automatic, bankruptcy-proof enforcement advantage, and PRO’s calibration/exemption answers blunted the burden objections enough.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Q1 and follow-up framing exposed that CON could not guarantee actual retirement or meaningful payment once a license condition became unenforceable, letting PRO make the bond’s automatic consequence the central advantage.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO wins. Its core mechanism survived the full exchange: a bond changes the incentives of the party that privately knows its fuel margin and revenue, and it still works on a last mission, when the threat of losing a future license has no force. CON's strongest material was the pricing problem and the charge that the bond duplicates direct enforcement. PRO answered both. On pricing, it argued for hazard-based bands and said a bond only needs to tip a close decision, not outweigh a whole month's revenue. On the desperate final burn, it made release of the bond conditional on regulator authorization, so an unauthorized gamble earns no refund. CON's final dilemma ("redundant where observable, ineffective where not") sounds sharp but misdescribes PRO's claim. PRO never said the bond improves the regulator's view. It said the bond works precisely because the operator acts on private information the regulator lacks. CON's closing repeated that an operator would still gamble, without addressing the authorization condition. CON never squarely answered the last-mission gap. Its small-operator and pass-through points were reasonable but secondary. PRO was also more original and more economical in its framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's last-mission argument against CON's alternative of direct license enforcement, combined with PRO's A1/A2 answers and closing. CON's best attempt to neutralize it was the observability dilemma. PRO showed the bond operates in the gap between what a regulator can approve and what the operator privately knows: an extension can be compliant yet imprudent, and the bond makes the operator weigh that choice itself. CON never directly answered this.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built a much more resilient economic and institutional case and won every major clash by the end of the debate. Side B showed that threatening future launch licenses fails on an operator's final mission or when a firm folds, that regulators can set graduated bonds based on orbital hazard while leaving the privately informed operator to weigh marginal service revenue against forfeiture risk, and that pre-burn authorization stops desperate maneuvers while keeping the financial incentive focused on conserving fuel earlier. Side A (CON) pushed smartly in the middle rounds, but staked the debate on a flawed closing dilemma—claiming a bond is redundant if regulators can observe fuel margins and ineffective if they cannot—which overlooked that regulators can easily verify ex-post disposal outcomes while the bond compels operators to act ex-ante on their own private knowledge of narrowing margins.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: When Side A argued in [A3.2] and [A4.3]-[A4.6] that fuel-state observability either makes direct standards sufficient or makes bond verification impossible, Side B cleanly dismantled both halves in [B4.3]-[B4.4] and [B5.1]-[B5.2]: regulators can verify ex-post whether a craft reached its disposal orbit without seeing internal fuel margins months earlier, and denying a late burn cannot restore fuel already spent selling extra service—precisely the gap where putting the operator's refund at stake changes earlier decisions.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): B wins narrowly because its incentive case remained live: a hazard-priced bond can change operator decisions before disposal, especially on a last mission and where regulators can verify final retirement but not private margin erosion. A’s redundancy/ineffectiveness attack was sharp, but B answered it by separating early observability from ex ante financial incentives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was most decisive: it answered the pricing pressure with hazard bands and clarified that the bond works by making operators internalize retirement risk before regulators can observe narrowing fuel margins.
