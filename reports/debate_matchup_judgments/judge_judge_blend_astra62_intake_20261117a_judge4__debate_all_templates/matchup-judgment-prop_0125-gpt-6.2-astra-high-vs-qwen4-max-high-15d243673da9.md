### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a competitive debate because it kept the motion as written and showed why it holds. A carrier's finding that a route is not commercially viable explains why the private market will not build it. It does not settle whether public money should. PRO's bid-for-least-support mechanism, its ceiling based on alternative costs, and its technology-neutral approach (fund terminals where they work) made the case both workable and responsive. CON's strongest material was sharp and well expressed: - the risk of permanent operating losses, - lock-in by the incumbent at renewal, - single-bidder auctions in the sparsest areas. PRO answered these reasonably. Public ownership of the fiber with handover terms limits the incumbent's leverage. Routes where subscriptions cover upkeep need no recurring subsidy. Renewal risk is counted before any money is awarded. CON's endgame rested on recasting the motion as a "presumption" that PRO had abandoned for a mere "scale." That reframe is not in the motion's wording. It also sat awkwardly with CON's own concessions: - CON's alternative was a publicly funded satellite terminal, which is itself a broadband subsidy. - CON narrowed its opposition to "the trench." - CON answered "yes" to funding the valley road, where the carrier's forecast is correct and a subsidy still closes the gap. CON's "lucky Tuesday" line was memorable. But PRO's closing correctly noted that sharing a trench shrinks the gap without closing it, so the subsidy still does the decisive work despite commercial non-viability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Q1/Q2 pressure followed by A1 and the closing. PRO forced CON to concede two things: that subsidizing terminals for unserved homes is subsidized broadband, and that the valley-road grant should be funded. CON's opposition shrank to "against the trench." CON's counter, that every safeguard PRO adds turns a thumb on the scale into a scale, was its best rhetorical move. It depended on a "presumption" reading of the motion that the wording does not clearly support.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Both sides were exceptional, but B (PRO) held the more defensible reading of the motion as written. A's core strategy was to show that B's safeguards (ceilings, walk-aways, risk discounts) collapse into a case-by-case value test, so the "even when" presumption does no work. That was executed brilliantly — "a scale, not a thumb on its side" is memorable and sharp. But B repeatedly neutralized it: the valley-road example (A4/B4) instantiated exactly the scenario the motion describes — a carrier correctly forecasting non-viability while the county should still pay — and B's closing crystallized it cleanly: "a carrier can be right to decline an investment and a county wrong to decline it." More decisively, B's Q1 pressure question trapped A into conceding that publicly funded terminals/vouchers for unserved homes are themselves subsidized broadband expansion; A's reply ("my position wearing PRO's label") was witty but functionally conceded the motion's substance, leaving only the narrower "presumption vs. permission" semantic frame, which borders on swapping in a different motion. A's renewal hold-up attack was the strongest genuine damage, and B's county-ownership answer was only partially adequate ("no clause guarantees a second bidder"), but B's B4.5 weighing — that the three risks need not coincide — blunted it enough. A wins on rhetoric and rebuttal sharpness; B wins on the actual path to victory, because the framework B built from the opening (a carrier's balance sheet is scrutiny, not veto) survived the full exchange and A conceded its outputs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Pressure Question Q1 ("are you arguing against subsidizing broadband or just against digging a trench?") forced A to concede that subsidized terminals connecting unserved homes count as broadband subsidies (A4.1), gutting CON's practical case and leaving only a semantic dispute over "presumption." A's sharpest counterpunch was A4.2's "lucky Tuesday, not a policy" against B's valley-road example, but B's closing — "the carrier saves money by walking away. The taxpayers save money by building" — restored the example as a rule, not a coincidence.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by defending the motion as written: commercial non-viability is not a veto when reliable service beats the next use of public money. Side A’s sharper attacks—lock-in, thin auctions, and “permission versus presumption”—beat a reckless always-trench policy, but that was a heavier claim than the proposition. B answered both questions directly, separated a construction gap from permanent operating support, and counted cheaper wireless as subsidized expansion rather than a concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: the valley-road case showed a carrier can correctly refuse while trenching still wins, and public ownership plus an ex ante risk discount unbundled the lock-in A had treated as inevitable.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins because it framed the motion as a decision rule and held that frame through the debate. Private payback is not the right test, and for genuinely unserved addresses the only thing that connects a home is construction. CON (A) was careful and intellectually honest. Its points on the opportunity cost of the next dollar, upkeep shortfalls, and strategic waiting by carriers were sharp, and its "prefers one, not needed one" correction was a good catch. But CON's answers conceded several key points: - It accepted that the test is public benefit against lifetime cost, not private payback. - It admitted that no subscription dollar connects an unserved house. - It accepted that construction "deserves consideration." - It offered no principled exemption that would apply to broadband but not to roads and mail. Those concessions left CON arguing about how to allocate subsidy rather than against subsidizing when carriers refuse to build. PRO exploited this effectively, noting that CON's safeguards (reserve prices, clawbacks, open bidding) all presuppose a subsidy program. PRO had one overreach. It claimed lifetime public benefit exceeds private return "by construction," which conflates benefit exceeding revenue with benefit exceeding cost, and CON's closing rightly flagged this. That kept the margin competitive rather than lopsided. CON's two-students closing was vivid but still conceded the core of the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 line: a voucher buys nothing where no service exists, and CON's procurement safeguards are "a procurement manual called an argument against building." This was then cashed out in the closing using CON's own concession that the test is public benefit against lifetime cost, not private payback.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side B won a close, high-level debate. A built the sharper analytic frame (opportunity cost, affordability-vs-availability, maintenance and strategic-waiting problems) and posed excellent pressure questions. But A's honest answer to B's Q2 — "the test is public benefit against lifetime cost, not private payback" — conceded the decision rule at the heart of the motion, and B converted that concession into the decisive weighing: once private payback is disqualified, "not commercially viable" becomes the definition of the roads public money exists to reach. A's remaining escape route (helping the student beside a line rather than the student on an unwired road) stayed live through the closing two-students example, but B had partially absorbed it with the reserve-price ceiling ("money goes where it connects more people") and the deferral-vs-permanent-exclusion contrast. B's rebuttals did more damage, and the closing crystallization ("Careful buying is still buying"; "A deferral can end; a principle of waiting cannot") was the memorable frame a third-party reader would leave with. A was never crushed — the affordability tradeoff was never fully neutralized — but B made the debate easier to decide and won the key concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answer to A's Q2. A conceded that roads, mail, and broadband should all be judged by public benefit against lifetime cost rather than private payback, and B seized it: every safeguard A demanded (reserve prices, clawbacks, open bidding) presupposed the subsidy A claimed to oppose. That single exchange reframed A's entire procurement critique as a manual for implementing B's policy, and B rode it through the closing.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side B wins the motion as written: commercial non-viability is a carrier's payback test, not a reason to refuse expansion where benefits fall outside that ledger and no non-construction dollar connects an unsignaled home. Side A was more precise about opportunity cost and rightly killed the claim that beating private return automatically approves every trench, but that supports Side B's capped program rather than a veto. Side A's willingness to leave a road unwired indefinitely left private payback in practical control of the hardest cases.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's answers to the pressure questions turned Side A's concessions—no subscription dollar serves a house no signal reaches, and private payback is not the test—into the vote: a reserve price defers a bad bid, while Side A's rule can exclude that road permanently. Side A's closing blocked the further leap that any externality justifies the trench, but did not restore commercial non-viability as a veto.
