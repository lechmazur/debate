### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO built a clean causal story: synthetic tools fabricate performances, standard releases hand studios broad rights, and non-union performers lack protection. It then forced CON into a bind. CON leaned on union AI agreements as proof that the market solves the problem. PRO turned that cleverly: the deals show both that baseline law was inadequate and that explicit AI terms are workable. CON's legal-remedy claims were asserted rather than shown. "Unfair competition" and contract breach were never tied to the actual harm. CON's A1 conceded that non-union contracts grant broad edit-and-repurpose rights, which undercut its own breach theory, and PRO exploited this in the closing. CON's best pressure points were the vagueness of "material alteration," the injured-actor holdout, and the absence of documented harm. PRO answered each with upfront riders, the reshoot analogy, and a prospective-policy framing. CON's closing mostly recapped and kept attacking "per-use consent tracking" after PRO had already explained how upfront riders cover planned uses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange began with CON's A2, which argued that union models work because they rely on upfront riders. PRO answered in A4.3 and A5.3 that the mandate harmonizes with upfront riders rather than overriding them. PRO also pointed out that CON's own A1 conceded broad boilerplate rights, which removes any breach-of-contract remedy. Together these moves neutralized both CON's "redundant/override" argument and its "existing law suffices" argument.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) consistently controlled the decisive clashes. B's materiality carve-out blunted A's friction and "hostage/extortion" scenarios, and B's answer that consent can be priced upfront in an AI rider neutralized A's redundancy/override objection to union contracts. A's central defense—that existing contract and tort law already covers the harm—was undermined by A's own concession that standard releases grant studios "broad, bargained-for rights to edit and repurpose footage and audio," which B seized on to show there is no breach when AI alteration falls within that boilerplate. B also exposed the non-union coverage gap that A never closed, and answered the "documented case" pressure with sound preventive-policy reasoning. A landed real points (materiality vagueness, indie compliance costs, windfall veto risk) but repeated them without adapting to B's upfront-rider resolution. B's pressure questions were sharper and directly exposed contradictions; B's Rebuttal 2 opened with clean A1/A2 answers and crystallized the weighing. Grounding was solid on both sides with no fake citations, but A occasionally asserted legal outcomes ("already illegal," "straightforward breach") with more confidence than the reasoning supported.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 (B2.1–B2.5) dismantled A's tort and union-contract defenses while the materiality analysis ("if it's truly minor, the rule never blocks it") defused the broken-bone/extortion scenario; B then sealed it in the closing by quoting A's own admission that releases grant broad repurposing rights, defeating A's contract-breach fallback.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): B establishes a plausible protection gap for non-union performers and explains how explicit, compensated authorization can occur upfront. A repeatedly attacks mandatory per-use renegotiation even after B expressly permits advance agreements, substantially weakening its central cost argument. A’s materiality and holdout concerns retain some force, while B overstates both legal certainty and compliance cheapness. Nevertheless, B better answers the central pressure and weighs performer autonomy and displaced compensation against negotiation costs; A largely repeats sweeping claims of existing protection and industry-wide harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s clarification that planned de-aging can be authorized and priced upfront directly undermines A’s claim that the mandate necessarily overrides negotiated terms or requires duplicate payment. A subsequently praises upfront union riders without explaining why those same arrangements cannot satisfy the proposition.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): CON wins because it set the decisive clash early and controlled it. Its opening framed the dispute narrowly: scams and uncontracted sequels are already illegal, so the real question is whether paid, contracted performances need fresh consent and pay before AI alteration. PRO's first rebuttal tried to shrink the rule to unfilmed prequels, unsigned sequels and ads. CON exposed that as rewriting the motion. Its pressure question then forced PRO to concede that de-aging an on-set flashback and synthetic foreign dubbing both trigger consent and compensation. CON used that concession to revive its holdout and veto argument. PRO's main defense was that manual de-aging and dubbing require the actor's participation and extra pay. CON plausibly rebutted this: VFX artists and foreign dubbers work without the original actor. The Studio A/Studio B comparison also gave CON a clean, memorable reason why the tool alone shouldn't create new value. PRO's labor-power point and its "unlimited generation" worry had some force, but neither was developed into a clear weighing. PRO also carried real execution costs. Three of its speeches were clipped. It relied on unsupported precision, such as the "99% of working performers" figure and the vague 2023 scam claim. Its closing misdescribed CON's B4.3 as an admission. CON's speeches were tighter, adapted across rounds, and made the ballot easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q1 forced PRO to admit in A4.1 that on-set flashback de-aging and synthetic foreign dubbing require new consent and pay. In B4.2–B4.3, CON then showed this contradicted PRO's earlier narrowing in A2.2. It also showed the admission revived the post-production holdout problem. Finally, CON refuted PRO's claim that manual de-aging and dubbing require the original actor's participation.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won the decisive clash. The core dispute was whether AI de-aging/synthetic voice differs categorically from manual post-production. PRO's rebuttal [B4.2] claimed manual de-aging and human dubbing already require actor participation and extra pay under standard contracts — a claim CON flatly and correctly dismantled in [A4.3]: VFX artists de-age footage after wrap without sign-off, and foreign dubbing is done by third-party voice actors with no bonus to the original cast. PRO's closing then made things worse by claiming manual de-aging is "unconnected to the original performer's identity" while artists literally reshape the actor's own face, which CON dispatched in [A5.2]. CON's identical-screen-result parity argument (two studios, same flashback, one manual one AI) was never neutralized. CON also converted PRO's own A1 admission [B4.1] — that on-set flashback de-aging and synthetic foreign dubbing trigger the rule — into concrete hostage-leverage hypotheticals, damaging PRO's "materiality threshold protects routine edits" defense. PRO's surviving routes (power imbalance for non-star performers, deepfake scams) were adequately answered: scams are covered by fraud law, and unions' collective minimums/residuals address rank-and-file protection without individual vetoes. Additionally, PRO had three turns clipped for word-limit violations, an execution cost. CON's speeches were tightly cross-referenced, factually grounded, and progressively narrowed the dispute to a clean choice.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3 exposing B4.2's false claim that manual de-aging and human dubbing require actor participation and extra pay, then A5.2 finishing off B5.1's "unconnected to identity" spin — this collapsed PRO's categorical distinction between AI and manual tools and left the double-dipping/veto argument standing.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): A won the contracted-performance clash through its comparison of identical results produced by manual and AI tools. B conceded that filmed flashbacks and localization trigger the rule, then defended the distinction with unsupported claims that manual alternatives require performer participation and approval. B’s bargaining-power argument remained relevant but did not resolve that comparison. A overstated inevitable post-production holdouts, since consent could be secured upfront, limiting its winning margin. Nevertheless, A answered pressure more effectively and exposed consequential shifts in B’s account of the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A directly dismantled B’s claim that manual de-aging and dubbing require the original actor’s involvement. It then distinguished compensation for VFX artists or replacement voice actors from additional compensation for the original performer, undermining B’s central justification for treating AI differently.
