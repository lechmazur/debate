### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): A won by exposing the unresolved dilemma in B's case: margin-sensitive compliance either lowers safety/noise protections or shifts costs to the public. A also drew a workable principled line between public/nonprofit cultural assets and private nightlife, while B's closing leaned on a false dilemma that ignored A's direct-funding alternative. B's key tools—phased compliance, scaled sound rules, and cultural ground-floor mandates—were damaged by A's landlord-capture and physics rebuttals, and B never fully answered them after the second rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was A's rebuttal that B's "neither weaker rules nor subsidies" collapses: phased upgrades defer safety compliance, scaled noise standards ignore the physics of bass transmission, and stadium subsidies are one bad handout used to excuse another.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): CON more convincingly showed that treating small private nightlife venues as infrastructure when zoning, licensing, or policing threatens survival requires diluting safety or noise rules, freezing scarce land, raising housing costs, or deterring landlords from offering cheap rooms. PRO never resolved that binary, and equating basements to museums or utilities failed against CON’s distinctions on externalities, governance, and direct public funding of artists and stages.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal, which answered both pressure questions and showed that phased or scaled compliance, anti-flip rules, and development-rights tools still defer life-safety, transmit midnight bass, freeze the parcel, or lock out tomorrow’s venues.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B won by making the decisive clash about who bears the cost of preserving private venues. It convincingly argued that infrastructure status only changes outcomes when venues cannot meet legitimate safety, noise, or housing tradeoffs, and that the cleaner cultural policy is direct public support rather than forcing neighbors, renters, and landlords to absorb externalities. Side A’s cultural-incubator and museum/stadium asymmetry points were strong and memorable, but B’s answers on landlord incentives, safety/noise costs, and the limits of the zoning toolkit gave the third-party reader a more concrete reason to vote CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 pressuring PRO’s “realistic compliance paths” as either diluted protections or subsidies, plus the landlord-incentive answer that anti-flip conditions could deter leasing to venues, materially weakened PRO’s middle-ground case.
