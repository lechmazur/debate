### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins a competitive debate because it built several independent routes to victory and kept pressing all of them: regressive incidence, the mismatch between design control and what actually drives collection costs, the governance trap, and duplicate systems. PRO's "burden should follow control" frame was clear and well grounded, and eco-modulation and an independent regulator were reasonable answers. But CON's pressure question forced PRO to concede that clean, recyclable staples must carry permanent fees to keep revenue from vanishing. CON then used that concession well: once well-designed containers keep paying for truck routes, payment no longer tracks design control. CON also gave a concrete alternative in direct material standards plus progressive general revenue with low-income waivers. PRO's late answers were only partial: the claim that standards do not price volume, and the reminder that CON had not proven the total bill rises. CON countered both with incidence arguments about markups, pass-through, and landlords. Some of those claims were asserted rather than demonstrated, so the edge is clear but not large.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3 turned PRO's A1 concession (B4.1), that recyclable cans and bottles still pay ongoing fees for trucks and sorting, against PRO's core "burden follows control" thesis. A clean container's fee buys no further design improvement, which reduces the scheme to a cost shift onto groceries. PRO's closing reply about volume and overpackaging only partly repaired this.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side B more convincingly defended the proposition by showing that producer cost-sharing internalizes packaging volume and design choices that standards alone leave externalized, while offering workable mitigations (modulated rates, audited reimbursement, residual public share). Side A landed sharp hits on regressive incidence and limited producer control over routes and contamination, but those points did not fully displace the free-rider and incentive case or prove the total household burden must rise.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2, answering that everyday staples carry real but lower modulated fees and that an independent regulator plus residual public funding blocks both a blank cheque and unilateral industry caps, undercutting A’s false dilemma.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): A won narrowly by exploiting B’s concession that even clean, recyclable everyday staples would bear permanent collection fees. A connected that concession to its regressivity and weak-design-incentive objections, while offering direct material standards and more progressive public finance as an alternative. B answered the pressure questions unusually directly and preserved a live argument about packaging volume and residual externalized costs, but its closing largely asserted cost replacement without adequately answering A’s distribution and added-cost arguments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B’s A1 in Rebuttal 2 followed by A’s Rebuttal 2: B honestly confirmed ongoing fees on recyclable cans and bottles, and A used that answer to argue that the funding mechanism had moved beyond design responsibility into a permanent, regressive grocery charge.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Both sides were sharp, but PRO (B) held the cleaner route to victory. PRO built a clear causal story: producers control design, taxpayers pay for disposal, and shifting costs realigns incentives. CON's strongest attacks were pass-through regressivity, the "steep vs. gentle" dilemma, and the claim that safeguards concede the motion is insufficient. CON landed a real hit when it noted that a uniform fee weakens PRO's grocery-competition claim. PRO answered with material-differentiated fees: aluminum and clear plastic owe less, while dyed bottles and pouches owe more. That reply largely defuses the needle-threading dilemma, because the fee can be steep for bad packaging without raising every price. PRO also tied the motion's "rather than taxpayers" language to offsetting public charges, which blunts the "pay twice" claim. CON's response that local taxes "rarely fall" stayed an assertion. The "motion as written" objection was weak: reimbursing documented collection costs is a plausible reading of paying those costs, not a new motion. CON's alternatives also have a live gap PRO kept pressing: standards and deposits fund no collection, and pay-as-you-throw sits awkwardly with CON's own regressivity concern. CON's curb-rebate patch for that was thin. CON had the more memorable rhetoric, but PRO answered the pressure more directly and stayed closer to the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2 answered CON's sharpest pressure question: if every major producer faces the fee, who forces absorption? PRO replied that the same fee schedule does not mean the same bill. Material-differentiated costs penalize dyed and composite packaging while clean packaging owes little, which undercuts CON's later steep-vs-gentle dilemma.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the decision on who should bear collection costs and showing that a shift to major producers can replace general taxpayer bills while using differential net recovery costs and competition to push redesign without a uniform price spike. Side A landed memorable hits on calibration risk, pass-through, and bureaucracy, but its alternatives left hauling and sorting mostly on taxpayers and its “pay twice” critique assumed no offset, contrary to the motion’s “rather than.”
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s first rebuttal exposed the tension between attacking checkout regressivity and endorsing pay-as-you-throw, and its later differential-cost replies kept that pressure from collapsing the fairness case into a single unworkable fee level.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B built the stronger case for the motion by linking producer payment to both funding recovery and differentiating design costs, while showing that CON’s alternatives often left taxpayers funding collection. B directly answered the universal-fee and safeguard questions and exploited CON’s tension between condemning regressive checkout costs and recommending pay-as-you-throw. CON’s pass-through and “needle-threading” critique remained substantial, but its claims of unavoidable double payment and municipal misuse were less firmly established, and its alternative policies did not adequately replace the motion’s funding function.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal sharply exposed the regressiveness contradiction in CON’s position; B’s second rebuttal then answered both pressure questions directly by explaining differentiated net fees and why documented payment for collection and recycling is inherent in the proposition rather than an extra rescue clause.
