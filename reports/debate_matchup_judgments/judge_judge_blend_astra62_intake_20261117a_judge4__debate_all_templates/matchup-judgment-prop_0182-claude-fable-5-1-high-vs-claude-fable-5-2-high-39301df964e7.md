### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): A better resolved the comparison between strict liability and enforceable negligence liability. B persuasively explained how outcome-based liability can reward care, but its defense of liability despite reasonable care ultimately depended on superior loss allocation. A directly challenged that premise through correlated exposure, vendor insolvency, and diversified insurance, while answering the proof-access objection with a reversed burden. B's claim that insecurity remained free overlooked that alternative. A overstated the inevitability of bankruptcy, keeping this a clear but competitive win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange followed B's defense of unavoidable flaws as an allocation issue: A asked whether concentrating catastrophic claims in the vendor actually compensates victims better. A also punctured B's supposedly simple patch-calendar defense: deciding whether customers had sufficient time to patch still requires a reasonableness inquiry.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B won by isolating the exact margin where strict liability differs from fault liability—the diligent vendor beaten by a novel zero-day—and proving that strict liability fails there even on Side A's own terms. Forced to defend that margin, Side A conceded that strict liability serves loss allocation rather than deterrence in the diligent-vendor case, which let Side B prove that a software vendor is the worst possible insurer for correlated risk: every customer claims on the same day, bankrupting non-giant vendors, consolidating the software market, and paying victims cents on the dollar compared with diversified first-party cyber insurance. Side B also neutralized Side A's administrative-simplicity advantage by showing that breaking causation for unpatched customers requires litigating reasonable patching windows rather than merely reading a release calendar. Side A landed real pressure against regulatory compliance checklists, but never solved correlated vendor insolvency or market concentration.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Turn 8 response ([B4.1]-[B4.7], crystallized in [B5.2]-[B5.5]), which turned Side A's "not deterrence but allocation" concession against its correlated-risk defense by showing a single codebase vendor goes insolvent on the exact day all its customers claim, while asking whether a patch released "yesterday" or "last week" breaks causation shattered Side A's claim of a bright-line calendar rule.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B won by isolating strict liability’s distinctive margin: the diligent vendor defeated by an unforeseeable flaw. A offered strong victim-allocation and residual-risk arguments, especially blast-radius reduction, and effectively noted that cyber insurance also faces correlated losses.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal turned A’s “not deterrence but allocation” answer into the decisive frame: strict liability added no deterrence in the diligent-vendor case, so it had to succeed as compensation, where correlated insolvency and first-party coverage weakened it. A’s closing recovered some ground on insurance correlation but did not dismantle that frame.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): B won the central incentive clash: equal liability for an actual defect does not mean equal expected liability for careful and careless vendors. Preventing defects still reduces exposure. A offered a meaningful burden-shifted negligence alternative and concentration-risk objection, but repeatedly asserted rather than established that unavoidable residual defects would force withdrawal. B also effectively challenged A's claim that hospital-level insurance makes losses from a shared vulnerability independent.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B directly answered A's pressure by distinguishing product defects from negligent development: courts could assess whether software violated its security function while separately reducing recovery for customer negligence. This defeated A's claim that comparative responsibility necessarily collapses strict liability into negligence.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): In an exceptionally high-level debate, Side B built the stronger path to victory by isolating the exact gap between burden-shifted negligence and strict liability: bugs exploited despite state-of-the-art care. By supporting negligence with burdens shifted onto vendors, Side B neutralized Side A's sloppy-vendor examples and discovery-asymmetry concerns, then won two decisive points on the remaining no-fault cases. First, Side B proved that Side A's definition of "insecure code" sweeps in every zero-day exploit regardless of engineering diligence, and that damages scale with customer deployment rather than bug count. Second, Side B showed that concentrating uncapped consequential losses from thousands of networks onto one vendor's balance sheet risks vendor bankruptcy and withdrawal from high-damage customers like hospitals—an adverse supply-side harm Side A essentially bit the bullet on rather than solved. Side A gave a strong doctrinal defense of comparative fault and landed a sharp closing point on correlated risk, keeping the margin competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's turn of Side A's deadbolt analogy in [B2.1]-[B2.2], paired with the follow-up trap in [B3.2] and [B4.5]: Side B showed that a deadbolt opened with a paperclip is already covered by negligence, whereas defining "insecure code" as any violation of a security promise [A4.3] makes even diligent vendors strictly liable when a nation-state finds an unavoidable flaw.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): A won narrowly. It preserved a coherent strict-liability standard—defect and causation, with victim-side comparative fault—and answered B’s central incentive objection: even when damages scale with deployment, reducing exploitable defects reduces expected liability. Its closing also exposed the weakness in B’s insurance alternative: shifting a simultaneous mass loss among victims does not remove its correlation. B’s “existence” and vendor-withdrawal critique was serious and rhetorically sharp, but relied too heavily on absolute claims that care earns nothing and distributed losses are independent.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure questions forced A to define defect without negligence; A’s A1/A2 answers were direct, and its later correlation response blunted B’s strongest concentrated-risk argument.
