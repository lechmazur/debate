### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): A more convincingly separated effective procedural protections from a guaranteed human decider. Its pre-decision notice mechanism answered B’s timing challenge, while B’s narrower hybrid still left substantial workload concerns unresolved. B’s bounded-discretion and unforeseen-exception arguments preserved a meaningful case, but its repeated assertion that automation necessarily lacks accountability did insufficient comparative work. A’s nonexistent reference IDs and overstatement about human decisions being appeal-proof count against it, keeping this competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A directly challenged B’s concrete examples: missing letters, mistranslations, and hospitalization records demonstrate a need to correct information, not necessarily to require human adjudication. Adding notice before adverse action made that response substantially stronger. B’s unforeseen-exception reply identified a remaining limitation, but did not overcome A’s combined consistency and capacity objections.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B built the clearer comparative case: transparent criteria, pre-decision notice, and record correction answered A’s main examples without assuming human impartiality, while the scale objection remained substantial. A effectively narrowed the right to consequential adverse decisions and preserved a real accountability concern, but too often equated a named human with genuine answerability without demonstrating why institutional review of automated decisions could not provide it. B’s discretion dilemma and unequal-override argument left A’s route to victory narrower and less secure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s bounded-discretion defense was decisive: if officials apply fixed legal criteria, their protections can be codified uniformly; if they depart from those criteria, overrides risk arbitrary and unequal mercy. A’s closing appeal to unforeseen exceptions answered part of this, but did not fully resolve the consistency and scale costs.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the proposition easier to defend as written: it defined a narrow, workable right to a designated human with override authority, answered the scale objection by triaging automation toward intake and uncontested approvals while reserving human authority for adverse high-stakes outcomes, and kept the focus on accountable ownership of irreversible state power. Side A’s delay, bias, and catch-22 arguments were sharp and created real pressure, but they never fully answered why transparent rules and correction mechanisms replace the legitimacy value of a human decision-maker in deportation, detention, benefit termination, or criminal-risk contexts. B’s rebuttals also used A’s concessions more effectively and gave the cleaner comparative weighing: volume is not a reason to make high-stakes state power unaccountable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 was most decisive: it directly answered the pressure questions, clarified that “fewer automated high-stakes actions” meant fewer invisible adverse decisions rather than abandoned services, and turned CON’s data-correction concession into support for a hybrid system with human final authority where harm is irreversible.
