### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): PRO wins because it held its core route throughout: deleted code is irreversible, while unrunnable but saved code can still be recovered later. PRO answered CON's main objections directly: - **Film analogy:** celluloid also decays and needed migration, yet it was still deposited. - **Capture vs. running:** archives need to capture builds now, not run every live service continuously. - **Selection:** notice-based thresholds of reach and recognition give a workable trigger. - **Small creators:** no penalty applies until formal notice, which neutralizes the "guessed wrong" burden. PRO's sharpest move was turning CON's own alternative. If funded public collections can curate, run emulators and secure access, then those operational objections cannot be fatal to legal deposit. What remains is the question of acquisition, where PRO argued only a mandate captures private server logic and works caught in contracts. CON's most durable point was that statutory override of third-party licenses was asserted rather than established. That point never fully went away. However, CON's closing largely restated "stored bytes are a promise" without rebutting PRO's reversibility asymmetry. CON also incurred execution costs: - Two turns were clipped for exceeding the word limit. - It cited nonexistent reference IDs ([C2.1, C2.5]). - Its answer to Q2 ("opt-in and bounded") was partially undercut by PRO's notice-based, bounded design.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Q2 and its follow-up in A4.3–A4.4. PRO argued that CON's endorsement of "funded public collections" concedes that curation, emulation and terminal security are workable. That narrowed the debate to acquisition. PRO then turned CON's own middleware-contract point (B2.6) into a reason a statutory mandate is needed, since voluntary donation is exactly what those contracts block.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO built the stronger path to victory by establishing that voluntary alternatives cannot capture private server logic or override third-party middleware contracts, and that irreversible deletion outweighs CON's feasibility and burden objections. CON's sharpest attack—that stored bytes are not preserved culture—was substantially answered by PRO's point that saved code enables future emulation while deleted code is permanent. CON's funded-public-collections alternative also undercut its own operational objections, and PRO's notice-based selection model defused the gatekeeping concern. CON's rule-of-law worry about statutory override was plausible but less decisive than PRO's necessity case. Side A also had two clipped turns, which slightly weakened its execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 turned CON's endorsement of funded public collections into a concession that archives can curate, secure terminals, and maintain emulators, then showed the real divergence is capture: voluntary donation triggers breach of third-party contracts, while statutory deposit overrides them and can compel private server logic before deletion.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the decisive comparison clearer: selective notice-based deposit can use the same curation, emulation, and security that Side A already accepts for funded collections, while uniquely overriding middleware contracts and capturing unreleased server builds before deletion. Side A’s category-error, fragment, and litigation objections stayed live but never fully neutralized that acquisition gap or the irreversibility of lost code. Clipped speeches and a nonexistent citation also cost Side A execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal: answering the penalty and emulator questions, then showing that A’s voluntary alternative shares the operational burdens while failing where contracts and private server logic block preservation.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): PRO built a clean accountability frame: an archive can preserve only what someone is obliged to hand over. That point stayed live through the closing. CON did more damage overall. CON's opening dilemma and its follow-through pinned PRO between two options: - **Distributed builds only.** For networked software, this yields a non-functional client. - **Compelled source and server code.** This breaks the "just as books and films" analogy and raises third-party licensing and security problems. PRO's tiered answer ("deeper materials where rights permit") let CON show that the mandate's distinctive value collapses at both ends. Where rights don't permit, the deposit is uncompilable fragments. Where they do, the state is stockpiling live production code. CON also answered PRO's key pressure question directly. Archivists can buy market copies, and circumvention exemptions let them document protocols before shutdown. That undercut PRO's closing claim that exemptions are "a right to copy nothing," a claim PRO never squarely addressed. PRO also bears execution costs: - Its opening was clipped. - It cited nonexistent reference IDs ([C1.2–C1.3], [C4.1–C4.2]). - Its security answer (sealed records, corporate filings) was distinguished by CON as static text versus live code, and PRO never rebuilt it. CON's "zero-day blueprint" framing was somewhat overstated. Its end-of-life transfers remain voluntary, which keeps PRO competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1–B2.4, reinforced in B4.3–B4.4, exposed the tension between "finished work as distributed" and "source where it exists." The follow-up was sharper still. Limiting source deposit to material where the depositor holds the rights leaves uncompilable fragments. Ignoring third-party rights forces studios to breach contracts. This forced PRO into a qualified retreat that CON exploited through the closing.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): B wins because the decisive burden was whether any actor is legally obliged to hand over software before it disappears. A's DRM exemptions and end-of-life grants are voluntary and cannot guarantee access in bankruptcy or server shutdown. A's technical objections were strong, but B's tiered, restricted, periodic deposit answered them as design constraints rather than refutations, and A never fully neutralized the obligation gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was B's pressure question and follow-up that a copyright exemption to copy is useless when the only copy sits on a server about to be wiped; A's answer remained voluntary and therefore could not ensure material survived.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side A kept the written motion's "just as books and films" test alive and showed that Side B's retreat to tiered deposit "where rights permit" either leaves networked games and apps as unusable fragments or requires surrender of unowned live code. Side B's accountability frame was forceful, but its closing treated Side A's protocol-capture and end-of-life answer as mere voluntary handover, and execution slips (a clipped opening, nonexistent C citations, and a premature claim that questions went unanswered) left the double bind unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal, which showed that "both, where rights permit" recreates either a dead login screen or unauthorized third-party deposit rather than book-and-film legal deposit.
