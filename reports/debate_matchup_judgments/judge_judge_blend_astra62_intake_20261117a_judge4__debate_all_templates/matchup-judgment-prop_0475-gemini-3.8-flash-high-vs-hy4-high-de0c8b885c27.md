### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won by making the motion's "far more" qualifier the decisive battleground and giving a concrete alternative: performance-based contracts paying only for verified tonnage, price, nutrition, and net emissions after import leakage. This captured most of PRO's climate benefits at lower public risk. PRO (B) never resolved CON's "scale trap" — that land savings are modest if precision fermentation stays a high-value ingredient business, while true staple scale multiplies upstream sugar, energy, and purification burdens — and instead repeated the thermodynamic ceiling and solar analogy across rounds. PRO's A1 answer dodged the tonnage/price question by asserting herd displacement from ingredient substitution, and its A2 dismissed conditional payments as a "bribe" without addressing leakage sequencing, which CON then exploited in A4.6. CON answered PRO's thermodynamic pressure question honestly and directly (A4.1: a qualified "yes" via grass, residues, and fossil-heavy electricity), while PRO's answers were more evasive. PRO also cited nonexistent reference IDs ([C2.1], [P1.1], etc.) in Rebuttal 2, an execution and epistemic-discipline cost. CON's progression narrowed the dispute cleanly; PRO's later speeches widened back to opening themes. Clear but competitive: PRO's core physics point survived, but it proved less than the motion required.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's "scale trap" in Rebuttal 1 (A2.2–A2.4): the clean land-and-emissions comparison works only by shrinking the job to whey and fats, and PRO's own concession that PF excels at ingredients undercut its staple-scale substitution story. PRO never escaped this, and CON sharpened it into the closing line: "displaced milk, meat, and eggs cannot be assumed from a bioreactor."
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by keeping the motion’s “far more” burden live. B separated reforming livestock subsidies and paying for verified net cuts from a large, premature sectoral bet, and answered the thermodynamic question with a qualified whole-system comparison rather than absolutism. Side A had a strong inefficiency case and a memorable solar analogy, but treated performance contracts as a bribe, never specified when “far more” would be earned, and weakened its second rebuttal with nonexistent reference IDs. B’s closing made the cleaner choice: physics may justify reform, but it does not justify buying factories first and measuring displacement later.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it granted microbes the edge on identical refined sugar, then showed grass, residues, power, and dedicated crops can reverse that edge, and reframed A’s catch-22 as evidence before “far more,” not parity before any help.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won the decisive definitional clash: the motion says shift "far more" support, not abolish food subsidies, and B repeatedly exposed CON's conflation of the two. A's inflation route ("stripping the baseline manufactures scarcity") was directly pressured by B's Q1 and answered with a glidepath/procurement/safety-net mechanism in B4.3, but A's A4.1 response ("schedules the scarcity") just reasserted the contraction claim without engaging the phasing or the fact that a subsidy budget is not the grocery shelf. B also won the resilience comparison: Q2 exposed A's selective framing (feed mills, refrigeration, slaughterhouses all need power), and while A's "pasture cattle survive storms" reply in A4.2 was vivid and partially effective, B's counter—that enteric methane, conversion losses, and manure are structural while tank risks are manageable industrial risks—was the cleaner weighing. B's "denominator" point (whole-body overhead vs target molecule) and the symmetry turn on subsidy rules (CON trusts subsidy rules for methane capture but calls open licensing "wishes") did real comparative work. A's case was competent and grounded in plausible mechanisms, but leaned on repetition of the infant-tech and fragility claims rather than resolving B's burden-shifting answers. B made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q1 (why does "shifting far more" become "slashing the baseline" with "explosive food inflation"?) was the debate's pivot: it exposed the gap CON needed to bridge, and A's subsequent answers never closed it, while B4.3's "subsidy budget vs grocery shelf" answer crystallized why the inflation route fails against a phased shift.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by keeping the motion as a phased reallocation of the next public dollar, not an overnight dismantling of the food floor. It answered both pressure questions with a glidepath, retained safety nets, and a conversion-efficiency comparison, then damaged Side B’s idealized, solar-powered cow by pointing to grain-fed production and electrified supply chains. Side B’s inflation, feedstock, and batch-fragility objections stayed live and were vividly framed, but they depended on equating any large shift with immediate scarcity and never fully engaged bridge payments, sequencing, or portfolio rules. A’s closing therefore gave the cleaner vote: managed diversification versus lock-in that pays twice.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it separated sterile processing from new monoculture acreage, refused the cliff-edge reading of “far more,” and turned B’s “power cuts, the batch rots” line into a selective comparison that ignored feed mills, cooling, slaughter, and methane that a cleaner grid cannot erase.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A built the stronger case for a phased, conditional reallocation rather than an abrupt dismantling of food support. It answered both pressure questions directly, preserved a plausible transition path, and consistently compared structural livestock emissions and supply risks with manageable industrial risks. Side B raised legitimate concerns about scale, feedstocks, affordability, and resilience, but repeatedly treated inflation and supply contraction as inevitable, idealized conventional livestock as distributed pasture, and made overly absolute claims about fermentation inputs and fragility. Its later speeches mostly restated those claims rather than neutralizing A’s glidepath, portfolio, and milestone-based response.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered the feedstock and inflation questions directly, then exposed the selective resilience comparison by restoring feed mills, refrigeration, slaughterhouses, and cold chains to the conventional system. B’s reply largely repeated “scheduled scarcity” and pasture-versus-tank fragility without resolving that broader comparison.
