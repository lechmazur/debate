### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Both sides were crisp, well-grounded, and honest about tradeoffs, making this a close, high-quality debate. Side A's path to victory was cleaner: the option-value/lock-in reframe (walls commit capital and politics to permanent defense) survived Side B's reversibility counter, and A's answers to pressure questions were direct and clarifying — especially conceding zoning behind levees while correctly noting zoning freezes exposure while buyouts remove existing exposure. The decisive moment was Side B's one-word "Buyout" answer on the thrice-flooded barrier island, plus B's acceptance of no third rebuild; A exploited this effectively in closing by defining the contested high-risk coastline (excluding B's dense-core carve-outs, which A had excepted from the opening). Side B's counters — the moving-frontier arithmetic, the double-standard-on-zoning trap, and the closing charge that A "defined the answer into the question" — were genuinely strong, and B's dollar-share argument for "prioritize" was a live route. But B progressively converged toward A's policy while insisting it won on framing, which blunted the clash. A's "wall is a subscription" and "choose the strategy whose failures are gradual" lines made the weighing easier to hold. Narrow edge to A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2): by accepting identical zoning behind levees while showing zoning only caps rather than removes existing exposure, A defused B's double-standard trap, then forced the barrier-island default question that produced B's "Buyout" concession — the exchange the whole debate turned on.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side B won a high-level debate by holding Side A to the motion's comparative burden while systematically turning Side A's strongest economic and governance arguments. Once Side A exempted dense cores, major ports, and fixed infrastructure and ruled out compulsion, Side B persuasively showed that hard defenses still cover most of the people, asset value, and capital spending on high-risk coastlines, whereas ending subsidies for third-time rebuilds is an instrument-neutral fiscal rule consistent with site-by-site triage rather than prioritizing retreat over defense. Side B also punctured Side A's Dutch and Katrina examples, blunted the levee-effect argument via symmetric zoning capacity, dismantled the claim that buyouts are a one-time cost, and exposed Side A's closing attempt to exclude defended areas from thehigh-risk coastline map as circular. Side A argued crisply and scored well on distinguishing freezing versus reducing exposure, plus suffered a minor procedural slip in Turn 7 by claiming Side B declined to answer a question before Side B's answer turn arrived, leaving Side B with the much cleaner route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B2.1-B2.2], Side B used Side A's own multi-century sea-level-rise premise to flip the catchy slogan that a buyout is paid once while a wall is a subscription [A2.6-A2.7], showing that rising seas turn retreat into a subscription with a moving frontier that repeatedly buys out the next row, pulls back utilities, and destroys the tax base—then sealed the exchange in [B4.5] by noting against [A4.8] that market-price demolition is never cheap and barrier islands have no inland block to recede to.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO defined priority as the funding default for high-risk, non-core coastline and consistently defended that framing. Its distinction between zoning freezing new exposure and buyouts removing existing repeatedly flooded homes answered CON's strongest pressure, while CON's concession that a thrice-flooded barrier island should receive a buyout and no third public rebuild left CON's triage label without a different default. CON won points on cost, lock-in, and reversibility, but did not displace PRO's flipped-default account of the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 answer that zoning merely freezes exposure while buyouts eliminate existing risk, followed by its use of Side A's direct 'Buyout' answer in closing, was the decisive exchange.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built a coherent, punchy case around the "endless subscription" of hard defense and the marginal next dollar, and its rebuttals were sharp, especially catching CON's "never" answer and reframing it as escalation rather than maintenance. But CON established the more decisive frame for a motion about prioritization: by any people- and value-weighted metric, wealthy countries' exposure concentrates in dense cities that PRO itself conceded must be defended, making defense the priority and retreat the margin. CON blunted PRO's strongest escape — the "next dollar goes to bungalows" reframe — by noting that barrier upgrades and harbor protection are also live next-dollar spending that PRO already labeled the subscription (B4.6-4.7). CON also landed the sharpest turn of the debate: PRO's own deadline mechanism (subsidy expires) reproduces the uncompensated abandonment of the poorest that PRO had condemned (B4.4-4.5), and PRO's closing answer was more rhetorical than responsive. PRO won slices of the terminal-state clash but never fully escaped its opening concession or answered the cost-per-person-protected comparison head-on. Clear but competitive win for CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.4-4.5, where CON turned PRO's buyout-deadline answer into a self-contradiction: expiring subsidies and insurance for holdouts is exactly the uncompensated, unmanaged retreat PRO had attacked, just "with a published deadline." This materially damaged PRO's fairness route and was only partially repaired in PRO's closing.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by controlling what "prioritize on balance" means and trapping Side A in its own concessions. Once Side A granted in [A1.4] that defending dense, high-value cities like Rotterdam makes sense, Side B rightly argued that coastal population, infrastructure, and economic value in wealthy nations concentrate overwhelmingly in metro regions rather than empty miles of shoreline. Side A landed crisp points distinguishing escalating sea-defense costs from routine road maintenance [A2.2] and noting how levees create anti-zoning constituencies [A2.6], but its attempts to reclaim the priority metric collapsed. First, counting shoreline kilometres prioritized land over people [B2.1]. Second, Side A's pivot that cities are "already walled" so the "next dollar" goes to beach bungalows [A4.1] directly contradicted its own core claim that urban barriers require expensive, ongoing upgrades [B4.6]-[B4.7]. Side A also hurt its credibility by twice claiming post-Sandy Staten Island buyouts occurred while homes were "still dry" [A3.2, A4.6]—which Side B cleanly refuted [B4.3]—and by prematurely faulting Side B in Turn 7 [A4.5] for not yet answering a Turn 5 question.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B4.6]-[B4.7], Side B dismantled Side A's "next dollar" pivot [A4.1]-[A4.2]. When Side A argued that dense cities like Manhattan and Rotterdam are already walled so marginal spending goes to beach bungalows, Side B noted that New York's harbor still needs protection, London's barrier needs upgrading, and Side A itself had argued in [A2.2] that urban defenses require escalating upgrades—meaning future budgets still flow primarily to defending places Side A conceded must be defended.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side A won the priority clash by weighing exposure by people and value rather than coastline length. B's concession that major cities should be defended, plus A's showing that upgrade spending is still the live next dollar there, made retreat look like a marginal tool. A also exposed B's subsidy-deadline as leaving poorer holdouts uninsured and uncompensated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it answered the endless-defense charge with cost per person protected, reframed retreat's moving front line, and turned B's expiring subsidy into the unmanaged retreat B condemned.
