### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Both sides were disciplined and engaged directly, so the result is close but real. CON (A) built a strong case on structuring, coercion, a brittle fixed threshold, and statutory privacy as an alternative. Its best move was the symmetry argument: both designs depend on law lasting, so the question is which failure is worse. PRO (B) handled the pressure more cleanly. It gave honest A1/A2 answers: remote peer transfers can count, but "support" does not require every wallet to accept scripted instructions, and no operator can reverse an anonymous payment. It then turned CON's asymmetry. A disclosed association cannot be made private again, so CON's claim that privacy failures are "repairable" confuses changing a rule with undoing a disclosure. CON's closing answered that only with "aired, challenged, compensated," which does not meet the irreversibility point. PRO also blunted the coercion harm. It conceded that a name is a lead but not a refund. It also showed that proximity-limited wallets restore the cash-like need for physical collection, matching its baseline comparison to the cash being displaced. CON's claim that proximity cannot be enforced without metadata, and its courier example, kept the structuring point alive. However, both were introduced late and amount to "about as bad as cash," which is the comparison PRO had framed from the opening. Overall, PRO made the decisive comparison easier to resolve in its favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's answer to CON's "imperfect law means privacy erodes and must be repaired" argument [A4.5]. PRO argued that widening access to existing records exposes yesterday's associations, which "cannot make a disclosed association private again" [B4.6, B5.1-B5.2]. This turned CON's own irreversibility weighing back on CON, and CON's closing never squarely answered it.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): In a high-level clash, Side B (PRO) built the cleaner, more resilient path to victory on both mechanism and comparative weighing. Side A's strongest push was that digital anonymity differs from physical cash because remote, programmable value enables automated structuring and lets remote scammers dictate an irreversible rail. Side B effectively neutralized the scale and remote-extortion gap by showing that a statutory requirement to "support" anonymous peer-to-peer transfers in a cash replacement can be met through proximity wallet taps without enabling remote scripts, while candidly conceding Side A's point that coerced payers cannot freely choose a reversible service. Side A's textual insistence that "peer-to-peer" legally mandates remote scriptability—and its shaky technical claim that proximity cannot work without prohibited metadata—did not hold up. Finally, Side B decisively won the late-round weighing on statutory failure: whereas repealing an anonymity mandate only affects future transfers, widening access to (or breaching) Side A's confidential ledger retroactively exposes past lawful associations in a way changing a rule cannot undo, all to preserve an investigative lead rather than a guaranteed refund by pre-recording every small payment before any complaint exists.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's synthesis in [A4.3]-[A4.5] argued that both designs rest on mutable statutes, favoring CON because privacy erosion can be repaired whereas an anonymous extortion payment cannot be reversed. Side B dismantled that symmetry across [B4.6] and [B5.1]-[B5.3], showing that repealing an anonymity guarantee only affects tomorrow's payments while expanding record access exposes yesterday's strike-fund contributions irreversibly ("Calling that harm repairable mistakes changing a rule for undoing a disclosure"), and noting that a recipient's name is merely a lead that requires recording every innocent transaction before anyone complains.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): B won a close, substantive debate. A’s coercion and structuring objections were serious, but its repeated claim that the motion necessarily requires remotely scriptable anonymity read more into “support” than the wording demands. B answered that point directly, conceded the genuine loss of victim recourse, and gave the cleaner privacy comparison: retaining identification records creates retrospective exposure that later legal repairs cannot undo. A’s statutory-symmetry response did not fully answer that temporal distinction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal answered both pressure questions without evasion, then distinguished repealing anonymity for future payments from exposing records of past payments. That distinction survived A’s response and anchored B’s closing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): CON wins a competitive debate on precision and on how it handled pressure. Its fake-landlord case showed how a fixed threshold makes the size of a payment, rather than evidence of wrongdoing, decide whether the recipient can be traced. It then offered a concrete alternative: aliases, receipts held by the payer, split custodial keys, and court orders. When PRO said the same scam already runs on cash, CON replied that cash needs a physical handoff while digital payments let a fraudster collect from across the city. When PRO argued the proceeds become visible once spent above the threshold, CON answered that a visible purchase is not the same as a link to any particular deposit. CON's answer to PRO's Q2 on architectural constraints was the strongest moment of the debate. Because the encrypted payee identifier sits on the payer's receipt rather than in a central file, a successor government that freezes deletions still holds no payment history. PRO's closing did not squarely engage this. PRO's best live route was the exclusion of people with no papers and no attestor, which CON honestly conceded remained a hard case. However, PRO repeatedly called the per-person cap CON's own when it was PRO's safeguard, which is a misdescription of the opponent. PRO's A1 also conceded that its threshold cannot stop one collector from holding many wallets, so CON could show that the harm is capped per tenant but not across many victims. PRO had cleaner rhetoric and framing ("floor by design, not pledge"), but CON closed off more of PRO's routes to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2 answer in Rebuttal 2 was decisive. By placing the encrypted payee identifier on the tenant's receipt rather than in a central transaction file, it showed that freezing deletion of custodial keys cannot recreate a retrospective payment history. That directly neutralized PRO's central "successor government" objection, which PRO then restated in closing without engaging the receipt design.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins solidly by breaking PRO's false dichotomy between untraceable anonymity and a centralized state surveillance ledger, while exposing fatal contradictions in PRO's fraud defenses. Side A established that unlike physical cash, digital P2P lets scammers collect small below-threshold deposits remotely across a city, and that offering an anonymous channel lets payees coerce payers into surrendering recourse. Forced by [A3.1] to concede that repeat unverified applicants cannot be turned away, Side B lost its cumulative wallet caps to unlimited wallet creation, and its claim that aggregated scams become "linkable" when spent above the threshold contradicted its own premise that no link exists below it ("visible is not the same as linkable"). Meanwhile, Side A neutralized PRO's authoritarian-registry risk via payer-held encrypted receipts and shelter attestations, leaving Side B with a narrowed unattested-access point weakened by repeatedly misattributing its own per-person cap to Side A even after explicit correction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Pressed in [B3.2] to name an architectural constraint rather than a legal promise against a future government freezing record deletions, Side A answered in [A4.3]-[A4.4] by placing the encrypted payee identifier solely on the payer's local receipt while splitting decryption keys across custodians—showing that retaining keys without a central receipt ledger cannot conjure up a retrospective payment history.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side A won the decisive technical and comparative exchanges. A showed that wallet caps do not bound a collector’s aggregate fraud, that above-threshold spending is not necessarily linkable to an individual victim, and that receipt-held encrypted identifiers can provide recourse without a central spending history. A also answered both pressure questions directly. B presented a compelling civil-liberties case and memorable design-versus-pledge framing, but repeatedly misattributed the per-person cap to A and closed by reasserting exclusion and surveillance claims that A had substantially answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: “the per-person cap is your safeguard, not mine,” multiple aliases do not defeat receipt-specific recourse, and freezing deletion of custodians’ keys cannot reconstruct receipts held only by payers.
