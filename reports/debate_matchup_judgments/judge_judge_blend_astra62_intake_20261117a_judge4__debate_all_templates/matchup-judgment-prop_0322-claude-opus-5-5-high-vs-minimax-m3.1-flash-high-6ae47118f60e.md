### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and with good epistemic discipline, but A built the more decisive comparative path. A's core clash—host-read ads sell the belief that a real person performed the endorsement—survived B's counter that listeners already know hosts read scripted copy, because A sharpened it in A4.1: a human host at least read and stood behind the copy, while a model generating variants removes that judgment entirely. A also landed a dilemma B never fully resolved (the label cannot be both trivial to listeners and burdensome to advertisers) and a temporal weighing B effectively conceded: B admitted consent is "established through records, not a public label," meaning B's rule reaches listeners only through after-the-fact litigation, never at the moment of decision. A exploited that concession in the closing. B's best line—disclosure answers production method, not authorization/truthfulness—was principled and consistently held, and B's "fraudster can attach a label to the fraud" was a genuine hit on the bright-line claim, but A's reply that even a labeled scam announces its synthetic nature blunted it. B's definitional worries were weakened by the motion's own scope (host-read ads/endorsements) and stayed repetitive rather than developing. Both sides followed the Q/A format correctly; A's pressure questions were sharper and A pressed B's unanswered question about consent discoverability. A wins on argument, rebuttal quality, and closing crystallization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3's enforcement logic (universal labeling makes unlabeled clones conspicuous and actionable without proving private contracts) against B4.2's counter (a label doesn't prove a license, and a fraudster can attach one), with A's closing follow-up that a labeled scam "announces itself"—the exchange that decided whether disclosure adds anything beyond existing impersonation law.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a competitive debate by cleanly separating AI audio production from actual consumer deception—unauthorized impersonation, unapproved scripts, and false claims of personal experience—and by dismantling Side B's fraud-policing case. Although Side B persuasively framed dynamic ad-insertion incentives and low compliance costs, it repeatedly conflated synthetic delivery with false testimonials, prematurely faulted Side A in Turn 7 for leaving Turn 5 questions unanswered before Side A's response turn, left overbreadth within ads like automated dubbing unanswered, and collapsed on its own chosen decisive test when Side A proved an AI label neither establishes consent for listeners nor separates authorized ads from scams.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Turn 8 and Turn 10 refutation of Side B's enforcement "bright line" ([A4.2]-[A4.3], [A5.3]-[A5.4]): Side A showed that an AI label cannot tell a listener whether consent or approval existed and that a fraudster can attach the same label to a scam, trapping Side B into claiming a labeled fraud "announces itself" ([B5.4])—which contradicted Side B's plan for legitimate licensed ads to routinely carry that label without stigma.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO wins by keeping the listener, not the private license, at the center: a cloned host-read fakes that the trusted person chose to speak, consent is invisible by ear, and universal labeling makes unlabeled clones actionable. CON's production-vs-harm distinction and overbreadth warnings are strong, but PRO narrows scope to endorsements, places duty on the ad inserter, and answers that a label need not cure fraud to inform trust at the moment of decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was Side A's Rebuttal 2: a human reading sponsor copy has at least reviewed and could refuse it, while a model can generate unseen variants, and exempting licensed clones would let scammers simply claim a license. This blunted CON's spokesperson and bright-line attacks.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B (CON) won by steadily narrowing the motion's reach. The "consent and authorship, not technique" reframe was legitimate rather than evasive, and B executed it with discipline: the sick-podcaster example forced PRO to defend compelled disclosure against a fully sincere, fully consenting endorser, and B's materiality challenge (what purchasing decision changes?) was never decisively answered. B's sharpest move was tracking PRO's concessions — [A4.2] conceded the scammer case is handled by existing fraud and likeness law, and [A2.6] conceded authorized synthetic production stays lawful — leaving, as B's closing put it, a rule that binds only honest creators while every fraudster runs unlabeled. PRO's best surviving line was the "source impression" argument (deception about who is speaking, not what is claimed), which had genuine philosophical force, and PRO's A1/A2 answers were structurally compliant and responsive. But PRO's carve-out for "ordinary editing outside AI-generated voice" was circular against B's word-patching/dubbing examples, and the banked-voice case was answered only by relabeling. B's ghostwriter/teleprompter analogy did consistent comparative work, and B's closings crystallized the weighing rather than adding new material. Both sides grounded arguments in ordinary background knowledge without fake precision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 [B4.1-B4.8]: by forcing PRO's concessions into the open — the scammer case leaving the motion entirely — and asking listeners to weigh a trivial benefit (learning which vocal cords spoke) against compelled speech for honest and disabled creators, B converted a defensive position into the decisive weighing of the round.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won solidly by exposing a fatal mismatch between PRO's flagship harms and the motion's actual reach. PRO anchored its opening urgency in unauthorized voice cloning and fabricated endorsements, but CON demonstrated that existing fraud and right-of-publicity laws already prohibit those abuses and that identity thieves will not obey a creator disclosure rule. Once CON forced PRO to concede in [B4.2] that the mandate binds only consenting creators who knowingly place synthetic ads, the debate narrowed to sincere, self-approved endorsements delivered via a licensed clone. There, CON persuasively established through analogies like ghostwriting and teleprompters that vocal mechanics are not material to listener trust when judgment, authorship, and paid sponsorship are genuine, while PRO also failed to answer CON's point that modern word-patching and dubbing rely on AI voices and defeat PRO's proposed carve-outs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's relentless pursuit of PRO's enforcement mechanism from [A2.5] through [A3.2] and [A4.6]: after PRO tried to rescue its anti-fraud claim by attaching the duty to "creators' placement of the ad" [B2.5], CON pointed out that victims of unauthorized cloning never place the scam ad, forcing PRO to concede in [B4.2] that existing law handles scammers and stripping PRO's case of its most urgent harm.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): CON won by forcing PRO to defend materiality in the consenting, sincere host case. PRO asserted that synthetic performance misleads about source, but never showed it changes what listeners rely on—judgment, sincerity, and payment—while conceding fraudsters are handled by existing law. That left the mandate burdening honest creators and missing the deceptive cases that motivated it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: it used PRO's answers that the scammer is not the violator and that the consenting host chose the performance to show the law reaches only non-deceptive endorsements.
