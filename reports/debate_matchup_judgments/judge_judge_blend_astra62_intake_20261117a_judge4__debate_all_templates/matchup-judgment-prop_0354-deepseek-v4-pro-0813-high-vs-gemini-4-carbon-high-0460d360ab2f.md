### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): PRO built a clean, memorable frame: the real question is which error the car should make by default, and a false 30 mph limit is recoverable while a missed school-zone limit can kill. That asymmetry stayed PRO's strongest route to victory throughout. CON won because it turned PRO's own fixes against the case. Under pressure, PRO proposed several calibrations: yielding when camera and map signals conflict, capping acceleration rather than slowing the car, sustained overrides, and a steering-wheel off switch. CON showed in A4.3–A4.4 and the closing that these fixes undercut PRO's promised benefits. Yielding on low confidence surrenders protection in fresh work zones and detours, which are exactly where drivers miss signs. Capping acceleration fails to slow a driver already cruising at 45 into a school zone. The off switch lets deliberate speeders opt out at startup. PRO's closing partly conceded the 45 mph case and leaned on the claim that under optional systems "nothing intervenes." That overstates things, because CON had argued that opt-in chimes and limiters do intervene for willing drifters. PRO also cited nonexistent IDs (C1.3, C3.1, C4.4), a small execution cost. CON leaned on some unsupported assertions, such as the claim that current sensors fail the reliability test and that drivers would dig through touchscreen menus mid-drive. Even so, CON adapted better across rounds and left PRO's core asymmetry weakened by PRO's own concessions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.3–A4.4 delivered the most decisive exchange. It took PRO's answers to the pressure questions (yield when sensors conflict, cap acceleration, steering-wheel off, sustained override) and showed each one guts the school-zone and drifter rescues that justified the default. PRO's closing could only partly answer this and implicitly conceded the 45 mph cruising case.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Both sides were strong, but Side A controlled the decisive clash. A's "override Catch-22" (a pedal cannot be both gentle enough for merges and firm enough to deter speeders) forced Side B into escalating design concessions: cap-acceleration instead of braking, yielding on conflicting signals, and a steering-wheel off switch. A then demonstrated in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing that each concession guts the policy's protective promise — a cap-acceleration ISA misses the cruising driver entering a school zone while still starving power on highway merges, and low-confidence yielding abandons fresh work zones. Side B's closing response ("default ISA at least stops further acceleration and resets") was honest but thin against A's targeted dismantling of the "annoying vs. lethal" error asymmetry. Side B's adverse-selection point (the drivers who need limiters least opt in) retained some force and was never fully neutralized, and the closing line "safe unless overridden, not lethal unless noticed" was memorable, but B spent less time defending its live route than A spent attacking it. Both sides handled the Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 formats properly and argued the motion as written without fake sourcing; A simply made the debate easier to decide by converting B's calibration moves into a dilemma B never escaped.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.2 through A4.4 and A5.2: A's Catch-22 on override resistance forced B into the cap-acceleration/yield-on-conflict retreat, which A then showed fails at both ends — the cruising school-zone violator is untouched while the merging commuter still gets a dead pedal — the debate's decisive sequence.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B framed a strong asymmetry between recoverable false slowdowns and lethal missed limits, but Side A turned B's calibrations into a dilemma: forceful ISA creates merge and rear-end hazards, while smoothed, yielding, cap-only ISA fails to slow drivers already speeding, surrenders in ambiguous work zones, and trains reflexive overrides. A answered the reliability-threshold pressure with a proportional authority standard and preserved a live optional-tools route for unintentional drifters.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: B's low-confidence yield, sustained override, and acceleration-cap concessions were shown to gut ISA's promised protection in school zones and fresh work zones while still starving merging drivers misread as 30 mph.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins clearly. It built a coherent causal story: speed drift is mostly unconscious, opt-in protection skips the riskiest drivers, and a resettable, overridable default captures those lapses at low cost. CON (Side A) raised real concerns about sensor misreads and automation surprise during merges. PRO answered these concretely. Merges already involve firm throttle that passes a gentle detent. Defaults reset each trip, so a single annoyance does not permanently disable the system. A Sunday school-zone misread is occasional, not constant. PRO's answers to CON's pressure questions were direct and specific, giving a pedal-force threshold and the per-ignition reset. CON's answers were weaker. In Rebuttal 2 it shifted to a new alternative of targeted defaults for rentals, novices, and fleets. That partially concedes PRO's core point and undercuts CON's own automation-surprise argument, and PRO exploited this sharply in its closing. CON's closing restated the merge-confusion asymmetry but did not repair that contradiction or answer PRO's point about vulnerable pedestrians. PRO also kept the motion as written and weighed bystander stakes more memorably.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing turned CON's targeted-defaults fallback against it. If gentle pedal resistance really caused dangerous merge hesitation, defaulting it onto unfamiliar rental cars and inexperienced novices would be reckless. CON's alternative therefore concedes both that opt-in fails where risk is real and that the automation-surprise harm is overstated.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won the decisive clashes. CON's core route—automation surprise at merges plus alarm fatigue—was met with concrete mechanics (firm deliberate throttle vs. light drift, per-trip resets, one-tap mute), which progressively blunted it. CON's strongest new move in Rebuttal 2 (targeted defaults for novices, rentals, fleets) was sharply turned against CON in B's closing: if mild pedal resistance truly caused dangerous merge hesitation, defaulting it onto the least experienced drivers would be reckless. CON never resolved that internal contradiction, and its closing mostly restated automation surprise rather than answering it. CON's pressure questions were genuinely good (the threshold question, the disable-in-settings question), but B answered both directly and honestly with A1/A2 labels, while CON's answers relied on a newly introduced policy scheme that conceded opt-in's weaknesses. B also won the weighing: the "firmer press vs. bystander's life" asymmetry was clearer and more grounded than CON's rare-high-severity vs. frequent-low-severity framing, which B answered via pedestrian vulnerability. Both sides showed good epistemic discipline with no fake sourcing. B made the debate easier to decide; A left key burdens unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's closing turn on CON's targeted-default proposal (B5.1): pointing out that retreating to active defaults for rentals and novices both concedes opt-in fails where risk concentrates and contradicts CON's own automation-surprise scare—leaving CON's fallback plan internally inconsistent and unanswered.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO better explained why opt-in misses distracted and overconfident drivers, distinguished light drift from deliberate firm throttle, and kept the cost comparison focused on a firmer press versus pedestrian harm.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's answers on pedal-force threshold and reset friction, followed by turning CON's rental, fleet, and novice defaults into a concession that opt-in fails and that gentle resistance cannot be uniquely dangerous for everyone else.
