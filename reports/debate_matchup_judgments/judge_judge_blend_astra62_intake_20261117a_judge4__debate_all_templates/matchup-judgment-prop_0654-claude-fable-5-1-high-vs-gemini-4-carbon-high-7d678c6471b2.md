### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won a technically sharp, closely fought debate. A's opening framing ("a rating is not a measurement") was excellent and its Fern Hollow pressure question was strong, but A's two best routes were eventually neutralized. First, the I-35W/modal-monitoring line—A's claim that accelerometers catch undersized gusset plates via global frequency shifts—was decisively answered by B's point that daily temperature swings alter global stiffness far more than a local flaw until collapse is nearly underway, forcing either false positives or silence; A never answered this. Second, A's "the designer's model was wrong" attack was turned by B's closing contradiction: if models can't be trusted, scaled sensor placement freezes the same narrow guess into concrete. A's scope-shrinking of "all" (from every asset to "critical structures" and "operating units") also looked like retreat under sustained pressure. A's capital-vs-maintenance budget distinction and live-trend "forces a decision" framing were its best surviving material, but B's "neither a clipboard nor a dashboard pours concrete" and the mandatory-thresholds rebuttal held the funding clash. Both sides used the A1/A2 and Q1/Q2 formats cleanly; B simply landed more unanswered damage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.3) was the decisive exchange: the observation that outdoor temperature swings mask local damage in global frequency readings until collapse is imminent directly broke A's modal-monitoring answer for small crossings and gusset plates, and A's closing never repaired it.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won on the motion as written. A offered a vivid case that continuous measurement beats calendar inspection, but the sensor package kept expanding when coverage was pressed and shrinking when cost was pressed, and the late rewrite of "all" grid assets into critical structures conceded B's core point. B also left live the harder burdens: known decay was already ignored at Fern Hollow, alarms do not pour concrete, thin probes still miss unpredicted local flaws, and temperature can swamp the global-frequency story A never answered in closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal, which tied the mutating cheap-sensor package to the unanswered temperature-confounding objection and treated A's narrowing of "all" power-grid assets as a concession.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won narrowly by making the central clash clearer: periodic inspection gives intermittent, deferable snapshots, while embedded monitoring gives continuous trends and action thresholds. B also kept inspectors in the model and plausibly scaled the mandate to asset type. Side A landed strong scope, cost, maintenance, and modal-monitoring objections, but never fully neutralized B’s Fern Hollow argument that known poor ratings still ended in collapse, nor its broader framing that guessing where sensors belong replicates the failure risk.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): A narrow win for PRO. The debate resolved into the definitional clash over "all," and A's reading — universal coverage with scalable complexity, legal floor never zero — is a fair defense of the motion as written, anchored by the apt brakes-on-all-vehicles analogy. B's counters ("wheelbarrow gets none," the shed) subtly swap in assets outside the motion's categories of bridges, dams, and grid assets, which all carry real consequence. A also won key technical exchanges: passive embedded fiber answered the obsolescence attack, global-behavior sensors answered the "unexpected failure point" objection, and Miami being brand new damaged B's "new assets are safest" line. B landed real hits — the institutional-failure reading of Miami, unpriced recurring costs on powered pole sensors, alert fatigue — and B's closing crystallization was the most memorable rhetoric in the round. But B's strongest route, "PRO stopped defending 'all,'" depends on conflating engineers choosing sensor density with engineers choosing whether sensors exist, a distinction A drew explicitly and B never squarely broke. A's closing turn — that CON's zero-floor fallbacks (tripping breakers, post-flood walk-arounds) only trigger after failure begins — left B's safety story looking reactive, which was the decisive unresolved pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The definitional exchange in Rebuttal 2: B's A1/A2 answers (event-triggered scour inspection, substation protection already catching faulted lines, tiered codes like rebar) were crisp, but A's counter in closing — that B's fallbacks discover the failing pole only after live wires fall and spark a wildfire, whereas an embedded gauge flags a leaning pole days before it snaps — converted B's own concession into the strongest single turn of the debate.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): CON won by keeping the ballot’s word “all” as the decisive burden and showing that PRO’s workable case repeatedly collapsed into scaled, consequence-tiered monitoring plus institutional reform—the position CON defended from the opening. PRO made a serious pre-failure case for poles and scour, but never cleanly priced universal hardware, data, cybersecurity, and false-alarm burdens or showed why a non-zero floor on every culvert and pad-mounted asset beats event-triggered inspection and risk-based codes. CON’s answers to both pressure questions were direct and left that comparison clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s first rebuttal was decisive: it treated “universal coverage, not identical complexity” as a concession that tiers are set by judgment, then used building-code exemptions and unpriced small-asset costs to separate PRO’s workable version from the motion’s “all.”
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): B won narrowly by defending a plausible universal minimum-sensor floor rather than identical instrumentation, directly answering the “all” objection with asset-class baselines, and keeping the debate focused on hidden damage, blind intervals, and short-term incentives. A’s textual and cost pressure was sharp, but its low-consequence analogies weakened against bridges, dams, and energized grid assets, and it never fully neutralized B’s scalable-sensor and lifecycle-safety case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The tiering clash: A pressed who decides sensor tiers and claimed PRO had conceded risk-based judgment; B answered with mandatory minimums where the floor is never zero, then A’s wheelbarrow/shed analogy let B reply that traffic-bearing bridges, dams, and live grid assets are not trivial objects.
