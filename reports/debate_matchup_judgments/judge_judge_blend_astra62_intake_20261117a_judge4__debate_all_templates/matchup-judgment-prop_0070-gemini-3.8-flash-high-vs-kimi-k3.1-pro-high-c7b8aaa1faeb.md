A's pressure question on the evidence threshold for exemption forced B to concede (B4.2) that adaptation can be genuinely sustainable in some cities — a concession A then used effectively in rebuttal and closing to argue the proportionate policy is viability assessment, not a universal mandate, and PRO's closing never returned to repair this overbreadth problem. PRO (B) built a compelling inevitability/urgency frame and a real-world-plausible funding mechanism (conditional grants tied to certified hazard plans), and its equity argument about insurer withdrawal stranding the poor was never fully neutralized, but B's closing recycled the physics/lead-time framing without resolving the "paper mandate" and overbreadth objections that had accumulated against it. A's progression across rounds was tighter and more responsive to the specific wording of the motion, giving a marginally cleaner path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question on the evidence threshold for exemption (A3.2), answered by B's viability-based threshold (B4.2) conceding adaptation can be sustainable — which A then weaponized in Rebuttal 2 and Closing to expose the motion's overbreadth, a point PRO never fully re-addressed.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by keeping the decisive distinction live: early preparation and contingent retreat options do not require a mandatory relocation premise, and the mandate itself does not supply money, creditor buy-in, or receiving-community consent. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): The debate centered on whether mandatory relocation planning necessarily causes immediate capital flight and self-fulfilling blight. A's strongest weapon was the market-collapse mechanism, but PRO repeatedly narrowed the motion to conditional, reviewable, trigger-based planning that maintains defenses and services until thresholds are met, and pressed that unplanned retreat already suffers market discipline (insurers repricing, lenders already pulling back from repeatedly flooded areas) without any official announcement. CON never convincingly explained why a published, conditional trigger framework—distinct from an immediate condemnation notice—would cause lenders to freeze mortgages today rather than price risk incrementally as they already do absent any plan; this is the load-bearing claim of CON's whole case, and it was asserted with confidence but not demonstrated against PRO's distinction between 'planning' and 'announcing abandonment.' PRO's answers to pressure questions (A1/A2) directly engaged, offering concrete safeguards (maintain defenses until triggers, fund compensation first, use criteria beyond property values) that blunted the redlining and capital-flight charges, even if not airtight. CON's answers to PRO's Q1/Q2 restated the market-collapse assertion without resolving why existing market discipline under the status quo is preferable to a structured, contestable version of the same pressure. PRO's closing more effectively exploited this asymmetry, framing CON's position as reliant on an illusory 'guarantee' of non-disclosure actually preserving value, which it does not. CON's case also carries tension: it praises Tokyo/Netherlands-style defense while implying mandatory planning precludes defense, but PRO rightly notes planning can select defense where viable. Overall PRO did more work comparing outcomes and answering the decisive mechanism question, while CON largely repeated the capital-flight claim without fully rebutting PRO's distinction between disclosure-triggered collapse and risk-driven market behavior that occurs regardless.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's answer to CON's Q1 (A1 in Turn 7) distinguishing conditional, staged triggers from condemnation, combined with the point that insurers/lenders already reprice risk absent any plan — this most directly undercut CON's core mechanism claim that mere planning causes immediate fiduciary collapse.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON won a competitive debate by keeping capital flight and self-fulfilling disinvestment as a live structural consequence of formal relocation triggers, not mere bad implementation. PRO’s conditional-plan and justice frame was coherent and answered capture concerns well, but never fully escaped the multi-decade underwriting logic after conceding cities cannot lock private lenders in place; CON’s comparative case that unfunded backstops turn foresight into pre-blight therefore remained the cleaner reason to reject the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2/closing use of PRO’s “no city can guarantee private lending” concession to collapse conditional planning into immediate economic eviction.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully arguing that the economic and physical harms Side B fears are already occurring due to climate risk and insurance market realities, meaning official silence only delays honest decisions and worsens post-disaster triage. Side A also provides a more compelling account of how mandatory planning protects marginalized communities through enforceable criteria, compared to the highly unequal nature of discretionary post-disaster recovery. Side B's economic attack was sharp, but relied on an unrealistic assumption that capital markets would ignore physical vulnerabilities and insurance trends if the city simply avoided drawing a retreat map.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal in Turn 3 (A2.2), which dismantled Side B's market argument by pointing out that official silence does not stop insurance withdrawals or physical damage from destroying property values, effectively reframing Side B's "self-fulfilling prophecy" as merely a delay of inevitable market corrections.
