### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): B won by turning A's own concessions into the decisive clash. A's case rested on custody-without-authority, distribution fairness among absent claimants, and settlement leverage. B neutralized the leverage point by showing the invader gains nothing from signing when the settlement would consume the whole pot, and then exposed the asymmetry in A's honest concession (A4.1): A would allow a conditional return of funds if affected states judge peace worth it, yet even a unanimous victim petition cannot release a dollar—so the only operative difference is "the conqueror's signature," a framing A never escaped. B also answered A's pressure on competing courts (unambiguous-proof threshold, interpleader-style pro rata with reserved shares) and A's double-counting worry (immunity blocks the second court collecting anyway). A's best surviving route was the epistemic asymmetry of "verified damage" systematically undercounting occupied cities, which B's "conservative reserves" answer only partially resolved, and A's closing crystallized it well. But A's repeated retreat to principle (title, mandate) while conceding indefinite freeze "costs the victim dearly" left B with the cleaner reason to vote PRO. Both sides argued at a high level with disciplined, citation-free reasoning.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1–B4.5) was the decisive exchange: by answering both pressure questions directly (unambiguous aggression threshold; pro rata with reserved shares) and then weaponizing A's own concessions—that immunity makes A's "agreed credit" worthless to the invader, and that blocking courts overrides the victim's own choice—B converted A's honest answers into the debate's deciding asymmetry, which B's closing then sealed with the "conqueror's signature" line.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A won by turning Side B’s concession—that courts should still deny a victim’s petition indefinitely if grants fail and no settlement exists—into the decisive comparison. A showed that demanding a binding settlement can function as an aggressor veto, while a narrower judicial permission (clear aggression, victim petition, audited funds, pro rata holdbacks, and staged public lifelines) answers the worst distribution risks without guarding the invader’s reserves as cities remain ruined. Side B kept a serious live objection about title, irreversibility, and verification asymmetry, but it did not outweigh that clearer reason to permit the transfers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s leverage critique, sharpened by its Q1 and Side B’s Rebuttal 2 answer that the court should deny relief even at great cost to the victim, then crystallized in A’s closing: the practical difference was the conqueror’s signature, not fairness among victims.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): B built the stronger comparative case by framing judicial permission as optional, bounded restitution rather than automatic confiscation. A’s title, competing-claimant, and occupied-territory objections were serious and well argued, but B answered with strict aggression findings, unified funds, proportional allocation, notice, and reserved tranches. Most decisively, A conceded that its rule could require indefinite protection of the aggressor’s assets even when the victim petitions and grants fail, leaving reconstruction dependent on donors or the aggressor’s settlement consent. B made that status quo look less defensible than imperfect but reversible-in-stages judicial administration.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure question about indefinite freezing forced A’s explicit A1 concession in Rebuttal 2. B then converted that answer into its strongest weighing point: A permits negotiated returns to the aggressor while categorically blocking court-supervised reconstruction, making the decisive distinction the conqueror’s signature rather than fairness to victims.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by progressively narrowing to a defensible core — a binding treaty award, paid first from frozen commercial assets, in staged installments, with confidential judicial stays for diplomacy — and showing that Side A opposes even that clearest case. Side A's strongest pressure (litigation takes years, so courts cannot fund urgent repairs; B conceded this) landed, but B recovered well by reframing the stakes as cost allocation: grants and bridge loans merely change which innocent public pays, while collection puts the bill on the aggressor — a route A never truly neutralized, answering only that allied taxpayers "can absorb" it. A's systemic risks (reciprocity, reserve flight, treaty exit) were plausible but speculative, and B's responses — a freeze already means political loss of access, pretext seizures are already possible, consent to adjudication already existed — held up. B's staged-stay mechanism plus the "counting the same reserves twice" rebuttal blunted A's leverage argument, and B's closing weighing ("uncertainty alone supplies no endpoint") gave the cleaner reason to vote. Both sides followed format honestly; B simply made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A3.1's trap — that B's own jurisdictional discipline delays payouts for years, killing the "rebuild now" promise — was A's best moment, and B4.1's honest concession plus the pivot to debt retirement was the decisive recovery; B then crystallized the cost-allocation clash in B5.1-5.2, which A's "allied budgets can absorb it" answer never fully answered.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won by using A's own concessions to shrink court collection from urgent reconstruction into rare, late cost-shifting after treaty awards. That limited benefit did not outweigh live costs to leverage, future treaty consent, retaliation, and reserve confidence, while allied bridge funding plus maintained freezes remained a workable alternative. A's staging, commercial-asset priority, and confidential stays were serious mitigations, but they left the diplomacy-timing and precedent problems intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal was decisive: it turned A's admission that judgments cannot fund wartime repairs into a reframing of the proposal as late accounting, then showed why judicial stays still spend leverage before negotiators know what will buy peace.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): A won a clear but competitive debate by exploiting the gap between B’s urgent-reconstruction framing and its concession that courts cannot fund urgent repairs before potentially lengthy litigation. A then compared the remaining benefit—allocating bridge-financing costs in relatively rare enforceable cases—with systemic risks to treaty consent, reserve exposure, retaliation, and diplomatic leverage. B offered the cleaner moral account of who should pay and defended a compelling narrow case involving commercial assets and a binding award, but never fully neutralized A’s concerns about future treaty avoidance and preliminary peace negotiations. Some of A’s systemic claims were assertive rather than demonstrated, keeping the margin modest.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s pressure questions forced B to concede that grants or bridge loans must handle urgent reconstruction and to specify a judicial stay process for diplomacy. A’s second rebuttal used those answers to recast collection as a late-stage financing mechanism and to argue that requiring concrete ceasefire prospects before stays would protect leverage too late. B’s response that debt allocation still determines who ultimately pays remained strong, but did not erase the narrowed practical benefit.
