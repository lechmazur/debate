### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): PRO wins on the core comparative mechanism. It argued that a frozen retirement age creates costs that compound faster than the economy. Progressive base-broadening, by contrast, merely grows with national income, so systems eventually fall back on payroll taxes. CON's best answer was that uncapping and taxing capital are "structural," but its closing then appeared to concede that a frozen age compounds costs faster than the economy, which undercut its own reply. PRO also turned CON's fairness case. A frozen age hands the largest lifetime public subsidies to long-lived affluent professionals. Under indexing, worn-out workers are protected through disability and seniority pathways. CON never directly answered that turn. CON's A2 sidestepped the question of why it would keep subsidizing healthy white-collar retirements. CON's labor-market and cost-shifting points remained partly live. PRO's "massive net savings" and "healthy majority" claims were asserted more than demonstrated, so the margin is not wide. CON's regressivity framing was rhetorically strong but became repetitive and did not engage the subsidy reframe.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing response to the claim that base-broadening "grows with national income" was the decisive exchange. PRO argued that revenue tracking the economy cannot cover an obligation that outgrows it, which reinstated payroll-tax reliance as the endpoint of CON's model. CON's final speech effectively acknowledged this premise rather than refuting it.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A won a close debate by defending the fiscal logic of indexing better than Side B defended progressive alternatives. A showed that payroll-tax reliance punishes labor, while frozen ages make pension costs grow faster than GDP; B’s structural base-broadening answer did not fully meet that demographic-compounding objection. A also turned the fairness clash: frozen ages give the largest public subsidies to affluent professionals, while seniority/disability/bridge safeguards can protect shorter-lived manual workers. B’s regressivity warning remained live but was substantially blunted, so A’s path was cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s reframing that freezing the headline age, not indexing, creates the regressive windfall by taxing ordinary workers to fund long affluent retirements most damaged B’s central fairness objection and set up A’s win.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A won the decisive clash: the compounding-arithmetic argument that progressive revenue growing only with national income cannot cover obligations that outgrow the economy as the retiree-to-worker ratio worsens. Side B's structural-base-broadening answer asserted that productivity value "can be captured," but never squared that with benefit obligations rising relative to the wage base, and B's late appeal to slower benefit indexing quietly smuggled in a benefit-cut lever that concedes A's framing. On fairness, B's regressive-burden case was strong, but A's reversal—showing a frozen age showers the largest lifetime subsidies on long-lived affluent professionals—plus concrete mitigation via seniority pathways, disability insurance, and bridge support blunted it without being fully neutralized. Both sides followed format honestly, answered pressure directly with A1/A2 labels, and avoided fake sourcing. A's speeches were slightly better at narrowing and weighing the dispute across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure Q1 and closing crystallization: forcing B to explain how one-time progressive fixes (uncapping, capital taxes, top-end trims) could finance perpetually compounding longevity costs. B's B4.1 answer—that base-broadening grows with national income—was directly undercut by A's closing line that revenue growing with the economy cannot cover an obligation that outgrows it, the exchange that decided the debate.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B wins on the fairness clash. B set the main comparison early and carried it to the end: indexing takes equal years from unequal lifespans, while payroll taxes scale with earnings. The 72-versus-88 arithmetic made the point concrete and easy to follow. A tried to reverse the regressivity charge by pointing to the status quo. That reply never explained how raising the age reduces the upward transfer, and B showed it intensifies it. A's own Pressure Question about the "double levy" backfired. B simply agreed that the double levy falls on indexing, which turned the question against A. B also exploited A's concession that productivity outpaced median wages. B argued that this makes uncapped payroll taxes on high salaries the right fiscal tool. A's reply on the tax base, that pensions face a ratio problem lifting caps cannot fix, was competent. However, B's point that existing caps are a policy choice, not a natural ceiling, blunted it. A's strongest asset was the "fix the denominator" framing in the closing. It came late, and it leaned on safeguards that B had already pressed as either too costly to fund or too thin to protect worn-out workers. A answered that dilemma only partially. B also turned A's warning that young workers face "retirements that start later and may never arrive" back against indexing, which by design pushes retirement later.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to A's Q1 (B4.1) was the most decisive exchange. B accepted that indexing is a double levy on short-lived workers, who lose benefit years while paying in longer. That converted A's attempted gotcha into a restatement of B's central regressivity argument. A never squarely answered it, falling back on the status-quo deflection in the closing.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won the decisive fairness-and-workability clash. A's structural denominator argument was real, but B neutralized it enough with progressive payroll financing, productivity gains, and cap-lifting. More importantly, B showed that longevity indexing double-penalizes shorter-lived manual workers, while A's targeted bridge/disability protections could not rescue the exhausted middle without erasing fiscal savings or locking people into poverty. B gave the cleaner reason to vote against age indexing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's arithmetic rebuttal was the most decisive: a short-lived worker loses three of seven retirement years while an affluent retiree loses only three of twenty-three, making longevity indexing a double levy rather than a fairer solution.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Both sides debated cleanly with proper Q/A format and no fake sourcing. Side A built a credible sustainability case (contributor-to-benefit-year ratio, informality costs of taxing work) and a sharp "denominator vs numerator" frame. But Side B controlled the decisive clash: the unequal-lifespans arithmetic (an executive losing 3 of 23 benefit years vs. a roofer losing 3 of 7) made indexing look regressive in a concrete, memorable way, and A's counter— that the status quo also redistributes upward—was blunted by B's point that payroll contributions scale with earnings, especially with lifted caps. B also won the exchange over the bridge-employment dilemma: A's answer that phased retirement plus disability rules catch the exhausted middle was pressed hard by B's observation that strict disability gates and employer ageism strand those in between, and A never resolved this. On A's informality claim, B's rebuttal that top-heavy formal salaries won't flee to cash-in-hand work was more specific than A's repeated assertion. B's answers to pressure questions (B4.1, B4.2) were direct and damaging, and B's closing crystallized "you can tax unequal dollars progressively but cannot take equal years fairly from unequal lives"—the cleanest weighing in the debate. A remains strong on intergenerational solvency, but that route was more contested than B's fairness route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1/B4.3: B embraced A's own "double levy" framing from Q1, confirming that a worker dying at 72 forfeits nearly half their retirement while paying three extra years of taxes, then extended the attack by showing A's disability safety-valve only rescues those who medically cannot work at all, stranding the exhausted middle with reduced benefits and no wages.
