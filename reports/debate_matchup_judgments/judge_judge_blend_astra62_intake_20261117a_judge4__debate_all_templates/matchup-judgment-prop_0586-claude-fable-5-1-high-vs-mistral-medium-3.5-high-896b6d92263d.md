### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A (PRO) built the cleaner path to victory. The core "bite" mechanism — that a large uniform increase imposes the highest burden on low-wage regions with no pricing power — went essentially unanswered; Side B repeatedly asserted "economy-wide price adjustments" as the equilibrium without ever explaining how a Mississippi diner absorbs the same dollar floor as a Seattle hotel, and A's closing line ("identical pressure is not identical capacity") crystallized that gap memorably. A also won the capture clash by inverting it: the frozen federal minimum ($7.25) shows the legislative instrument is the more captured one, while the NY board delivered $15 — a point B never rebutted. A dismantled B's capture dilemma (boards search for the middle number) and answered the coverage attack squarely with the fact that gig, agricultural, and domestic workers are already excluded from the statutory floor. B's boundary-distortion argument was its best material, and A's response (concentrated restructuring under a $5 gap implies worse restructuring under a universal $5 jump) was a genuine comparative turn B never fully escaped. Both sides followed format (proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2), and both used plausible background knowledge without fake precision. B's closing was largely recap, while A's closing sharpened the decisive concessions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B2.5's capture dilemma versus A4.3–A4.5: B argued boards must either deliver trivial raises or job-killing ones, and A dissolved it by showing boards exist precisely to find the number in between — then flipped the capture point entirely by noting the uniform instrument's actual record ($7.25 frozen since 2009, $2.13 tipped minimum) versus the NY board's delivered $15. B never recovered, and A's closing sealed it by noting B's own concession that employer representation "tempers" targets, which is lower bite and therefore fewer job losses — the motion itself.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the motion's comparison intact: a calibrated board can raise the next dollar with a lower bite where employers and customers cannot absorb it, while a large uniform jump cannot. Side A raised real coverage, capture, and boundary-distortion problems, but never neutralized identical pressure versus unequal capacity. B answered both pressure questions directly, mirrored the uniform floor's own exemptions and freeze, and treated fairness gaps as secondary to employment effects. A's closing restated the equal-cost price-pass-through claim and twice mislabeled its own restructuring point as B's concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B turned A's boundary argument—if a modest covered/uncovered gap drives cuts, a universal high bite drives more—and closed on the point A never met: identical cost pressure is not identical ability to pass it on.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A kept the debate centered on the motion’s comparative test, directly answered both pressure questions, and effectively challenged B’s claims about universal coverage and capture. B’s covered-versus-uncovered competitiveness argument was a substantial counter, but B too categorically assumed that uniform cost increases naturally produce price adjustments rather than layoffs. A’s distinction between identical nominal cost pressure and unequal capacity to absorb or pass through that pressure provided the cleaner comparative weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B4.2’s uniform-cost argument against A5.1’s response: identical pressure across competitors does not give a rural low-margin employer the same pricing capacity as a high-income-market employer, preserving A’s calibration advantage.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B (CON) won by converting PRO's own defense into a concession. B's core mechanism—narrow coverage invites substitution at the sector boundary and labor-for-capital substitution, while broad floors raise all rivals' costs together—was never seriously damaged. A's claim that grouping McDonald's and Burger King means customers "face the same price signal" handed B the decisive trap: that is the broad-floor mechanism applied to a smaller circle, and B pressed it in pressure questions and both rebuttals. A's answer that delis "can be covered by their own sectoral board" conceded the recursion B exploited memorably ("a broad floor assembled badly"). B also neutralized A's two evidence pillars: New York's premium converged with the statewide floor, and California produced carve-outs. B's closing reframe—boards that raise pay "only where it is sustainable" deliver fewer dollars raised, not fewer losses per dollar—was the cleanest weighing in the debate. A executed the required A1/A2 structure and offered plausible historical examples, but mostly repeated NY/Seattle points rather than resolving the substitution clash, and its metric shifted between "fewer absolute losses" and "fewer losses per dollar raised" under pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1-B4.6): answering A's evidence demand by showing New York's premium was a converging ramp, then seizing on A's "delis can be covered by their own sectoral board" as a concession that sectoral design recursively rebuilds a broad floor with carve-outs attached—this collapsed A's central mechanism and set up the winning closing reframe.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by turning Side B's own "same price signal" defense into the case for broad coverage, then showing that cross-sector and capital substitution, plus jobs lost per dollar raised, favor across-the-board floors. Side B never neutralized that metric, leaned on a converging New York ramp and absolute-loss arithmetic, and its closing retreat to raising pay only where sustainable conceded fewer dollars raised rather than fewer losses. A's pressure questions and the "own board" concession trap made the choice clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal and closing: treating B's offer to give delis their own board as rebuilding a broad floor badly, and reading "raise only where sustainable" as skipping workers rather than protecting them.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B turned A’s own “same price signal” defense into the decisive comparison: sector boards still leave cheaper cross-sector substitutes and automation opportunities, while broad floors align more competitors. B also exposed A’s coverage-based “fewer absolute losses” metric and showed that tailoring raises only to sustainable sectors can skip vulnerable workers rather than protect them. A’s New York and New Deal evidence remained weakly established, and its closing mostly repeated earlier claims without neutralizing these attacks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttals that sector-wide alignment merely redraws the substitution boundary, and that giving every uncovered substitute its own board rebuilds a broad floor “badly,” decisively undercut A’s central mechanism; the critique of fewer absolute losses as an artifact of narrower coverage compounded the damage.
