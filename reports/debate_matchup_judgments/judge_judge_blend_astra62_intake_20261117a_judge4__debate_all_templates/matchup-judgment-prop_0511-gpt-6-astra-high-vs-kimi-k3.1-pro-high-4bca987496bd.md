### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly without fake sourcing, but B built the sharper comparative case. B's opening immediately reframed the clash correctly (LVT's "no penalty on improvement" was being compared implicitly to a building tax rather than to the actual rivals, transaction taxes and rent caps), and B steelmanned those rivals (modest transaction levy funding infrastructure, credibly exempted rent caps) rather than attacking strawmen. Under pressure questioning, B extracted a key concession from A: that an unavoidable land-tax bill "does not increase the construction project's operating return," only changes holding economics. B then used that concession decisively in Rebuttal 2 and the closing to show that ownership pressure need not translate into additional homes where other constraints (zoning, infrastructure, financing) bind — directly undercutting A's supply mechanism. A's closing tried to recast this as "dual alignment" without fully repairing the causal gap, while B's closing sharpened the live clash by demanding A establish "more," not merely "different." A's case remained coherent and well-structured, and the opening's clipped format is a real execution cost. On balance, B did more of the comparative work the debate asked for and landed the most consequential rebuttal exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2/A1 exchange: pressing A's concession that an identical land-tax bill does not raise a project's operating return, which B leveraged to argue that ownership-pressure need not create additional housing where other constraints bind — the single most damaging blow to A's supply mechanism.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the motion’s dual test intact and easier to decide: LVT is the only instrument whose supply and finance effects point the same way, while transaction taxes buy volatile receipts with transfer friction and rent caps buy tenant protection without general revenue or added homes. B landed the sharper technical hits on mechanism and net accounting—especially that an unchanged bill is not a construction subsidy and that admin/deferrals plus avoided emergency costs must be netted—but mostly showed LVT’s gains can shrink under binding constraints rather than that the alternatives win the combined comparison. A correctly held the burden to comparative improvement, not guaranteed construction under every constraint, and used B’s own sale-pressure pathway against the transaction tax. Across the full exchange A’s structural dual-alignment frame survived; B’s “alignment ≠ more” closing was strong but did not reverse the ranking on the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first-rebuttal capture that B’s fallback (force a sale) collides with the transaction tax B defends, paired against B’s strongest technical hit that an identical land bill does not raise a project’s incremental return for a solvent owner.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A delivered a decisive analytical critique by exposing a flaw in Side B's framing: Side B's primary supply argument—that LVT does not penalize building improvements—relies on a comparison to property taxes, not the transaction taxes or rent caps specified in the motion. Side A convincingly applied microeconomic logic to show that a fixed land tax does not alter the marginal return on construction, effectively neutralizing Side B's direct supply mechanism. Furthermore, Side A provided a more rigorous weighing of public finance trade-offs, successfully defending the targeted utility of transaction taxes for infrastructure funding and the fiscal value of avoided emergency costs under rent caps. While Side B was highly articulate and defended the comparative nature of the motion well, they could not fully recover from Side A's dismantling of their core causal story.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2, specifically the argument that a carrying charge is a fixed cost that makes the owner poorer without increasing the incremental return of construction, and the sharp observation that "removing fiscal penalties on improvement" falsely imports a comparison with building taxes rather than the actual alternatives in the motion.
