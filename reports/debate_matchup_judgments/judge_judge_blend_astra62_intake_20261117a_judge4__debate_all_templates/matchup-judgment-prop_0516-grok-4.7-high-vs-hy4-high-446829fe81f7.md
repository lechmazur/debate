### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): CON ran the sharper analysis. It showed that a passport ceiling creates no beds, that price still rations the remaining rooms, and that the cap faces a dilemma: it is either symbolic or a fiscal shock. Its strongest move was the redundancy dilemma: once PRO accepts the verified-bed test, the "international" label seems to do no work. That strategy cost CON on the motion as written. The proposition contrasts capping international recruitment with leaving capacity unconstrained. In the questions round, CON answered that no further international offers proceed once beds run out ("None") and that zero unhoused students should be recruited for revenue ("Zero"). That amounts to accepting a severe-shortage limit on international recruitment, even if CON applies the same limit to everyone. PRO adapted well. It narrowed the cap to unhoused offers, which spared the housed students whose fees fund dorms and aid and so blunted CON's fiscal-harm objection. It also said domestic offers could be covered. PRO then used CON's answers to argue that CON's alternative is the cap under another name. CON's rejoinder, that a universal rule is not an "international recruitment cap," is reasonable. Even so, it leans on reading the motion as a nationality-only quota, which the motion does not strictly require. On the motion's own decision rule, PRO's path is slightly cleaner, though CON's rebuttals were often more incisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's dilemma against B's use of A's own answers. CON argued in A4.5 and A5.4 that if a binding bed rule "is the cap," then an international-specific cap does no independent work. PRO turned this in B5.1 and B5.2, pointing out that CON's answers ("None" further international offers once beds run out, "Zero" additional unhoused recruits) concede exactly the limit on international recruitment that the motion demands over unconstrained capacity.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): B wins the central policy comparison. The proposition does not require a separate passport-only quota or prohibit constraints on domestic enrollment. B therefore reasonably defends a housing-linked recruitment ceiling that exempts housed students. A’s universal verified-bed rule also imposes that ceiling on international recruitment, weakening its claimed alternative. A answered pressure directly and raised credible targeting and financing concerns, but continued attacking an additional nationality quota that B did not require. B’s suggestion of an implementation gap remained unsupported, but its core route to victory survived without it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange connected A’s answers—“None” and “Zero” additional unhoused recruits—to the proposed capacity limit. Combined with B’s exemption for housed students, this substantially neutralized A’s claim that the cap necessarily sacrifices housing-financing students or adds an arbitrary passport barrier.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins a sharp, competitive debate by holding firm to the proposition's comparative decision rule and exposing an internal contradiction in Side A's case. The motion asks whether public universities should cap international recruitment during severe housing shortages rather than leave capacity unconstrained. Side A explicitly rejected that comparison ([A1.1], [A2.6], [A5.5]), proposing instead to waitlist every unhoused international, domestic, transfer, and graduate applicant. Through pointed pressure questions ([B3.1]-[B3.2]), Side B forced Side A to concede that once verified beds are exhausted, zero unhoused international offers should remain unconstrained and zero unhoused students should be recruited to keep revenue smooth ([A4.1], [A4.3]). That concession abandoned unconstrained capacity, undercut Side A's earlier objections that sudden caps inflict fiscal shocks and fail to build beds or end price rationing, and ignored Side B's institutional point that public universities cannot treat domestic resident access as optional the way they treat discretionary international targets ([B1.6], [B4.2]). Side A kept the round close by forcing Side B to spare housed international students and rightly criticizing Side B's overreach in calling a universal bed rule "the cap under another name" ([B4.6], [A5.4]), but Side B maintained the cleaner, more coherent route to victory on the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's turn on Side A's "symbolic vs. fiscal shock" dilemma in [B2.5]—arguing that if only a large cut moves rents then unconstrained growth already mattered, and if revenue stability requires exceeding local housing capacity then stability is bought by displacement—paired with [B3.1]-[B3.2], which forced Side A in [A4.1]-[A4.3] to concede zero unhoused recruitment.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a competitive debate. Its timing argument ran throughout and was never neutralized: housing supply is slow, but a university controls the actively recruited international inflow this cycle. PRO also gave a plausible marginal-demand rationale: fewer bidders means less pressure. CON (Side A) was sharp and rhetorically strong. It pressed hard on nationality targeting, arguing a cap leaves domestic demand open, and it raised the risk of a "standing bar" that never lifts. But CON's preferred alternative, conditioning every offer on a verified bed, would itself constrain international recruitment. That makes it closer to a broader version of the motion than a reason to leave capacity unconstrained, and PRO's "coexistence" answer exposed this. CON's purpose-built-stock mechanism was speculative. PRO's dilemma defused it: if the beds exist, recruit up to them; if not, recruits add bidders to the open market. PRO's A1/A2 answers were direct and supplied a concrete off-switch. CON's second rebuttal was clipped for length, which is a real execution cost. CON's "the off-switch is the shortage itself" landed but does not show that unconstrained recruitment is better during the shortage. Grounding was disciplined on both sides.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response to the purpose-built-stock turn was the most decisive exchange [B4.5, B5.2]. If recruits really use dedicated student beds, recruitment continues up to those verified beds; if not, they add bidders to the open market. This turned CON's most original mechanism into support for PRO's capacity test. CON's reply that "beds already verified make this a capacity rule, not an international cap" [A4.6] partly conceded the point.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): B won the immediate-demand comparison: a cap need not cure underbuilding to prevent additional pressure during severe scarcity. A raised legitimate financing and unequal-treatment concerns, but repeatedly treated continued domestic demand as negating any marginal benefit. B also answered the purpose-built-housing argument cleanly: recruitment into verified beds remains possible, while future construction cannot shelter the current intake. B directly addressed both pressure questions; A’s answers largely preserved its disputed assumptions, with its clipped second rebuttal adding a small execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B exposed the unsupported leap from “cap international recruitment” to “cap only international recruitment.” A conceded that other restrictions were allowed but argued their omission defeated the policy. B persuasively explained why a partial restraint can still reduce harm without constituting a complete housing solution.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built the cleaner, more decisive case on the actual comparative set by the motion: capping a discretionary, actively recruited inflow during severe shortages versus leaving capacity unconstrained. Side B established an inescapable timing asymmetry—admissions expand in one cycle while housing permits and halls take years—and showed that in near-zero-vacancy markets, reducing marginal bidders eases rent spikes and family-home conversions even if other demand sources exist. Side A (CON) pushed back cleverly with segmented student housing (PBSA), tuition cross-subsidies for future halls, and the motion's silence on domestic admissions, while also suffering a minor clipping cost at the end of Turn 8. However, Side B neutralized both legs of Side A's case: exposing that capping international recruitment does not forbid domestic or supply measures, and proving that unbuilt future halls cannot shelter this September's extra arrivals without shifting immediate displacement onto local renters.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dilemma in [B4.5] (reinforced in [B5.2]) decisively dismantled Side A's PBSA and fee-pipeline turn ([A2.5]-[A2.6], [A4.4]): if dedicated student beds already exist, recruitment continues up to verified capacity, and if they do not exist yet, admitting uncapped cohorts now forces a zero-vacancy market to house people today on the promise of buildings financed for years later.
