### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO wins because it built a clear causal case and kept that case alive through the whole exchange. Its core route was the churn/free-rider market failure: one insurer pays for treatment while a rival captures the downstream savings, so voluntary coverage drifts toward exclusion and access rationed by wealth. It paired this with a credible account of how clinical-indication criteria, step therapy, continuation rules, value caps, and outcomes rebates discipline spending. CON raised a genuinely strong concern: lower unit prices can still mean higher total spending once many more patients are eligible for long-term therapy. That point stayed partly live. However, CON made damaging concessions under pressure: - It admitted voluntary coverage creates a free-rider problem. - It admitted blanket exclusions of comparable chronic therapies would be wrong. Those admissions largely handed PRO the necessity and analogy arguments. CON's churn rebuttal in A2.5 was also internally muddled. Both of CON's late speeches were clipped, which cost it the full weighing in its second rebuttal and closing. PRO had real flaws: - It cited nonexistent reference IDs (P1.4, C2.3, P4.1). - It never fully reconciled total-volume spending with its claim to "control spending." Its answer was mostly to reframe that spending as the legitimate purpose of insurance. Even so, PRO made the debate easier to decide and answered the pressure more directly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's pressure-question pairing and its follow-through in Rebuttal 2 and the closing. Q1 asked why statins should not be excluded under CON's logic; Q2 asked how voluntary coverage overcomes the free-rider penalty. CON answered by conceding that excluding statins would be wrong and that voluntary coverage has a free-rider problem. PRO then turned both concessions into a reason a mandate is needed, leaving CON with only the unresolved total-volume spending concern.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing why member churn and free-riding make voluntary coverage collapse into wealth rationing, then using the statin analogy and continuation/rebate guardrails to show the mandate can discipline payment without blank checks. Side B raised real doubts about attribution and total spending but conceded exclusions of similar drugs are wrong and never offered a workable non-mandate fix; clipped later speeches left those doubts incompletely pressed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's statin-analogy pressure and free-rider replies forced B to admit blanket exclusions are unjust while still failing to explain how voluntary negotiation avoids attracting high-need patients and losing downstream savings.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO built the cleaner live route: churn creates a free-rider failure and voluntary coverage rations by wealth, while value caps, competing formularies, continuation rules, and outcomes rebates discipline price. CON raised a strong total-spending-versus-unit-price objection, but conceded both that blanket exclusions of chronic therapies would be wrong and that voluntary coverage creates a free-rider problem, without showing a workable alternative. CON's clipped rebuttal and closing also weakened execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal and closing most decisively turned CON's concessions: once CON admitted voluntary coverage suffers a free-rider penalty and excluding analogous chronic therapies is wrong, PRO argued only a universal mandate with price/outcomes guardrails avoids that result, while manufacturer refusal is checked by competing FDA-authorized drugs.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins by building two clear routes to victory and keeping both alive through the full exchange. The first route is the leverage contradiction. A mandate removes the insurer's ability to walk away, and walking away is the threat that wins discounts. The second route is volume. A fair price per patient does not make spending affordable once tens of millions of people qualify for chronic therapy. Short-term endpoints like weight loss also mean outcomes contracts will rarely trigger refunds. A's pressure question forced PRO to admit that insurers cannot deny coverage if a manufacturer refuses every discount. PRO then moved to a statutory value-based price ceiling. That was a reasonable reading of the motion, but A answered it twice: - A cap on what insurers reimburse does not force the manufacturer to sell at that price. - A per-member price is not a cap on aggregate spending. PRO's main counterweight was the cost-shifting argument. It stayed largely assertive, and A plausibly answered that out-of-pocket spending and optional employer coverage do not raise baseline premiums. PRO's demand for quantified evidence was fair, and A answered it only with qualitative "actuarial math." That is a real gap, but PRO did not exploit it effectively. A also lost some credit for citing nonexistent reference IDs (C1.2, P1.2). PRO's closing was clipped, which is an execution cost. Overall, A made the decisive clash clearer and adapted better across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Q1 and A4.1–A4.4 were the decisive sequence. The question extracted PRO's concession that insurers must pay even when a manufacturer refuses discounts. A then showed that PRO's fallback, a statutory per-member price, neither compels manufacturers to accept it nor caps total spending across millions of chronic users.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B won the decisive clash over whether the motion's spending controls survive a coverage mandate. A eventually conceded insurers cannot deny coverage if a manufacturer refuses discounts, and B then showed that voluntary ceilings collapse while a statutory per-patient price neither compels the manufacturer's price nor caps pool-wide spend. A's cost-shifting story stayed live but never answered the pharmacy gap, the aggregate-budget problem, or the regressive premium comparison as cleanly, and the clipped closing left that pressure unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal and close, using A's A1 concession that insurers must pay even if the manufacturer refuses every discount, then splitting the statutory fallback into an unpaid price gap and an uncapped population bill.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): A won the central clash over bargaining leverage and aggregate affordability. B conceded insurers cannot deny coverage when a manufacturer refuses discounts, then pivoted to a statutory reimbursement ceiling; A showed that ceiling neither compels a manufacturer price cut nor caps total spending when multiplied across millions of chronic responders. B's safeguards and cost-shifting arguments remained plausible but never solved that uncapped volume problem, and its clipped closing added an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it used B's A1 concession to collapse voluntary outcomes contracts and risk corridors, then distinguished a per-member price from a population budget cap.
