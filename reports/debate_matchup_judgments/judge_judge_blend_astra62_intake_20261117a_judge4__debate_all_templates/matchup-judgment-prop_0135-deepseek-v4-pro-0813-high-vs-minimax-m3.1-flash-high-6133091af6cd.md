### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): CON opened strongly by arguing that targeted tools such as shore power, coach permits, and timed berths are better than blunt caps, and that displacement and scarcity rents make caps costly. PRO answered well by stressing the conditional trigger and separating load harms from emission and routing harms. It also argued that targeted tools are complements to a cap, not substitutes for one. The pressure questions were decisive. PRO's Q1 and Q2 forced CON to concede that no non-cap mechanism sets an aggregate ceiling and that a passenger-flow limit is simply a cap measured in people. After that, CON's case narrowed to "passenger count is not social cost" and "the motion is categorical." Neither point squarely defeats a conditional motion that lets a city choose the unit being capped. CON's closing effectively granted that caps can be appropriate when targeted tools fail, which is close to the proposition itself. PRO answered CON's questions directly, gave concrete observable thresholds for the "outweigh" judgment, and folded the overnight-stay objection back into the trigger. CON's second rebuttal was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Q2 and the A1 that followed. PRO asked whether CON's passenger-flow limit is just a cap measured in people, and argued that if targeted ceilings are enforceable they implement the proposition under another name. That forced CON to concede in B4.1 and B4.3 that any binding ceiling is a cap. CON's alternative-policy route never recovered from that concession.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by converting Side A's own concession into the decisive frame. B's pressure question Q2 ("is a passenger-flow limit not simply a cap measured in people?") forced A to admit in A4.1 that any enforceable total visitor ceiling is a cap, which B then leveraged across B4 and the closing: the dispute is no longer whether to cap but how to count the load. A's late defense that the concession establishes "terminology, not the proposition" was its best move and genuinely sharp, but it arrives weakened because A also conceded that "a cap may be included in that package" — which, under the motion's conditional trigger (cap only when harms outweigh revenue), comes close to accepting the proposition as written. B's rebuttals were more adaptive: it reframed targeted tools as implementation of a cap rather than alternatives, answered displacement and scarcity-rent objections directly (regional coordination, taxing rents back), and gave a plausible non-monetized trigger standard in B4.3. A's case was coherent and well-grounded (composition gaming, instrument matching, housing supply causes), but its Rebuttal 2 was clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost at the pivotal moment, and its final position collapsed into "caps are sometimes right" without a clean reason to reject the motion as written. B made the debate easier to decide and controlled the decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The B3.2 / A4.1 / B5.1 sequence: B asked whether a passenger-flow limit is just a cap in people; A conceded that any binding total visitor ceiling is a cap regardless of label; B's closing then framed this as ending the main dispute, reducing A's remaining case to instrument-design quibbles that the motion's conditional trigger already accommodates.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won solidly by establishing that finite historic spaces face an aggregate load problem that per-unit regulations cannot solve without a hard ceiling, and by locking Side B into a decisive concession on that point. Side B opened with sharp economic critiques—disaggregating pollution, crowding, and housing externalities, highlighting vessel-versus-passenger mismatches, and noting that replacing day-trippers with overnight visitors could worsen housing pressure. However, Side A neutralized regional port displacement by noting return coaches can be counted under the bus cap, answered B's measurement challenge with observable civic thresholds, and used Pressure Questions to force B to admit in [B4.1] that timed berths, quotas, and dispatch windows merely redistribute overload unless backed by an aggregate cap. Side B's late attempt to retreat to denominator design and policy completeness could not overcome that concession, especially given B's Rebuttal 2 word-limit clipping and garbled phrasing in [B4.2]-[B4.3].
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Pressure Questions [A3.1]-[A3.2] and follow-through in [A4.1]-[A4.2] and [A5.1] formed the decisive exchange. Pressed on what non-cap mechanism prevents aggregate visitor overload and whether passenger-flow limits are simply caps measured in people, Side B conceded in [B4.1] that no non-cap mechanism can do so and that without an aggregate limit, timed berths and dispatch windows merely schedule or redistribute overload—undercutting B's core claim that targeted management could replace caps.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): PRO narrowly wins because the motion is conditional. It applies only when harms already outweigh revenue gains, and PRO used this consistently. CON's central claim, that revenue funds mitigation, was answered effectively: revenue that cannot cover the harm does not make a negative-balance situation positive. PRO also framed the cap as a backstop to slots, pricing, and emissions rules, which absorbed much of CON's case for better tools. CON's strongest route was substitution: denied visitors would come back by car or rideshare. CON pressed it well and turned it into a memorable line. But the point is overstated for cruise passengers, who mostly travel on fixed ship itineraries. PRO's core reply also holds: the harm being capped is the concentrated release of passengers. CON's "if pricing works, the cap is redundant" argument is clever, but PRO's answer that a cap is simply capacity-based rationing keeps the cap viable as a backstop. PRO paid real execution costs. Its Q2 handed CON the substitution weapon. Its Rebuttal 2 was clipped for length. Its housing link through overnight visitors is weak for cruise passengers, who sleep aboard. Its substitution answer in the closing ("onward-travel reservations", regional port planning) came late. Together, these keep the margin thin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2 in Rebuttal 2, "a cap stops the visible ship, not the visitor," which turned PRO's own question about substitution into CON's strongest live attack. PRO partly blunted it by insisting that the harm targeted is the concentrated surge, not every visitor trip.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently without fake sourcing. CON's sharpest line was the substitution/causal-gap attack ("a cap stops the visible ship, not the visitor") plus the redundancy dilemma: if pricing works the cap is redundant; if it fails, raise the price. PRO survived this by (1) grounding repeatedly in the motion's own conditional trigger—if revenue truly exceeded harms, the cap never activates, defusing CON's revenue-funds-mitigation case; (2) answering both pressure questions directly with plausible mechanisms (matched-day attribution for crowding, reduced short-stay demand for housing); and (3) delivering the decisive weighing move that if congestion pricing can suppress the peak, that only confirms scarcity must be rationed—the cap is simply the capacity-based version, and pricing "permits traffic whenever revenue exceeds the charge," which cannot satisfy a harm-based decision rule. CON's substitution scenario stayed somewhat live but was answered by PRO's concentrated-release mechanism and coordination points. PRO's clipped Rebuttal 2 is a minor execution cost, but the substantive sequence was intact. PRO argued the proposition as written and made the debate easier to decide; CON's alternative (price and manage) was coherent but never squarely overcame the motion's own net-harm condition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The closing exchange: CON's "if pricing works the cap is redundant; if it fails, raise the rate" dilemma versus PRO's counter that pricing depends on elasticity and permits harmful traffic whenever revenue exceeds the charge, making the cap the capacity-based form of the same rationing logic the motion's trigger demands. This was where the debate was decided.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side B built the stronger comparative case by showing that capping cruise ships and tour buses targets two schedule-visible, easily taxed modes rather than the underlying harms, forfeiting mitigation revenue while allowing visitors to substitute into cars, rideshares, or neighboring gateways.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's mode-substitution and repricing rebuttal in [B4.1]-[B4.4], where Side B explained that failed slot or congestion schemes simply need higher prices and capitalized on Side A's self-damaging Q2 ([A3.2]) with "No mechanism does...
