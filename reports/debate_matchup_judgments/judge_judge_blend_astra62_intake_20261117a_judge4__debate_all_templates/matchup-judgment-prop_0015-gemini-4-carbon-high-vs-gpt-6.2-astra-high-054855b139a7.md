### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won the decisive clash: whether the threat of cutoff produces additional earnings, not just compliance. A's counterfactual framework — credit only wages attributable to the threat, and count harm that occurs during the exemption-sorting period even if later corrected — was applied consistently from opening through closing. B's core causal mechanism (threats break inertia; voluntary programs sit empty) was asserted rather than established, and A exposed the measurement gap on both sides of the ledger: voluntary-program participation shows attendance, not paid-work effects, while appeals logs and hospital intake miss people who silently forgo care. B's best move was shrinking the dishwasher scenario to a "vanishingly narrow edge case" via layered safeguards, but A's closing re-landed that the harm case need not slip past every safeguard — an undocumented Medicaid illness causes harm during sorting, and retroactive coverage cannot undo a medical event. B's "widespread stagnation vs. rare friction" weighing was plausible but its frequency claims were the least grounded part of the case. Both sides showed excellent epistemic discipline; A made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer to B's pressure Q1/A1 in A4.2 and A4.5: low participation in voluntary placements tells us attendance, not whether compelled attendees find paid work sooner — "fixing the portal does not give a volunteer a better reference than she would get from the same placement offered without a threat." This neutralized B's only direct answer to the causal question and left B's dependency-reduction benefit unproven.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): A built the cleaner comparative case: benefit exits, volunteer attendance, and existing employment do not establish additional self-reliance, while coverage or food losses can harm people before exemptions are resolved. B offered a coherent engagement mechanism and answered both pressure questions, but repeatedly asserted broad employment gains and rare, reversible harm without establishing either. A directly exposed those inferential gaps and used Arkansas to keep the real-world tradeoff visible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal decisively answered B’s strongest claim: low voluntary participation measures attendance, not whether compulsion produces paid work sooner, and better reporting cannot give a required volunteer a better reference than the same voluntary placement.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side A won by keeping the decisive burden on Side B: work requirements must create additional employment or self-reliance, not merely record compliance or remove people from rolls. A’s Arkansas point, additionality framing, and immediate-harm argument stayed live and were crisply weighed in the closing. Side B offered a plausible pro-work narrative and narrowed the harm case with safeguards, but relied too much on optimistic administrative assumptions and unsupported claims about broad gains.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal blunted Side B’s strongest pressure answer by distinguishing compelled attendance from additional paid work, and by showing that appeals and hospital logs miss people who silently forgo care after a cutoff.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a close, high-quality exchange by constructing and sustaining a decisive dilemma: real work requirements enforce paperwork deadlines that purge people who cannot comply, while Side B's repeated retreats (no penalty for missed letters, no proof of pain, no sanction absent near-confession) hollow out the claimed wage gains. Side B's recall-timing mechanism was original and survived partially — "rent tells her what a paycheck is worth, not which Friday to start looking" — and B answered pressure questions cleanly with A1/A2 labels. But A's unanswered point that laid-off workers already face job-search rules through unemployment insurance undercut B's core redundancy claim, and A's scale argument (millions of cases cannot run on note-free coaching) was never squarely resolved. B's final position conceded that benefits stay on through missed commitments, leaving A's weighing — illusory gains versus misread disability — as the cleaner route to vote. Both sides were disciplined, well-grounded, and rhetorically sharp; A simply made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4/A5's dilemma crystallization: B's redesign only avoids harming the unable by requiring near-confession before sanction ("I can apply now, but I won't"), which A showed forfeits the pre-enforcement wage gains while real-world paperwork mandates still cut off the depressed and impaired. B's B4.2 follow-up-confession answer was clever but left the asymmetry inverted.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A’s enforcement dilemma provided the stronger comparative case: meaningful verification and deadlines predictably burden people whose disabilities or instability make compliance hardest, while B’s increasingly protective design makes sanctions so delayed and difficult that its claimed employment incentive becomes uncertain. B offered a thoughtful recall-timing mechanism and answered both pressure questions directly, but established mainly that some earlier earnings are possible, not that broad dependency reductions outweigh the remaining misclassification risks. A occasionally overstated its case—especially by treating B’s safeguards as literally consequence-free—but more effectively narrowed the debate around feasibility, incentive strength, and comparative harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it used B’s answers to show that unavoidable contact may arrive only after the recall decision is over, while accepting unverified limitations and resolving doubt against sanctions leaves enforcement dependent on something close to an explicit confession. B’s search-deadline answer did not fully restore a credible, timely incentive.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): A won by turning B’s protective design into a decisive dilemma: if work requirements are enforced like real programs, paperwork and verification harm genuinely unable recipients; if softened as B proposed, they become too toothless to produce meaningful dependency-reducing gains. B’s deadline/commitment-device story was clear and humane, but it never fully answered the marginality of adding requirements to already cash-strapped workers or proved that pre-enforcement wage gains outweigh severe cutoff harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal and closing used B’s concessions—no proof required, workers may decline worse offers, and missed commitments bring no penalty unless refusal is confessed—to argue that B’s policy either collapses into an honor system or still punishes misread disability, undermining the proposition’s net-benefit claim.
