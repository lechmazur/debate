- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): B defended the presumption more convincingly by grounding it in information asymmetry and by making the "substantial consumer benefit" test workable through concrete evidence, commitments, and prior integration history rather than certainty. A's strongest attack was that B4.4 makes ambiguity fatal and therefore amounts to prohibition, but B answered that proof is not certainty and the firm is best placed to reduce the relevant uncertainty. A also somewhat pivoted on B's pressure question about what evidence would satisfy the standard, while B gave direct answers to A's pressure. A's startup-funding argument was plausible but did not fully overcome B's point that founder profit is not consumer benefit. B's clipped first rebuttal was a minor execution cost, but not enough to reverse the comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal to the "defense is impossible" attack was decisive: it listed concrete evidence such as comparable prior integrations, completed testing, and enforceable commitments, and explained that the rule requires substantial evidence, not guaranteed future outcomes, undercutting A's prohibition-by-ambiguity argument.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B built the cleaner path to victory by exposing the practical flaws in Side A's burden-shifting standard and winning the startup innovation clash. Although Side A credibly argued that proof requires substantial evidence and enforceable commitments rather than certainty, Side B used its pressure questions to draw out Side A's concession in [A4.4] that ambiguous evidence sustains the presumption—convincingly showing that because early-stage digital integrations (like acquiring an AI team) and startup counterfactuals are inherently probabilistic, resolving ambiguity against the deal blocks benign transactions. Side B also blunted Side A's information-asymmetry argument by distinguishing control of internal documents from control of future market outcomes, highlighted the paradox of requiring firms to prove benefits outweigh an unquantified, presumed harm, and established an ex-ante chilling effect on venture capital funding that Side A repeatedly talked past by reducing it to founder enrichment. Side A also incurred a minor execution cost for clipping at the end of its first rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pairing of its pressure questions ([B3.1]-[B3.2]) with its closing turns ([B4.4], [B5.1]-[B5.3]): after forcing Side A to admit in [A4.4] that ambiguity sustains the presumption, Side B showed that control of internal strategy documents cannot eliminate normal pre-closing ambiguity over digital outcomes, turning Side A's "rebuttable" defense into a systematic veto of probabilistic integrations.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): B won a competitive debate by keeping two comparative costs alive: the presumption’s default against ambiguous evidence and its possible chilling of acquisition-funded startup formation. A’s “proof is not certainty” response addressed the latter attack but never squarely neutralized the financing argument.

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B argued the cleaner path to victory by defending the presumption as a burden shift under asymmetric information and irreversible digital lock-in.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal 2 was the decisive exchange: it supplied verifiable merger-specific evidence for substantial consumer benefit and used A's claim that authorities can test harm to show they can equally test benefit, collapsing the unworkability objection.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A built a cleaner, more consistent case around asymmetric information and irreversible error costs in dynamic digital markets, while exposing a fatal contradiction in Side B's position. However, Side B unraveled in Rebuttal 2 ([B4.1]-[B4.4]) under Side A's pressure questions—explicitly reversing its earlier claims that verifying consumer benefit requires proving an unknowable counterfactual ([B1.2], [B3.1]), conceding that rebutting the presumption does not immunize harmful deals from ordinary review ([B4.3]), and garbling the comparative dilemma ([B4.4])—compounded by execution costs from a clipped first rebuttal and nonexistent P2.x reference IDs.
