### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Both sides were disciplined and clear, but CON built the stronger comparative path. CON isolated what the motion uniquely changes. Opt-in leaves the outrage-driven ranking machinery intact for everyone who says yes, which PRO effectively conceded. It also gates political discovery for disengaged users and creates incentives to over-suppress borderline civic content. PRO's best route was a structural consent frame: a "standing wrong" of uninvited political recommendation, set against a merely "contingent" lost discovery. That frame stayed coherent. However, it leaned on an assertion that CON fairly flagged as assuming the conclusion. PRO also answered only partially CON's point that "prior authority" proves too much, since every feed item precedes consent. PRO's answer was that politics has unusual leverage. PRO's onboarding-switch answer to the motivation problem showed how the switch would be presented, not why disengaged users would use it. PRO's classification turn was its sharpest move: CON's own civic-quality ranking also requires judgments about what counts as politics. CON blunted it reasonably by distinguishing revisable ranking decisions from categorical default suppression. PRO had the more memorable rhetoric, including the gym and conscription images. CON's weighing on unique effect was cleaner and decided the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came in Rebuttal 1 and the questions round. In A2.3 and A3.1, CON pressed that one opt-in click "authorizes" the same outrage loops PRO condemns. That forced PRO, in B4.1 and B5.1, to concede that volunteers still need safeguards. CON then used the concession in A4.1 and A5.1 to argue that the motion's unique effect is not to reduce manipulation but only to withhold discovery from non-opters. PRO's reply, that thin consent still beats no consent, never fully restored a material harm reduction.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): A wins the policy comparison by distinguishing manipulative ranking from political discovery and explaining the costs of default exclusion for disengaged users and borderline civic speech. B establishes genuine protection for non-opt-in users, but relies too heavily on declaring unsolicited political exposure inherently wrongful rather than weighing those costs. A also answers the classification challenge directly: unavoidable ranking judgments do not require making exclusion the default response to uncertainty.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s “presentation, not motivation” response directly undermines B’s visible-switch solution. Showing users an opt-in does not explain how someone without an existing political interest discovers why participation matters. This preserves A’s central discovery argument despite B’s assurances about accessibility.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the central clash about pre-exposure consent rather than overall feed quality. It conceded that opt-in would not cure all manipulative ranking, but limited the motion’s burden to stopping uninvited political insertion and gave a plausible visible-switch answer for civic discovery. Side A’s discovery and overclassification pressures were strong, but its better-ranking alternative remained vulnerable to the same classification problem and never fully proved that post-exposure controls would protect unwilling users as cleanly as an opt-in default.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered A1/A2 directly, conceded that opt-in does not sanitize outrage loops, then narrowed the issue to ending unconsented exposure and turned CON’s classification objection against civic-quality ranking.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO built a clean, memorable consent frame: access versus intrusion, and authorization before algorithmic initiation. The paternalism charge was effective. CON, however, developed the more decisive comparative case across the rounds. Its timing argument held that consent is solicited before relevance exists, making the prompt a "choice in the dark." It paired this with an asymmetry of errors: a missed deadline is invisible and irreversible, while an unwanted glimpse is visible and manageable. CON also pressed persistently on why politics alone should be singled out when ads and other categories remain recommended. PRO's best answers were "consent is authorization, not clairvoyance" and the claim that recommendation never guaranteed delivery. But its turn to "dependable direct notification" let CON argue that essential information must bypass the gate, turning the principle into a carveout. PRO's "a glimpse cannot be unseen" overstated the harm, and CON exploited that directly. CON carries minor costs for citing nonexistent IDs (P2.1, P4.2). PRO's closing was clipped. On balance, CON made the decision easier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.5–A4.7 response to PRO's A2 answer was the most decisive exchange. PRO conceded that expired deadlines are irreversible and proposed universal notification. CON replied that such notification only works by bypassing opt-in, which collapses the principle. It then reversed PRO's severity weighing by contrasting a skippable headline with a permanently missed registration date.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): A more convincingly connected the default to unequal civic discovery and explained why later opt-in cannot recover missed opportunities. B strongly distinguished authorization from foreknowledge, but did not sufficiently justify excluding useful political recommendations alongside outrage rather than constraining manipulative ranking. Preserved access also did not fully answer discovery losses for users outside civic networks. A's mistaken reference IDs and overreach about direct notifications narrow the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response that “the issue is chance” exposed the weakness in B's argument that recommendations never guaranteed timely notice. Removing a potentially valuable discovery route still matters without guaranteed delivery, and B's closing did not adequately resolve that comparison.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): B narrowly wins because its consent/authorization frame stayed live after pressure: political recommendation is an unchosen intrusion, opt-in preserves access, and post-hoc controls leave insertion power intact. A’s incidental-discovery and missed-deadline arguments were strong, but B blunted them by conceding recommendation is not a reliable civic-alert system and by contrasting that with explicit user authorization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s A2 answer to the missed-deadline pressure: it conceded irreversibility but argued engagement-ranked recommendation never guaranteed timely notice, shifting the decisive comparison to direct notification versus unchosen insertion.
