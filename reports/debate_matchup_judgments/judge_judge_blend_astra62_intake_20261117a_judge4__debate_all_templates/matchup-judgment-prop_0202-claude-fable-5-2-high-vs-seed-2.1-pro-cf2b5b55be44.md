### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): A established the stronger comparison: interoperability directly reduces network coordination barriers, while baseline obligations and security safeguards constrain the alleged costs. B never adequately justified why increased access would yield only thin contestability, and its regulatory-chilling case increasingly depended on unsupported claims of automatic fines. B’s bulk-harvesting argument exposed A’s overstated safety assurances, and A’s repeated clipping was an execution cost. Nevertheless, A better explained both the competitive benefit and why B’s strongest predictions were not inevitable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s distinction between mandated baseline functions and optional new features substantially weakened B’s innovation-freeze argument. B responded mainly by predicting regulatory overreach rather than establishing that interoperability actually required universal feature standardization. B’s distinction between screenshots and continuous automated harvesting was its strongest successful counter, but did not establish that those risks outweighed reduced lock-in.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a competitive debate by securing the contestability and innovation clashes cleanly while containing Side A's strongest security objections. Side B neutralized the "innovation freeze" claim by pointing out that DMA-style mandates apply asymmetrically to baseline functions rather than new native features, and dismantled the "free-riding" frame by distinguishing platform infrastructure from users' own social graphs and exposing the tension between claiming contestability gains are "thin" while warning that opt-in rivals will win users away. Side A stayed very live through a strong Turn 8 distinction between manual screenshots and silent client-level exfiltration plus a smart institutional argument on asymmetric regulatory incentives, and Side B incurred real execution costs for clipping three speeches and prematurely claiming in [B4.7] that Side A had not answered Q2 before Side A's Rebuttal 2 turn. Even so, Side B's Turn 7 non-discrimination test and Turn 9 comparative turn—showing that silent harvesting is also a risk of captive monopolist apps from which users cannot otherwise exit, whereas interoperable rivals are opt-in, vetted, and revocable—gave the cleaner path to the ballot.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The climactic exchange from [B3.2] to [A4.3] to [B5.2]-[B5.3]: Side A sharply defeated Side B's "contacts can already screenshot chats" defense by showing that a third-party client can silently and continuously harvest chat histories and metadata at scale ([A4.3]), but Side B decisively turned that mechanism in closing ([B5.2]-[B5.3]) by noting that silent harvesting is a risk of trusting any messaging app—including the incumbent's—except interoperability lets users leave a harvesting gatekeeper while subjecting rival clients to opt-in, vetting, and revocation.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side B more convincingly showed that contestability gains are thin and distorted while security and quality costs are structural. B neutralized A's contradiction claim, distinguished silent scalable exfiltration and non-opt-in spillover from ordinary forwarding, and kept the asymmetric-penalty incentive alive as a design feature rather than a mere implementation fear. A's strong lock-in frame and mitigation story remained partly live, but clipping truncated several speeches and the closing understated conversation-level harms, leaving B the cleaner path to a net-negative verdict.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 differentiation of discrete screenshots from silent, continuous, spillover exfiltration, tied to fines for over-blocking but not under-blocking, most damaged PRO's claim that security harms are bounded and opt-in.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B wins a competitive debate by exposing the gap between A’s proposed safeguards and its conclusion that security costs are trivial. A establishes a genuine network-effect benefit; free downloads and email concentration do not adequately refute it. But A never convincingly bounds endpoint risks or resolves the tension between demanding safeguards and affordable entry. Its closing largely repeats those assurances, while B sharpens the comparison around compliance costs and uncompensated disclosure harms. B’s overstatements and repeated clipping limit the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest response challenges A’s reliance on audits, liability, and revocation: these cannot guarantee subsequent endpoint behavior or undo leaked messages, and legal liability does not ensure compensation. A answers who formally bears responsibility but never establishes why the remaining security costs should count as small.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) earned a solid win by proving an architectural asymmetry: the security and product-quality losses of mandated interoperability are built into cross-platform bridging, whereas the contestability gains depend on a fragile chain of conditions. Side B made a coherent intuitive case on network lock-in, but overstated the status quo as 100% exclusion, dropped Side A's refutation on SMS multi-carrier standardization slowing feature innovation, and walked into a fatal contradiction between imposing costly security audits and legal liability while relying on tiny startups to drive entry once privacy-first rivals like Signal opt out. Side A's margin is slightly tempered only by minor execution deductions for hitting the word cap and clipping trailing words in three speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In Turn 8 [A4.3-A4.8], Side A dismantled Side B's Q2 email example and Turn 7 answers simultaneously: first showing that when users switch to privacy email providers, messages to contacts on giant providers still sit readable on the giants' servers ("the user switched, but the security did not survive the bridge"), and then exposing that Side B's ex-post remedies of mandatory audits and legal liability [B4.1] neither prevent endpoint leaks nor allow the "small, innovative teams" cited in [B4.2] to afford entry.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B won the motion’s comparative burden: contestability gains must exceed security and quality losses. B flipped email into evidence of abuse, failed universal encryption, and giant-led consolidation; trapped A in a high-bar/low-bar dilemma; and showed that audits, liability, and revocation cannot see inside a third-party endpoint. A never named an entrant the mandate makes viable, overstated “100% exclusion” against free multi-homing, and closed by restating lock-in rather than answering those live points. B’s three clips are an execution cost, but the surviving case still supplied the cleaner vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s demolition of A’s A1/A2 answers: post-decryption “verification” was only after-the-fact paperwork and lawsuits against possibly judgment-proof startups, and the supposed beneficiaries were unnamed categories least able to bear compliance, while privacy-first challengers were conceded free to opt out.
