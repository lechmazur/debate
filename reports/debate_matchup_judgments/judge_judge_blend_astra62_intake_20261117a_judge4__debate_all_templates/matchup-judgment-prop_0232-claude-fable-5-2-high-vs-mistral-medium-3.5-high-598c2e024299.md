### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by forcing the debate onto the correct comparative baseline: the motion only requires weakening, not destroying, organized crime. CON showed legalization is imperfect, but B repeatedly converted CON's own evidence into a case for displacement: partial black markets are still smaller than prohibition's total criminal market, and CON's concession that U.S. legalization reduced Mexican exports proved B's mechanism working. CON's strongest late argument—that losing cannabis let cartels grow through fentanyl—never answered the counterfactual that cartels would have fentanyl plus cannabis revenue under prohibition. CON repeated its capture and adaptation claims without showing net strengthening, while B cleanly resolved the main clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's decisive turn was using CON's own examples against it: California and Uruguay's remaining black markets still meant large shares had left criminal hands, and CON's admission that U.S. legalization reduced Mexican exports showed B's cannabis-displacement mechanism working. B then closed the counterfactual: CJNG grew because of fentanyl, not because it lost cannabis revenue.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) controlled the decisive clash from Rebuttal 1 onward by fixing the comparison standard: the motion asks only whether legalization "weakens" organized crime, not whether it is perfect. B systematically converted CON's own evidence into PRO mechanism — noting that cannabis is a small slice of Mexican cartel income precisely because U.S. legalization collapsed the export market — and pressed an unanswered counterfactual: a cartel with fentanyl plus cannabis revenue is richer than one with fentanyl alone. CON (Side A) repeatedly asserted that the "hybrid model" and capture amount to a "strategic upgrade" but never supplied a mechanism showing how losing a majority of a market leaves criminals stronger, and A's own closing conceded "Prohibition fails," which B exploited. B's pressure questions exposed real contradictions (why pivot if the loss cost nothing; why not prohibit alcohol if capture is decisive), and B answered A's questions directly with clean A1/A2 structure. A also answered with proper labels but its answers leaned on restated assertions rather than new weighing. B's closing crystallized the counterfactual cleanly; A's closing introduced mildly new framing (banking infrastructure) rather than resolving the live clash. Clipped turns in B's opening and closing were minor execution costs that did not obscure the argument. Both sides grounded claims in plausible background knowledge without fake citations; A's grounding was thinner because its core causal claim (capture = empowerment) was never mechanistically defended against B's revocable-license and total-ownership comparisons.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 turn of CON's Mexico evidence: "ask why cannabis is now a small slice of Mexican cartel income. It is because legal American growers took the export market. CON's best fact is my mechanism working," paired with the dilemma that cannabis cannot be both trivial to cartels and a threat when kept illegal. CON never recovered from this, and B's closing hammered the unanswered counterfactual — no organization gets richer by losing a revenue stream.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): A wins the counterfactual comparison: diversified cartels retaining cannabis revenue versus diversified cartels losing some of it. B established risks and persistence, but not why adaptation or capture would outweigh displacement. Its closing portrayal of cartels as purely illicit under prohibition also contradicted its own alcohol example. A answered the central pressure more directly and consistently distinguished weakening from elimination. Its exaggerated half-market arithmetic, mistaken attribution of the alcohol example, and repeated clipping limit an otherwise solid win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's “addition, not replacement” response directly challenged B's inference from cartel diversification to legalization's failure. A then explained that growth through fentanyl does not establish growth because cannabis revenue disappeared. B repeated successful adaptation without resolving that causal comparison.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won the decisive metric clash. PRO’s case rested on denying cartel cannabis revenue, but CON showed the largest such shock already happened in the export market and Sinaloa nonetheless pivoted to fentanyl without losing armed power or territorial control. PRO’s own best example, abandoned Golden Triangle grow sites, became proof of product-line loss, not cartel weakening. CON also exposed the state-capture problem and PRO’s tension between cheap legal cannabis and taxed capacity-building. PRO never answered the “fewer guns, less territory, less fear” test, while CON’s pressure questions forced self-defeating admissions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s handling of Sinaloa was the decisive exchange: PRO called abandoned cannabis operations evidence of weakening, but CON showed the same organization became a leading fentanyl supplier and stayed just as armed and feared, neutralizing PRO’s strongest proof.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) controlled the decisive clash from the opening onward: the natural experiment of US legalization, which collapsed Mexican cartels' cannabis export revenue while the cartels pivoted to fentanyl and extortion and remained just as violent. PRO never escaped this trap—indeed PRO's own best evidence (Sinaloa abandoning Golden Triangle grow sites) was a cartel that became a leading fentanyl supplier, which CON hammered in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing. CON also won the metric debate: PRO argued in "dollars denied," CON argued in guns, territory, and fear, and PRO never showed any organization actually weakened on that standard. CON's pressure questions were sharp and exposed real burdens (name one cartel made less dangerous; what stops license capture where cartels control local government), and PRO's answers (federal oversight, competitive bidding, low initial taxes) were thin and partly self-undermining, as CON noted by pointing out that low taxes give up the revenue that was supposed to build state capacity. PRO's shift from "undercutting prices" to "low initial taxes" was a visible retreat. CON's argument that adaptation into extortion makes organized crime worse to live under—shifting costs from willing buyers to coerced victims—was original and damaging. Costs for A: both rebuttals were clipped for exceeding the word limit, an execution penalty, though the key arguments survived. Costs for B: repeated reliance on the same Sinaloa example, a "multi-billion-dollar pillar" claim with brittle precision, and a closing that largely recapitulated rather than resolved the metric problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing against PRO's Sinaloa evidence. When CON demanded one cartel left with fewer guns, less territory, or less capacity for violence, PRO offered Sinaloa's abandoned grow sites; CON reframed it devastatingly: the organization that left those fields became a leading fentanyl supplier and did not become less armed or less feared—"the wallet can be hit while the grip stays the same."
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B wins by exposing A's unresolved leap from displacing cannabis sales to weakening criminal organizations overall. A establishes a plausible competition mechanism but inadequately answers replacement income, coercive power, and the shift toward extortion. B also directly resolves the export-versus-domestic revenue challenge. B overstates what continued violence proves and presents an overly rigid price-versus-tax tradeoff; its clipped rebuttals also cost execution. Nevertheless, its comparative case survives substantially better than A's repeated revenue-loss assertions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response to the Sinaloa example was decisive: abandoned cannabis fields demonstrate a lost product line, not necessarily diminished organizational power. Combined with its unanswered challenge about shifting income onto coerced victims, this materially undermined A's claim that every displaced cannabis dollar necessarily weakens organized crime.
