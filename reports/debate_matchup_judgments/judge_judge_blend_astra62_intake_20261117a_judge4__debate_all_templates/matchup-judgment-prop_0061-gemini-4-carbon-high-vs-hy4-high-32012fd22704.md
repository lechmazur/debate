### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): This was a close, high-quality exchange. CON's best material was its institutional-capacity critique: onboarding queues, credentialing that does not scale, and the mobility dilemma for regional visas. It also gave an honest answer on the pension sacrifice, namely slower benefit growth for better-off retirees plus modestly higher contributions. PRO edged the debate on scale. Its core comparison was that redesign gains are finite and one-off while retirements compound every year. That argument stayed live through the closing, crystallized in "you can delete a paper form only once" and "hands per bath" having a floor of one human being. CON never showed how task redesign keeps pace with a cohort that shrinks every year. CON's own concession that "no sector has a standing surplus" reinforced PRO's point that redesign eventually runs out of people. PRO also answered CON's onboarding pressure reasonably: training scales one-to-many, while bedside care is one-to-one. Its claim that family integration avoids a treadmill was contestable, but it was not fatal. CON's warning that a national priority would crowd out reform was plausible but rested partly on assertion. On balance, PRO gave the clearer reason why its lever matters more at the scale of the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Q1 on retraining surplus against CON's turn in B4.4. CON argued that if no domestic surplus exists, there are no spare teachers, assessors, or nurse-supervisors to onboard migrants either. PRO answered with one-to-many scaling and the point that efficiency gains are finite while retirements compound. That reply held up better: CON never squarely answered the compounding problem, and its own "no standing surplus" concession was used against it in the closing.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side A won by keeping the vote on priority, not on whether care needs humans. It answered the pension and surplus-worker pressure directly, then left Side B with an unresolved dilemma: onboarding consumes scarce licensed staff, and permanent residents cannot be locked to midnight wards or shrinking towns without becoming tied guest labor. Side B’s physical-floor and compounding-retirement case stayed live and more vivid, but the children-as-stopping-rule and “labor stays tight enough” assurances never showed why mass settlement should outrank redesign as the national priority.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal: bedside supervision does not scale like classroom teaching, and permanent integration either lets workers leave shortage posts or collapses back into tied guest labor.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): PRO kept a live physical-floor argument: paperwork and lifts give finite gains while retirements and care demand compound, and CON's admission that no sector has surplus people plus its concession of slower pension growth and higher contributions confirmed the cost of relying mainly on redesign. PRO also answered the treadmill pressure with a second-generation taxpayer mechanism and answered onboarding pressure with one-to-many training leverage, blunting CON's strongest attacks without denying integration costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's regional-visa dilemma and scarce-supervisor mirror in Rebuttal 2 were the sharpest counterpressure, but PRO's closing reply—that possible departure still beats guaranteed empty wards and that months of supervision unlock decades of care—contained it.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): This was a close, high-quality debate, and CON edged it on how the clash resolved. PRO had the cleaner core frame: automation multiplies existing labor, consolidation rations scarcity, and only immigration adds people. PRO also landed a real double-standard charge, noting that CON trusts the state to manage consolidation and automation but not immigration. CON did more to control the decisive question of scale. Its dilemma held up across rounds: curated, governable inflows only help "at the margin," while inflows large enough to bend the dependency ratio outstrip integration capacity. CON then used PRO's own "at the margin" language to argue that immigration cannot be the priority engine, since automation must carry the main load anyway. On mobility, CON's pressure question got PRO to concede that retention is impossible and continuous inflow is required. CON then turned that concession into a "treadmill" that fills cities rather than towns. CON also gave a substantive answer to PRO's night-ward question by pointing to native cohorts that could be redeployed out of routine retail and clerical work. PRO's replies were reasonable but leaned on assertion ("curation scales through systems"). CON's cohesion argument was somewhat generic and drifted toward a "rapid engineering" strawman, which keeps the margin thin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The pivotal exchange was CON's B4.1–B4.3, which answered both pressure questions and then converted PRO's "at the margin" and "continuous inflow" admissions into a priority argument. PRO's A5.2–A5.3 recovered partially by reframing marginal gains as how demographic repair works. That reply did not fully dissolve the scale dilemma.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by pinning a live scale contradiction: governable, curated inflows stay marginal and therefore cannot be the priority, while flows large enough to bend dependency outrun linguistic and civic capacity. A also converted B’s mobility admissions into a damaging treadmill—continuous permanent inflows fail to hold place-bound care and regional jobs, accumulate in cities, and risk the cohesion needed to fund pensions and care. B’s core claim that only immigration adds people remained intuitive but was not squared with those constraints or with A’s coherent alternative of automating routine work, shifting native cohorts into care, and consolidating services.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal, which treated B’s “at the margin” and free-movement answers as concessions that immigration cannot be the main engine and that refilling rural wards requires endless waves that do not revitalize the towns B opened on.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B won the priority clash by exposing a scale dilemma: inflows curated enough for intensive integration would only help at the margin, while inflows large enough to bend Japan's dependency ratio would strain linguistic and civic capacity. Side A's answers on gradual expansion and worker mobility were honest but were effectively turned into concessions that automation and consolidation must still do the main work, while continuous replacement creates urban accumulation and tiered labor markets.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive, using A's "at the margin" and nurse-mobility admissions to argue immigration cannot be the main engine and that refilling regional posts requires endless waves of settlers.
