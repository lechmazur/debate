### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Both sides were disciplined and avoided fake precision. CON (A) argued with real sharpness. The beef-versus-noodles substitution example and the "last increment" framing exposed a genuine weakness in pricing health externalities. CON's pressure questions also drew concessions from PRO: no health surcharge of unknown sign, and no collecting before support is in place. PRO (B) absorbed those concessions cleanly by defining "full" as the defensibly estimated net external cost. That kept the motion intact rather than retreating from it. By the end, CON had conceded several key points: emissions and discharge pricing, health charges once they are credibly estimated, higher prices, and imperfect compensation. CON's remaining route was a narrow carve-out. It applied only where the final increment of the tax threatens adequate nutrition and assistance cannot close the gap. PRO's closing answered that carve-out directly. If some families are at risk, the fitting remedy is extra targeted help for them. A rate discount instead benefits every purchaser, including affluent ones, and leaves the residual harm on others. CON's reply was that assistance may fall short and that "neither rate is perfectly targeted." This was plausible but did not fully overcome the targeting asymmetry. On the motion as written, PRO's principle held up better against pressure, while CON's case shrank to a conditional exception.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's closing reply to CON's last-increment test. PRO asked why a nutrition threat to some families should be met with a price discount for every buyer rather than additional protection for those families. CON's answer, that advance payments may be too small for the food available locally, restated the risk. It did not explain why undercharging everyone beats topping up targeted support.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): B wins a close, high-quality debate by preserving the proposition through disciplined targeting rather than accepting a blanket price discount. A successfully forced important concessions—especially that a diet-related health surcharge must not be collected when its net external sign cannot be credibly estimated—but B incorporated this without abandoning the case. The decisive move came late: B turned A's last-increment equity concern into a question about remedy design. If the final tax increment threatens a poor family's nutrition, the better response is additional targeted support and workable access, not a lower meat price for rich and poor alike. A's reply that assistance may still be inadequate was forceful, but B had already argued support can be coordinated in advance and keyed to income and local access. B therefore gave the cleaner path to a yes vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal that A's last-increment test compares the wrong remedies—targeted support can protect vulnerable households without giving affluent consumers a subsidy for harmful production—was the most decisive turn in the debate.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): A won narrowly. B disciplined the motion around net, credibly estimated external costs and gave strong answers on sequencing and untargeted discounts. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): This was a high-quality, disciplined debate with no fake precision on either side. CON's best route was to separate "make some damage count" from "charge the full estimate." It then used Q1 to extract a clear concession: PRO would keep full pricing even where a lower rate would keep an excluded family fed. CON pressed that concession well. Its willingness-to-pay critique was also sharp, arguing that a poor household's low food budget understates the value of the nutrition it gives up. PRO, however, defended the motion as written. The motion explicitly accepts higher prices for lower-income households, so the concession was the motion itself rather than a hidden weakness. PRO also gave the clearest answer to CON's "why full?" question. Its half-rate example showed that a partial charge lets units go ahead whose benefit to farmer and buyers is smaller than the damage downstream, and that a discharge limit cannot screen expansions that stay below it. PRO then neutralized CON's moral framing in two ways: - **Symmetry:** downstream families' dinners are squeezed by water bills, and they cannot opt out of that cost. - **Targeting:** a cap delivers relief according to how much meat someone buys, not according to need. CON's answers ("both dinners matter," "earmarking isn't delivery") reasserted its tradeoff rather than defeating PRO's marginal logic. CON's own sequencing alternative also left the residual-damage problem only partly addressed. PRO also answered Q2 honestly by conceding that category rates miss within-category improvements, while salvaging the effects on output and on buyers' choices between production systems. Overall, PRO built the cleaner decision rule tied to the motion as written, while CON relied on a conceded hard case that PRO had already weighed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange runs from PRO's Rebuttal 2 into the closings. PRO's half-rate example established why the charge should be the full estimate, not a partial one. CON's response was that willingness to pay understates the value of a poor family's nutrition. PRO answered that a downstream family's dinner shrinks through the water bill just as surely, and that a cap targets meat buyers rather than people in need. That blunted CON's strongest weapon, the "PRO accepts hunger" concession.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO gave the cleaner route to victory: a full residual-damage charge after controls makes remaining externalities count and avoids relieving poverty through regressive meat-price discounts. CON's strongest hardship objection was serious, but PRO answered it directly and reframed a lower cap as poorly targeted help that still leaves downstream poor families paying pollution costs. CON kept the food-insecurity gap live, yet did not fully neutralize PRO's point that discharge limits still miss below-limit expansions and that progressive funding is available under either policy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's answer to the forced food-insecurity question was decisive: it accepted full pricing even where a lower rate might help an excluded household, then showed a cap relieves people by meat purchases rather than need while still leaving downstream households exposed.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): B won a close, high-quality debate. A sharply exposed the distinction between some damage pricing and full pricing, and forced B to concede that full pricing would continue even when a lower rate could prevent an excluded household’s food insecurity. B nevertheless preserved the clearer affirmative case: residual damage persists below regulatory limits, partial charges can permit socially costly expansion, and caps distribute relief according to meat purchases rather than need. B also answered A’s willingness-to-pay objection by showing that downstream water bills can impose similarly hidden losses on dinner budgets. A’s sequencing alternative remained plausible, but less decisively explained why those residual costs should remain unpriced.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first pressure question extracted the debate’s most damaging concession: B prioritized full pricing over a stipulated preventable hunger case. B recovered best by combining an honest answer with the marginal-expansion argument and the comparison between need-based relief and a poorly targeted meat-price cap.
