### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A (PRO) built and defended a sharp, consistent boundary — passive accommodations (meal opt-outs, anti-bullying, faith excusals) stay, while active policing of the child's own compliance and peer interactions must shrink — and applied it ruthlessly across every round. Side B's strongest route (classification problem: "mainly personal" is judged by administrators who don't share minority beliefs; the seven-year-old swap makes opt-outs theatrical) was genuinely live and well-executed, but B progressively conceded the motion's actual target (no shunning, no ingredient lists, no guarantee of obedience), narrowing to a modest duty to redirect observed trades and listen to parents. That residual claim is coherent but drifted toward defending reasonableness rather than the "highly restrictive" enforcement the proposition targets, and A flagged this drift early. A answered both pressure questions directly with the required format, gave objective triggers for bias, and crystallized the concession in closing. B was more rhetorically vivid and original in reframing, but A did more comparative weighing and made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1–A4.2 answering B's pressure questions: A conceded the voluntary swap stands absent coercion and offered objective triggers (clinical documentation for health, observed misconduct for safety, passive accommodation for faith), which blunted B's neutrality critique and exposed that B's enforcement required exactly the "constant surveillance" B itself admitted schools cannot sustain. B's best counter was B4.4/B5.3 — that watching a seven-year-old trade away an opted-out lunch makes the opt-out "theatrical" — the sharpest single exchange of the debate, but A's closing (that blocking the swap polices the child against herself) held the line.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): B won by consistently distinguishing accommodation of family choices from compulsory enforcement of private obedience. It preserved opt-outs, anti-harassment protection, and attention to early safety concerns, substantially answering A’s minority-inclusion case. A created meaningful pressure around young children and feasible, occasional intervention, but never established why declining enforcement constitutes actively overriding parents. B also better explained why individualized enforcement remains burdensome even without guaranteed surveillance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest response separated parental safety warnings from parental commands: teachers can investigate early cruelty without automatically enforcing exclusion. Its direct answer on lunch trading likewise clarified that an opt-out constrains what schools serve, not every voluntary choice children make. This undercut A’s claim that meaningful accommodation necessarily requires compliance policing.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the debate focused on the proposition’s key limit: schools should still provide opt-outs, safety protections, and anti-bullying duties, but should not enforce highly restrictive personal lifestyle rules through active teacher policing.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure questions and follow-up forcing A to define “ordinary supervision” were decisive. A answered directly, but B turned A’s narrowed enforcement request into proof that even modest lifestyle policing requires bespoke tracking and draws schools into private compliance.
