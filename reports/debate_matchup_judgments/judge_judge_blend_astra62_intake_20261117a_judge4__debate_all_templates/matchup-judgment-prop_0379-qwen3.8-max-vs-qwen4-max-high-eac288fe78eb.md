### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B won by repeatedly forcing A back onto the same damaging concession: if independent spending can still win the candidate's gratitude and return the phone call, the ban removes the visible receipt while the dependency survives. A never isolated a harm that the corporate donation ban alone removes, and B showed that A's own fixes—disclosure and coordination rules—would do the real regulatory work without banning disclosed donations. B also exploited A's reliance on public financing and free airtime, which were not in the motion, and turned the lower caps into an incumbent-protection problem. A's political equality case was plausible but became more assertion than mechanism once the displacement challenge landed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive exchange was using A's admission that the phone may still ring: the favor and access survive independent spending, while only the receipt disappears. B then turned A's proposed disclosure/coordination rules against the ban, arguing that those rules, not the ban, are the load-bearing regulations.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B wins a competitive, high-level debate because its core causal and philosophical architecture held up under scrutiny while Side A's central turn fractured logically and retreated from the motion as written. Side A wrote with exceptional rhetorical sharpness and scored real points on the individual-caps plank by stripping out Side B's unmandated public-financing assumption and exposing unresolved asymmetries around self-funding tycoons, media proprietors, and challenger seed money. However, to avoid defending billionaire mega-donors, Side A advocated moderate donation caps, effectively conceding the motion's contrast against treating money in politics as ordinary speech. More decisively, on the central corporate-ban clash, Side B isolated harms unique to direct checks—fungible operating cash for a candidate's own machine and structural treasury dependency that anti-bribery laws cannot prosecute—cleanly answered the union objection via voluntary member contributions, and dismantled Side A's claim that coordination rules make a ban redundant. Side A simultaneously argued that disclosure and coordination rules work yet treated outside spending as inevitably dark and coordinated, leaving Side B with the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response across [B2.3], [B4.1]-[B4.2], and [B5.3] to Side A's flagship "removes the receipt" and "coordination rules do the work" critique: Side B honestly conceded in A1 that "the phone may still ring" from independent spending, then drew a decisive functional line between outside ads persuading voters and direct checks funding a candidate's operating machine, showing that coordination rules enforce a ban boundary rather than replace it and that democracy should not preserve corporate dependency merely because it is easy to audit.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON wins the mechanism clash: PRO never isolates a harm the ban removes that disclosure, coordination rules, moderate caps, or prosecuting quid pro quo would not, while CON shows displacement to less auditable spending and caps squeezing challengers' mid-tier donors while incumbents and self-funders remain untouched. PRO's direct-donation versus independent-advocacy distinction was credible, but its reliance on public financing outside the motion and later breadth claim were answered by moderate-cap comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2: if coordination rules can police covert candidate-group collaboration, they can police a disclosed cheque even better, so the ban's unique effect is moving money from an auditable channel to an unauditable one.
