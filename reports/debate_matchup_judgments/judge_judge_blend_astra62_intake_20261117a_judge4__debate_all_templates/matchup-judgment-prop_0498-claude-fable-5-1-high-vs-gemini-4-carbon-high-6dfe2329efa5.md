### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): B wins the necessity clash. A built a strong motive-based case for separation, but B showed the remedy still requires conduct-style line-drawing and does not escape enforcement problems: a separated platform can shift favored functions into the OS or auction placement. B’s use of A’s own closing concession that some rules are “simple, checkable” was decisive because it turned A’s remedy into evidence that targeted conduct rules can bite today. A never fully answered the system-feature-under-the-store problem or the hungrier-gatekeeper/pay-to-play cost. Despite a clipped opening, B’s later speeches gave the cleaner reason to reject the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 pivot: a separated firm would stop shipping an app icon and bake payments, password management, or tracking features into the OS as system features, moving self-preferencing beneath the store and undercutting A’s bright-line OS/user-facing-app boundary.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): A built the cleaner path to victory by keeping the burden on necessity and showing that separation still requires conduct regulation, relocates boundary disputes into the operating system, and may intensify fee and placement extraction. B answered pressure directly and gave a serious incentive-based case for separation, but its claim that a standalone platform profits only when developers thrive was never adequately defended against A’s monetization critique. A’s closing effectively used B’s reliance on simple, checkable rules to question why those rules cannot govern integrated firms too.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both questions directly, contrasted checkable interoperability requirements with perpetual plumbing-versus-app disputes, and used the browser choice-screen auction to puncture B’s claim that neutral setup screens eliminate pay-to-play. Its observation that the separated platform profits when developers pay, not necessarily when they thrive, damaged B’s central incentive argument.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): CON won the necessity clash: PRO showed self-preferencing but relied on regulator-policed interfaces, setup screens, and bans that CON showed would also work against an integrated firm. CON also created live costs PRO never fully neutralized—boundary litigation over what counts as core, functions buried as OS features, and separation replacing house-app competition with pay-to-play auctions that hurt smaller developers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was B's Rebuttal 2 and closing use of the choice-screen auction and PRO's own concession that banning auctions and steering gags is simple and checkable, undercutting the claim that only structural separation is enforceable.
