### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B more effectively exposed and exploited the central tension in A's position: that a workable ownership restriction, once it must "look through" to controlling investors, fee structures, and exit incentives rather than the PE label itself, collapses into the same universal conduct regulation B was advocating all along. B's pressure question (B3.1) forced this contradiction into the open, and A's answer in rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.3) oscillated between banning PE control outright and welcoming PE funds that accept conditions, a tension B's rebuttal 2 (B4.3) precisely named. B's closing then used A's own closing language ("that is not the model we restrict") to argue A had effectively conceded that conduct, not ownership identity, is what matters — a sharp, last-word capitalization that A had no chance to answer. A's unique-vulnerability framing and "financial architecture" argument remained rhetorically strong throughout, and its capital-gap answer (public bridge funding) was reasonable in theory but unsupported by the motion as written, a gap B repeatedly flagged. On balance B provided the more logically consistent throughline and the more decisive closing argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure question (B3.1: "if a PE fund meets the exact same debt limits... do you still block its ownership") combined with B4.3's follow-up, which caught A's rebuttal 2 contradicting itself between "passive capital only" (A4.2) and welcoming a PE fund that "accept[s] stable capital and delayed exit" (A4.3) — a contradiction A never cleanly resolved before B's closing turned it into a case-ending concession.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): CON won the decisive clash: once universal financial and control-condition guardrails work, a PE-ownership restriction is either redundant or underinclusive (family chains/REITs escape), while bare PE bans worsen capital-and-closure risk the motion does not fund. PRO’s strong extraction frame and fee/exit-clock points never fully escaped the later concession that conditioned PE control is fine, collapsing the case toward conduct rules rather than ownership-class restriction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3: PE barred from control even when meeting strict rules, then welcomed as rescuer if capital/exit are conditioned—exposing PRO’s ownership restriction as conduct regulation by another name.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively exposing the conceptual flaw in Side A's case regarding what constitutes an "ownership restriction." Side B effectively argued that Side A's proposed regulations (fee caps, escrow, capital requirements) are universal conduct rules, not ownership bans. Side B trapped Side A into a fatal contradiction: Side A initially claimed it would block PE funds even if they met financial caps, but later conceded in the closing that a PE fund meeting strict conditions could own a facility. Side B also maintained a stronger, more realistic stance on the capital crisis and the underinclusivity of a PE-specific ban, making its comparative case much cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it dismantled Side A's semantic distinction between "ownership restrictions" and "conduct rules," highlighting Side A's contradiction between barring PE control and allowing PE rescue if they accept strict financial conditions.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but A sustained a more vivid and consistent causal story (leverage, sale-leaseback stripping, fixed reimbursements, shell-game liability evasion, bankruptcy escape) and used it effectively through every round. B's strongest weapon was the "symmetry" argument—that any test capable of identifying PE control for exclusion is equally capable of triggering conduct-based conditions, making conduct regulation strictly broader in coverage—but B undercut its own capacity argument in Rebuttal 2 by conceding that "capital can still enter as equity, nonprofit sponsorship, or public investment" once extraction is blocked. A seized on this in closing to argue B's main cost-of-restriction claim had evaporated, leaving B's case resting mainly on an abstract coverage/evasion argument that A had already partially neutralized by showing the same enforceability problem cuts both ways. B's ex-ante conditions response (open-book reporting, staffing floors, audit rights) was reasonable but never squarely answered A's concrete "whack-a-mole" point that extraction can migrate across channels faster than inspectors can audit once a finite-life fund holds control. The pressure-question round was well executed on both sides, but A's answers (clear definitional test for "control") held up better under cross-examination than B's capacity claim did.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing seizure of B's concession in [B4.1] ("capital can still enter as equity, nonprofit sponsorship, or public investment") as removing B's capacity objection, paired with A's earlier "whack-a-mole" extraction-migration point in [A4.4], which B's ex-ante conditions answer never fully neutralized.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): CON won a close debate by making the decisive comparison clearer: the live harms are leverage, stripping, affiliate fees, staffing cuts, and liability shields, which can be conditioned on control at acquisition, whereas a PE-ownership restriction is underinclusive of non-PE extractors, evadeable via the same webs both sides discuss, and not cleaner once an objective control test is admitted. PRO’s structural short-horizon extraction story was vivid and kept pressure alive, but CON repeatedly neutralized the capacity and “cash is gone” routes by flipping PRO’s own control test into universal license conditions and by holding that legitimate capital need not be leveraged flip capital.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s enforcement-symmetry replies (A2.3–A2.4 / A4.3–A4.4): if regulators can identify PE/control well enough to restrict ownership, they can attach leverage, rent, staffing, and liability conditions instead; if they cannot, the restriction fails too.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully linking the structural incentives of private equity (finite lifespans, high-yield mandates) to the extractive conduct, proving that conduct regulations alone cannot neutralize the unique danger of PE ownership. Side A also brilliantly trapped Side B in the closing regarding capacity: if strict conduct rules make PE investment unprofitable, then banning PE costs zero legitimate capacity. Side B argued competently for conduct-based regulation and coverage, but failed to fully overcome Side A's structural critique of the buyout model.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it defined PE objectively to answer the pressure questions and then used Side B's concession about alternative capital to dismantle the capacity defense.
