### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): A won a close, high-quality debate. A's decisive route was the double-standard clash: B candidly conceded that foreign panels cannot supervise collections nations already hold (sovereignty), which meant the supervision regime binds only peoples who were robbed. A crystallized this memorably ("the burglar's better safe, with a committee attached") and paired it with the conceded absence of any calendar deadline and the fact that no returned object was ever shown destroyed, while the harm of ongoing retention is certain. B fought back well: the neutral-vault answer in B4.1 genuinely defused A's "object stays with the taker" line, the Brazil fire example was a real, plausible anchor for irreversibility, and B extracted a real concession (A admitted museums cannot veto even shipment into shellfire). But B's extreme-case argument remained hypothetical against A's weighing of speculative versus certain harm, and B's sovereignty answer never fully explained why theft should cost the victims decision rights. A's clipped opening and clipped Rebuttal 2 were minor execution costs; B answered pressure questions directly and cleanly. On balance, A made the debate easier to decide and landed the more damaging, more disciplined comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 [A4.5]-[A4.10] narrowed the dispute to a rare corner case, exposed that CON's machinery applies only to the robbed via the Brazil answer, and reframed the weighing as speculative risk versus certain ongoing loss. B's strongest counter was [B4.1], where the neutral-custody plus finite-baselines answer directly dismantled A's "the taker keeps the objects by default" route, forcing A's closing to lean on the deadline and sovereignty points instead.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B wins the narrowed clash over genuinely dangerous transfers. A explicitly accepts return despite foreseeable destruction; B offers immediate title restitution, funded conservation, and neutral interim custody, substantially weakening A's claim that preservation necessarily preserves the taker's control. A's sovereignty objection and pressure on indefinite delay remain substantial: B does not establish that peace or panel agreement must arrive. Nevertheless, B better weighs irreversible destruction against access losses that its alternative partly mitigates. A's reliance on owners' good intentions does not adequately answer negligent stewardship, and its demand for an example of a destroyed returned artifact imposes an unnecessarily restrictive evidentiary burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B directly undermines A's reassurance that no nation wants its patrimony destroyed: Brazil illustrates how catastrophic neglect can occur without destructive intent. Whether those collections had previously been repatriated does not defeat the preservation mechanism. This makes A's explicit willingness to return objects into severe danger consequential rather than merely hypothetical rhetoric.
