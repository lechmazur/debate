### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): CON wins. Both sides accepted that antibiotics need guaranteed revenue, so the live clash was whether countries should accept high unit prices as part of the deal. CON framed this cleanly: the guarantee is necessary, and the high price is optional. CON then used PRO's own design choices against it. PRO's dollar-for-dollar offset meant the unit price funds no domestic research. PRO's carve-out reimbursement meant price no longer disciplines prescribers. By the end, PRO had shifted the work of stewardship onto diagnostic gates and audits, which CON correctly noted survive under a subscription model. PRO's strongest material was international reference pricing, multipayer fragmentation, and the Q1 contradiction between "no list price" and a binding ceiling. These pressed CON genuinely. CON's answer was adequate: a ceiling is not a published list price, it can be confidential or near peer levels, and PRO's own audits also require a per-course price. PRO's closing turn, that a "peer-market" ceiling concedes high prices, had real force. CON blunted it by distinguishing a negotiated price from the motion's waiver of negotiation, though that distinction leans somewhat on CON's reading of the motion. Overall, CON narrowed the debate more effectively and made the decision easier. Its edge is clear but competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1 and B2.4 were decisive. If unit sales offset the guarantee dollar-for-dollar, the innovator's revenue is identical whether a course costs fifty dollars or five thousand. And if carve-outs remove ward-level price friction, then price was never the steward. Together these collapsed PRO's twin claims that high prices fund innovation and enforce stewardship. PRO never fully recovered beyond its foreign reference-pricing argument.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): B wins a competitive debate on the proposition’s actual scope. Permitting commitments despite high prices does not require abandoning negotiations, ceilings, or access conditions. A substantially undermines price-based stewardship and shows that high prices add no domestic research revenue beneath the guarantee. But B supplies remaining fiscal and international-pricing reasons for permission, while A never adequately answers the possibility that suppliers reject its preferred terms. A’s repeated equation of permission with permanent price surrender leaves its central opposition misdirected.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s closing turns A’s peer-market ceiling into a concession: a negotiated, capped price can still remain high. This directly damages A’s claim that retaining bargaining leverage requires rejecting the motion. A’s stronger stewardship rebuttal survives, but cannot establish that high-price commitments should be impermissible.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B wins on the motion as written. Permitting advance market commitments even when unit prices stay high is narrower than a blank-check ban on negotiation, and B kept that distinction while showing why multipayer reimbursement and international reference pricing can make a high list price a condition of a viable deal. Side A was sharper on offsets and stewardship: the guarantee, not the sticker, funds domestic R&D, and diagnostics and audits do the rationing. But A then conceded ceilings near already-high peer prices and never showed why a country must refuse permission when a referenceable cap can make innovators walk. That left the subscription alternative either recreating high prices or failing the live institutional objection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's first rebuttal turned B's dollar-for-dollar offset against the claim that high stickers fund innovation, but the decisive exchange was B's second rebuttal and closing: A could not impose a binding unit-price ceiling without creating a referenceable price, and "ceilings near peer-market levels" conceded the high prices the motion asks countries to permit.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): This was a close debate. PRO had the cleaner frame for the motion: "permit" keeps every contract lever, refusal is irreversible, and every harm CON named is a contract term a buyer could write differently. PRO also landed a fair point that CON's dilemma about unused balances applies to delinked schemes too. But CON did more damage on PRO's core justification for keeping unit prices high. In Rebuttal 1, PRO defended high prices as cost-sharing with clinical purchasers that "shrinks the public envelope." In the same speech, to escape bedside rationing, PRO had governments buy doses and issue them to wards free. CON caught this tension cleanly and pressed it through the questions. In A2, PRO confirmed that the government pays. That left high unit prices doing little fiscal or innovation work. PRO's closing tried to recast this as CON caring only about budget labels. That move effectively concedes CON's central point that high prices add nothing, so the contest shifts to clinical access. On access, CON's rationing-by-wallet argument survived wherever carve-outs don't exist. CON's global reference-pricing and resistance chain was more speculative, and PRO's tiering answer partly blunted it. CON's argument that conditioning AMCs is not banning them was somewhat semantic. Still, CON made the more coherent case that high unit prices are an unnecessary term. That gives CON a thin but real edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's B2.3–B2.4, reinforced in B4.3. CON showed that PRO's cost-sharing defense (A2.3) and PRO's free-ward-supply fix (A2.4) cannot both hold. PRO's later A2 answer, "the government pays," confirmed the problem and left high unit prices without a clear rationale.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): A wins the comparative policy argument. It shows that a volume cap leaves sales incentives below the ceiling, while government-funded free distribution undermines B's claimed public-budget savings. A also offers pooled, postapproval commitments that B repeatedly mischaracterizes as requiring upfront, single-country funding. B's permission framing and distinction between procurement prices and bedside charges meaningfully weaken A's blanket harm and double-payment claims. Nevertheless, B never restores its “expensive versus nonexistent” comparison or adequately answers access risks outside its proposed safeguards.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange links B's financing and access defenses: if hospitals pay, affordability barriers remain; if government buys doses and distributes them free, the claimed treasury savings disappear. B answers who pays but compares that expense with untreated infections rather than A's alternative innovation funding.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side B won because the motion only requires permission, not an optimal design. Side A landed the sharper mechanism critique—high per-course drawdowns either push volume or become redundant once the treasury pays unused balances and issues doses free—but Side B converted that into a budget-label objection and kept access, tiering, and caps as contract terms a buyer can write. Side A never showed why a feasible high-unit-price commitment must be forbidden rather than conditioned, so the cleaner vote was to permit the tool and renegotiate terms instead of clearing the table.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal pinned B's hospital cost-sharing and free-issue stories as mutually cancelling, but B's closing answer—that a treasury-paid structure is a label preference, not a reason to prohibit—better matched the permissive motion and kept B's route alive.
