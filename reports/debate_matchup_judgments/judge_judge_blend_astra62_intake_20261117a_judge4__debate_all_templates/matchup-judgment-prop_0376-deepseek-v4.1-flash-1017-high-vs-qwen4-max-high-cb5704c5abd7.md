### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): PRO built the clearer path to victory. Its core point was that card APRs cluster in the twenties across very different risk profiles, which suggests they reflect margin rather than accurate risk pricing. CON's only reply was that advertised rates hide variation, and that never fully neutralized the point. On that basis PRO argued that a cap mostly reprices existing accounts, while exclusion is confined to a narrow tail. PRO answered CON's pressure questions honestly: it named a ceiling in the high teens to low twenties, conceded that tail accounts would be cut, and placed substitute harms within a call to regulate payday lending as well. PRO's Q2 then forced CON to name a ceiling of roughly mid-thirties APR. PRO used that admission effectively: CON now accepts capping in principle, and its own policy excludes the same kind of tail. CON's closing reply, that a non-binding backstop is not this motion, has some force. However, CON never squarely answered PRO's competition argument (a lender that closes a performing account loses it to a rival bound by the same ceiling) or the clustering evidence. CON also slightly overstated PRO's concessions, which applied only to the tail and not the whole book. CON's substitution-and-rationing case was coherent and its credit-union reply was decent, but it stayed more static across rounds. PRO has a minor grounding slip in implying that card APR already folds in fees.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 and closing exchange decided the debate. PRO asked whether substitution logic would permit 40-60% APRs, which pushed CON to concede a roughly mid-thirties APR cap. PRO then reframed the debate as a dispute over where to set the dial, not whether to cap, and noted that CON's ceiling cuts off the same tail. CON's reply that a high backstop "is not this motion" only partially recovered.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a close, well-executed debate. Both sides were disciplined and grounded. B's core case — APRs cluster in the twenties across different risk profiles, the benefit lands on existing revolvers while exclusion hits a narrower tail, and the harm asymmetry between compounding revolving debt and a one-time denial — survived A's pressure intact. The decisive sequence was B's Pressure Question forcing A to name a ceiling; A conceded a mid-thirties backstop in Rebuttal 2, and B's closing converted that concession into the debate's cleanest vote: the exclusion A calls disqualifying is the price of any cap, including A's own. A's counter — that the motion as written requires a cap that bites, so a non-binding backstop is a different policy — was fair and kept the dilemma alive, and A's credit-union-as-niche response and "statement may worsen" point landed real damage. But B answered both pressure questions directly and concretely (naming a number, accepting the tail cost, offering the same-ceiling competition mechanism), while B's "changed statement vs fresh denial" framing gave the clearest weighing in the debate. A never fully neutralized the no-stopping-point argument. Edge to B on argument quality, rebuttal progression, and the sharper crystallization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2: by naming a cap level, explicitly accepting the excluded tail under the motion's "even if," and exposing that CON had offered "a dilemma instead of a number," B set up the closing concession flip that decided the debate. A's strongest riposte was the Rebuttal 2 argument that credit unions prove only a member-based niche can survive a high-teens ceiling, not universal access.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): B narrowly wins. A established that exclusion can cause lasting harm, but its cost-above-cap example did not rebut savings on accounts that remain profitable. A too often treated rationing itself as dispositive rather than establishing why its harms outweigh those savings. B’s closing also exposed tension between that objection and A’s own proposed cap: preserving mainstream pricing does not establish that marginal borrowers retain access. B’s unsupported certainty about narrow exclusion and blanket claim that APR includes fees keep the margin small.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest rebuttal turned A’s mid-thirties backstop against its categorical anti-rationing argument. A’s closing distinguished cap levels but did not adequately explain why its preferred ceiling escaped the same marginal-exclusion logic.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): CON won by showing that the cap's distinctive effect is the conceded exclusion, while its promised protection depends on other rules. Two mechanisms carried this. First, lenders price at approval and cannot tell the short-term "bridge" borrower from the long-term revolver, so a cap declines both. Second, at a capped rate the treadmill persists: interest on a $3,000 balance is still substantial, and minimum payments still barely reduce principal. Only amortization rules actually force principal down. PRO had a good opening frame (treadmill versus access) and a memorable lead-paint analogy, but its later answers weakened its case. It conceded the separation problem and then named roughly 20% without explaining how that rate would preserve the bridge borrower. It answered the group-size question by appealing to "harm, not headcount," which sidestepped the question. It also borrowed CON's "reversible" description of private denial to soften statutory denial. CON answered PRO's pressure questions directly. Its A2 conceded that both regimes deny some borrowers, then distinguished them by trigger: ability-to-repay rules exclude by design, while a cap excludes repayers as collateral damage. CON's closing turned PRO's list of fallback options against it, since those lenders are bound by the same ceiling. CON slightly overstated that secured cards would vanish, but PRO never squarely neutralized its core mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B2.1 and B4.5–B4.7. PRO claimed that short bridges "would survive any reasonable cap." CON replied that lenders price the whole pool at approval, so the bridge borrower is declined alongside the revolver. PRO then conceded it could not separate the two groups yet still named about 20% as the cap. CON exposed this as the arbitrary "excludes nobody" number its pressure question had predicted, and showed that relabeling a declined borrower as "not a bridge" only happens after the denial.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won the decisive clashes. The core exchange was B's claim that short bridges "would survive any reasonable cap" versus A's point that lenders price at approval, not repayment, so the cap declines the bridge borrower along with the spiral. B conceded the inability to separate borrower types, then still named ~20%, which A skewered as "the wish I asked about." A also caught B borrowing A's "visible, immediate, and reversible" description of private denial to soften the cap's own categorical exclusion, and the closing turned B's list of fallback options (credit unions, secured cards) against the cap itself. Meanwhile A's affirmative alternative — amortization and ability-to-repay rules that attack the treadmill without a price ceiling and need no appropriation — was never squarely answered; B's "borrowers don't comparison-shop" reply doesn't touch amortization. B fought well: the harm asymmetry (closed door vs. compounding debt), the lead-paint analogy, and the principled defense of a categorical line were genuine routes to victory, and B's pressure questions exposed a real tension in A's position on exclusion. But A answered B's pressure more cleanly (the error-correction/contestability answer and the trigger distinction), while B's answers leaned on relabeling excluded borrowers as rescued. Both sides were grounded and disciplined with plausible arithmetic and no fake sourcing. A made the debate easier to decide and controlled the weighing in the final two speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2: after B conceded lenders cannot separate the three-month repayer from the three-year revolver, A showed that naming 20% anyway was arbitrary, then caught B describing the cap's exclusion with A's own language about reversible private denial — the moment B's weighing framework collapsed.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): A more convincingly distinguished expensive but useful bridge credit from unaffordable debt, and showed that denying credit leaves the underlying emergency unresolved. B repeatedly treated excluded loans as necessarily traps and relied on alternatives whose availability it never established. A also answered the exclusion objection with a meaningful distinction between repayment-based safeguards and price ceilings. A overstated exclusion’s permanence and understated interest savings, limiting the margin, but B never adequately resolved the central comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s approval-stage pricing rebuttal defeated B’s claim that short-term bridge loans would survive a reasonable cap: lenders cannot know repayment duration when approving credit. B conceded that information problem but then repeated that a product requiring a high rate cannot be a bridge, leaving A’s central objection intact.
