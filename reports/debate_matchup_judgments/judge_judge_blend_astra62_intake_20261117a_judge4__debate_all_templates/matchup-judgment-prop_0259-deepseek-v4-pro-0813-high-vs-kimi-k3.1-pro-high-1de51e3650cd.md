### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): A built a strong opening using well-known historical reversals (US 1970s, Russia) to conclusively defeat permanent summer time, and framed a clean "morning light is compulsory" heuristic. But the motion's most contested comparison was permanent standard time versus seasonal DST, not versus permanent summer time, and B consistently pressed exactly that gap: B2.4 and B4.3/B4.5 repeatedly showed that A's historical evidence indicts locking in late sunrise year-round, not the seasonal practice of reverting to standard time in winter and shifting only in summer when dawn is naturally earlier. A's rebuttals (A2.5, A4.1-A4.2) mostly reasserted that the "lag" persists through DST months without ever quantifying or directly refuting the winter-to-summer conflation B identified, and A's closing again leaned on the compulsory/optional asymmetry without closing that loop, which B's closing explicitly named as question-begging. Pressure-question answers were roughly symmetric in vagueness, but B's sustained rebuttal pressure did more cumulative damage to A's live route to victory than A's pressure did to B's, even accounting for the format-clipping penalty on B's opening.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.4/B4.5's repeated point that A's US/Russia evidence refutes permanent summer time but does not show seasonal DST produces comparable winter-style harm, which A never fully answered.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by exposing the missing comparison in Side B’s case. B plausibly established transition costs and the dangers of permanent summer time, but repeatedly treated deviation from solar time as proof of sustained net harm without showing that summer morning losses outweigh usable evening daylight across varied countries. A directly answered the pressure questions, conceded genuine harms, and explained why seasonal DST avoids B’s winter examples. The clipped opening was an execution cost, but not enough to outweigh A’s stronger rebuttal and burden analysis.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s most decisive response was that evidence against dark winter mornings under permanent summer time does not refute seasonal DST, which returns to standard time in winter. A then showed that calling DST an ongoing one-hour “lag” describes the clock arrangement but does not establish months of impairment or complete the net-harm comparison.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won a clear but competitive debate by building the cleaner path on morning-light circadian stakes and then adapting: after CON forced weighing, B reframed DST as transition spike plus months of delayed morning light, and crystallized morning activity as compulsory versus evening use as optional. CON (A) landed sharper burden hits—especially that permanent-summer history does not settle seasonal DST, and that “solar lag” is not automatic injury—and recovered well despite a clipped opening, but never showed seasonal evening gains outweigh the admitted disruption and shifted mornings, so B left the easier third-party reason to affirm permanent standard time.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s R2/closing move that CON’s “brief transition vs months of benefit” frame fails because seasonal DST also runs an ongoing one-hour morning lag for the whole DST period, so evening gains must be discounted rather than treated as pure upside.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): A built a clean, repeated asymmetry argument (universal, unavoidable clock-change costs plus biologically scarce morning light vs. a substitutable, locally-captured evening-light benefit) and sustained it through PQ and rebuttals, directly answering B's strongest counter ("fragments of summer time") by distinguishing optional, targeted scheduling from a coercive universal clock shift, and crucially stressed that nonparticipants need not follow others' voluntary later hours. B's closing largely restated the "fragments" and "months not a Monday" framings without squarely rebutting A's opt-out distinction, leaving that exchange tilted toward A. B's case was solid and well-structured, especially the point that winter protection is already secured by the status quo so permanent standard gains little while losing usable evening light, but it did not fully neutralize A's substitutability logic by the end. A's two clipped speeches are a real execution cost and slightly hurt its grounding/discipline score, but the substantive content that survived was still the more decisive and better-resolved line of argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1) distinguishing "de facto summer time" from targeted scheduling, paired with the closing's "coordination burden belongs primarily to those seeking the benefit" line, most damaged B's "fragments" argument, since B's final speech reasserted the claim without directly engaging the opt-out distinction.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by showing that the status quo already preserves winter morning light while also capturing usable summer evenings. Its “summer time in fragments” response exposed the weakness in B’s proposed substitute: widespread local rescheduling would recreate adaptation and impose less predictable coordination burdens. B distinguished targeted choice from universal disruption well, but never fully answered the network effects involving schools, childcare, transit, and employers, nor established that abundant summer morning light outweighs months of lost evening light. B’s two clipped turns were an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange combined marginal comparison with coordination: standard time already protects the difficult winter mornings, while permanent standard mainly changes summer, when dawn is abundant and evening light is highly usable. B’s local-scheduling answer then conceded the demand for later activity without adequately resolving the resulting fragmented schedule conflicts.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): B built the cleaner path to victory by making substitutability decisive: summer evening activity can shift locally without a national clock move, while sleep disruption and winter morning light cannot be manufactured by schedule changes. A mounted a strong coordination and “fragments of summer time” critique and correctly noted that the status quo already buys winter mornings, but B neutralized that pressure by confining residual coordination costs to benefit-seekers and by using A’s own winter-morning concession against both permanent summer time and the seasonal trade. Across rounds B narrowed the clash more effectively and weighed the unavoidable costs against the replaceable benefits with greater force.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 split: targeted seasonal hours for willing parties are not de facto national summer time and do not conscript hospitals, bakeries, or nonparticipants the way a universal clock shift does.
