### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins a competitive debate. Its case had a clear structure: tools and large distributors are chokepoints, so a process signal can raise the cost of synthetic media at scale without registering speakers. CON (A) had real pressure on two fronts: labels can be stripped or bypassed, and "unlabeled" content is ambiguous for platforms. Its question about what the mandate actually punishes was sharp. However, CON's main route came to depend on identity-based accountability. PRO had already shown that identity does not restore a stripped signal, and CON effectively conceded this in [A2.4]. PRO then reframed what identity adds as "a person to punish," and argued that punishment is not a cue at the moment of viewing. That left CON defending a registry the motion explicitly rejects, without showing it closes the bypass. CON's definitional-chill and label-fatigue points were answered reasonably: labels mark gray zones rather than ban them, and the goal is a reliable signal for those who check. PRO did leave one tension partly unresolved. If unlabeled content is not presumed synthetic, it is unclear how amplification limits catch stripped fakes. Still, PRO's comparative framing was cleaner: the same bypass exists either way, but identity adds a toll on speech.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 and closing exchange decided the debate. PRO forced CON to admit that a named account can strip the signal just as easily, so identity verification adds a punishable person but no restored provenance. PRO then landed the point that "punishment is not a cue": a threatened lawsuit does not mark the file a voter is watching. CON's attempt to weigh consequence over cue never showed that the identity regime actually closes the bypass it complained about.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): CON wins a close but clear debate by exposing an enforcement gap at the core of the proposal. PRO's strongest turn was that identity does not restore provenance, but CON reframed identity as accountability rather than pixel restoration. More decisively, PRO conceded that stripped files may remain lawful and that unlabeled uploads are not presumed synthetic. That leaves a screenshot or re-encoded fake able to travel as ordinary ambiguous content while honest creators carry labeling costs and platforms become private arbiters. CON's "two-tier floor made of stickers" framing crystallized that defect better than PRO resolved it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's answer on amplification made the decisive turn: once PRO said unlabeled uploads are not presumed synthetic, a screenshot or stripped deepfake can avoid demotion as legitimate device-captured content, so the mandate either lets the lie spread or forces platforms to demote lawful anonymous speech.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won narrowly by keeping the debate focused on the proposition’s actual design: a process-based provenance signal that can improve epistemic cues without imposing centralized identity verification. A’s pressure question forced B to concede that identity verification would not restore stripped metadata, and B’s later pivot to liability, while coherent, was less tightly connected to the labeling objective and did not sufficiently answer A’s privacy and anonymity harms. B’s enforcement and platform-ambiguity points remained live, but A gave the cleaner overall reason to prefer the mandate as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Q1 exposed that identity verification would not fix the stripping problem; B’s R2 pivot to accountability was direct, but A’s closing effectively framed it as punishment rather than the real-time provenance cue the motion requires.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins because it pinned PRO to the point of harm: the deceptive copy, such as a screen recording or group-chat forward, arrives with no mark at all. PRO conceded in A1 that this viewer may still be misled. That retreat shifted PRO's case from viewer-facing transparency to after-the-fact traces for intermediaries. CON then built a clean fork. If absence of a signal carries consequences, unsigned authentic evidence loses reach. If it does not, the stripping violation is theater. PRO's reply was that the duty attaches only to distributors who strip, while absence-as-status is forbidden. That reply never explained how a stripped synthetic file is distinguished from a never-marked authentic one, which was CON's closing point. PRO had a reasonable "partial beats nothing" and status-quo comparison, and its safety-seal analogy was rhetorically useful. However, it underused its strongest route: default marking by mainstream generative tools would catch casual fabrication. It also repeatedly waved off CON's signing-authority gatekeeping concern as "implementation." PRO also cited nonexistent reference IDs ([C1.x]), a minor execution cost. CON's speeches progressed and narrowed the clash, while PRO's later speeches largely restated the "provenance, not papers" frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.5 and A5.2 decided the debate. CON argued that PRO cannot both make a stripped signal an actionable distributor violation and forbid platforms from treating missing provenance as a negative status, because the deceptive forward and the witness's protest video arrive as the same unmarked file. PRO's B5.3 answer, that the duty attaches only to stripping distributors, did not resolve how absence is read at the point of belief.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): A wins because the decisive fork survived the exchange: if missing or stripped provenance is penalized, it pressures unlabeled authentic speech; if absence is protected, enforcement against stripped synthetic media becomes theater. B conceded that the harmful cropped or forwarded copy may still mislead, then tried to separate strip liability from absence-as-status, but A showed the file itself cannot be distinguished from ordinary unmarked evidence. A’s screen-recording and protest-video examples made the tradeoff vivid and harder for B to escape.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was A’s later fork: B wanted regulators to punish removal while also forbidding platforms from treating absence as negative, and A showed those are the same unmarked file read two ways.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): B built the cleaner live route: the mandate only works if missing or stripped signals are treated as adverse, but that harms unlabeled authentic media and creates de facto gatekeeping; if absence is spared, the signal fails at the point of deception. A’s partial-transparency and no-papers framing was strong, but it never fully solved the indistinguishability of stripped synthetic media from never-marked real media, and the misplaced reference IDs were a small execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttal 2 and closing fork: A needed both punishable stripping and no penalty for mere absence; B showed the forwarded file arrives as absence, so either the deceived viewer cannot tell or unsigned real speech is downranked.
