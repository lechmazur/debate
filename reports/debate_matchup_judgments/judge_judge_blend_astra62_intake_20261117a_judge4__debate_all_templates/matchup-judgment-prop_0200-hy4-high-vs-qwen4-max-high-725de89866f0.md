### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): This was a close, high-quality debate, and CON (A) edges it. PRO built a strong practical case: legality creates permits, defendants, and ledgers, while a ban lands only on accountable commercial actors. Its line that CON's rule "selects for the least accountable taker" was memorable. But CON kept a sharper conceptual distinction alive throughout, between recording a taking and authorizing a title. That distinction defused PRO's main impact. CON showed that a later treaty can require state declarations and charge unauthorized consumption to the responsible state without anyone recognizing sale. That makes PRO's ledger benefit separable from the motion. PRO answered the cap-setter question honestly with "Nowhere," which kept it credible. CON then used that answer to show the motion's only conditions, transparency and debris control, police conduct but do not supply the authority to turn common ice into private property. PRO's best reply pointed back to Articles I and II and to severed material, but it arrived largely in the closing and never fully answered the lock-in concern. CON argued that recognized, insured sales create vested expectations that are costlier to unwind than wrongful depletion. PRO said "expectations are negotiable," and CON answered that point fairly directly. Both sides were disciplined and avoided fake precision. CON's argument structure was slightly cleaner at the decisive point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange began when CON's Q1 asked where the motion supplies a common cap-setter or tribunal. PRO answered "Nowhere" in B4.1. CON's A4.2 then distinguished an unrecorded loss chargeable to a state from a licensed title chain that a treaty must ratify or buy out. That neutralized PRO's argument that only legality produces the accounting a future treaty needs. PRO's closing comeback, that CON's remedy itself requires a ledger, was sharp. However, CON had already separated declaration requirements from sale recognition.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B wins by distinguishing transparent extraction from justified ownership of finite resources. A never establishes its central counterfactual: that withholding commercial recognition necessarily prevents reporting or supervision. B directly concedes that withholding title cannot stop state consumption, while explaining why it still restrains commercially financed extraction and avoids creating vested claims. A’s existing-law argument remains relevant, and B overstates the inevitability of future compensation, but A does not adequately answer depletion under the obligations actually proposed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure on extraction caps elicited A’s admission that caps were merely possible regulatory additions, not guaranteed obligations. B then separated recording removals from legitimizing ownership, undermining A’s claim that documented, licensed extraction adequately protects later allocation.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): CON wins by holding PRO to the motion as written: transparency and debris control police conduct but do not supply a common authority to convert finite shared ice into private title. PRO's strongest counter—that a ban merely selects for unaccountable state extraction—was answered by CON's distinction between physical taking as a chargeable wrong and recognized sale as a deed creating vested expectations a later treaty must ratify or buy out.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 were decisive: PRO's proposed cap, log, and revocable permit were shown not to be in the proposition, and PRO's later “Nowhere” concession confirmed there is no common cap-setter, inspector, or tribunal to bind absent states.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins on a clearer central question: what does title add that a licence cannot? PRO opened well. Its severed-mass versus territory distinction was sharp, and in Rebuttal 1 it scored a real point: if CON's licensing hooks can supervise operators, they can also enforce sale conditions. CON then rebuilt the case with a precise split. A licence regulates conduct and can be revoked. Title is a claim against everyone and creates a ratchet. CON also argued that defeasibility voids paper while the geology stays gone. In the question-and-answer exchange, CON answered PRO's downstream-buyer question directly. The catch-certificate analogy showed that traceability can follow material without property. CON also turned PRO's "ice is gone either way" point into a clean asymmetry: the ice loss cancels out, but the "unowned" presumption survives only under supervision. PRO's answer on forums ("wherever the interest is asserted") was left exposed as no single authoritative forum. Its answer to CON's precedent question was a deflection rather than a counterexample. PRO's closing kept insisting that title adds consequences, but it never explained why a buyer would check title rather than a regulatory certificate. CON's final framing was tighter: the consequences cancel out, but the ratchet does not.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2 was decisive. The catch-certificate answer showed downstream traceability needs no ownership, which defused PRO's claim that "information is not identical." Conceding that consumed ice cancels out isolated the compensation and ratchet asymmetry as the remaining difference between the paths. PRO's closing never dislodged that framing.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B more convincingly showed that reporting requirements and trade restrictions could deliver A's claimed accountability benefits without recognizing contested ownership. B also exposed the unresolved adjudication problem behind A's assertion that rival ledgers would identify a losing chain. A's distinction between extracted material and territorial rights substantially weakened B's compensation and entrenchment claims, keeping the debate competitive. Nevertheless, A never adequately answered why regulatory sanctions were mere paperwork while title conditions supplied uniquely effective consequences.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest response separated traceability from ownership: certificates and import conditions can follow material downstream and make noncompliant cargo commercially unusable. This directly answered A's buyer-tracking pressure question and undermined its central claim that recognized title supplies otherwise unavailable enforcement leverage.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by separating licensing conduct from property title: supervision can impose transparency and reach downstream buyers without vesting ownership, while recognition creates contested claims and an owners' ratchet before a treaty. A's answers preserve the key asymmetry—the unowned-Moon presumption—whereas B never supplied an authoritative forum for rival flag-state titles or a convincing non-compensation precedent.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: its fisheries catch-certificate example answered B's downstream-buyer pressure without granting ownership, and its concession that consumed ice cancels out isolated treaty freedom as the surviving difference.
