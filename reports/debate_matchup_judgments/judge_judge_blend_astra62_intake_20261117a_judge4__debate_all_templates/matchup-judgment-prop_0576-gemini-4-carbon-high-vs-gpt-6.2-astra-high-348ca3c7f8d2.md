### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): A (CON) won with a cleaner comparison. A conceded that campaigns must pay their own way, then offered a concrete alternative: separate, disclosed campaign accounts, disallowed deductions, and no exemption for campaign space. From there A asked the key question: what does institution-wide forfeiture add once the campaign has paid in full? B never gave a convincing answer. B's strongest points were the alleged contradiction between A's entanglement worry and A's cost allocation, the "moral halo" claim, and the claim that institutions cannot be split like spreadsheets. A handled each directly. Cost allocation prices resources without judging whether worship was spiritually significant. Forfeiture, by contrast, requires officials to weigh the ministry itself to decide whether politics has come to predominate. A also showed that B's own phone-bank answer requires pricing below the forfeiture threshold, and that B's separate-charity fix does not avoid cost allocation for shared buildings. B's later case rested mainly on the political usefulness of trust and fellowship. A reframed that as an objection to free association rather than to any subsidy. B's closing restated the halo and infrastructure claims without dislodging A's point that forfeiture shifts the cost onto the charitable work. A's Q&A answers were direct and properly labeled. B's answers were reasonable but partly conceded A's pricing logic.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's exchange in A2.1, A4.2 and A4.5 was decisive. A showed that B's "independent charity with zero audit friction" still requires allocating costs for shared space. A then showed that B's own fair-rent answer prices use below the forfeiture threshold. Finally, A forced B to choose between examining resource diversion under either arrangement and admitting that the real objection is the political usefulness of trust itself. That left forfeiture without a distinct payoff beyond burdening the charitable work.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): A narrowly wins a high-level, tightly matched exchange. Both sides argued the motion as written with real clash. A's path to victory was threefold: (1) the motion's own "overt partisan actor rather than primarily charitable/spiritual" criterion absorbs B's hardest objection (line-drawing about predominance), since the proposition presupposes that classification; (2) A's first rebuttal caught a genuine contradiction — B objected to officials judging worship-versus-campaigning [B1.4] while B's own accounting remedy [B1.2] requires exactly that pricing, from a congregation's first political act rather than only at a clear outer boundary; (3) A's institutional-subsidy weighing ("tax exemption subsidizes an institution as a whole, not an itemized rental tab") survived B's "what does forfeiture buy once the campaign pays in full" test only partially answered — B's fallback that A is really taxing "the political usefulness of trust itself" is philosophically elegant but concedes that tax-exempt institutions can lawfully serve as party infrastructure, which is the very harm the proposition targets. B's counterproposal was the more original construct, and B's pressure questions and closing ("Make politics pay its own way. Do not make relief pay for being outnumbered") were excellent, forcing real concessions from A (arm's-length pricing, crowd-cost charging). But B ultimately defended a remedy rather than defeating the proposition, and its strongest burden — that forfeiture penalizes genuine relief — was met with the independent-charity answer that B dented but never broke. Razor-thin but real edge to A on motion fit and the entanglement asymmetry.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's Rebuttal 1 contradiction catch: B's warning that officials must not decide whether a politicized service is worship or campaigning [B1.4] directly conflicts with B's own requirement to charge a separate account for staff time, mailing lists, and space [B1.2]. B's partial recovery — distinguishing record-keeping from worship investigation, and noting A conceded arm's-length pricing anyway [B4.1-B4.2] — was skillful, but the asymmetry stood: forfeiture triggers only at the clear outer boundary while B's cost rule drags every congregation into accounting from its first organized appeal.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): A wins a close but clear remedy comparison. Both agreed campaigns should not be tax-subsidized, but A showed that once recruitment, facilities, lists, and overhead are charged to nondeductible political funds, institutional forfeiture collects nothing more from the party and only strips favorable treatment from genuine relief. B never squarely priced the residual “halo” or volunteer-trust subsidy, and its own fair-rent concession plus spin-off still required the factual sorting it called unworkable. B’s bargain frame and entanglement pressure stayed live, but A gave the cleaner vote test: make politics pay its way without making relief pay for being outnumbered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s paid-in-full test (A2.7, A4.3–A4.6, A5) was decisive: after meeting B’s volunteer-army pressure by charging crowd recruitment rather than endorsement seconds, A showed forfeiture’s distinctive effect is a bill to the kitchen, not an unpaid campaign subsidy.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO wins because it built a narrow, administrable rule and then answered each CON pressure point concretely. Its two-offices donor-parity frame made the stakes easy to grasp, and the rule stayed focused on sustained, primary partisan activity with notice, independent review, and a burden on the government. Under pressure, PRO gave direct answers: inquiries need public grounds, disputed dollars are resolved for the congregation, and enforcement waits until after the election and applies only going forward. CON landed real hits. It showed that issue-framed electioneering will escape the rule. It also argued that even limited records requests risk selective targeting. Its strongest point was that all-or-nothing revocation could tax a sanctuary's property, and PRO never fully neutralized that. But CON's case pulled in two directions. It called PRO's rule both too intrusive and too toothless to matter, and PRO exploited that tension. The safeguards PRO added shrink both the intrusion and the coercion CON warned about. CON's evasion point only shows that the rule is imperfect, not that clear cases deserve a permanent subsidy. CON also conceded in A1 that "invoices settle the math" in the clear case. That let PRO's closing argue that the religious firewall does no work where the evidence is unambiguous. CON's closing restated its harms rather than resolving that concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B4.1–B4.6 answering CON's Q1/Q2 on audits and pre-election coercion. PRO answered by delaying enforcement until after Election Day, making any loss prospective, and resolving doubtful cases for the congregation. It then reframed the issue: uncertainty about hard cases cannot justify a permanent tax privilege for an operation clearly identified as a campaign. CON's A1 concession that "invoices settle the math" strengthened PRO's position, and CON's later "the target is fictional" reply could not fully recover from it.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side A (PRO) won a competitive but clear debate. A's opening two-offices parity frame set the burden, and when B attacked on entanglement, auditing impossibility, pre-election coercion, and evasion by lawyered churches, A answered each pressure directly: external conduct evidence (invoices, payroll) rather than sermon-policing, counting disputed dollars toward the congregation and placing the burden on the government, prospective post-election enforcement judged across cycles, and restraint toward doubtful cases. Crucially, A held a live route B never fully neutralized: even if sophisticated actors evade, the undisguised campaign-church with clear records should not keep deductible terms, and "uncertainty about the church down the street cannot justify a permanent tax advantage for the campaign office we can identify." B's strongest surviving material—the candor/sophistication disparity and all-or-nothing revocation harm—was blunted by A's showing that coded operations with standing arrangements can still be caught and that revocation can be prospective and pattern-based. B's closing relied on calling A's case a "fictional" hypothetical, which conceded A's clear-case scenario and retreated to a conclusory firewall claim. Both sides argued cleanly without fake sourcing; B's evasion catalog in Rebuttal 2 was sharp, but A's weighing made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B's Pressure Q2 (pre-election coercion vs. post-election evasion) against A's A2 answer: A conceded delay but reframed it as a cost, not futility—prospective enforcement judged across election cycles, so a congregation that genuinely returns to ministry is safe while a repeat offender cannot reset each November. B's Rebuttal 2 catalog of evasions (issue framing, budget caps, mixed duties) then did real damage, but A's closing weighing—that underinclusiveness cannot justify subsidizing the campaign office we can clearly identify, and that B conceded "invoices settle the math"—kept A's core equity route alive and decisive.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the motion narrow—sustained, overt partisan work as the primary undertaking—and then meeting the live objections with a government burden, post-election prospective review, and disputed dollars counted for the congregation. That preserved a clear equity case for identifiable campaign operations while protecting doubtful and merely outspoken congregations. Side A raised real concerns about candor, evasion, audits, and sanctuary-wide tax loss, but overstated how far those safeguards nullified the rule and never justified a deduction for explicit election spending once the record is clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both pressure questions directly, used post-election prospective revocation to defuse coercion, and kept A’s concession that clear invoices settle the math from collapsing the case.
