### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side B won solidly by dismantling the economic mechanics of portable training accounts and winning the central weighing on incidence and incentives. Side B cleanly separated completed skills (which workers already keep when changing jobs) from unspent tuition budgets (which remain generous precisely because utilization is partial and firms recoup actual training outlays during tenure). Through pointed pressure questioning, Side B forced Side A to commit to an HSA-style cash-funded account and concede that equilibrium wages would adjust downward to finance it. Side B then showed how that trade harms both groups of workers: non-enrollees lose unrestricted take-home pay into illiquid training credits, while nursing assistants who actually need schooling lose generous tuition coverage in favor of shrunken vouchers. Side A argued resourcefully that guaranteed property beats a revocable benefit, but suffered execution costs in Rebuttal 2—getting clipped at the word cap while prematurely accusing Side B of dodging a question before Side B's turn to answer—and never overcame Side B's proof that portability shrinks real training opportunities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's multi-turn sequence across [B2.1]-[B2.6], [B3.1], and [B4.1]-[B4.2] (crystallized in [B5.1]-[B5.3]): after exposing that employers cannot recoup productivity from unspent tuition balances, Side B forced Side A to choose between an unvestable unfunded promise and funded cash per worker; once Side A chose funded cash paid partly from equilibrium wages in [A4.1], Side B demonstrated that PRO won the ownership label only by shifting the cost onto the worker's paycheck while shrinking employer training budgets.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by forcing the motion onto a workable account and breaking both versions. Credentials are already portable; an unused tuition claim is not a funded pension and cannot honestly vest. When Side B made the account prefunded cash, it conceded wages may fall to finance it, so workers may trade pay for restricted credits while offers shrink. Its move from single-firm accounts to pools and back to a voluntary consortium never showed why a rival would honor the balance without a different compulsory policy. The lock-in frame stayed live, but it did not outweigh those incidence and design holes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's cash-out-versus-earmark exchange, followed by its use of Side B's admission that some of the worker's own pay may fund the account, was decisive: portability either vests nothing real or stops being employer-funded training.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): A offered a compelling lock-in and earned-property case, but B consistently exposed the distinction between portable skills, consumed training, and unfunded benefit promises. A’s funded-account answer came late, conceded possible wage reductions, and left the voluntary-pool and employer-incentive problems unresolved; its clipped rebuttal further weakened execution. B directly answered both pressure questions and turned A’s concessions into a clear worker-level tradeoff, especially for short-tenure employees.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it distinguished funded pension assets from tuition promises, used A’s wage-incidence concession against the ownership analogy, and showed that skills already move while portable funding could reduce training offers and pay.
