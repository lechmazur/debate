### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO built a cleaner and more disciplined case. The schedule directly removes instructional time. District savings and retention gains are conditional and design-dependent. Remedies such as Friday programs are evidence that a gap must be bought back, not proof that the model works. CON leaned heavily on brittle precision with no visible grounding: $20,000 per departing teacher, 1–2 months of lost learning, 20–30% lower attrition, and 10–15 minutes per transition. It also misattributed its own statistics to PRO's opening, claiming PRO "explicitly acknowledges" the novice-teacher learning gap. CON's offset math was weak, since roughly one reclaimed hour does not offset 7–8 lost hours. "Funded entirely by savings" was asserted without support. Most damaging, CON's pressure questions attacked its own claims, asking how attrition gains could be causally tied to the schedule and whether blocks raise achievement. This handed PRO free concessions, which PRO used effectively in its second rebuttal and closing. PRO answered with proper A1/A2 labels and kept narrowing to a clear comparison: direct harm versus conditional benefit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2.3–A2.4 and A2.6 turned CON's Friday enrichment and redirected-savings argument into an admission that the four-day structure creates gaps requiring new spending. PRO then reinforced this in A4.1/A4.3, using CON's own self-undermining questions to show that the retention and block-scheduling benefits were causally unestablished.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A dominated on substance, epistemic discipline, and execution. Side A locked Side B into an inescapable dilemma: removing 7–8 weekly instructional hours directly harms learning (especially for young and high-need students) because reclaiming roughly one hour of transition time leaves most lost time uncompensated, yet trying to close those gaps with Friday camps, childcare, tutoring, and Friday busing proves the schedule creates deficits while consuming the marginal utility and transportation savings used to justify it. Side A also dismantled the retention link by noting selection bias, alternative ways to provide planning time, and the difference between preserving teacher quality and restoring cut hours. Side B compounded its internal contradictions by repeatedly attributing its own opening claims about novice teachers and fragmented schedules to Side A, and by mistakenly cross-examining its own statistics and arguments during Pressure Questions ([B3.1]–[B3.2]).
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's "buy-back" rebuttal against Side B's Friday enrichment defense ([A2.4]–[A2.7], [A5.3]–[A5.4]): when Side B argued that Friday utility and bus savings fully fund Friday camps, childcare, tutoring, and even Friday transportation ([B4.2]), Side A showed that needing those remedies proves the four-day model creates instructional gaps while erasing the very cost savings Side B claimed.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A built the cleaner comparison: the conceded 7-8 lost hours are direct and universal, while savings shrink once Friday supports are paid for and retention remains only observational. Side B never showed how roughly an hour of reclaimed transitions or optional enrichment offsets that loss, relied on brittle precise figures, and repeatedly misattributed its own novice-teacher claim to PRO. Its pressure questions also challenged its own retention and block-scheduling claims, giving A damaging concessions to exploit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A's Rebuttal 1 and Pressure Q1: B conceded 7-8 weekly hours lost, while its transition-time offset amounted to about an hour, leaving longer blocks as time management rather than proven replacement instruction.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): CON wins because it set a clearer decision rule and showed that PRO did not meet it. CON's rule was that the motion requires net harm, which means comparing learning losses against savings and retention. CON also separated harms that come from a compressed calendar from harms that come from cutting services. PRO's strongest line was a real one: the districts that adopt four-day weeks are often cash-strapped and cannot afford safeguards. PRO's Q1 also pulled a candid concession that CON could not name a specific district. PRO's weakness was evidence. It leaned heavily on unverifiable, brittle figures: "double digits," "$10,000 lifetime losses," "matched-pair studies," "$1,200 remediation," "15-20% higher turnover," "$15,000 per teacher." Most of these were introduced or escalated in Rebuttal 2, and none had grounding. Its burnout and achievement claims rested on assertion more than mechanism. CON answered both pressure questions directly. It gave a concrete workload guardrail and an honest admission on A1. It then turned PRO's safeguard-cost argument into a netting problem: implementation costs belong inside the accounting, not stacked on top of it. CON's "categorical" framing slightly overreaches, since the motion is a general comparison rather than a claim about every implementation. Even so, PRO never gave a credible net accounting, so CON leaves the debate with the cleaner path to the ballot.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's Rebuttal 2 response, B4.1–B4.3. CON conceded honestly that it could not name a district, then argued that PRO's safeguard and remediation costs must be netted against gross savings rather than added as separate harms. It also flagged PRO's $1,200 and $15,000 figures as having no baseline. This blunted PRO's best pressure question and exposed that PRO's numbers were doing work they could not support.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won a competitive debate through far stronger epistemic discipline, sharper causal separation, and a decisive advantage in the Pressure Questions and second rebuttal exchange. Side A pressed a resonant real-world critique—that cash-strapped districts rarely save enough to fund the Friday safeguards Side B conceded vulnerable students need, whereas Side B leaned too heavily on an overly strict categorical burden standard. However, Side A undermined its own path to victory with careless slips (claiming a four-day week costs students two days per week), dropped opponent mechanisms (preserved weekly instructional hours, improved attendance, fewer transitions), and severe brittle fake precision in Rebuttal 2—inventing exact turnover rates, remediation dollar figures, and three-year matched-pair study details when pressed. Side B exposed those unsupported numbers, modeled closed-book candor when declining Side A's district-naming trap, and neutralized the teacher-burnout claim with a concrete protected-planning-day contract design.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Pressure Questions ([B3.1]-[B3.2]) and follow-up in [B4.1]-[B4.3]: B exposed that Side A's headline achievement and cost claims lacked baselines or separation from simultaneous budget cuts, dismantled the unsupported "$1,200" and "$15,000" figures Side A invented in response, and directly answered Side A's teacher-workload challenge in [B4.2] by pairing four teaching days with a protected fifth planning day.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won by framing the burden correctly: PRO had to prove categorical net harm, not merely harm under unprotected schedules. B repeatedly distinguished calendar design from service cuts, showed safeguards and workload could be preserved, and exposed PRO's reliance on unsupported exact cost and turnover figures. PRO's real-world objection was forceful but largely asserted rather than established.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it honestly conceded it could not name a verified district, then turned the point by arguing safeguard costs must be netted against savings rather than double-counted as separate harms, while giving a concrete workload guardrail.
