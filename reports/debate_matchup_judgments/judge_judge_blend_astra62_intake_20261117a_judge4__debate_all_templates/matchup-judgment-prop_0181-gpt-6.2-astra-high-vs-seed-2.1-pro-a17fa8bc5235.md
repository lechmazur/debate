### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by showing that a court-ordered query of only expressly opted-in adult profiles can produce an unknown kinship lead from the volunteer’s data and crime-scene sample, while barring action on kinship alone. Side B never squarely neutralized that mechanism distinction or the point that a private download helps only when someone already knows which family to ask. Its mission-creep claim was also met by the symmetric fact that a legislature can repeal a ban, so the cleaner vote is against a strict prohibition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal, which answered both pressure questions by separating a kinship inference drawn from a consenting profile from exposure of relatives’ records, and showed why voluntary downloads cannot replace a limited query when neither side knows the connection.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B built the clearer path to victory by defending a narrowly court-ordered, opt-in homicide query and consistently distinguishing a kinship lead from grounds to investigate relatives. A’s consent and mission-creep concerns were serious, but became repetitive and overstated, while its allowance of direct voluntary disclosures weakened its claim that the kinship inference itself is impermissible. B answered the main pressures directly and preserved a concrete benefit that A’s strict ban would forbid.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal turned A’s voluntary-download answer against the ban: if direct disclosure is acceptable, the kinship inference alone cannot be decisive, while a limited database query can identify the relevant family when neither the volunteer nor police knows whom to approach.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B defeated the strict ban by separating a court-ordered kinship comparison among expressly consenting adults from intrusive investigation of relatives, requiring independent grounds and forensic confirmation. Side A asserted inevitable scope creep but never adequately answered that a statutory ban is equally repealable and that illegal backdoors violate either rule. B also showed voluntary downloads cannot substitute when neither volunteer nor detective knows which family to ask.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: it clarified that its safeguard was a statute binding police, not changeable platform terms, and turned Side A's mother-download concession against it—if police may infer kinship from a voluntarily shared file, the inference itself cannot justify forbidding the same limited platform match.
