### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a close, high-quality debate. Both sides crystallized the clash well around the "last lane" question, and B's equity framing ("whoever pays vs whoever needs it") was the most memorable rhetorical move. But A did the stronger argumentative work: A's answers to B's pressure questions were direct and effective, especially the compounding-asymmetry argument (routine commuters pay daily and shift modes while rare crisis trips face only a cheap one-off toll), which B's closing dichotomy ("set it low and it clogs, set it high and it excludes") never truly neutralized. A also squarely dismantled B's claim that a ban leaves only a "trickle" of "occasional guests," showing that continuous freight, ambulance, accessible, and cab traffic in a major metropolitan core requires real lanes and kerbs regardless — and B never recovered on that point. A further exposed that B's rescue of hard cases rested on an assumed, unfunded regulated taxi service while B conceded executives could still buy cab rides, blunting the wealth-filter frame. B's surviving routes (price cannot distinguish crisis from whim; toll-funded transit creates dependency on traffic) were real but insufficient against A's weighing that pricing funds transit, clears gridlock, and preserves a lifeline. B's clipped opening was a minor execution cost. Margin is clear but competitive: A made the debate easier to decide by answering more of the live pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was the "last lane" sequence: B's closing (B5.1–B5.2) cleverly converted A's concession of one kerbed lane into "CON's answer is whoever pays; mine is whoever needs it," but A's closing (A5.1–A5.3) landed the harder blows — noting B had already admitted executives ride that lane in cabs on a whim, that prohibition merely outlaws using the car you own, and that the compounding asymmetry of daily vs one-off tolls keeps the lane clear without pricing out emergencies. Also decisive was A4.3's demolition of B's "trickle of occasional guests" claim about central-district motor traffic, which B never repaired.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that a full ban does not uniquely remake major-city streets or reserve the last lane for genuine need. Pricing still deters habitual trips through compounding costs, funds transit, and leaves a cheaper one-off escape valve, while B’s taxi alternative remains wealth-accessible, more expensive for families, and unfunded. B’s equality and space frames were sharp but did not neutralize residual vehicle volume, the funding gap, or the admitted cab loophole.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal and closing turned B’s “whoever needs it” claim: after B admitted executives can still ride taxis on a whim, prohibition neither rations by need nor raises transit revenue, while modest recurring tolls can keep one necessary lane clear without blocking rare crises.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): CON won the central comparison: pricing can clear gridlock and reclaim redundant lanes while preserving a cheap, occasional escape valve and funding transit. PRO's space and equity claims were narrowed by the one-lane service-vehicle reality, the persistence of wealthy taxi access, and the unfunded need-based taxi system required to replace private cars.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: repeated modest tolls deter routine commuting through compounding cost without blocking a rare emergency trip, while one kerbed lane remains necessary for buses, freight, ambulances, and accessible vehicles anyway.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won the decisive clashes. The "ban as infinite price" frame organized the whole case, and A's rare-trip examples (nurse, daughter driving frail father) survived every exchange because B's answers required escalating concessions: a growing exemption list, a shuttle fleet introduced in the fourth speech, and "no 3 a.m. exemption." A's frequency-based answer to B's toll dilemma (peak charges bite routine commuters, not rare necessary trips) defused B's strongest equity attack. B's physical-transformation argument was A's weakest moment initially, but A neutralized it by noting the ban's own streets still carry buses, freight, and shuttles, and that priced cities like London and Stockholm reallocated freed space anyway. A's closing crystallized with the memorable "what happens on the night it fails" fallback test, which B never answered beyond labeling it a false bargain. B was competent and rhetorically capable but repeatedly borrowed A's framing (admitting selective street bans work, relying on perimeter drop-offs and promised night buses) without resolving the day-one and rare-trip burdens. Both sides followed the Q/A format properly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.2 and A4.5-A4.6: A's frequency answer demolished B's flat-toll dilemma, and A's dissection of B4.1-B4.2 exposed that the ban's functionality depended on an uninvented shuttle fleet and rescheduled buses with no fallback, turning B's own required answers into the concession that decided the debate.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side A more convincingly showed that an absolute ban creates rigid hard cases and no fallback when promised transit fails, while speed and space gains can largely be won by pricing plus selective closures without an infinite price on rare high-need trips. Side B’s equity and geometry case stayed live, but its answers increasingly depended on new shuttles and flawless day-one service that did not neutralize those costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal and closing: after B barred taxis and offered perimeter shuttles plus rescheduled night buses, A showed those fixes still leave no exit if they fail, whereas a high peak charge bites daily driving by frequency while still allowing the rare trip and funding the bus.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side A built the cleaner comparative case: pricing can distinguish routine from rare trips, vary by time and frequency, fund transit, and be corrected, while a total ban sorts vehicles rather than needs and offers no fallback when transit fails. B's threshold and fairness arguments remained forceful, especially on pricing alone not transforming streets, but B had to ban taxis, rely on promised night buses and newly introduced shuttles, and never adequately answered the revenue or failure-mode pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: the frequency answer dissolved B's toll dilemma, the scale answer distinguished corridor closures from a district-wide perimeter, and it exposed B's taxi ban, perimeter transfer, and invented shuttle fleet as non-door-to-door substitutes dependent on flawless launch-day transit.
