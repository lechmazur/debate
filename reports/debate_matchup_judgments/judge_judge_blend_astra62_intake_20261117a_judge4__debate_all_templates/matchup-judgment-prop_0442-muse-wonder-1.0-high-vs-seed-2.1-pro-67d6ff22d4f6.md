### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins. Its case was the more coherent one, and it did more to narrow the clash. PRO's strongest moves were three: - **Enforcement symmetry.** If evasion defeats a fuel standard, it should defeat CON's own preferred measures too. - **Clarifying the mechanism.** A carbon-intensity timetable is phased, which defuses the charge that it is "rigid" and "premature." - **The endpoint question (Q2).** Without a binding endpoint, what ever ends heavy fuel oil use? CON's answer to the endpoint question listed complementary measures: slow-steaming caps, shore power, pollutant limits and bunkering investment. None of these actually requires ships to stop burning heavy fuel oil. PRO's closing exploited that gap cleanly. CON had genuine strengths: - The equity argument about bulk essentials was its best line. Food, fertilizer and medicine carry a high freight share for import-dependent states. - Its A1 answer was sharp. A two-to-four-fold cost gap creates stronger evasion incentives than shore power or speed rules do. CON also had weaknesses: - It kept attacking a "rigid, immediate zero-carbon" version of the timetable that PRO had explicitly disavowed. - It leaned on shaky precision, such as single-digit cost premiums under the sulfur cap and "documented" evasion. PRO had soft spots of its own: - Its claims that insurance and charter terms would "follow the vessel" were thin. - Its penalty-rebate idea was fairly called unenforceable. Even so, PRO left the clearer reason to vote. A finite, phased cost buys a dated end to fossil fuel, against an alternative with no endpoint.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Q2 and the follow-up in its closing. PRO asked what mechanism or date ends heavy fuel oil without a binding timetable. CON replied with a list of complementary measures, none of which mandates leaving fossil fuel. That let PRO recast CON's "dirty loop" warning as a description of the unregulated status quo extended indefinitely. CON's best counter was the cost-differential answer to PRO's enforcement-symmetry question. It was strong, but it did not repair the missing endpoint.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins because it keeps the decision on the motion: a binding, phased IMO fuel timetable is the only proposal that actually ends heavy fuel oil, and the motion already accepts higher freight. Side A lands real hits on bulk-commodity equity and the larger evasion prize versus the sulfur cap, but never supplies a mechanism or date that forces carriers off the cheapest fuel. Its alternatives remain complements, and it keeps attacking an overnight zero-carbon shock after Side B repeatedly narrowed the case to progressive intensity cuts, port-state checks, and demand that builds supply. Side B also answers the pass-through and dirty-loop questions more directly, so the cleaner reason to vote is B's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pressure that slow steaming and shore power use the same ports Side A trusts, yet still leave heavy fuel oil legal, and Side A's failure to name any endpoint that makes large carriers surrender that cost advantage.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): A won a clear but competitive debate by defending a phased, technology-neutral timetable that directly addressed supply scale-up, investment certainty, and enforcement while honestly conceding freight-cost pass-through. B offered concrete equity and evasion concerns, especially its cost-differential argument, but often treated the mandate as an immediate zero-carbon switch despite A’s phase-in. Its alternatives delivered nearer-term reductions without a convincing mechanism or endpoint for eliminating fossil shipping, leaving A with the stronger answer to the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s demand for a mechanism or date ending heavy fuel oil use was decisive: B answered with slow steaming, shore power, and preparatory investment, but no comparable requirement to complete the transition. B’s strongest counter was that cheaper measures are easier to verify because they lack the enormous evasion incentives created by costly fuels.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): CON built the cleaner path to victory. It offered a clear counter-model: a binding, port-state-enforced carbon-intensity limit that leaves the method of compliance flexible. It then pressed a dilemma that PRO never fully escaped: either deadlines hold regardless of fuel supply, punishing buyers, or they slip and destroy the investor certainty PRO promised. PRO did answer the pressure questions directly, citing core-lane phasing and port state control. But CON turned both answers. Core-lane phasing creates geographic regressivity. Port state control equally supports an intensity standard, which makes a fuel mandate unnecessary. CON also caught PRO's drift toward a "performance bar," which concedes the flexible approach. PRO's closing kept describing CON's alternative as "fragmented," "voluntary," or "loose." That misdescribes CON's explicitly binding standard and leaves CON's main comparison unanswered. PRO's climate-harm weighing had force, but it never showed why a fuel mandate specifically, rather than a binding emissions limit, is needed to avert those harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.4 turned PRO's enforcement answer from B4.2. If uniform port state control can test fuel, fine, and detain ships, it can enforce a binding intensity limit just as well, so the fuel mandate adds compulsion without adding enforceability. This was paired with A4.5, which argued that the low-sulfur precedent involved a drop-in fuel from an existing refining system rather than new fuels, engines, and infrastructure.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by holding the motion to a fuel-specific timetable and showing Side B never escaped the supply-deadline dilemma without sliding into performance bars, core-lane exemptions, or port-state enforcement that would equally support a binding intensity limit. Side B’s climate-cost weighing stayed live, but the closing recycled the failed-voluntary-targets claim after A had distinguished flexibility of method from whether cuts are binding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal converted B’s port-state and core-lane answers into concessions: the same detention mechanism can enforce emissions cuts without dictating scarce fuels, and a core-lane phase-in creates geographic regressivity rather than resolving the dilemma.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B built the stronger comparative case: a binding fuel mandate risks scarcity and regressive costs before supply exists, while an enforceable declining emissions standard preserves the same climate obligation without prescribing the costliest method. B consistently exposed A’s unresolved deadline-versus-certainty dilemma, distinguished the sulfur and solar precedents, and turned A’s port-state enforcement answer against the fuel mandate. A answered the pressure questions and offered a plausible investment-certainty case, but its core-lane phase-in created further equity problems and its closing mischaracterized B’s explicit binding alternative as loose delay.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both pressure questions directly, showed that core-lane phasing shifts burdens onto peripheral states, and demonstrated that the port-state testing, fines, and detention invoked by A could enforce a binding intensity limit just as effectively, making the fuel-specific mandate unnecessary.
