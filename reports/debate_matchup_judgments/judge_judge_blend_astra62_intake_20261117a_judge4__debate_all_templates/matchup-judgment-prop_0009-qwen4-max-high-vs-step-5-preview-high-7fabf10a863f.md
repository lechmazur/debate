### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): PRO wins on the decisive practical clash. CON's purpose-based definition of torture was a serious line, and its pressure questions about force-feeding and day-one knowledge forced PRO to sharpen its test. PRO handled that pressure credibly: it located torture in deprivation that is itself the mechanism of harm, imposed when a gentler alternative exists. It also explained why duration is the hinge, since justification decays once the crisis has passed. PRO's "wall versus void" distinction was never neutralized. CON argued that staffed separation would collapse into isolation when staffing fails, but PRO's Q2 turned that claim against CON's own reliance on staff-run review. PRO's Q1 then exposed that CON's "enforceable cap" had no ceiling. CON's answer amounted to a fifteen-day trigger followed by indefinite institutional certification, and PRO framed that as the very discretion the motion indicts. CON's argument that abolition would drive isolation into informal channels was plausible but stayed speculative. CON's closing largely restated its purpose test without recovering the cap problem. Grounding was comparable on both sides, with neither relying on fake precision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 asked CON to name the number for its "enforceable caps." CON's A1 conceded that no day is safe in the abstract and that its cap was continued isolation upon independent review. PRO's closing turned this into "a permission slip with a signature line," contrasting a calendar anyone can audit with "present, articulable danger" that only the jailers can check. That exchange decisively undercut CON's regulation-over-abolition alternative.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins clearly. CON reframed the debate as conditions versus calendar and then used PRO's own concessions to hold that frame. PRO's first rebuttal said a prisoner with daily contact "is not in solitary." CON turned this into a dilemma: either the cap is redundant once contact is mandated, or it forbids separation regardless of conditions. PRO's answers in Rebuttal 2 worked against its own case. PRO put the dangerous prisoner in a supervised unit with daily contact, which is CON's package. PRO also fixed the "consecutive days" loophole by requiring "meaningful human contact," which is CON's criterion. That let CON show that "duration itself is the harm" had effectively been withdrawn. It also produced the memorable line "you cannot call twenty days torture and twenty years management." CON's "permission slip" point, that the motion allows fifteen days of what PRO itself called torture, was never answered. PRO's strongest late argument was that the clock serves as an enforceable backstop when contact lapses. It came only in the closing, and CON rebutted it efficiently: the clock cannot detect its own trigger without the same monitoring PRO said would fail. PRO's appeal to human rights standards had a plausible basis, but its reliance on "evidence" for the fifteen-day threshold stayed vague.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.5) was decisive. It showed that PRO's answers to both pressure questions relied on CON's own contact-based criterion: the substantive-interruption fix for the consecutive-days loophole, and the supervised unit for the prisoner who stabbed two cellmates. This reduced the motion's distinctive content to a fifteen-day permission plus a delay.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side B won by converting Side A's own definitions and concessions into a live contradiction. Once contact-bearing housing was declared not solitary and acceptable for years, the fifteen-day ban either became redundant with B's conditions package or a fifteen-day permission to inflict the very mechanism A called torture. B also showed that enforcing any real interruption still requires measuring contact, so the clock adds delay rather than an independent backstop, while failing the early-vulnerable case that B's exclusion addresses immediately.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2–B4 and the closing: A was forced to house the dangerous man in a supervised contact unit and to treat token resets as non-interruptions, which imported B's conditions test and left the calendar as a licence and a delay rather than a distinct protection.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B won the definitional clash: once A agreed meaningful contact means a placement is not solitary, B showed the 15-day cap is either redundant or a permission for 15 days of the alleged torture mechanism. A's answers adopted B's supervised-unit and contact test, while B answered the backstop argument by showing a clock still needs the same monitoring as conditions regulation, only later.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 and closing: A repairs the consecutive-days loophole and houses the dangerous prisoner only by importing B's contact-based test, leaving the cap as a fifteen-day licence stapled to B's regulation.
