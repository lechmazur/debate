### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): A wins by separating necessary subgroup enrollment from the motion’s proportionality requirement. B persuasively establishes that missing participants mean missing knowledge, but does not establish why population-matched ratios outperform mandatory, question-driven subgroup studies enough to justify material delay. A answers the frailty challenge directly and correctly rejects B’s closing portrayal of its mandatory studies as discretionary. The margin remains competitive: A unjustifiably treats proportionality as a ceiling on oversampling, while neither side adequately grounds its confident delay estimates.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s answer to the frail, polymedicated patient was decisive: demographic representation can coexist with exclusion criteria that leave precisely those patients unstudied. This materially weakened B’s claim that proportional enrollment necessarily supplies the clinical knowledge missing from A’s smaller pharmacology studies.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): CON won by separating enrollment from knowledge and tying that gap to the motion as written. PRO made a strong case that missing patients cannot be analyzed and that sex and age are real variables, but CON showed a proportionate quota can be satisfied while the question stays open, as with women already in the zolpidem trials, and that age buckets still miss frail polymedicated patients. PRO's claim that a proportionate rule "obviously" requires analysis was an unwritten amendment, while CON's mandatory targeted cohorts compel an answer without gating approval on a demographic photograph.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's zolpidem and frailty turns: a quota would have been compliant throughout the twenty-year dose error because women were already enrolled, and filling age buckets still excludes the frail patients screened out by comorbidity rules.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): CON showed proportionate enrollment is neither sufficient nor well-targeted: zolpidem had women but no analysis, genetic harms track genotype not ethnicity, and frail elders are excluded by comorbidity criteria not age ratios. Its parallel, question-sized subgroup studies preserve pre-approval learning without gating approval. PRO's necessary-not-sufficient concession and reliance on an regulator who 'obviously' demands analysis left that clash unresolved, while CON's redundant-or-materially-delaying dilemma stood.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2: conceding blood levels cannot capture frailty, then showing PRO's age quota also cannot because frail polymedicated patients are screened out by eligibility criteria, so proportionality certifies a trial while the key gap survives.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): A won the incremental-policy comparison. B offered substantive discovery and generalizability arguments, but did not adequately establish why proportional enrollment itself supplies the knowledge used to justify material delays, rather than requiring targeted studies. Neither side fully justified its absolute claims about knowledge gained or lost. The decisive advantage was A's demonstration that stalled recruitment could withhold an effective treatment from already-studied patients. B honestly accepted that consequence but never sufficiently weighed it against the mandate's uncertain additional benefit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response to the cancer hypothetical was decisive: demonstrated efficacy in enrolled patients is compatible with difficulty recruiting another group. This defeated B's alleged contradiction and preserved a concrete harm to patients whose treatment need not depend on resolving every subgroup's uncertainty. B's closing returned to the unstudied elderly patient without answering that broader withholding cost.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): B won by holding the motion to its actual instrument: a proportionate enrollment quota despite stipulated material delay. B showed that quota does not produce the subgroup knowledge A promised, separated seats from powered analysis or targeted pharmacology, and answered both pressure questions. A's leverage and discovery case stayed partly live and was often more lyrical, but it leaned on bigger trials and studies the motion does not require, and A never showed a proportionate slice itself discovering the difference. B then turned A's cancer-drug concession: recruitment failure makes everyone wait, while a small slice can stamp patients "studied" without ending the guessed dose.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal and close: proportionality does not require the evidence tools A kept borrowing, and owning a hold on an effective cancer drug is a recruitment queue, not proof the frail were protected. The inversion of "delay ends, ignorance doesn't" was decisive.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): CON won the central mechanism clash: proportionate enrollment alone yields underpowered subgroup slices, so it reliably imposes the stipulated material delay without reliably producing the knowledge PRO promised. PRO's strongest leverage and unknown-differences arguments were forceful, but answering them required bigger or oversampled trials beyond the quota, worsening the delay, and PRO never supplied the requested discovery example while owning delay for an effective cancer drug.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: proportionality neither forbids nor requires oversampling or targeted studies, so after stripping away CON's borrowed tools, the quota's distinctive contribution is delay rather than powered evidence.
