- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): This is a close call, and CON edges it. PRO's Q1 also exposed a real weakness in CON's integration alternative. CON's answer to it, that integration needs "deliberate community-specific updates," never supplied a mechanism to ensure universal coverage. However, PRO's case leaned heavily on repeated statistics from the opening. CON's overconfidence and irrelevant-curriculum points were speculative, but they stayed live because PRO's only reply was a one-line assertion. CON also made unsupported precise claims, notably that gains were "driven entirely" by wealthy districts, which earns no credit. On balance, CON adapted more to the clash. Its A2, distinguishing multi-year credentialed core subjects from a one-semester tack-on, blunted PRO's "abandon math too" parity argument. CON also kept a live route through crowd-out and box-checking harms, while PRO mostly restated the universal-floor point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's B2.1 turn on PRO's own computer-science and media-literacy examples. CON reframed them as proof that new mandates crowd out remedial and career support in poor districts while rich ones add on top. PRO never directly answered this. That left CON's crowd-out harm standing against PRO's universal-floor benefit.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): PRO wins because it built a clear comparative frame and held it through the debate. The frame was a guaranteed baseline versus a "birth lottery" in which family background decides who learns about debt, taxes, and budgeting. PRO then answered CON's main attacks directly: - Knowledge decay: PRO argued for timing the course to senior-year decisions such as FAFSA, loan offers, and leases, so students apply what they learn immediately. - Displacement: PRO said the time could come from low-value filler rather than core math, science, or arts. - Weak implementation: PRO treated this as a solvable funding and training problem, not a reason to abandon the requirement. CON raised real concerns about implementation in underresourced schools and about crowding out other subjects. Its "two-tier" entrenchment claim, however, was underdeveloped, since wealthier districts already have the advantage today. CON also never showed the mandate would leave disadvantaged students worse off than receiving nothing. Its integration alternative suffers from the same teacher and materials shortages it used against PRO, and it was asserted rather than defended. CON's A2 also mischaracterized PRO's timing point by calling the course "years before" decisions arise. PRO had one weakness: it somewhat conflated CON's integration into required courses with the optional status quo. CON also repeated its implementation and two-tier points across rebuttals and the closing without much development.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A2 in PRO's Rebuttal 2. CON had pressed on knowledge decay and asked for evidence that a semester course could protect students under real pressure. PRO answered that a senior course synchronized with FAFSA, loan comparisons, credit cards, and leases turns the class into applied, immediate practice. CON never squarely rebutted this and instead misdescribed the course as years removed from those decisions.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): A narrowly wins the policy comparison. Its clarification that financial instruction would occur in required subjects undercuts B’s central contrast between a standalone course and optional exposure. B’s closing still calls that alternative optional rather than addressing it. A also directly challenges B’s assumption that advisory and study-hall time is disposable. B effectively defends durable financial skills and senior-year application, but does not sufficiently establish why those benefits require a separate semester. Both sides overstate learning effects and implementation outcomes, limiting the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second pressure answer identifies required math, social studies, and career-tech classes as vehicles for universal exposure. This directly challenges B’s birth-lottery framing. B subsequently repeats the optional-exposure characterization instead of explaining why compulsory integration cannot deliver an adequate baseline.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by making the central clash clearer: imperfect implementation is fixable, while the status quo leaves vulnerable students without a guaranteed baseline. A’s senior-year timing answer directly blunted B’s knowledge-decay objection, and B never fully proved that a mandated course would be more regressive than existing unequal access or that its integration alternative would be equally serious, coordinated, and reliable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 treated B’s hollow-checkbox and decay objections as implementation and timing issues rather than fatal flaws, arguing that a senior-year course aligned with FAFSA, loans, leases, and credit decisions gives students immediate applied use. That reframing preserved A’s equity case while forcing B to defend the status quo’s harms.
