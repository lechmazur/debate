### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A won on framing and execution. A's core move—"the motion compares policies, not graduates"—kept the debate anchored on what the marginal dollar buys, and A's pressure questions forced B to supply a 60% completion rate that A then weaponized: by B's own admission, the largest single outcome group the subsidy creates earns roughly high-school wages with debt. A answered B's pressure questions directly with A1/A2, offered the three-assets answer to skill obsolescence, and closed by crystallizing the floor-versus-bill asymmetry rather than recapping. B's strongest live route—the compounding bachelor's premium through workers' 40s-60s—was repeatedly asserted ("pulls ahead by early 40s") but never grounded, and B's closing mislabeled it an "unchallenged fact" after A had pressed it twice. B's defense of full-cost-of-attendance stipends as within "enrollment subsidies" was a stretch that A exposed. Both sides were competent and disciplined; A made the decisive clash clearer and landed the sharper exchanges.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure question demanding the completion-rate assumption behind B's "pulls ahead by early 40s" claim, followed by B's concession of a 60% rate in A1 of their second rebuttal, which A converted into the closing's decisive weighing: the policy's largest outcome group is a non-completer with debt, versus an apprentice whose worst case is mid-career retraining funded by debt-free savings.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): B better connected completion risk, paid experience, credentials, and advancement opportunities to the comparison between marginal policy beneficiaries. Its promotion-pathway examples also undermined A's blanket earnings-ceiling claim. A plausibly explained how living-cost assistance could improve completion, but repeatedly asserted rather than established that the resulting earnings gains would outweigh apprenticeships. B's advantage is limited: it prematurely accused A of leaving questions unanswered, incorrectly called 40% the largest outcome group, and overstated apprenticeships' guaranteed downside protection. Neither side firmly established the expected lifetime-earnings comparison, but B supplied the stronger comparative mechanism and more effective rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure question about A's early-40s crossover exposed a central evidentiary gap: supplying a 60% completion assumption did not substantiate the claimed lifetime-earnings advantage. A's distinction between within-trade credential portability and cross-sector flexibility was its strongest surviving response.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a competitive debate by grounding the comparison in what the marginal policy dollar actually buys. Side B showed that expanding college subsidies pulls in fence-sitting students at open-admission campuses with high dropout risk, used near-free community colleges to show tuition aid alone does not fix non-completion, and trapped Side A into inflating traditional enrollment subsidies into full living stipends while still conceding a 40% dropout rate. Side B also neutralized Side A's mid-career ceiling and portability critiques with unanswered arguments on contractor/supervisory ladders, employer-funded degrees, and white-collar office automation. Side A stayed competitive through disciplined focus on the 40-year earnings horizon and employer scaling bottlenecks, while Side B's margin was narrowed by two unforced slips: prematurely accusing Side A in [B4.7] of ignoring questions before Side A's turn to answer, and mislabeling 40% as the "single largest outcome group" alongside a 60% completion rate in [B5.1].
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B2.2], Side B answered Side A's claim that financial aid fixes college dropout by pointing to dismal completion rates at already near-free community colleges; this forced Side A ([A2.1], [A4.1]-[A4.2]) to stretch "traditional enrollment subsidies" into costly full-cost-of-attendance living stipends while still conceding 40% marginal non-completion, which Side B leveraged effectively in [B3.2] and [B5.5].

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): CON (Side B) controlled the decisive clash throughout. PRO's case rested on the marginal-student dropout/skewed-premium argument, and CON answered it directly with the cutoff and tuition-subsidy research point, which PRO never genuinely engaged—A4.4 and the closing merely restated the elite-skew objection. CON's structural traps were sharp: (1) PRO's own escape hatch from the German plateau was "transferable college credit," meaning the pathway's long-run ceiling borrows from the institution PRO wants to stop subsidizing; (2) PRO's strongest claim was "on par" mid-career earnings from small selective US programs, while the motion demands "more than"; (3) the only scaled apprenticeship system (Germany) shows a deficit at forty-five, and PRO offered no scaled counterexample when pressed twice. CON also flipped PRO's recession point and turned the dropout statistic into an argument for subsidies via affordability. PRO executed the format correctly (A1:/A2: present) and had a coherent compounding/low-risk story, but repeated the same points across speeches without adapting to the pressure. CON's line about scoring college against the status quo and apprenticeships against their brochure crystallized the asymmetry memorably. Slight discount for CON's confident appeals to research it cannot verify, but the claims were framed as ordinary background knowledge rather than fake citations.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4: CON's second rebuttal opened with clean A1/A2 answers, caught that PRO's "on par" claim came from a boutique tail of programs, and delivered the killer weighing: "Parity for a boutique program is the ceiling of PRO's case; a verified deficit at scale is its floor"—directly answering the "more than" burden of the motion.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): A makes the stronger expansion-to-expansion comparison: subsidies can reduce the dropout risk B treats as fixed, while favorable apprenticeship outcomes also face marginal-participant and scaling problems. B repeatedly attacks average college returns without adequately answering A's separate marginal-enrollee argument or employer-slot vulnerability. The margin is limited by A's unsupported empirical certainty and misleading suggestion that expanding apprenticeships necessarily means defunding colleges.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest reversal applies B's selection critique to apprenticeships: promising programs cannot automatically represent outcomes after broad expansion. Combined with the subsidy-completion mechanism, this exposes B's comparison of ideal apprenticeships against unchanged college failures.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) won solidly by dominating the lifecycle, scaling, and marginal-return clashes. Side B opened with a plausible expected-value case around college dropouts and early compounding, but repeatedly attacked the million-dollar average premium late in the debate rather than engaging Side A's evidence that marginal, low-income admits gain substantially and that tuition subsidies directly improve completion. Meanwhile, Side A demonstrated that scaled apprenticeship systems like Germany's front-load wages in the twenties before trailing university graduates in middle age, exposed Side B's reliance on transferable college credit to escape that plateau, and showed that Side B's best U.S. examples only achieved parity in small boutique programs rather than higher long-run earnings at scale.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A4.1]-[A4.4], following Pressure Question [A3.2] and Side B's concession in [B4.3], Side A flipped Side B's elite-selection critique back onto PRO: Side B dismissed college averages while ignoring marginal-enrollee evidence, yet relied on small, selective U.S. apprenticeship programs whose mid-career earnings were merely "on par" with college graduates while offering no scaled system that outperforms university degrees by age forty-five.
