### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): This was close. CON built the sharper decision frame with its dilemma: either the motion reaches mere stylistic influence and overreaches, or it reaches only counterfeit personas and duplicates existing law. That framing is CON's best path to victory, and PRO's narrow reading of "style" does strain the text somewhat. PRO, however, identified a real middle case and kept it alive: a studio sells a disclaimed, non-deceptive AI vocal that fans recognize as Whitney. PRO argued this is commercial use of a likeness without consent, even when no confusion exists. CON's answers to that case did not hold up: - CON's final reply, that this is "fraud in disguise," begs the question by assuming the only wrong is deception. - CON's own concessions undercut it. It said courts can act when "a specific persona is counterfeited," and that uneven publicity law should be "repaired." Both effectively concede that permission is the right rule for a specific likeness. - That shrinks the live dispute to the meaning of "style," where PRO's specificity answer was imperfect but defensible. PRO's Q1 also exposed that CON's student-film example falls outside the motion's "studios and labels." CON's answer recast it as a "logic test," which was only partly successful. CON also bore execution costs. Its second rebuttal was clipped, and it cited nonexistent reference IDs ([P2.1], [C1.2], [C2.3], and others). Net, PRO holds a slight but real edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over disclaimers. PRO argued in A2.3 and A4.5 that a disclaimed AI Whitney track escapes deception-based law yet still exploits her likeness without consent. CON responded in B5.3 that this is merely fraud in disguise, which assumes deception is the only wrong rather than answering PRO. That left PRO's middle case standing against CON's "redundant or overbroad" dilemma.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): CON wins a competitive debate. PRO's appeal to dignity is intuitive and memorable, and its "consent versus convenience" framing lands. Its harm story, however, rests mostly on asserting that unconsented appropriation is wrong, and it never squarely shows who is harmed once deception is removed. CON built a clean dilemma that stayed live through the closings. Either the rule reaches bare style, which turns it into a veto over influence and tribute, or it reaches only passing off and cloned recordings, which existing law and disclosure already handle. CON paired this with a sharp "transferable veto" point: if an heir's yes permits the same track that PRO calls exploitation, the rule protects heirs' bargaining power rather than the artist's dignity. PRO answered the pressure questions directly. Its A2 bit the bullet on style, but this partly conceded CON's overbreadth concern. Its redefinition of the harm as "accumulated sound and persona" was credibly attacked as style rebranded. PRO's best counter, "nobody means the studio decides," came only in the closing and was answered by CON: style is ownable by neither studio nor heir. PRO had a potentially strong point in the existing legal recognition of post-mortem publicity rights. PRO underdeveloped it and never pressed CON's contestable claim that "the dead have no interests." Both of these left CON's framework standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange runs from CON's B2.1–B2.2 through the A2 answer and on to B4.5. CON forced PRO to choose whether bare style is covered. PRO said yes, and CON then showed that this lets heirs veto tribute while also licensing the very synthetic tracks PRO condemns. PRO never fully escaped that dilemma.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins a clear but competitive debate by holding Side B strictly to the motion's full text ("style or likeness") while exposing the gap between artist dignity and heir licensing. Side B built a resonant case separating consumer deception from identity exploitation and landed real pressure by forcing Side A to concede [A4.3] that a label could release a clearly labeled "new Amy Winehouse" AI track over her estate's objection. However, Side A proved that extending permission mandates beyond copyrighted recordings and honest labeling into "style" sweeps in AI-assisted homage and musical phrasing, forcing Side B into an evasive definition that bare style is covered only "as a product in their likeness" [B4.3]. Combined with Side A's sharp dismantling of [B2.5]'s defamation analogy and its sustained point that dead artists cannot consent—so estate permission merely creates a proxy tollbooth to either monetize the same synthetic singles or censor tributes—Side A established the more coherent route to the ballot.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's scope trap across [A2.1], [A3.2], and [A4.5]: after Side B narrowed its rule in [B2.6] to "commercial generation in likeness, not tribute... not homage," Side A pressed the motion's inclusion of bare "style," forcing Side B into the circular answer in [B4.3] that style is covered when generated "as a product in their likeness," which Side A cleanly exposed as rebranding style as persona while leaving AI-assisted stylistic homage vulnerable to heir vetoes.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by making the decisive clash easier to resolve. PRO vividly separated deception from commercial appropriation and rightly said disclosure does not by itself answer consent. CON still left the stronger reason to reject the motion as written: estate permission is an alienable veto, not the dead artist’s dignity, because heirs can license the very synthetic performance PRO calls theft, while the rule either overreaches into style and homage or collapses into passing-off already handled by labeling and copyright. PRO’s later narrowing stayed fuzzy and never fully explained why a profit-capable estate, rather than the artist, should hold the refusal right.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s rebuttals pinning PRO between overbroad style control and already-illegal passing-off, while showing an estate “yes” turns the condemned track into a licensed product, were the decisive exchange.
