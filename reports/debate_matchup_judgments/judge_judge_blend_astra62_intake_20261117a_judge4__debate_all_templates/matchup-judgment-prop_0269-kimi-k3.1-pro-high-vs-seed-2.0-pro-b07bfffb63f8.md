### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side A won by masterfully controlling the debate's central clash over incentives. Side B offered a strong alternative of "fault-based liability" for items platforms explicitly warrant, but Side A decisively neutralized this in Rebuttal 2 by pointing out that such a standard simply rewards platforms for choosing to check nothing. This left Side A's core argument—that platforms must absorb general risk to be forced to fix systemic problems—as the most robust path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (A4.4) delivered the decisive blow by pointing out that Side B's fault-based liability standard actively incentivizes platforms to leave items unverified, directly undermining the goal of a functioning market.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON won the core clash: PRO's incentive argument justified fault-based liability for items stamped "verified," but PRO admitted in Rebuttal 2 it wants guarantees for uninspected listings. CON showed that is insuring uncertainty, not standing behind a judgment, and that spread costs reappear as higher fees, rejected legitimate goods, and collusion risk. PRO dismissed return fraud as an edge case and answered A3 Q2 with "platform decides" without solving evidence. Targeted accountability vs compulsory insurance became the clearer comparison, and CON left it standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A1's Rebuttal 1 / Pressure Q2 on switched-item returns: CON distinguished warranty for promised authentication vs blanket insurer, forced PRO to admit blanket coverage, and exposed that "platform decides" doesn't resolve vintage/tampering evidence, shifting costs to honest users.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully distinguishing between targeted, fault-based liability and the blanket insurance mandated by the motion. While Side A delivered highly persuasive rhetoric about aligning risk with reward and the unfairness of 25% commissions, Side B decisively proved that A's own examples only justified holding platforms accountable for explicit warranties. When pressed, A admitted the motion requires guaranteeing uninspected items, allowing B to effectively argue that this blanket absorption would distort the market by forcing platforms to reject legitimate but uncertain goods and raise fees for honest users. B's alternative of targeted liability and identity controls resolved A's incentive concerns without the negative externalities of A's policy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where it dismantled Side A's "verified authentic" argument by showing it only supports narrow fault-based liability, and then capitalized on Side A's admission that the motion covers uninspected items to highlight the market-distorting effects of "insuring uncertainty."
