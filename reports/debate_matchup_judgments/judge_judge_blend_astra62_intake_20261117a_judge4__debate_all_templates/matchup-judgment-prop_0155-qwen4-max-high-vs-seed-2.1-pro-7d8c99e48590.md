### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won. It built a clean decision rule, "suspicion before the search," and kept it sharp across every round. It showed that a warrant supplies a predicate, a scope, and a record, while a live scan has none of these. It also adapted well under pressure. On CON's retention argument, PRO answered that the archive is a policy choice rather than a clause of the motion, and that thirty-day deletion fits warrant-based retrospective search. It added that CON's live-scanning alternative needs the same cameras anyway. On bystander invisibility, PRO conceded honestly that bystanders usually can't learn of searches. It then pointed to systemic remedies: suppression, audit logs, aggregate reporting, and councils that can shut programs off. CON (Side A) raised real concerns: broad warrants, an incentive to hoard footage, and bystanders who never learn they were searched. But it relied on asserted rubber-stamping and repeated the "30 seconds later" line without engaging PRO's point that a live dragnet has no endpoint. In its A1 answer, CON drifted toward a different proposal: requiring narrow, probable-cause warrants with descriptive filters. PRO's closing correctly framed that as an argument for implementing the motion well, not for rejecting it. CON's closing largely restated its opening themes and did not sharpen the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 and closing settled the exchange. CON's A1 asked for "prior, narrow judicial authorization tied to probable cause for a specific crime." PRO recast that as the motion's own rule plus optional add-ons. It then noted that CON's descriptive filter still requires examining every face. Finally, it pressed the point CON never answered: a robbery search ends when the footage runs out, but a live scanner has "no last frame."
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) consistently made the debate easier to decide. The core clash was whether the real-time/post-incident line is meaningful. B established a clear comparative frame early (dragnet searches people to find crimes; investigation searches a crime to find a person), and it survived every exchange. B's rebuttals did real damage: the thirty-second-footage example was flipped into a concession that a predicate existed; the permanent-archive harm was reframed as a policy choice compatible with the motion rather than a clause of it; and wrongful-arrest errors were answered with corroboration requirements while noting CON's regime adds an unrecorded second channel of the same error. B answered both pressure questions directly and sharply — the "finite footage has a last frame, a live dragnet does not" point and the honest concession on bystander invisibility paired with suppression/audit/council remedies were the strongest moments of the debate. Side A's (CON) best live points were the retention incentive and bystanders' inability to learn of searches, but A's answer to B's Q1 essentially described a regime of prior, narrow judicial authorization plus safeguards — which B's closing devastatingly identified as the motion's own rule restated. A also leaned on repeated assertions about rubber-stamped, broad warrants without much support, and the "to delete non-matches you must first examine everyone" retort exposed a tension in A's descriptive-filter remedy. A never squarely answered what terminates a live scan under CON's rule, and B's "no last frame" argument stood through the closing. B combined stronger grounding discipline, sharper weighing, and memorable framing ("keep suspicion before the search").
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing: B answered A's pressure questions head-on (the bounded-footage vs. endless-live-scan distinction; bystander invisibility as common to all records searches with systemic remedies), then in closing showed that CON's own proposed regime — prior, narrow judicial authorization with filtering, deletion, and audits — is the motion plus additions, collapsing CON's position into convergence with PRO while exposing that the descriptive filter requires examining the very bystanders CON condemns.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): B built the clearer comparative case: retrospective searches require a crime and have a judicially fixed endpoint, while live dragnets can continue without a predicate or natural boundary. B also showed that retention limits, filtering, deletion, and audits are compatible with the motion rather than reasons to reject it. A raised genuine concerns about broad warrants, wrongful arrests, and invisible bystander searches, but relied too heavily on unsupported claims about routine rubber-stamping and indefinite footage hoarding, then repeated them without resolving the motion’s central distinction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: its “no last frame” answer sharply distinguished bounded footage searches from live scanning, and its answer on bystander notice candidly conceded the transparency problem while comparing the accountability mechanisms available under each regime.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins because it built one consistent thesis and kept pressing it: the motion draws its line at timing, while the real harms come from scope and from unverified matches. A pinned B to the motion as written. It showed that B's defenses relied on safeguards the text does not contain, namely an exigency exception and mandatory corroboration. It also noted that the documented wrongful arrests came from post-incident searches, which is the side the motion permits. B had some fair points. Its rebuttal of the "scan last month's protest" overstatement landed, and its closing argument that live scanning lacks a natural anchor to narrow scope was its best line. However, B mostly repeated its opening framing and never neutralized two of A's points: that scope caps and corroboration are available in both modes, and that the gallery problem persists with a warrant. B's closing line that the harm "comes from running that matching on everyone" was turned by A into a concession that the issue is scope, not the clock. A's exigency dilemma also went effectively unanswered: either the ban leaves emergencies dark, or it collapses into a warrant rule for both modes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 answered B's A1/A2 defense directly. First, exigency excuses a warrant but does not unlock a banned technique, which leaves B choosing between a categorical ban and an unwritten exception. Second, A broke B's wiretap analogy: wiretaps carry minimization, but face search must process every face in the gallery. Together these neutralized B's main attempts to rescue the post-incident carve-out.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) consistently exposed that Side B kept defending a motion augmented with guardrails the text does not contain — an exigency exception, mandatory human corroboration, judge-enforced scope narrowing — and then converted that into a sharp dilemma: either the prohibition is categorical (and the child-abduction case fails) or exigency applies (and the "ban" collapses into a warrant rule for both modes). B never resolved this dilemma, retreating instead to the claim that settled doctrine applies automatically, which A effectively countered with the chokehold/no-knock ban analogy. A's structural points were also more original and damaging: the wiretap minimization argument (finding one face requires processing every face), the observation that the documented wrongful arrests occurred on the post-incident side B permits, and the closing catch that B's own diagnosis ("the harm comes from running matching on everyone, all the time") is an argument about scope, not timing. B's best material — the pre-incident lack of any crime anchor to bound scope, and the racialized error-rate harm — was real and well-delivered, and B answered pressure questions with proper A1/A2 structure. But B's case depended on unwritten safeguards at exactly the moments A pressed, and B's closing did not answer the exigency dilemma or the minimization point. A made the debate easier to decide by narrowing the clash to "timestamp vs. scope" and winning that comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's exigency dilemma in Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.2): either "prohibited" means prohibited and the bus-terminal feed stays dark, or exigent circumstances unlock live searching and the motion is just a warrant rule for both modes with the clock erased. B had asserted in B4.1 that exigency applies automatically to every search rule, but A's counterexample — cities that ban chokeholds or no-knock raids do not discover hidden emergency exceptions inside those bans — left the contradiction standing through the closing.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A built the stronger critique of the motion as written: it distinguishes timing from scope, shows that warrants neither verify matches nor eliminate mass processing of innocent faces, and exposes Side B’s reliance on safeguards absent from the text. Side B’s best answer was that live systems lack a reported-crime anchor and create greater scale and immediacy pressures, but its automatic-exigency and corroboration claims were legally and textually overconfident. Side A answered those points directly and repeatedly turned B’s preferred safeguards into arguments against the timestamp-based rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it gave the exigency dilemma, answered the false-match-flood point with scope and watchlist controls, and broke B’s wiretap analogy by noting that face searches must process the whole gallery rather than minimize irrelevant faces.
