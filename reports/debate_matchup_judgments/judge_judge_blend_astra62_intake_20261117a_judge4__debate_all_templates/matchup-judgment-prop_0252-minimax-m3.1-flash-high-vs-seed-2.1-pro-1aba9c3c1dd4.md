### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): CON wins on sharper comparative weighing. PRO makes a strong equity case against pay-to-play internships, but CON successfully shows the proposed remedy's dependence on accreditation/safe harbors favors resourced organizations and does not itself cure the wealth barrier if genuinely educational placements remain unpaid. CON's objective conduct conditions, duration caps, pay-for-productive-work rule, and ban on unpaid hiring screens answer exploitation more directly. PRO's city-level evidence was plausibly correlation, and its concession on short flexible volunteer work let CON reduce the live dispute to duration and supervision rather than formal educational status. A's clipped rebuttal is an execution cost, but not enough to erase the cleaner final case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4's rebuttal turned PRO's safe harbor and empirical claims: accreditation can be bought by larger firms, unpaid educational placements still require family support, and rising paid postings are correlation rather than proof. A also weaponized PRO's concession on short volunteer work to show the operative line is duration/supervision, not formal education.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B (CON) won a competitive debate by attacking the proposition as written. B's core line—both sides agree on banning displacement, indefinite duration, and fake training, so the real dispute is whether "educational placement" is the right dividing line versus a conduct-based rule—was coherent from opening to closing and never broken. B's closing equity-collapse point (restricting noneducational internships does nothing to make unpaid *educational* placements affordable, so the wealth barrier survives PRO's own carveout) landed on A's central case and went unanswered. B also exposed A's concession that short casual volunteer work would be untouched, showing the operative line was duration and supervision, exactly B's objective conditions. A's strongest material was the default-rule framing and effective pressure questions, but A's rebuttal 2 relied on an ungrounded multi-city causal claim that B correctly flagged as correlation, and A never explained why institutional accreditation is less gameable by large employers than B's checklist. B's clipped second rebuttal is an execution cost, but the visible content was the sharpest weighing in the round. Both sides were disciplined on evidence; B edged on grounding by avoiding brittle specificity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4: B conceded A's pressure premise (no poor candidate takes a months-long unpaid post) but flipped it—the remedy is to shorten, pay, or ban unpaid experience as a hiring screen, not to bless the label "educational"—then dismantled A's city-evidence as correlation and showed A's own safe harbor relocates paperwork discretion to wealthy firms.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A better exposed that the proposed heavy restriction plus educational carve-out does not cure the wealth barrier it targets, since unpaid educational placements can still exclude those who cannot forgo pay, while institutional safe harbors favor large firms. A’s conduct-based alternative—payment triggered by displacement, routine work, duration, or hiring screens, checked by observable conditions—more cleanly reached the agreed harms without swapping one discretion for another. Side B’s equity and status-quo critique was strong but never fully neutralized that architectural comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s closing comparison: equity requires payment to follow productive or screening activity, not an educational label or accredited sponsor, so PRO’s safe harbor preserves the exclusion it claims to end while taxing smaller opportunities.
