- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins clearly. Its opening was clipped, which is a real execution cost, but its case built a clean comparative frame. The status quo is drift and a ratchet, and sunsets flip the default so that continuation has to be justified. In rebuttal, PRO turned CON's core objections back on CON. Fear spikes, bundling, and manufactured urgency all apply with greater force to the original grant, which CON accepts must happen. CON's alternative also never solved the scheduling problem. CON offered some creative points: hoarding before deadlines, and renewal votes "laundering" legitimacy. Its central fallback was the claim that stale powers erode through judicial skepticism, and PRO rebutted it with the long life of the 2001 force authorization, a point CON never squarely answered. CON's A2 distinction between the first vote and renewal votes was thin. CON also had a grounding problem: it confidently claimed the 2020 Patriot Act lapse was reversed within days. That is shaky, overconfident specificity, and CON then used it as decisive proof. PRO's closing tightly crystallized the unanswered burden. CON's closing largely restated the rubber-stamp narrative.

CON answered that committee subpoenas and gradual judicial narrowing would do it. PRO replied that committees can hold hearings but cannot force a vote past executive agenda control and veto. PRO added that stale powers accrete rather than erode, citing the force authorization that persisted for two decades. That left CON's alternative hollow.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): A won because B never supplied a workable scheduler for its alternative: committee hearings and judicial narrowing may speak, but they cannot force a renewal vote or overcome executive agenda control. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins a competitive debate because it used PRO's own admissions more effectively. PRO conceded that renewal votes "pass reliably" during real crises and that political pressure "vanishes entirely" once a crisis fades. CON combined those concessions into a clear dilemma: the vote is a rubber stamp when a crisis is visible, it happens in a low-attention setting when the crisis fades, and it creates lapse risk in contested emergencies. PRO had a real structural answer available. Under an automatic sunset, inaction means the power expires rather than continuing, and PRO's opening and closing gestured at this "flip the default" point. However, PRO never sharply used it against CON's claim that low-salience renewals would simply "pass by consent." PRO's rebuttals also tended to repeat the voter-backlash asymmetry rather than engage CON's polarized-crisis scenario. That scenario covered contested pandemics and border emergencies, where blocking renewal could rally a base instead of being politically suicidal. CON had weak spots of its own: - Its claim that lingering powers are "usually harmless" was weak. - Its repeated "consent" framing glossed over the sunset mechanics. Even so, CON was sharper in framing the contest and in exploiting pressure, so it carries a clear but modest edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 [A4.5] and Closing [A5.1] combined PRO's A1 and A2 answers into a dilemma. PRO says the mechanism is inert mid-crisis because votes pass reliably, and says it is low-attention afterward because pressure vanishes. This turned PRO's asymmetry defense into a concession. PRO's closing restated the "flip the default" idea but did not explicitly use it to show that inattention now causes expiry, so the exchange largely went CON's way.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): B won by turning A's own salience asymmetry into a nullification of the proposal. A convincingly argued that renewal votes pass during visible crises and that political pressure fades afterward, but B showed those admissions leave the mechanism either a rubber stamp when action is needed or a low-attention consent vote when entrenchment supposedly occurs. B also kept live the contested-emergency risk: in polarized crises, blocking renewal can rally a base and create a dangerous lapse deadline. A's default-flip point was clear and important, but B's closing crystallized the tradeoff more decisively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal effectively used A's own admissions: mid-crisis votes pass reliably while post-crisis pressure vanishes, making the mechanism a rubber stamp when safe and a hostage opportunity when contested.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A won solidly by dismantling Side B's central defense against cliff-edge lapse risk and turning Side B's pressure-question answers into a clean comparative dilemma. While Side B articulated a plausible structural case for flipping the default of legislative inaction to curb low-salience repurposing of old powers, Side B repeatedly asserted zero mid-crisis lapse risk on the premise that obstructing emergency authority is politically suicidal. Side A systematically refuted that premise—showing that must-pass deadlines invite rider hostage-taking, blame diffuses across divided chambers, and polarized crises like contested pandemics reward obstruction as base-rallying—while Side B never answered the polarized-crisis or blame-diffusion arguments in its final two turns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's counters in [A2.3] and [A4.3]-[A4.5] to Side B's political-suicide argument: Side A showed that urgent must-pass deadlines attract leverage precisely because stakes are high, explained how divided government diffuses blame and polarized crises reward obstruction, and synthesized Side B's own concessions in [B4.1] and [B4.3] to show the mechanism is inert when unanimous, routinely consented to when salience fades, and hazardous when crises are contested.
