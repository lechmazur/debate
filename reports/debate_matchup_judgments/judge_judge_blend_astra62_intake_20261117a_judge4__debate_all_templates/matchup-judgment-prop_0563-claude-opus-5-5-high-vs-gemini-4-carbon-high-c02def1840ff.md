### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): B wins because the resource/precommitment clash ultimately cut harder. A made a strong deterrence and false-economy case, but B showed that A's own adjustments—scaling down after ceasefire, shifting bulk support to Europe, yet still pinching interceptors during a Taiwan crisis—amounted to conditional, judgment-based aid under another label. A never supplied a real bound on scarce physical assets, and B's closing decision-forcing scenario made that cost vivid. A's durability and burden-sharing arguments were competitive, but B answered the pressure more cleanly and kept the motion's central trade-off in focus.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's exploitation of A's Taiwan-crisis answer: once A admitted Washington would keep Ukraine's air defense flowing while pinching scarce interceptors elsewhere, B framed that as the motion's true price and showed it was the precommitment CON opposed, not the flexible aid A claimed.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B (CON) built the cleaner path to victory. B's opening set up a disciplined five-point framework centered on leverage, scarce interceptors, and European burden-sharing, and later speeches escalated rather than repeated. B's decisive move was the convergence trap in Rebuttal 2: after A conceded that aid scales down after a ceasefire and shifts to Europe in a Taiwan crisis, B argued A had adopted CON's conditional model under a new label, while the only thing the motion adds is precommitting Ukraine to win every trade-off before the trade-off is known. A did hold a real line—the "survival floor" of essential air defense even in a crisis—but that concession let B land the motion's own text ("even if it reduces resources for other priorities") as the price A had now owned, sharpened by "you cannot budget your way into interceptors that do not yet exist." A's best exchange was exposing an apparent tension between B's claim that Putin waits for repeal and B's complaint that entrenchment makes repeal too costly; B answered honestly in closing (repeal stays possible either way; entrenchment merely makes mistakes harder to correct), blunting it. A's pressure questions were strong and forced B's Patriots admission, but B handled A1/A2 directly and turned the answers into further leverage. A's opening leaned on heavy institutional name-dropping (CSIS, Atlantic Council, AEI, Quincy, etc.) with little substantive content drawn from them—a grounding cost B avoided entirely, relying on reasoning and the motion's own language. Both sides argued the proposition as written; B simply made the trade-off weighing easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.6–B4.7, the fork: if PRO's commitment adjusts to circumstances it is CON's model relabeled, and if it truly holds "even if it reduces other priorities," then Washington still owes Ukraine the interceptors Asia needs—which is the blank cheque PRO never bounds. This forced A to defend pinching scarce interceptors mid-Taiwan-crisis and made B's closing weighing decisive.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side A was polished and landed a memorable Taiwan-interceptor frame, but its “blank cheque” and “unbounded by definition” characterization never fully answered Side B’s distinction between a standing security floor and a fixed wartime burn rate.
