### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON (Side A) built one precise causal mechanism and carried it through every round. Once liability attaches on notice, a truthful objection ("my face, no consent") makes removal cheaper for a cautious host than defending a newspaper's exposé. That suppression happens before any public-interest defense is ever tested. CON also conceded PRO's strongest ground honestly: a private claim does help against a known, solvent producer. It then showed that PRO's way of extending that benefit to anonymous cases is to make intermediaries pay for waiting, which is exactly the leverage that produces the suppression. PRO (Side B) offered compensation and deterrence as real benefits and fairly attacked CON's public system as unfunded. However, PRO mostly repeated that "a public-interest defense protects journalism," which CON had already shown does not operate at the moment the host decides. PRO's A2 answer even confirmed CON's point that platforms face liability for delay. PRO's closing restated rather than answered the central clash. CON made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's Q2 on whether hosts face damages risk while review is pending. PRO answered that delay triggers contributory liability, which creates urgency. In A4.3–A4.5, CON turned this into the core of its case: the same truthful notice cannot tell the smear apart from the exposé, so liability for waiting hands the candidate a practical removal power. PRO never answered that turn.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B wins because A never resolved the decisive speech-cost problem: the private right works by making cautious intermediaries fear damages during the review window. A's public-interest defense may protect a newspaper at trial, but B showed it does not stop a host from removing a legitimate exposé before adjudication. B also narrowed A's compensation advantage by showing that anonymous or insolvent uploaders still produce no payment unless liability shifts to intermediaries, which is precisely the suppression mechanism. A made the harm vivid and established that remedies could coexist, but B made the sharper comparative case on ex ante chilling.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal that a public-interest defense cannot protect a story the host decides is too expensive to defend, and that PRO's own secondary-liability mechanism gives a candidate the practical power to stop publication on truthful notice alone, was the most decisive exchange.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A won the central clash over how a private action would actually operate. B plausibly established compensation and deterrence against identifiable, solvent wrongdoers, but needed platform or advertiser liability to reach anonymous uploaders. A showed that this very mechanism lets an image's subject suppress a legitimate exposé merely by giving truthful notice, before any reviewer assesses context. B confirmed that delay creates liability risk and relied on a later public-interest defence, which A convincingly argued cannot protect speech a cautious host removes immediately. A's prompt public removal, enforcement, penalties, and restitution alternative therefore preserved much of the victim remedy without the same speech cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 answer on timing was decisive: B's damages risk begins when the candidate objects, whereas A's system sanctions removal only after independent review, turning B's claimed platform urgency into pre-review suppression.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins because it built a precise gap argument that CON never fully closed. Defamation fails when everyone knows the clip is fake. Disclosure-based privacy fails when no real fact is revealed. Harassment usually needs repetition, and appropriation usually needs commercial use. Prosecution is not in the victim's hands. PRO also drew a narrow line: a realistic, explicit, identifiable depiction, knowing or reckless nonconsent, and injunctions limited to the specific clip. It openly conceded the speech cost, using the satirist-and-minister example, and then weighed that cost against the victim's lack of any way to opt out. CON leaned on generic chilling-effect claims and kept misdescribing the proposal. It said "any aggrieved party" and "competitors" could sue, even after PRO clarified that only the person depicted could bring a claim. CON's best attempt at a direct answer was that appropriation and distress-based restraining orders cover a single act. PRO turned this in its closing: those routes require commercial use or severe distress, which a resilient victim should not have to prove. CON's argument that open-ended torts chill speech less than a specific statute was asserted but never explained. Its closing largely repeated earlier points.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Q2 against CON's chilling argument. PRO asked why an injunction threat is less chilling under open-ended torts than under a statute that requires plaintiffs to identify the clip and plead knowledge of nonconsent. CON answered that flexible case-by-case doctrine is safer, but never showed why vaguer standards chill less. PRO's A1/A2 also cleanly exposed that the existing remedies require severe distress or commercial use, as captured in its line "which colleague should have to receive the second copy."
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): A built the cleaner path: an explicit claim fills a concrete gap where existing torts require commercial use, repeated harassment, or severe distress, and where prosecutors hold the charging decision. B's chilling argument never overcame A's comparison that a defined scienter requirement and early screening are less chilling than the open-ended torts B prefers. The nurse/satirist asymmetry stayed live and persuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was A's answer to Q1: B asserted existing appropriation or harassment claims reach a first distribution, but A exposed the missing element—no commercial use, no severe breakdown, no repetition—and forced the question of which colleague must receive a second copy before the victim can act.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO identified a concrete elements gap in defamation, privacy, harassment, and appropriation claims for a known fake distributed once, then sustained the asymmetry weighing: a satirist can still make the underlying criticism without circulating realistic pornography, while the depicted person has no equivalent way to undo the exposure. CON asserted existing remedies and chilling effects but never squarely answered the first-distribution, noncommercial, no-severe-distress case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing turn on Side A's proposed torts was decisive: severe-distress and commercial-use requirements would leave a resilient nurse in a workplace group chat without the targeted stop order, so better enforcement cannot supply missing legal elements.
