### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins because it set and held a clear decision rule. On that rule, CON had to show that the structural losses from a shift would wipe out the science-per-dollar advantage, not merely that some losses would occur. PRO's line that "even if some funding disappears, the remainder can still buy more science" put that burden on CON. CON never met it with more than assertion; its closing claim that the gains "vanish entirely" was declared rather than shown. CON's best material was real and deserves credit: - the industrial-base and zero-failure engineering argument; - the reply that rovers and Webb grew alongside the Shuttle and ISS, not in place of them. Those points kept CON competitive. But they also cut against CON's earlier claim that only deep-space human missions sustain political will, since the Shuttle and ISS stayed in low Earth orbit. CON also overreached in places. Its "small probe contract every five years" was a strawman that PRO's Q1 punctured cleanly. Its "majority-share crewed program" claim in the closing was unsupported. PRO stayed nuanced throughout. It conceded a role for crews (the Hubble repairs, the Surveyor-Apollo synergy) and answered the fixed-overhead question honestly. It also turned the rescue argument into a point about spreading missions across sites. Its closing then narrowed the debate cleanly: a crewed program can be worth sustaining without being worth most of the money.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 in Rebuttal 2 was the decisive exchange. PRO pointed out that for decades after Apollo, astronauts went no farther than low Earth orbit, yet Congress kept funding Mars rovers and kept Webb alive through overruns. That directly undercut CON's claim that deep-space human missions are indispensable for political will. CON's counter that these grew alongside the Shuttle and ISS was its strongest moment. But it implicitly conceded that a smaller, non-deep-space crewed program could anchor support, which is close to PRO's own model.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO built the cleaner comparative path: crewed missions add life-support and return costs that do not themselves produce science, while robotic missions can buy more observations. CON's strongest systemic and political reply was substantially answered by PRO's direct procurement point and the post-Apollo record of sustained robotic funding even without deep-space crews. CON's warning that smaller crewed programs cannot anchor budgets remained speculative, and PRO plausibly showed a smaller crewed program is compatible with shifting most exploration money.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's treatment of the rescue and political arguments: an astronaut can only rescue a rover if a crewed expedition is already at the same costly site, and post-Apollo Congress funded rovers and Webb while human crews stayed in LEO. This directly undercut CON's claim that robotic exploration depends on a majority-share deep-space crewed anchor.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A won every major clash by pairing a clear physical-economic case—life support and return vehicles multiply mass and cost without adding scientific detectors—with surgical comparative weighing against Side B's structural counterarguments. On industrial base and overhead, A exposed B's false assumption that a majority-robotic budget would mean sparse contracts, showed that reallocating existing shared infrastructure does not raise total agency bills while habitats and return vehicles do, and answered B's late pivot to human-rating rigor by noting extra landing difficulty only pays off if it buys more science than exploring additional sites. On politics, B never answered A's threshold burden—that even partial budget retention increases science if crewed premiums are eaten by bringing astronauts home—and contradicted its R1 claim that low-Earth-orbit missions cannot move Congress when forced to rely on the Shuttle and ISS in R2. Finally, A cleanly won and forced B to drop the on-site human judgment and repair argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In Turn 7 ([A4.3]-[A4.6]), Side A simultaneously turned Side B's claim that funding tracks "human deep-space missions" against it—pointing out Congress funded Mars rovers and Webb across decades when astronauts never left low Earth orbit, forcing B to contradict its earlier dismissal of LEO flights—and ended the rover-rescue clash by noting an astronaut can free a stuck rover only if we already paid for a crew expedition to that exact site rather than diversifying across independent targets.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): PRO, Side B, opened with the more intuitive case: crewed missions carry large life-support and safety overhead, and robots can reach places humans cannot. That core claim was never fully dislodged, and CON's honest concession on Q1 helped PRO. However, PRO's later speeches leaned on escalating assertions without showing them. Examples include "orders of magnitude," "ten cents on the dollar," and "a thin stream of peer-reviewed results." PRO also never engaged the specific mechanisms CON raised. CON, Side A, built a sharper path to victory. It framed the motion as a marginal question: what cancelling the next expedition actually buys. It then exposed a real tension in PRO's own plan. PRO promised to keep a crewed capability for "rare cases," yet priced the savings as though that capability were shut down. CON pressed this through Q2 and its second rebuttal, and PRO's answer ("one expedition per decade, just enough staffing") walked straight into the dilemma. PRO's closing restated its mandate instead of resolving it. CON also landed the Hubble point cleanly, arguing that redundancy is not correction. Its fieldwork argument, about how many leads a team can pursue, was more concrete than PRO's assertion that sample return yields "the same materials." CON is not a blowout winner. Its case often proves "targeted cuts" rather than directly defeating the science-per-dollar gap. Still, it handled the decisive clash better and showed better epistemic discipline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's response to PRO's "retain a small crewed capability for rare cases" claim. Across [A2.5], [A4.3] and [A5.3]–[A5.5], CON argued that you either pay to keep lander teams and hardware ready, which shrinks the savings, or you disperse them, which removes the promised capability. In short, "the public cannot spend both." PRO's one-expedition-per-decade answer never escaped this dilemma. A strong runner-up is [A2.1], where CON argued that "a spare camera looking through the same flawed mirror sees the same blur," which undercut PRO's claim that redundancy replaces servicing.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): A (CON) won a close debate by exposing a solvency gap in B's majority-transfer plan. B had a strong core case about crewed overhead and extracted an honest concession that past crewed flight had no clearly unique discovery, but A showed B could not simultaneously claim large savings and preserve a ready crewed capability. A also defused the Hubble cost attack by noting the whole Shuttle program lifetime cost is not the marginal alternative and redundancy is not correction. B never fully resolved the readiness dilemma, leaving PRO's path persuasive but not clean enough after the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's readiness rebuttal was the most decisive: after B proposed cutting lunar landings to roughly one per decade while retaining rare-case crewed capability, A showed keeping specialists and hardware ready consumes savings, while standing them down forfeits the promised flexibility. That left B's solvency claim unresolved.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A built a strong macro-case on crewed life-support overhead, neutralized the Hubble example by noting retired Shuttle servicing does not apply to deep-space observatories, and forced a candid concession on the 20-year orbital discovery record. However, Side B won by reframing the choice around marginal avoided costs, defending human fieldwork with concrete stratigraphic boundary-tracing mechanisms that Side A brushed past, and exposing a fatal contradiction in Side A's "most, not all" plan. When pressed to specify its trade, Side A proposed one lunar landing per decade while cutting the standing contractor workforce to fund flagship probes. Side B dismantled that model in Rebuttal 2 by showing that keeping specialists and hardware requalified consumes the promised savings while dispersing them forfeits ready capability, and Side A left that decisive critique unanswered in a largely repetitive closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side B's Q2 forced Side A to define its "rare cases" crewed reserve as one lunar expedition per decade without a standing workforce, Side B's Rebuttal 2 exposed the aerospace operational catch—released lander specialists require retraining and idle systems require requalification—showing Side A tried to spend both the savings of standing a team down and the flexibility of keeping it ready.
