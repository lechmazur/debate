### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won by targeting the word "only." The motion requires stronger transit, sanitation, and noise plans together before any later closing time. A argued that cities should instead impose the right condition for the actual harm, and B never broke that line. A's hospital-route music room example forced PRO to defend denying permits where the existing safeguards demonstrably work. A then answered each PRO mechanism with a more targeted tool: - **Cumulative overload:** a published district capacity cap that counts permits already granted. - **Magnet effect:** a cutoff on new admissions, separate from the later closing time. - **Litter:** frontage clearing or a later sweep tied to the actual gap. - **Fairness among applicants:** lottery-allocated fixed terms. A also answered PRO's pressure questions directly and concretely. It explained how the cap would protect the hospital's bus cushion and required agency load data rather than operator assurances. PRO's best points were the free-riding objection and the fact that judgments about whether existing service is enough are open to lobbying. But PRO's answer to A's Q1, that it would deny the permit even when inspectors confirm the sweep suffices, exposed the cost of a rigid rule. A then reframed that objection as being about "who pays," not about clean streets. PRO's closing slightly misdescribed A's inspection standard as judging an empty sidewalk, and A cleanly answered that both rules require a forecast. A's speeches progressively narrowed the clash, while PRO's leaned on generic assertions about lobbying and on vivid but repeated imagery.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal ([A4.5]-[A4.7]) was decisive. It accepted PRO's legitimate hazards, such as glass during the show and noisy corners, and offered targeted conditions for each. It then showed that the mandatory upgrade floor only changes the outcome where safeguards already pass. That made PRO's insistence on denial look like a fee-extraction rule rather than harm prevention.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a technically strong exchange by narrowing the dispute to the one live question: what does a mandatory upgrade floor add once a capacity-and-compliance test is in place? A's progression was excellent — the checking-vs-strengthening distinction in the opening, the district cap and admission-cutoff answers in Rebuttal 1, direct A1/A2 responses built on load figures, lotteries, and fee-pooling, and a closing that crystallized on the word "only" and the demand that the city "name the harm a condition prevents." Side B (PRO) argued vividly and consistently (the 3:30 a.m. magnet, cumulative-failure waivers, the free-rider/slack argument, lobby-proofing) and answered pressure honestly, but its core burden — why deny a permit when all three dimensions demonstrably pass — was defended mostly by the taxpayer-slack principle, which A partially neutralized by charging venues for cleanup they cause and counting the hospital cushion into the cap. B's floor also remained vulnerable to A's symmetry point that "stronger" is itself a matter of degree and thus lobbyable. Both sides showed clean grounding with no fake sourcing and correct Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 formatting. A made the debate easier to decide and landed the cleaner weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B's pressure Q1/Q2 versus A's Rebuttal 2: B pressed that a static cap rations free spare seats and that capacity tests invite zero-cost lobbying; A answered with busy-night load caps counting lockup staff, fixed-term lotteries, and mandatory agency load figures and inspector checks published against uniform thresholds — then flipped the clash by noting B's A1 answer had shifted from dirty streets to who pays for clean ones, and that "the upgrade floor changes the verdict where safeguards pass," which A's closing sealed with "neither does a receipt for an extra sweep."
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B wins by defending a capacity-based alternative that addresses transit, sanitation, and noise where each is actually inadequate, while exposing the cost of A's mandatory triple upgrade: denying a venue even when existing services demonstrably cope. B answered cumulative-demand, fairness, and lobbying pressures with counted caps, protected hospital cushion, lotteries, and published agency/inspector tests. A's free-riding and slack arguments were real but did not justify redundant services in every case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: after conceding added cleanup or backup rides when needed, it showed A's floor changes the verdict only where safeguards already pass, shifting the objection from preventing harm to forcing payment for an unnecessary service.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): This was close, and both sides were competent. CON built a real dilemma: if plans are genuinely "stronger," they price out small or transit-poor venues; if they are diluted into rideshare reminders, they deliver little extra while still adding red tape. CON also raised a sharp chicken-and-egg problem for late transit. PRO edged ahead for three reasons. First, it answered pressure directly, including biting the bullet: "a missing bus is not the test; a missing way home is." That makes denial in genuinely stranded cases a defensible outcome rather than a hidden cost. Second, it reframed the comparison away from "curfew vs. staggering," since both sides allow staggering. CON kept leaning on the simultaneous-closing crush, which did not answer that reframe. Third, its cumulative-impact argument showed why per-applicant plans add information a licensing map cannot supply: several "low-impact" bars relying on the same pickup bay. CON replied that the map tells the city where to require plans. That reply is reasonable, but it drifts toward conceding conditional plans in the places that matter. It also somewhat overstated PRO's position as imposing vicarious liability for idling drivers. PRO's proportionate, tailored plan model also stayed closer to the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over CON's Q2 and the rebuttals around it: whether a standalone bar in a transit-thin area is banned, or whether rideshares count as "stronger transit." PRO answered that rideshares count only with a demonstrated way home, and that the extension should otherwise be denied. PRO then turned to the four-venue shared-corner problem that one-at-a-time targeted review misses. CON's licensing-map answer was solid but not fully sufficient.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Both sides were disciplined and adaptive, but B (PRO) did more to earn the win. B consistently defended the "only when" condition as scaled, operational commitments rather than heavy infrastructure, and had pre-seeded that scaling in [B1.3], blunting A's sharpest line that B's position "contorted" under pressure. A's dilemma — enforce strictly and crush small venues, or water down and gut "transit" — was the strongest CON route, but B navigated it with the coherent middle path (confirm carpools, reserve staff cabs, check pickup corners) and an honest embrace of the burden ("a missing bus is not the test; a missing way home is" [B4.4]). B's cumulative-impact blind spot ([B3.2], [B4.5], [B5.1]) was the debate's most original and productive offensive point: a licensing map shows where doors are, not whether four operators are counting on the same rides. A's answer — the city already sees block density and can attach targeted rules — was solid but converges toward requiring plans where risk clusters, which B's closing turned into a concession that commitments are what should drive the decision. A's chicken-and-egg transit critique was answered by B's trial co-approval and never re-established as decisive. A's vicarious-liability point about punishing venues for outside drivers idling was real but late and underdeveloped. Both sides followed the A1:/A2: format cleanly; B's answers were slightly more direct and its closings better crystallized the live clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The [B4.5]–[A4.2]–[B5.1] exchange over the shared pickup corner: B exposed the one-application-at-a-time blind spot, A countered that city licensing data already reveals cumulative density, and B's closing landed the decisive rejoinder that a map shows where doors are, not whether the street can actually absorb staggered departures — keeping the conditionality live as the deciding question.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B won by forcing Side A into a persistent dilemma: meaningful stronger plans impose costs and transit barriers that exclude small or isolated venues, while inexpensive versions become routine checks or rideshare arrangements that do not clearly satisfy the mandate. B also defended targeted, risk-based permitting as able to address crowded residential corners without imposing a universal three-part gate, leaving A's cumulative-impact concern insufficient to justify the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: it used A's own answers that shifting a sweep was routine and a sign could not stop idling, then showed that making those measures genuinely stronger restored the labor, backup-transport, and enforcement burdens A had tried to scale away.
