### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): CON wins clearly. PRO built its case on stacked fabricated precision: a "25% drop in referrals," a "2018 European no-recommendations shock" with a 12% anxiety reduction, a "2022 Feed-Freedom study" with an 18% drop, and city data showing "double the odds." These claims are presented as checked evidence and deserve no credit. PRO also contradicted itself. Its opening said month-long phone bans reduced anxiety "dramatically," but its Q2 claimed those same bans produced no improvement. Its Q1 was strange and self-undermining, since it essentially asked CON's own cross-national question. Two PRO turns were clipped for exceeding the limit, which is a further execution cost. PRO never meaningfully answered the cross-national divergence argument. It offered only a one-line assertion in the closing, which came too late. CON stayed epistemically disciplined: it flagged PRO's suspicious round numbers and pressed endogeneity and reverse causality. It also drew a sharp distinction between reversibility and primacy, arguing that muting an amplifier lowers symptoms without proving it is the source. CON answered both pressure questions directly with A1/A2. CON's own claim that cross-national outcomes track welfare and school pressure was asserted rather than demonstrated, but it was plausible general reasoning and went unrebutted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and closing landed the decisive reframing: every PRO experiment, even if granted, shows only that turning the feed down reduces distress. CON argued that this is what an amplifier or coping tool would also do. A primary driver should instead predict convergence across similar-exposure countries. PRO never addressed that non-convergence, which left CON's route to victory intact.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): Side A (CON) won decisively through superior comparative framing, sustained flow coverage, and strict epistemic discipline. Side A established a clean decision threshold distinguishing a reversible symptom/amplifier from a primary root cause, pressed a cross-national divergence argument that Side B ignored until a one-line handwave in closing, and exposed reverse causality and confounding in Side B's timing claims. Side B offered one strong mechanism rebuttal on partial-reinforcement schedules, but severely undermined its case with fabricated late-stage studies and brittle fake statistics (such as an invented 2022 "Feed-Freedom" trial and a 25% district infinite-scroll referral drop), directly contradicted its own opening claim regarding blanket school phone bans, clipped two speeches, cited nonexistent reference IDs, and accidentally phrased its first pressure question against its own thesis.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's "reversible dial" rebuttal in [A4.4]-[A4.5] and [A5.1], paired with its relentless press on cross-national non-convergence ([A2.7], [A4.1]), was decisive: even granting that muting algorithmic feeds lowers short-term anxiety, Side A showed that turning down an amplifier or taking a painkiller reduces symptom volume without proving what originated the underlying crisis—especially when high-penetration countries exhibit starkly different trajectories.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B built the cleaner case by distinguishing amplification and reversible symptom relief from primary causation, while consistently pressing endogeneity, confounding, and cross-national divergence. Side A offered a useful falsification proposal, but its case depended on implausibly precise, unverifiable interventions and statistics, including a major new evidence dump in Rebuttal 2. Its clipped speeches, misplaced references, weak first pressure question, and late attempt to explain divergence through algorithmic intensity further damaged execution. Side B’s convergence test was somewhat overstated, but Side A never squarely defeated it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal most decisively reframed all of Side A’s claimed experiments as evidence that distress can be turned down, not evidence that social media created the underlying crisis, while retaining the unanswered endogeneity and cross-national challenges.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): PRO (Side A) wins clearly. It set a reasonable reading of "primary driver" as a leading causal lever rather than the sole cause, and it backed that with three lines: mechanism, timing, and dose. It then defended that frame consistently. Its rebuttals were sharp. The tobacco analogy answered both the "pre-existing trend" point and the "protective capacity" point. The aggregation argument answered the "small effect size" objection. CON's case weakened over time. It made an anachronistic claim tying a 2014 kink to the "first wave of COVID-19." It conceded within-region dose-response in B2.4, which PRO exploited well. Its late case then rested almost entirely on invented precision: variance shares of 5-10%, 30%, 25% and 20%, interaction terms below .03, and attribution to YRBS. That is exactly the brittle fake authority the rules discount. CON also lost content to clipping in two speeches. PRO had minor flaws. It cited a nonexistent reference [C4.1–C4.2], made some unsupported claims about non-exposed teens, and its "self-selection is a mechanism" move was a stretch. Even so, it held the cleaner, better-grounded path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 seized on CON's own concession that account-holders in low-penetration regions show higher distress than offline peers in the same environment. CON was pushed into a self-selection retreat padded with invented sample sizes. PRO's closing then turned that retreat into a feedback-loop mechanism, neutralizing CON's "macro stressors dominate" route.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built a much more coherent, epistemically disciplined case and won the central clashes on mechanism, developmental chronicity, and within-group dose-response. Side B persuasively showed why modest short-term experimental effects compound across universal adolescent exposure, neutralized the protective-features defense, and exploited Side A's concession that users in low-penetration regions show higher distress than offline peers. Side A hurt its credibility with a major chronological error (linking a 2014 kink to COVID-19), two clipped turns, and heavy late-round reliance on brittle fake precision—misdescribing the cross-sectional YRBSS as a longitudinal panel and inventing exact variance shares and regression interaction terms. Despite a minor reference-tag typo ([C4.1–C4.2]), Side B earned a solid win.

Its rebuttals showed why earlier trends and protective uses do not exclude social media as an accelerant, and its pressure questions exposed weaknesses in CON’s account.
