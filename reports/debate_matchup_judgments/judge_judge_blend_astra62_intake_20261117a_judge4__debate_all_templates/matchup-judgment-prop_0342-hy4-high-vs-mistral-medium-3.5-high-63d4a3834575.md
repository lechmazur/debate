### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): PRO built a clear decision rule: the choice is guaranteed ownership versus optional exposure. CON never neutralized that comparison. CON's strongest lines were time trade-off, obsolescence, and resource strain on poorer schools. PRO answered each one. On obsolescence, PRO separated a stable assessed spine from changing case studies. On time, PRO described a protected sequence rather than a rival silo. On resources, PRO argued that shared rubrics are cheaper than every teacher improvising. CON then conceded standardized, funded, nationally distributed modules with targeted training. PRO exploited that concession: what remains of "optional" is only the ability to skip it. CON's A1 answer on adaptability did not explain why updates require optional status. Its A2 answer, that existing essays prove mastery, was thin. CON's closing equity argument was reasonable but recycled earlier claims. It never answered the floor-versus-postcode-lottery point. CON's Rebuttal 1 was also clipped, a minor execution cost. CON's opening partly attacked a caricature ("Shakespeare for chatbot tutorials") that PRO effectively exposed. PRO did hedge on whether hours are lost, though it acknowledged a possible small trade-off honestly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (A4.5) and Closing (A5.1) turned CON's concession of standardized, funded, nationally distributed modules into a self-defeat. Once optional modules carry all the infrastructure of core, the only remaining difference is a weaker guarantee of access. CON never squarely answered this.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built a much cleaner comparative case and answered pressure far more directly across the debate. Side A framed AI and manipulation-resistant media literacy as protecting the corrupted inputs to every discipline—distinguishing traditional analysis inside a source from interrogating synthetic media, hallucinated citations, and engagement-ranked feeds—and neutralized Side B's obsolescence objection by pairing a stable conceptual spine with rotating AI case studies. Although Side A slightly understated implementation trade-offs in Rebuttal 2 and prematurely called its pressure questions unanswered before Side B's reply turn, Side B repeated its baseline claims about existing subjects with little adaptation, suffered a minor word-limit clip in Rebuttal 1, and contradicted itself between informal subject integration and nationally standardized optional modules.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's pressure questions ([A3.1]-[A3.2]) and closing ([A5.1]-[A5.3]) decisively dismantled Side B's defense of nationally standardized optional modules and existing subject tests ([B2.6], [B4.1]-[B4.2]). Side A showed that if materials and rapid updates are already standardized, making them optional merely removes the enforceable guarantee for overburdened schools, while scattered history or science assignments cannot verify mastery over synthetic media, ranked feeds, and fabricated citations.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by defining core status as an assessable, timetable-protected guarantee rather than a rival silo, then pressing Side B on what optional modules add beyond weaker guarantees. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): CON wins by attacking PRO's central mechanism, not the importance of the skills. PRO's whole case rests on the claim that "core status forces investment" and so guarantees equity. CON repeatedly shows that a mandate produces a schedule slot, not trained teachers, current materials, or protected time, and that the new slot must take time from literacy, math, and civics. PRO's A1 answer simply restates that scheduling requires teachers and materials. That assertion never addresses the paper-mandate and opportunity-cost objections. CON also offers a concrete comparative alternative: assessed, recurring tasks embedded in existing courses. This defuses PRO's "optional means checkbox" attack by separating "optional" from "unassessed." CON then turns PRO's own criterion, independent evaluation of new synthetic media, by arguing that this kind of transfer comes from varied contexts. PRO's later speeches largely repeat the opening's equity claims. They add unsupported assertions such as "already failed, as evidenced by widespread vulnerability," and never squarely address the contestability of "manipulation" or curricular obsolescence. CON narrowed the clash cleanly and closed by naming PRO's key word, "guaranteeing," and dismantling it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.4–B4.5: it quotes PRO's A1 mechanism ("schedule it, which requires assigning teachers and providing materials") and shows that it confuses a timetable slot with resources. It then turns PRO's A2 criterion of independent judgment of new media into an argument for distributed, transferable practice. This leaves PRO's core-status mechanism unsupported going into the closings.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B won decisively by showing that urgency about AI manipulation does not justify the institutional rigidity and opportunity costs of a permanent core subject. Side A staked its case—and especially its equity claim—on the assertion that a core mandate forces investment and guarantees universal skill. Side B dismantled that mechanism by explaining that a mandate only guarantees a timetable slot: in underfunded districts lacking specialists, it forces teacher reassignment and static vendor packets while crowding out foundational reading, math, and civics. Side A became repetitive in the second half and never answered opportunity cost, curriculum obsolescence lag, or political deadlock over standardizing definitions of manipulation, whereas Side B directly answered Side A's sharpest questions and integration claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's "core status forces investment" claim across B2.5, B4.4, and B5.1–B5.2—exposing that scheduling a required period creates a compliance slot rather than trained teachers or funding, while cannibalizing foundational subjects in poorer schools—neutralized Side A's central equity route to victory.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B built the cleaner comparative case: it neutralized PRO’s “core status forces investment” claim by separating a timetable slot from real resources, answered the pressure questions directly, and offered a workable alternative in assessed embedded modules. PRO kept strong equity rhetoric but did not adequately answer opportunity costs, curriculum instability, or how a mandate creates capacity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal turned PRO’s funding mechanism into a schedule-versus-resources distinction and reframed accountability as demonstrated judgment through recurring assessed tasks rather than a course label.
