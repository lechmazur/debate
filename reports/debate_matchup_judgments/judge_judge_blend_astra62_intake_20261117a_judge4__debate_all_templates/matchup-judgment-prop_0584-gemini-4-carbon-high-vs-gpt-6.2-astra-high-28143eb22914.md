### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): PRO built a coherent case: costs are asymmetric, inflation is a regressive and near-universal pay cut, and fiscal tools are better suited to cushioning job losses. PRO also asked the sharper pressure questions and answered its own A1/A2 directly, including a bold commitment to tighten even in CON's scenario. CON did more to break that case apart, for three reasons: - **Marginal cost reframing.** CON pointed out that tightening does not undo the initial price jump; it only prevents *additional* price growth. This meaningfully weakened PRO's "permanent pay cut" framing. - **Hidden employment costs.** CON noted that labor costs show up in cut hours, not just unemployment claims, so the price of tightening is wider than PRO's "modest rise in unemployment." - **Lopsided asymmetry.** CON showed that PRO's asymmetry compared the worst case for patience with an easy exit for tightening, since rate cuts also act with lags. CON also turned PRO's own concession that softer orders are "the exact discipline" into a question PRO never answered: why is that existing discipline insufficient? CON then gave a clear decision rule: tighten when the signals point to entrenchment, hold when they point to disinflation. PRO's later speeches leaned on repeating "permanent erosion" and "punitive rates and mass layoffs" rather than answering these mechanism-level points. PRO's closing also slightly reframed the contested scenario around elevated pricing plans. CON's closing pinned PRO to its admission that it would tighten even when later-year raises look normal, which made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange runs from CON's B2.5 through B4.3–B4.4. CON argued that the gain from tightening is only the additional price growth prevented, not the original jump. CON then showed that PRO's "exact discipline" point concedes that existing restraint is already working, and that PRO's asymmetry ignores lags in reviving hiring. PRO's A4.3–A4.4 restated the asymmetry and permanence claims but never squarely answered the marginal-benefit point.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly, used the required formats, and avoided fake sourcing. But Side B landed the sharper comparative blows: the accounting point that tightening only prevents *additional* price growth (it does not undo the initial regressive hit A leaned on), the symmetric-lags rebuttal that rate cuts also take months to revive hiring, and the closing reframe that the real question is whether the next hike prevents entrenchment, not what entrenched inflation would cost. A's core asymmetric-error-cost case survived but was weakened by B's charge that it compared a worst case for patience against an easy exit for firmness. A's strongest moment was forcing B to choose under conflicting signals; B answered honestly and plausibly (softer orders cap pass-through), while A's rejoinder largely assumed its own conclusion. B made the debate easier to decide and edged the decisive clashes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 [B2.5]: pointing out that returning inflation to target does not undo the checkout-level jump already incurred, so the gain from tightening is only the marginal price growth prevented — directly undercutting A's universal-regressive-pay-cut case — later sharpened in the closing with "a cashier's lost shift is no more reversible than yesterday's grocery bill."
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side A won the decisive clash over marginal benefit: distinguishing one-off pass-through and price-level jumps from persistent inflation, and showing weakening orders can already limit further pass-through. B's asymmetric-risk and regressive-inflation case remained forceful, but A showed B would tighten even when catch-up wages normalize and existing restraint is working, without proving that extra tightening prevents entrenchment rather than merely reaching target sooner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was most decisive: it directly answered both pressure questions, exposed B's asymmetry as comparing patience's worst case with tightening's easiest exit, and argued pricing plans show intent while softer orders show firms may be unable to pass costs through.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): This was a close, high-quality exchange. A wins narrowly because its central mechanism held up better under pressure than B's main alternative. A's mechanism was that multiyear contracts lock in expectations, so acting early is cheaper than acting late. A also corrected B's housing point with a clean distinction: a relative price can rise in one sector while overall inflation stays near target. A answered B's pressure questions honestly and directly. It would accept a miss rather than push unemployment beyond modest, and it explained why slower inflation protects families without raises. B's strongest material was its framing that contracts reset while career scars are permanent, together with its attempt to turn A's stopping rule into a "worst of both worlds." Both were rhetorically effective. However, B's alternative, a "firm ceiling," stayed exposed to A's timing argument. If inflation stays flat above target, the ceiling never triggers, and the higher rate becomes the one people plan around. B claimed that "flat renewals don't compound" and that contracts "reprice naturally." Neither claim answered why contracts would reprice down rather than at the entrenched higher rate. B's lag argument also cut against its own ceiling defense, a point A pressed in Q1 and in Rebuttal 2. B's assertion that tightening into rising demand "cools vacancies first" was thinly supported.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3 versus A4.4/A5.2. B argued that A's "hold and accept a miss" stopping rule still lets lagged tightening push layoffs past modest, possibly while missing the inflation target anyway. A answered that the same lags make B's late, ceiling-triggered tightening riskier. A also showed that a flat overshoot never triggers B's ceiling and becomes the rate people plan around. A's timing argument blunted B's best attack and decided the round.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Both sides were consistently strong, with clean format compliance, real concessions, and sharp comparative weighing. B landed the most original and memorable material (the "mathematically offset high rents by laying off non-shocked sector workers" point, and the closing contrast between a two-year contract expiring and a career scar never recovering) and effectively exploited A's "accept a miss" concession as worst-of-both-worlds. However, A built the cleaner decision rule and survived the decisive clash: the timing asymmetry that acting while the employment cost is modest preserves choices that delay erodes, because contracts lock in rates that later tightening cannot renegotiate. A's Q1 exposed a real tension in B's "firm ceiling," and A's closing crystallized the dilemma sharply — a flat overshoot never triggers the ceiling and becomes the rate people plan around, while climbing forces action with more already locked in. B's answer (escalation signals, expiring contracts reprice naturally, capacity arrives) was plausible but more optimistic and less mechanistically grounded. A also pre-empted and partially turned the scarring argument by noting delay hits the same marginal workers first via a sharper later contraction. A narrow but real edge on the live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was A's pressure Q1 versus B's defense of the "firm ceiling," followed by A's closing crystallization: B could only risk jobs when inflation actually climbs, but A showed that under that rule a flat above-target overshoot never triggers action and becomes embedded in renewed contracts, while acceleration forces later action with more lock-in and the same lags — the sharpest unresolved dilemma of the debate.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): B won by narrowing the motion to broadly stalled, persistent inflation and showing early modest action preserves credibility in contracts before costs lock in. A landed strong points on scarring, lags, and supply constraints, and effectively turned B's willingness to accept a miss, but never resolved why waiting for escalation would be safer when lags then require harder tightening against more entrenched contracts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 attack on A's firm ceiling: flat above-target renewals would never trigger action and become the planning rate, while a later climb would force tightening with the same lags but more locked-in increases.
