### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO wins mainly on the scope of the motion. The proposition concerns "democratic politics," and PRO repeatedly pressed the point that partisan cable news is largely a U.S. phenomenon, while polarization surged across democracies that never had it. CON's answer was that the test is "the machinery of governing" rather than "a headcount of countries." In practice that narrowed the motion to places where cable was strong. CON also implicitly conceded that social media did the polarizing work elsewhere. CON built a clear and memorable "authority, not velocity" frame. Its "narrative spine / nervous system" and "hostage still holds the gate" lines were genuinely strong. Even so, CON never fully neutralized PRO's two routes to victory: the global comparison, and the reversal in which cable hosts are disciplined by online audiences. PRO's "hostage reading lines written online" turned CON's bottleneck claim against it. PRO had execution costs: its Q1 cited nonexistent reference IDs ([C2.x]), and some of its claims about older voters' online immersion were asserted rather than developed. These costs trim the margin but do not reverse the result.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was over whether cable is still the "narrative spine." PRO's Q2 asked how cable could hold that role if hosts are punished for contradicting viral election myths. CON answered that cable sits at a "bottleneck" between radicalized feeds and the older, high-turnout electorate. PRO's closing then recast that bottleneck as a hostage reading lines written online, which made CON's concession look like evidence that platforms set the agenda.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): A won by broadening the comparison beyond cable-heavy U.S. institutions while also reversing the cable-dependency claim. B’s strongest frame was that cable reorganized high-turnout voters, donors, and party elites, but A neutralized it with the global point that many democracies lacked partisan cable yet polarized through WhatsApp, YouTube, and Facebook. A also showed cable hosts now fear audience revolt when they doubt viral election myths, turning B’s “narrative spine” argument into an admission that cable is downstream of online radicalization. B’s “hostage still holds the gate” reply was clever but conceded the decisive causal point: feeds drive the content and punish deviation. B never cleanly answered A’s global burden and relied too much on tabloids, radio, and elite television outside the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s reversal that cable is not writing the script but being disciplined by its viewers’ feeds was the decisive exchange. B’s bottleneck response reinforced rather than defeated it by conceding hosts face revolt when they contradict viral election conspiracies.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): B built the stronger comparative case: social media polarized democracies without partisan cable while also transforming participation, elite incentives, and agenda-setting inside cable-strong systems. A’s consequential-electorate and governing-strategy argument was coherent, but it repeatedly narrowed the comparison to places where cable was strong and relied heavily on the asserted television “bottleneck.” B’s pressure questions exposed both limitations, and its answers and closing turned A’s audience-revolt concession into a compelling reversal of cable’s claimed authority.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure questions and subsequent rebuttals were decisive: if cable hosts are punished for contradicting online-radicalized audiences, cable is not clearly supplying the narrative spine. A’s “hostage still holds the gate” response was memorable, but B more convincingly explained why holding the gate is not the same as controlling the pressure passing through it.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B wins on the strength of its comparison, not its imagery. PRO built a plausible mechanism story: engagement incentives, participatory identity signaling, and decentralized enforcement. Its best reframe was that "origin is not magnitude." But PRO repeatedly conceded the ground CON needed. It granted that cable supplied the script and split the polity first, that online groups "amplify cable content," that young users are "less sorted," and that platforms amplified effects "where cable took root." CON turned those concessions into a magnitude argument, the factory-versus-pipe framing. It also pressed two empirical tests that PRO never fully neutralized. First, polarization rose earlier and more among older, TV-reliant cohorts. Second, peer democracies with the same apps polarized less. PRO's A2 offered plausible confounds, such as different party systems and media rules. However, those confounds partly conceded that cable's presence was the key variable. CON's claims about viral challengers "routinely" losing and lawmakers ignoring online pile-ons were asserted with some overconfidence. CON also slightly stretched PRO's "less sorted" hedge into a concession about heavy users. Even so, CON made the debate easier to decide. PRO's closing largely restated the "no off switch" theme without answering the cross-national and cohort tests.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 was decisive. It answered PRO's Q1 with timing: older voters polarized before they joined Facebook. It then quoted PRO's own A2 admission that "where cable took root, social platforms amplified and decentralized its effects." That turned PRO's multiplication argument into an admission that cable was the primary driver and social media its echo.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): CON (B) built the stronger path to victory by converting PRO's key concessions into its own case. PRO conceded that cable created the original division and that online groups amplify cable content. CON used this to show social media is mainly a distribution pipe for cable's polarizing product, while cable did the harder institutional work of shattering shared reality and locking high-turnout audiences. The cross-national and age-cohort points were decisive: PRO's response that "similar apps need not produce identical outcomes" was weak compared to CON's concrete argument that peer democracies with the same apps avoided comparable fracture where partisan cable was absent. PRO's best counter—that social media removes the off switch and makes polarization self-enforcing—was meaningfully undercut by CON's evidence that online-only primary challenges routinely fizzle and that authoritarian synchronization is what disciplines politicians. CON was sharper in turning concessions and maintaining a coherent, grounded comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 was the decisive exchange. It took PRO's admission that online groups amplify cable content and that heavy young users remain "less sorted into party institutions," then used both against PRO: if feeds are merely recirculating cable and feeds alone fail to polarize parties and primaries, PRO cannot claim social media did more. CON also directly answered the discipline question by showing that viral primary challengers and online pile-ons often lack home-district voters, while prime-time cable reaches reliable older electorates. This neutralized PRO's "self-enforcing" argument at its core.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A built the cleaner comparative case: cable did not merely arrive first but continuingly supplied authoritative narratives, synchronized high-turnout electorates, and disciplined politicians. Side B offered a compelling account of social media as participatory enforcement, but never adequately showed that amplification exceeded cable’s contribution. Its answers about older Facebook users and differing institutions blunted, rather than defeated, A’s age and cross-national tests, and sometimes reinforced A’s dependence argument. A’s closing crystallized that advantage memorably without relying on fake authority.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions, then used timing, recycled cable content, and primary-electorate incentives to turn B’s “amplification” and “self-enforcement” claims into evidence of continued dependence on cable.
