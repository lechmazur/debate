PRO stayed disciplined, handled the French, Norwegian and Japanese examples well, and fairly argued that CON's regime also needs enforcement. CON's under- and over-inclusiveness framing made the decision cleaner.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): PRO wins a competitive debate by steadily narrowing the clash and then exploiting CON's concessions. First, PRO showed that CON's preferred remedies depend on disclosure. Faculty senates and conflict-of-interest committees cannot review gifts or clauses they never see. That reduced the real dispute to whether dependence itself may be limited. Second, PRO separated two kinds of control. Contract policing catches written vetoes, while funding limits target dependence. PRO's answer that "leverage only works on someone with something to lose" was an effective response to CON's argument that visa leverage survives a cap. Third, PRO's A1 answer gave concrete, observable triggers for a limit, assigned classification to an independent reviewer, and pointed out that CON's own "review of actual control" requires the same judgment. CON's strongest material held up well. The domestic-capture worry, the elasticity of "control" and "security stakes," and the point that visa and family leverage outlasts a cap were real pressure. CON was also disciplined and avoided fake precision. However, CON conceded under questioning that one ministry funding an entire institute may warrant concentration limits. In closing, CON said universities "can be bound" to diversify or cap support, which drifts close to the motion itself. PRO's closing turned that concession sharply: a voluntary limit hands the decision to the administrator the leverage is already working on. CON never fully answered that point. PRO's weighing, "a constraint you can contest versus a discretion you cannot see," was clearer and more memorable than CON's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A4.3–A4.4 followed by A5.1–A5.2. PRO answered the visa-leverage question by arguing that a limit removes what the threat aims at, rather than the threat itself. CON then conceded concentration limits in B4.2. PRO used that concession to argue that voluntary limits leave the decision with the already-captured administrator, which reframed CON's autonomy alternative as the capture mechanism itself.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side A won a narrow but real edge. PRO turned CON's concession that universities may set concentration limits into the central argument: limits are legitimate, so the live question is whether they must be required. PRO showed that internal administrators under foreign leverage cannot be trusted to self-impose limits when it matters, making mandatory disclosure and appealable limits the stronger remedy. CON's domestic-capture and overinclusion objections were serious and kept the debate close, but PRO's written-criteria, independent-review answer and emphasis on invisible anticipatory silence provided the cleaner path to decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was A's use of B's concession on concentration limits: once B admitted universities may cap one-ministry domination, A narrowed the clash to whether captured administrators can be trusted, turning B's safeguard into an argument for mandatory external limits.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): PRO defined disclosure and limits narrowly around state control, secrecy, vetoes, and dominant dependence, then answered the domestic-capture objection with published criteria, independent review, and appeal. CON showed caps do not eliminate visa or family pressure, but PRO persuasively replied that reducing the financial stake reduces what that pressure can purchase. CON never fully explained why confidential or voluntary safeguards work when decision-makers cannot see hidden terms or are themselves dependent.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing turned CON's concession that universities may impose their own concentration limits into the decisive point: the administrator expected to self-limit is the actor already exposed to the funder's leverage, making an external, contestable requirement necessary.
