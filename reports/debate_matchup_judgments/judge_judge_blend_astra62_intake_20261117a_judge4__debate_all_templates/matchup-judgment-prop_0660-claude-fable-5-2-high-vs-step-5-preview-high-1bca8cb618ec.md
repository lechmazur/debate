- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A built the cleaner, more decisive comparative case on the proposition as written. However, Side B weakened its case by conceding that France's negotiated model avoids the broad chilling effect it predicted, relying on an empirically unobservable compliance tax, and walking into Side A's closing turn by describing midnight analyst replies as unchosen cost-absorption. Side A incurred a real execution cost for having three speeches clipped at the word cap, which keeps the margin narrower than its substantive edge alone would warrant, but its core comparative logic clearly prevailed.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B won by separating the value of rest from the actual compliance and incentive effects of a right-to-disconnect statute in knowledge work. Under B's pressure, Side A conceded extra staffing needs, slower cross-timezone handoffs, and employer over-blocking of voluntary evening work; B rightly showed that relabeling these losses as "honest scheduling" is a moral judgment that does not erase real, recurring productivity costs. On welfare, B effectively pinched A's case: well-run firms already manage boundaries, risk-averse employers respond to statutory liability with blanket restrictions that strip working parents of flexible schedules, and poorly run firms evade enforcement through discretionary promotion and staffing decisions. Side A debated with admirable discipline and landed a sharp closing answer against pricing overtime, while Side B incurred a real execution cost from getting the ends of Turns 2, 4, and 8 clipped at the word cap, narrowing the margin—but B's comparative case was clearly more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In Turn 8 ([B4.6]-[B4.8]), Side B dismantled both pillars of Side A's R2 recovery: it defeated A's analogy to other employment laws by contrasting payslip-verifiable minimum wages with unspoken expectations around discretionary promotions, and refuted A's claim that productivity costs are merely "transitional" by noting that "Bangalore never moves closer to San Francisco" and extra headcount is a permanent payroll cost.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A built the clearer comparative path: B’s welfare gains became concentrated in badly managed firms and dependent on uncertain deterrence, while B expressly conceded staffing burdens, slower handoffs, and some over-blocking of voluntary work. A also turned flexibility into a welfare cost, not merely a productivity objection. B answered both pressure questions directly and gave a plausible structural-recovery case, but “honest scheduling” did not fully neutralize the admitted recurring costs or the enforcement problem. A’s repeated clipping is an execution cost, keeping this competitive rather than dominant.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first rebuttal decisively shifted the debate from whether costs existed to whether they were outweighed, then exposed the enforcement dilemma: the workers most needing protection were least able to risk invoking it. B’s record-and-deterrence answer was reasonable but conceded imperfect enforcement and employer over-blocking, which A converted into a clean closing comparison.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): CON won the central comparison: PRO conceded staffing, slower asynchronous handoffs, and possible over-blocking, while its welfare gain was narrowed to badly managed firms where enforcement against promotion-controlling managers remained speculative.
