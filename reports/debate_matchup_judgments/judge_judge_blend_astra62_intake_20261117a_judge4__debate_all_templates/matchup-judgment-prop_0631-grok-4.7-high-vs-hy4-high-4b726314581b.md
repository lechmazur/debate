### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined and engaged directly. PRO built a clear authorship-and-incentive case and landed a sharp Q2: if "I selected it" is enough, when is any output ever bare? CON answered that question cleanly through scope. Raw model details stay unowned, while human crops, layouts and rewrites are protected. That answer let CON grant PRO's core worry without granting the motion's documentation gate. CON then built a dilemma that PRO never escaped. PRO's A1/A2 answers defined documentation as drafts, edits or version history, and also said "we do not demand a separate compliance diary." Read that way, "document" either collapses into ordinary proof of authorship when challenged, which makes the motion redundant, or it makes an archived diff essential. In that case a freelancer with a lost chat history forfeits visible human choices to a copier. PRO's closing claimed CON had conceded the decisive ground. But the concession that raw output stays unowned already fits existing originality doctrine. PRO never showed what the added threshold buys. PRO also never answered the asymmetric burden on small creators or the "before-and-after proves alteration, not substantiality" point. CON made the decision easier because it isolated the motion's distinctive element, the documentation gate, and showed it either adds nothing or causes harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.4 was the most decisive exchange. It seized on PRO's A4.1/A4.4 answers to frame a dilemma: either "document" means ordinary proof of authorship, making the motion redundant, or it means an essential archived diff, which penalizes freelancers and rewards copiers. PRO's closing restated its authorship framing but never resolved either branch.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): PRO wins a competitive debate. Over the rounds the clash narrowed to two questions: whether choosing and recombining model outputs fixes human expression, and whether the documentation clause is redundant or an archive trap. PRO held a coherent line throughout. The burden sits on the claimant, and documentation means substantiation that can be checked against the work, not a surviving chat log. Protection extends to identifiable human expression, including arrangement. A4.1 and A4.3 answered the pressure questions directly and concretely. They covered what proof suffices without logs and how partial authorship works, which blunted CON's "confiscation" charge. CON's "redundant or trap" fork is rhetorically sharp, but it carries a weakness. CON repeatedly concedes that untouched one-click output already fails under ordinary doctrine. If the motion merely restates that doctrine, the proposition is close to correct as written, so the redundancy horn helps PRO more than CON. CON also partly misdescribed PRO in B4.5. It claimed arrangement authorship is excluded, but A4.3 explicitly includes "new arrangement." CON's A2 answer conceded that only ordinary proof stops bare assertion, which leaves PRO's rights-laundering concern only partly answered. CON's first rebuttal was clipped for length, an execution cost. CON's camera, film-cut and collage analogies were vivid, but PRO's distinction held: a model supplies the expressive content itself, whereas a camera's grain and flare are by-products of a scene the photographer chose. That distinction kept PRO's line administrable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was over CON's dilemma, "redundant or trap" (B3.1, B4.4), and PRO's answer in A4.1 and A5.1. PRO held that a work-specific declaration plus corroboration, checkable against visible edits or versions, satisfies "can document" without requiring a surviving log. Combined with CON's own concession that bare acceptance already fails, this left CON's fork mostly as a redundancy complaint rather than a reason to reject the motion.
