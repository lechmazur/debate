### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but B's line of attack proved more durable across rounds. A's core mechanism—that earmarking reverses the political default so that diversion, not inclusion, requires justification—was a strong opening move, but B's "proves too much" rebuttal (a binding default persists even when tourism volume falls, payrolls/debt outlive their rationale, and categories can be satisfied by visitor-serving rather than resident-serving projects) landed and was never squarely neutralized in A's final rebuttal or closing. A conceded repeatedly that earmarking guarantees only a "destination," not quality or targeting, which let B's closing crystallize the decisive framing: "Earmarking may protect spending from politics; only accountable allocation protects residents from ineffective spending." B also consistently showed that every safeguard A claimed as earmarking's advantage (audits, geographic targeting, sunset clauses, reserves) is equally available to general budgeting, narrowing A's unique advantage down to a burden-of-proof default that B's entrenchment critique undercut. A's opening was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost per the judging frame. Both sides executed the Pressure Questions round properly with clean A1/A2 answers, but B's answers more directly exposed the gap between "guaranteed category" and "guaranteed resident benefit."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's "proves too much" argument in Rebuttal 2 (B4.4)—that a binding earmark persists even when tourism revenue falls or needs shift, locking in payrolls/debt while a general fund could scale back—was the most damaging exchange, and A's closing never directly answered the entrenchment/obsolescence point.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing the debate around the political reality of municipal budgeting. By conceding that earmarks do not guarantee perfect project design, Side B neutralized Side A's attacks about wasteful spending, successfully arguing that the real value of an earmark is establishing a 'binding default' that protects residents from having to fight for repair funds annually.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 decisively shifted the debate by pointing out that Side A's alternative relies on an 'ideal budget process,' noting that general budgets are equally capable of funding wasteful projects but add the risk of politically marginalizing tourism's victims.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO won a close, high-quality exchange by making the decisive clash easier to judge: under ordinary city politics, concentrated tourism harms need a binding default, not another annual contest for general-fund priority. CON landed real damage on categories versus outcomes, volatility, and rigid claims that can outlive good projects, and both sides conceded design tools (audits, targeting, seasonal contracts, reserves) matter either way. But CON never fully neutralized the diversion/political-economy burden; its stronger annual conditions still looked revocable relative to PRO’s “primarily” priority-plus-flexibility frame. PRO’s pressure answers and closing crystallized that comparison cleanly enough to carry the motion as written.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but CON (B) landed the more damaging and sustained critique: that PRO's own safeguards for earmarking (enforceable baselines, audits, additionality rules, periodic review) are doing all the real protective work, while the "primarily earmarked" mandate adds only rigidity without a demonstrated independent benefit. This came to a head when PRO conceded in Rebuttal 2 that if the binding rule truly prevents offsets, "it is an earmark in substance," which CON correctly exploited in Rebuttal 2 to show the dispute collapses into semantics about labels rather than outcomes. CON also pressed harder and more consistently on the motion's core structural flaw — that mandatory priority for three categories persists even when an emergency or more urgent need arises — and PRO's attempted fix (a "causal test" plus "residual" category for emergencies) felt improvised and under-defended rather than a clean principle. PRO's best surviving argument (that earmarking forces a visible political decision before diversion, while general-budget diversion is invisible) was a good point, but CON's closing neutralized much of its force by noting that visibility/reporting requirements could be layered onto general budgeting without the rigid priority rule. B's opening was clipped per the format note, which is a real execution cost, but the content lost appears to be a restatement of an already-stated thesis rather than load-bearing argument, and B recovered fully in later rounds. On balance, CON made the debate easier to decide by consistently isolating the actual point of disagreement and forcing PRO into concessions that undercut the motion's necessity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 response to A1 ("if outcomes are excellent... the objection is then merely which account paid... If outcomes are poor, enforceable expenditure commitments and audits address the failure; an earmark alone does not") combined with the observation that PRO's "earmark in substance" concession shows the safeguards, not the label, are doing the protective work.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A delivered a logically airtight case by exposing that Side B's defenses against the substitution problem (baselines, additionality rules, and audits) are actually general budgeting mechanisms, not inherent benefits of earmarking. Side A successfully framed the debate around the idea that residents need effective services, not a specific tax ledger label. Side B also made a significant tactical error in the Pressure Questions by asking Side A what unique benefit earmarking adds, allowing Side A to easily reinforce its core argument that earmarks only add unnecessary rigidity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1 for identifying that PRO's defense against substitution relies entirely on general budgeting safeguards, effectively dismantling the unique utility of the earmark.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): CON won a competitive, high-quality clash by forcing the decisive comparison: once baselines, additionality, audits, and published outcomes are required, earmarking’s residual work is mainly compulsory source-of-funds priority, not better resident services. PRO’s fiscal-bargain and causation case stayed live and coherent, but never showed why that ledger rule must override demonstrated need when circumstances change. Side A’s clipped opening was a real cost, yet later rebuttals still made the vote clearer: public money should follow need, not tax origin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1/A4.4: PRO’s anti-substitution safeguards do the real protective work; the earmark then adds rigidity rather than unique service benefit—if outcomes are protected, ledger labels do not injure residents.
