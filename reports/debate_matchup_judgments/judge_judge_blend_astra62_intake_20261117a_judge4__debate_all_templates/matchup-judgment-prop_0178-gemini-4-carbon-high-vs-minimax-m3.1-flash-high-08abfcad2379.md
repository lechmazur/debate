### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO wins a clear but competitive debate. It built the more coherent causal story: reach multiplied by sustained compliance, mandates that work passively through defaults, and information that has to overcome price, time, and cravings on every shopping trip. It then adapted well across rounds. The strongest pieces were the point that CON's own compensation examples (crackers, soda) fall under the same caps, and turning CON's admission that inertia "preserves purchasing patterns" into the closing frame: defaults harness inertia, information fights it. CON kept a genuinely live line, and it was its best asset. CON argued that "exposure is not effect size" and that "any" demands superiority over the best feasible campaign. CON also exposed that PRO's assurance that compensation is minimal rests on plausibility rather than evidence. CON's closing dilemma (if labels induce reformulation, they deliver the mandate's supply-side benefit plus a choice mechanism) was clever. However, it partly inverted PRO's point, since PRO had already argued that labels let flagship products simply print the badge and keep selling, which mandates prevent. CON's compensation argument thinned out after the cross-category caps answer, retreating to vague talk of "meals and eating occasions." Its clipped opening is an execution cost. PRO loses some credit on grounding. Its A2 answer ("Experience across nutrition policy shows...") gestures at evidence it never specifies and borders on unsupported authority. Even so, PRO's case was the easier one to vote for.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange came when PRO's Q1 and A1 pointed out that CON's offsetting examples (salty crackers, sugary drinks) are themselves ultra-processed foods bound by the same caps. This undercut CON's central substitution mechanism and forced CON to retreat to diffuse cross-meal compensation. CON's best counter was the closing dilemma, which recast labels as inducing reformulation plus a choice mechanism. It was sharp but arrived late and did not fully answer PRO's point that labeled flagship products keep selling.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): B wins narrowly. A offered a compelling passive-reach mechanism and effectively challenged cross-product substitution under industry-wide caps. But its claimed superiority depended on overconfident assumptions about negligible compensation, calorie reductions, and compliance costs. B distinguished product coverage from net health effects and kept alive labels’ combined pathways through consumer substitution and manufacturer reformulation. A explained why mandates could reach further, but did not adequately establish why that advantage outweighs the strongest campaign’s potential effects.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s comparative-effect challenge exposed A’s weakest step: A answered by asserting that policy experience establishes superiority rather than developing a supported comparison. B then used label-induced reformulation to undermine the blanket contrast between information and supply-side change. A’s inertia framing was sharper rhetorically, but did not fully resolve that comparison.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A offered the more memorable affirmative mechanism: mandates exploit inertia while information must overcome it. But Side B more consistently exposed the missing comparative step from universal product exposure to superior net population-health effects. Caps apply to products, not whole diets, and A’s claims that compensation would be minimal and mandates would guarantee larger gains remained asserted rather than demonstrated. B’s dual-route argument—that labels can guide substitution and also induce reformulation—left A without a clean proof of superiority over the strongest informational alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal and closing turned A’s concession about label-induced reformulation into a dilemma: labeling can change recipes while retaining an additional choice mechanism. A answered that mandates are more universal, but did not establish that this universality outweighs labeling’s combined effects.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): A's core frame was clear and stable: reformulation changes exposure before choice, while labels depend on contingent behavior. But B built the more complete comparative case. B's "two engines" argument held that front-of-pack warning thresholds induce voluntary reformulation where recipes can tolerate it and keep warnings on unfixable products, which steers consumers toward whole foods. That absorbed most of A's passive-exposure advantage while adding a substitution benefit that A conceded caps cannot deliver. B also extracted damaging concessions through its pressure questions: A admitted the "satiating product structure" promise was an overclaim and conceded that standalone caps shift no diets toward whole foods. A's fallback to restricting or discontinuing unfixable products let B argue that this turned reformulation into prohibition, contradicting A's opening claim about preserving consumer autonomy. A's best counter was that mandated reformulation is more reliable than voluntary reformulation, and B's framing of policy causation answered it only partially. Even so, A never neutralized the substitution engine or the compensation and substitute-ingredient problem. Both of A's rebuttals were also clipped for length, which is a real execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.3–B2.4 was the most decisive exchange. B turned A's concession in A2.4, that warnings can induce reformulation, into the "two engines" frame: label-induced reformulation captures passive benefits where recipes allow, and persistent warnings drive substitution elsewhere. A's mechanism-versus-trigger reply in A4.6 and A5.2 never fully recovered the comparative ground.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won the central policy comparison: labeling deserves credit for reformulation it induces and can additionally encourage substitution toward whole foods. A retained a credible reliability advantage but never adequately weighed it against both pathways, repeatedly confusing the health mechanism with the policy causing it. B also successfully pressured A’s expanded mandate design into retreat. However, B overstated both the harms of ingredient replacement and how completely warnings would match mandated reformulation, keeping the win competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A2.4 was decisive: warning-induced recipe changes are benefits of labeling, including passive benefits for non-readers. A repeatedly claimed those changes favored mandates merely because reformulation was the mechanism, leaving B’s strongest comparative argument substantially unanswered.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won by turning A’s strongest passive-exposure argument into a comparative advantage for warnings: labels can induce feasible reformulation while also encouraging substitution toward whole foods. A’s claim that recipe changes cannot count as labeling benefits confused the health mechanism with policy causation. A also retreated from the satiety overclaim, conceded that standalone caps produce no whole-food substitution, and never established that its alleged guaranteed exposure reduction outweighs B’s two channels. B’s categorical claims were sometimes underqualified, but its case remained clearer and better adapted; A’s two clipped rebuttals added execution costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s “two engines” rebuttal was decisive: warning-induced recipe cuts supply passive benefits where reformulation is feasible, while persistent warnings address products that caps cannot salvage. A’s later concessions and prohibition fallback did not neutralize that comparison.
