### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): A won narrowly by preserving a clear, targeted rule: validated automation remains available, while unproven consequential inferences require justification before rejection. B’s compliance-cost pressure was effective and forced honest concessions, but its repeated machines-versus-gut-instinct framing understated A’s structured alternatives and conditionally permitted automated scoring. A’s risk-allocation argument—deployers should bear uncertainty rather than rejected applicants—remained the stronger live route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s cheap-preapproval question exposed a real evidential gap, but A’s response that an examinable system is not necessarily examined, combined with the difficulty of post-rejection challenges, most effectively answered B’s existing-law defense.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side A better defended the motion as written: heavily restricting unvalidated facial, vocal, and personality inference is not banning useful automation. A answered the cost and small-employer pressure by placing validation on vendors and preserving structured interviews, work samples, and validated scoring. B showed compliance costs and the weakness of human judgment, but never adequately answered why rejected applicants should bear the burden of discovering and challenging an opaque tool when it is merely examinable rather than actually examined.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A's rebuttal and closing turn on burden timing: B's admission that upstream enforcement changes who bears the risk became A's strongest argument that sellers and employers, not applicants, should prove fitness before deployment.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO won by making the decisive clash about risk allocation: whether unvalidated, opaque assessments may affect applicants first and be challenged later. CON’s cost and existing-law arguments were strong, but they never fully neutralized PRO’s core point that “examinable” does not mean “examined,” especially when rejected applicants lack access to the model or reason for rejection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s turn against CON’s examinability advantage: an automated system may be testable, but without upstream duties it may never be tested before candidates are harmed.
