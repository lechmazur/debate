### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO wins because it built a clear, workable model early and answered CON's main objections. That model was a personalized, revisable total based on stated needs, with components itemized and included extras priced at zero. CON raised legitimate concerns about preference instability, seat scarcity, and "bundling" optional extras. PRO neutralized each of them in turn: - **Bundling:** Pricing a stated need is information, not obligation. - **Instability:** Selections are adjustable, so families can change them and compare again. - **Scarcity:** Base fares face the same volatility. CON itself conceded this in B4.1 ("The same volatility affects base fares"), which undercut its availability attack. CON's alternative also involves fee schedules plus a calculator. PRO effectively recast that as the same calculation, only pushed onto households after an incomplete ranking has already steered them. CON's closing dilemma was weak on two counts: - It claimed an all-in total makes a bag-inclusive fare "appear to cost as much as" one that charges extra. This misdescribes PRO's model, which PRO had explicitly addressed (included bag = zero added cost). - Its claim that carrier choice hinges only on base fares and mandatory fees was largely asserted. PRO's opening example directly contests it. PRO's answers to the pressure questions were direct and substantive. CON's A2 answer was vaguer and did not explain why an optional calculator suffices if base-fare ranking can invert the true order.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3 versus B3.2 was the most decisive exchange. CON asked why differently bundled totals should be comparable rather than letting families apply the same choices to each base fare. PRO replied that applying the same choices to each fare is exactly the calculation the motion requires sellers to display, with included items counted as zero. That turned CON's own preferred method into PRO's policy and left CON's closing dilemma resting on a misreading of PRO's model.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won solidly by defending a clear, user-configured upfront pricing model and systematically neutralizing Side B's objections. Side A showed that asking families once for revisable baggage and seating needs prevents misleading base-fare rank reversals without forcing anyone to buy extras, co-opted Side B's optional-calculator counterplan to prove technical feasibility, and directly answered Side B's strongest point on price-dependent preferences by showing that itemized upfront totals make tradeoffs easier to adjust. Side B raised thoughtful early friction points, but its late speeches repeatedly attacked a strawman of site-imposed bundles and closed on a flawed false dilemma.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Q1 ([A3.1]) and follow-up in [A4.1]-[A4.2] decisively dismantled Side B's seat-scarcity objection ([B2.4]-[B2.5]) by showing that base fares also sell out dynamically without reservations, forcing Side B to concede in [B4.1] that the same volatility affects base-fare snapshots, while [A4.3] neatly turned Side B's Q2 ([B3.2]) by pointing out that applying uniform family choices across base fares is the exact calculation Side A requires sellers to display.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B built the cleaner path to victory: a personalized, itemized, revisable total prices the same family requirements without making extras compulsory. B directly answered the availability and comparability objections, while showing that A’s optional calculator and checkout total leave the initial ranking distorted. A raised legitimate concerns about preference uncertainty and provisional prices, but increasingly repeated a false include-or-exclude dilemma rather than answering B’s clarified mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: applying the same baggage and seating requirements across airlines is precisely the calculation B wants displayed, while scarcity affects base fares too and can be handled through current availability, labeling, and refreshing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined and engaged the real clash: whether a seller-computed total should be the default basis for comparison. PRO had a clear causal story. A low bare fare anchors families on a ranking that can reverse once foreseeable bag and seat costs are added. Its Q1 pressed fairly on whether CON's alternative leaves that anchor intact, and CON's answer ("nothing guarantees") was somewhat soft. CON nonetheless built the cleaner path to victory. It conceded the common ground early: no hidden mandatory fees, and fees shown beside the fare. That isolated the disputed mandate as an assumed basket that must precede comparison. CON then pressed a strong point that preferences are often outcomes of comparison, illustrated by adjacent seats versus parent-child pairs. PRO responded by drifting toward multiple labeled scenarios, ranges and "starting prices." CON plausibly argued that this either collapses into its own calculator approach or reinstates a default anchor. It also argued that this falls short of the motion's literal "full practical cost." CON also exploited PRO's availability answer, which required marking flights unavailable while rejecting pre-comparison verification. PRO's closing only partly patched this. CON's "arithmetic versus choosing what belongs in the sum" framing crystallized the decision more memorably. PRO's ranking-error point stayed partially live, so the edge is real but narrow.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 [B4.4]–[B4.6] was the most decisive exchange. It turned PRO's A1 against itself: a flight cannot be marked unavailable without verification. It then reframed the whole clash as who chooses the basket rather than who does the arithmetic. This blunted PRO's claim that only a mandatory total corrects the ranking error.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won solidly by isolating the exact burden of the motion—mandating an assumed family basket before comparison rather than prominent same-screen fee disclosure and an immediate calculator—and proving that compulsory defaults create their own ranking distortions. While Side A pressed a credible behavioral critique of bare-fare anchoring, Side B showed that family travel choices (such as paying for four adjacent seats versus accepting two parent-child pairs) emerge from price comparison rather than precede it, closed off Side A's pivot to multiple scenarios by showing it either requires user selection anyway or preserves a distorting default, and exposed an operational contradiction in Side A's stance on seat availability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Following its pressure question in [B3.1], Side B caught a direct internal contradiction in [A4.1] during [B4.4]: Side A insisted that sites mark a flight unavailable if the stated seating scenario cannot be met while simultaneously rejecting pre-comparison seat verification as too slow, allowing Side B to show that Side A's pre-comparison ranking could not reliably distinguish affordable seating from unavailable inventory.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): B wins narrowly because its central route survived: prominent fee disclosure plus an optional calculator still permits families to rank options initially by incomplete fares, whereas labeled, adjustable baggage-and-seating scenarios address that error before ranking. A delivered the sharper critique of compulsory assumptions, especially its argument that preferences emerge through comparison, but overstate the dilemma posed by multiple scenarios and did not fully neutralize B’s comparison-based case. B also adequately distinguished current availability estimates from guaranteed seats.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s split-seating example and claim that “preferences are often outcomes of comparison” was the strongest attack on a compulsory basket. B’s adjustable, multi-scenario response blunted it and preserved its ranking-error argument, though some default-anchor risk remained.
