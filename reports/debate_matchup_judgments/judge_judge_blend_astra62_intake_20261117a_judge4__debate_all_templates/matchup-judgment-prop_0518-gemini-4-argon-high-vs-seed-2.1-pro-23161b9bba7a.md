### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a competitive but clear debate. B's core path — trade secret law and NDAs already cover confidential information, carve-out categories like "client-facing" and "strategic" swallow the workforce, and employers should pay for retention rather than confiscate careers — stayed coherent throughout and was sharpened by good weighing ("guaranteed career paralysis on innocent workers to prevent hypothetical corporate harm"). A's strongest material was the unwritten-know-how gap and the detailed guardrail system (compensation thresholds, fee-shifting, paid terms) introduced in Rebuttal 2, which directly answered B's pressure question. But B's closing landed the decisive blow: A had argued paid retention tools are affordable only for cash-heavy incumbents, then proposed mandating full pay for the entire noncompete term — a reversal that, by A's own logic, hands incumbents a paid lock-up tool startups cannot match, and A's concession that startups must recruit from a "pipeline" of junior and mid-level staff effectively concedes B's mobility point. Both sides had small reference glitches (A's "P2.3/P2.4," B's "C1.1/C3.1"), roughly a wash. B made the debate easier to decide with cleaner clash resolution and the sharper framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's closing [B5.2-B5.3]: exposing that A's own argument (garden leave unaffordable for startups) contradicted A's later guardrail (mandatory full pay for the entire noncompete term), turning A's small-firm protection argument into a case for the near-blanket restriction.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the motion's choice clear and turning Side A's own startup rationale against it. Early claims that cash-constrained firms need unpaid noncompetes because paid retention favors giants were undercut when Side A later required substantial pay during the restricted term to cure abuse and fee risk. That reversal, paired with a persuasive collapse of leftover "know-how" into either confidential information or ordinary skill, left broad exceptions looking either easily gamed or incumbent-favoring. Side A never squarely closed that contradiction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing used Side A's shift to mandatory paid noncompete terms to explode the earlier claim that unpaid restrictions are what let startups survive incumbent poaching.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A built the cleaner path: broad categories like client-facing and strategic swallow ordinary workers, litigation costs chill mobility even if clauses fail, and NDAs/trade-secret law cover genuinely confidential launch information while leaving general skill and customer choice free. B's guardrails were plausible but kept shifting from broad eligibility to narrow thresholds, and A decisively exposed that B first said paid leave is unaffordable for startups, then mandated paid noncompetes that only incumbents could afford. B's closing repeated the guardrails without resolving that contradiction or A's talent-lockup argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A's closing/Rebuttal 2 contradiction press: B's mandatory-pay and fee-shifting fix concedes unpaid noncompetes are indefensible and, by B's own startup-affordability logic, hands deep-pocketed incumbents a tool startups cannot match.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) won the decisive clashes. Its symmetry counter to B's slippery-slope argument—if future lobbyists can erode statutory thresholds, they can equally repeal a near-total ban—was never genuinely answered; B simply reasserted erosion across multiple speeches without engaging the symmetry point, which became repetitive. A also caught and weaponized a real concession in B4.3 (specialists have "individual bargaining power to negotiate tailored, consensual terms") to argue that a universal ban outlaws exactly those consensual bargains. A's trade-secret detection point ("you cannot subpoena a mistake a rival never made") was concrete and memorable, and B's answer—an "observable paper trail" of suspicious decision-making—was comparatively hand-wavy. B's strongest live route (widespread wage suppression and loophole creep) was well-framed in the opening and closing, but its overclaims ("every jurisdiction," "consistently see higher" formation) were unsupported generalizations, and A plausibly defused the small-business-formation question by noting low-capital spin-offs are exempt under carveouts. Both sides followed the Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 format cleanly. A made the debate easier to decide with cleaner weighing and sharper progression.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1, where CON answered B3.1 by noting that modern bright-line thresholds never actually existed in the states B cited, then pivoted to B's own concession that specialists have individual bargaining power—directly contradicting PRO's case for banning their consensual agreements. B never recovered from this exchange.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by showing that high, objective thresholds and garden leave can free ordinary workers without a near-total ban. It turned Side B’s concession that specialists can bargain, matched the lobbying-fatalism argument with a symmetry reply, and left the dead-end detectability problem unresolved. Side B never squarely closed those holes, so its case against broad exceptions stayed weaker than its critique of low-wage abuse.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal and close: the bargaining-power concession, the symmetric slippery slope, and the point that a rival’s avoided dead-ends leave no file or failed experiment to prove.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B narrowed the clash to whether objective compensation and garden-leave exceptions can protect ordinary workers while preserving high-level bargains.
