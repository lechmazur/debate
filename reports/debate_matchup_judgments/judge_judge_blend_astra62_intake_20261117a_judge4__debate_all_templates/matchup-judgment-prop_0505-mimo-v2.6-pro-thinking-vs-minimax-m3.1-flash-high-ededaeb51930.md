### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won by making the debate turn on one question: who decides before a commercial model absorbs private communications? CON had real live material. Its multi-party objection was strong, and so was its point that source category is not the same as privacy harm. PRO's claim that absorption is itself the harm was asserted more than proven, and CON pressed that well. PRO, however, handled the pressure more directly. It answered the multi-party question with a clear rule: third-party content needs that person's consent or must be excluded or de-identified. It then reframed the resulting exclusion as the rule working as intended. It also gave a plausible "reasonably linkable" test for identifiability. CON's answers were weaker on the decision-maker. It rejected both platform terms and consent, then fell back on vague necessity and proportionality tests applied by courts. PRO exploited that gap in its closing. CON's closing tried to answer by listing pre-ingestion gates such as licensing, audits, and regulator conditions, but these arrived late and were thin. CON's concession that buried opt-out is weak further narrowed its ground. PRO's flip of the incumbency argument and its "stacking, not rivals" framing also blunted CON's alternatives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was PRO's A1/A2 answer in A4.1–A4.5. PRO accepted that unconsented third-party information simply stays out of training, framed that as the rule doing its job, and turned CON's own Q2 into an admission that most private communications would be excluded. It then argued that every safeguard CON proposed presupposes a prior decision to ingest. CON's reply that courts would apply necessity tests left the decision-maker question unresolved.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): A narrowly wins. B established a substantial autonomy argument and directly answered the multi-party consent and identifiability questions. But B repeatedly mischaracterized A’s alternative as buried opt-out or exclusively downstream enforcement. A explicitly proposed necessity, reasonable-expectations, and proportionality standards governing processing beforehand. That defeated B’s decisive “imperfect gate versus no gate” comparison, leaving insufficient justification for making opt-in the exclusive authorization route. A’s limited development of concrete beneficial uses kept the result close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second-rebuttal answer and closing distinguished pre-processing legal conditions from remedies after leakage. This directly undercut B’s claim that every alternative safeguard presupposes ingestion and leaves authorization entirely to the data-holding firm.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side B won a clear, competitive debate by establishing a cleaner institutional decision rule and neutralizing Side A's strongest objections. Side A ran a sophisticated privacy-law critique—highlighting multi-party non-severability, blanket prospective checkboxes, and coerced consent in dependent relationships—but never proved why commercial AI needs identifiable private communications when public, licensed, and genuinely de-identified data remain available. Crucially, Side B showed that ex ante opt-in consent and downstream leakage safeguards stack rather than compete (a point Side A never refuted), flipped Side A's incumbency argument cleanly enough that Side A dropped it, and exposed that Side A's multi-factor alternative leaves the initial decision to ingest sensitive messages—even Side A's own domestic-abuse email example—with the firm rather than the speaker.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange centered on multi-party messages and Side A's employee abuse-email example ([A1.2], [A2.1], [B2.1]-[B2.3], [B4.1]-[B4.2], [A4.1]-[A4.5]). When Side A pressed that replies expose non-consenting third parties and make clearance unworkable, Side B cleanly bit the bullet—arguing that keeping unconsented or un-de-identified threads out of commercial training is the rule working as intended, just like wiretap law—while exposing that Side A's alternative could permit firms to train on the abuse email without ever asking the victim so long as no downstream leakage occurred.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined and answered the pressure questions directly in A1:/A2: format. PRO built a coherent principled case: private speech should not become a commercial training asset without authorization. It also held its ground on third parties, stating that no participant can consent for someone else. However, PRO repeatedly accepted the costs CON identified without outweighing them. It conceded that large firms might gain an advantage and that competition becomes "less convenient." It also embraced exclusion or redaction as the default for messages that name third parties. Its main answer to the harm-locus attack was "output controls cannot reverse what a model learned." That response stayed fairly abstract. PRO's closing example of inference and profile-selling was the strongest version of the point, but it arrived late. CON could also plausibly treat it as a use harm reachable by purpose limits. CON kept three consistent lines across every round: - The named harms arise from use and output, which output liability and purpose limits target directly. - The "identifiable" standard is context-dependent and leads to mass exclusion of ordinary conversation. - An opt-in regime favors incumbents that already hold consent-walled data. CON also converted PRO's own clarifications into evidence for its case. PRO's "named model family / disclosed purpose" consent became, in CON's framing, a data license. PRO's "reasonably permits identification, including context" became the unworkability point. CON had one small slip: its closing claim that a ban "cannot reverse what a model learned" muddled its own point. Even so, CON made the debate easier to decide and adapted better to the live clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's Rebuttal 2. Answering PRO's Q2, CON took PRO's own definition from [A4.3], "reasonably permits identification, including indirect combinations and context," and showed that it makes exclusion the default for ordinary third-party conversation. In the same speech, CON recast PRO's A1 consent design (a named model family, a disclosed purpose, fresh consent for material changes) as developer-drafted licensing best administered by incumbents. PRO's closing never fully dislodged either point.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): B more convincingly defended control over repurposing private communications as a privacy interest independent of disclosure. Its bounded-consent and redaction answers weakened A’s claims of inevitable blanket waivers and wholesale exclusion. A’s safeguards addressed exposure and profiling, but calling authorization “licensing” did not defeat B’s separate rationale. A retained a plausible competition objection, while B’s misdirected pressure questions and incomplete engagement with purpose limits kept the result competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B answered the informed-consent challenge with model-family-specific permission and renewed consent for materially different uses. A largely relabeled that arrangement “licensing” rather than showing why bounded permission remained uninformed. This materially weakened a central CON objection.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won solidly by outmaneuvering Side B on operational workability, regulatory comparison, and strategic execution. Rather than walking into Side B's anticipated innovation-speed objection, Side A argued that upstream training opt-in is a licensing rule rather than a substantive privacy protection, and then proved that thesis using Side B's own Turn 7 concessions. Once Side B defined identifiability contextually while barring any participant from authorizing third-party details, Side A showed that everyday conversations mentioning relatives or colleagues default to wholesale exclusion or detail-stripping redaction, while complex model-family licensing entrenches large platform incumbents. Side A also cleanly intercepted Side B's concrete harms—including silent profiling in Closing—by pairing output liability with enforceable purpose limits at the auditable point of use, whereas Side B never explained how regulators inspect model weights and forfeited offensive leverage in Turn 5 by asking two pressure questions aimed at PRO's own burdens.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In Turn 8 ([A4.2]-[A4.4]), Side A quoted Side B's own Turn 7 answers ([B4.1], [B4.3]) to turn PRO's defense into CON's proof: defining identifiability through indirect context while forbidding third-party authorization makes excluding ordinary human conversation the only compliant move, while scoping opt-in to named model families, disclosed purposes, and deletion rules creates developer-drafted licensing paperwork that only consent-walled incumbents can administer at scale.
