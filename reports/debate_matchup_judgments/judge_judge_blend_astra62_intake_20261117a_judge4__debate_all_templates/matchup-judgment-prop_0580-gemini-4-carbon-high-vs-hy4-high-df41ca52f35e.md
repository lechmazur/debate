### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B (CON) won a clear but competitive debate. B established a disciplined metric early—cost-effectiveness as durable poverty reduction per dollar, not monthly income delivery—and never lost that frame. B's answers to pressure were direct and labeled (A1/A2 format followed by both sides), and the "cash is a transfer everywhere" crystallization in the closing neatly absorbed A's best attack (that many subsidy dollars buy no extra work). A's strongest material—the reach gaps (school-age children, disabled caregivers, kin-care replacement, unaffordable co-pays, supply costs) was real and pressured B, and A's pressure questions were sharp, but B defused most of it with the capped co-pay, targeted-shortage design and the concession that some subsidy dollars are transfers while cash is one everywhere. A's opening was clipped for exceeding the word limit and leaned heavily on stacked institutional name-drops without verifiable content, which B then weaponized via the A1.1 "concession" on labor supply and measurement—a hook A never fully escaped, answering only that it "concedes nothing fatal." A's rebound double-standard turn was A's best original move and B's response (expiration of cash reveals transfer-only effect; subsidized care leaves earnings behind) held up. Both sides met format obligations; B's execution was cleaner and the weighing more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answer A1 (B4.1): conceding that not every full-time parent adds hours, then reframing leverage as job retention and the next-shift decision, and capping it with "cash pays regardless of whether care is the binding barrier"—which neutralized A's zero-extra-work attack and set up the closing line "some subsidy dollars are transfers; cash is a transfer everywhere."
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): B more convincingly compared poverty reduction under equal budgets. It showed that childcare’s earnings leverage is conditional, while cash addresses poverty beyond care-constrained households. A also repeatedly treated dependence on continuing funding as a distinctive weakness of cash without resolving the same dependence for childcare. B’s unsupported institutional name-dropping, clipped opening, and sweeping cost claims limit the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s closing sharpened the central exchange: capped copays shift provider costs onto the public budget rather than eliminate them, and subsidies for parents already working need not generate additional earnings. This directly challenged A’s “two-job dollar” rather than merely asserting cash’s flexibility.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B won the motion as written. Equivalent spending exposes childcare’s high unit cost, overhead, and miss rate for school-age children, disabled caregivers, full-time workers, and stable kin care—problems Side A narrowed but never neutralized. ### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side A won on the cleaner comparative weighing. A's "dominance" argument survived the exchange: where childcare binds, cash can pay the same bill and produce the same net-resource gain, and where childcare does not bind, cash still reduces poverty while the subsidy produces nothing. B's best counters—the phase-out arithmetic showing leakage up the income ladder and the "costs the budget zero" self-targeting reframe—were genuinely sharp, and B's pressure questions were among the best turns in the debate. But A answered both pressure questions directly with proper A1/A2 structure, conceded the middle-income leakage point ("don't pay them"), and clarified in closing that the $800 figure was a same-dollar test, defusing B's "fatal contradiction" charge. B's case leaned repeatedly on asserted multipliers ("$2,000+ wages") and suffered a persistent execution cost: numerous nonexistent reference IDs ([P1.1], [C1.4], [P5.3], etc.) misdescribing the record, including in the closing. B never squarely neutralized the point that a poverty-targeted credit need not be diluted to non-poor households, and never answered that cash paying a care bill unlocks the same paycheck multiplier B claimed exclusively for subsidies. A made the debate easier to decide; B kept it competitive with arithmetic but left the decisive dominance comparison standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's closing dominance argument ([A5.1]-[A5.4]) against B's self-targeting defense ([B5.1]-[B5.2]). B argued that families using no paid care "cost the budget zero," preserving funds for concentrated subsidies; A flipped it—such families also get zero poverty reduction from the subsidy, while cash still works for them and matches the subsidy wherever a care bill binds. B's reply that a credit "cannot detect a care invoice" and must dilute across all children was B's strongest late move, but A had already defused the dilution premise by specifying a low-income plateau with in-band withdrawal, and B's arithmetic assumed either oversized credits or universal payment that A explicitly disavowed.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): A better resolved the fixed-budget comparison: broader reach does not itself establish greater poverty reduction per dollar. Its phase-out critique and employment mechanism exposed the unresolved allocation problem in B's claim that cash could match any childcare voucher. B argued flexibility, immediate relief, and access barriers effectively, but its closing did not reconcile equivalent total spending with sufficiently large individual payments. The win remains competitive because A assumed favorable subsidy uptake, overstated inevitable credit leakage, and repeatedly used incorrect reference IDs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest rebuttal distinguished recipient-level substitution from program-level cost-effectiveness: replacing one $800 voucher with $800 cash does not establish that a credit covering a larger eligible population can provide comparable grants. B's closing “same-dollar test” clarification left that threshold-and-allocation challenge substantially unanswered.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by holding the motion to a fixed-budget cost-effectiveness test and keeping a live contradiction in Side B’s case. B never reconciled a tightly targeted credit (the $300 / $18,000 phase-out answer) with the $800 same-dollar dominance claim, and never answered dilution across older children and non-care users. A’s wage-multiplier story is optimistic, but B’s elegant directness frame does not survive equivalent spending.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3: after B answered that gradual withdrawal need not reach middle incomes and that an $800 subsidy could be matched by $800 cash, A showed those claims cannot both hold under one budget—larger credits recreate the cliffs or six-figure phase-outs B had just disclaimed.
