### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): B wins by distinguishing easier monthly choices from costless reconfiguration with unchanged discounts. Posted flexible prices and optional annual commitments answer A’s strongest concern—revenue predictability—without abandoning consumer control. A establishes a plausible churn tradeoff, but repeatedly attacks a universal, zero-tradeoff mandate the proposition does not require. Its closing assertion that few customers would voluntarily commit is unsupported. B overstates administrative savings and the certainty that subsidies disappear, but provides the stronger comparison and clearer route to satisfying the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s question about dropping a service while paying the posted higher price exposed A’s assumption that flexibility must retain bulk pricing. A answered with lost stability and cross-subsidies; B countered that voluntary commitments could preserve predictable revenue while flexible customers paid more. A never adequately established why that differentiated model would fail.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a competitive, well-argued debate by cleanly separating legitimate commitment pricing from artificial friction and trapping Side A (CON) in a central contradiction. Side A built a credible economic case around high fixed-cost content licensing, cross-subsidies, and how near-zero friction encourages tactical churn ([A1.1], [A2.2], [A4.1]). However, Side B consistently showed that easier monthly pausing and splitting does not require giving away bulk rates for free: customers who drop a service or go month-to-month simply pay posted adjusted rates, while voluntary annual discounts remain available ([B2.2], [B4.3], [B4.7]). Side B also flipped Side A's operational and forecasting critique by showing that scheduled pauses beat costly cancel-and-win-back cycles ([B4.1]), despite a premature procedural jab in [B4.5]. Crucially, Side A claimed bundle savings come from viewers "voluntarily accepting" steadier packages ([A4.3]), yet argued in [A5.1] that few would choose locked annual rates if monthly reconfiguration were easy—confirming Side B's charge ([B5.1-B5.4]) that the revenue Side A defends relies on hassle holding unwilling subscribers in place.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A3.1], Side A pressed Side B on who pays the billing, support, and forecasting costs of monthly pausing and splitting; Side B's [B4.1] flipped all three by showing a scheduled pause is a cheap database flag that avoids customer re-acquisition ads and gives return-date visibility that cancellations hide, forcing Side A back to upfront licensing in [A4.1] and setting up Side B's closing turn ([B5.1-B5.4]) of Side A's "voluntarily accepting" concession ([A4.3]).
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): A won by consistently distinguishing easier monthly flexibility from a zero-friction, zero-tradeoff mandate. Posted flexible prices and voluntary annual discounts gave A a coherent way to preserve legitimate bundle economics while removing lock-in and opacity. B raised a real content-planning and cross-subsidy concern, but its closing depended on the unsupported assumption that too few customers would voluntarily choose commitment. A answered pressure more directly and crystallized the decisive clash more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure questions exposed whether B's savings depended on hassle rather than chosen commitment. Although B later gave direct answers, A's voluntary-annual-plan response and closing use of B's own "voluntarily" framing most effectively neutralized B's economic route to victory.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): A made the stronger comparative case: transparent commitments can support discounts, while disclosure and easy cancellation address concealment directly. B answered the pressure questions but failed to substantiate its crucial claim that removing inactive subscribers’ payments would preserve or lower prices. A better explained the potential cost to steady subscribers. However, A exaggerated “easier” flexibility into a universal mandate and treated discount elimination as inevitable; those overclaims limit the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A distinguished the low technical cost of enabling pauses from the economic cost of forgone subscription revenue. That directly damaged B’s claim that flexibility costs almost nothing and that savings on retention funnels could offset lost payments.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) won solidly by separating deceptive opacity—which plain-print disclosure and one-click cancellation can fix—from the multi-month commitment that funds bundle discounts. Side A showed that flexible monthly plans already exist, explained via ex-ante screening why steady families only earn discounts by promising commitment up front, and dismantled Side B's claim that bundle discounts could survive monthly pausing, splitting, and reconfiguring without raising prices on loyal subscribers. Side B (PRO) raised relatable complaints about dark patterns and asked probing questions, but offered self-defeating economics in Rebuttal 2 and dropped Side A's decisive turns in Closing (far outweighing Side A's minor [P2.1, P2.2] reference-ID typo in Turn 4).
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A4.4]-[A4.6], Side A dismantled Side B's answers in [B4.1]-[B4.2]: after Side B admitted bundle discounts are "padded by revenue" from inactive users yet claimed losing them would lower prices by saving budget on retention funnels, Side A showed that losing padded revenue eliminates the discount subsidy, cancellation screens cost pennies next to months of lost subscriptions, and the cost of pausing is forgone revenue rather than technical overhead.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): B built the clearer comparative case: opacity can be addressed through disclosure and easy cancellation, while monthly flexibility still has a revenue cost that someone must absorb. A answered the pressure questions directly but relied on assertions that retention savings and dormant-account revenue could preserve discounts. B exposed the tension in that answer, distinguished technical pause costs from forgone subscription revenue, and kept the steady-subscriber subsidy argument live through the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal decisively separated concealed terms from commitment-based discounts, explained why steadiness must be promised in advance, and used A’s “padded by revenue” concession to challenge the claim that flexibility would leave bundle prices untouched.
