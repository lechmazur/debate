### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): PRO wins a competitive debate by staying closest to the motion and owning the decisive mechanism. Once advanced fab tools are installed, they can make both civilian and military chips, so denying the equipment is the only control that works before production starts. PRO also answered the pressure questions directly. It honestly conceded that no capability becomes impossible, which is fine because the motion only asks to "slow" military AI. It then offered an observable test for whether controls are binding, and it framed timing as the reason to weigh near-term delay over speculative future substitution. CON had real routes to victory: the commercial revenue and learning loop that funds allied tooling, the role of mature nodes in battlefield AI, and the risk that bans accelerate an adversary's substitution. Its point that defense orders cannot replicate high-volume commercial feedback was a good answer to PRO's thin "governments can backfill" claim. However, CON's case weakened in three ways: - It recast the motion as a "blanket sector ban versus targeted denial." Its preferred alternative was itself a set of export restrictions on spares, process software and expertise, which partly concedes PRO's logic. - Its answer to the installed-tool problem ("a ban cannot inspect installed tools either") misses the point that a ban prevents installation in the first place. - Its framing of "certain erosion versus speculative delay" asserted that the commercial cost was certain more than it demonstrated it. PRO's backfill claim stayed somewhat under-supported, which keeps the margin modest.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The clearest exchange came in Rebuttal 2 and the Closings, over whether end-use controls could replace denying the equipment. In its A1 answer, CON admitted that no inspection regime can stop an installed fab from making military chips. It then argued that a ban cannot inspect installed tools either. PRO's closing turned this into its central voting issue: "leverage is lost the moment the equipment is installed." That neutralized CON's targeted-alternative route more than anything CON landed against PRO's timing argument.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A (CON) built the cleaner comparative path. PRO's strongest closing point was that leverage is lost once equipment is installed, but CON directly neutralized it: a future sales ban cannot inspect already-installed tools either, while targeted controls on spares, software, and expertise can preserve leverage without amputating dual-use production. PRO also narrowed its benefit by conceding no capability becomes impossible, leaving only delayed indigenous mass production, while CON showed existing chips, mature nodes, cloud compute, and open-weight models still support deployed military AI. PRO's dynamic commercial-cost answer was thinner than CON's installed-base and R&D-feedback argument. The debate was competitive, but CON's reframing to ban-versus-targeted-alternative and its leverage rebuttal gave it the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal that a ban on future sales cannot inspect tools already installed, so PRO's leverage-lost argument does not make equipment bans more precise than targeted controls, was the most decisive exchange.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a clear but competitive debate. B's case stayed anchored to the motion as written—deny the hardest-to-replace layer, buy time, accept bounded commercial cost—while A often shifted the comparison to "ban vs. targeted alternative," which B effectively dismantled with the enforceability argument: an installed fab tool is a dual-use factory that no end-use regime can police, so denying the equipment is the only pre-production boundary. A's riposte that a ban also cannot inspect already-installed tools was evasive, since A's licensing approach would let new tools in. A landed real damage by extracting B's concession that no deployed capability becomes impossible, and the substitution-acceleration point stayed live, but B's honest A1/A2 answers (reframing the stakes as indigenous mass production of high-performance edge AI) kept a coherent route to victory. B's weighing—"profit can be replaced; lost strategic surprise cannot"—was cleaner and more tied to the motion's own "even at commercial cost" clause than A's "certain erosion vs. speculative delay." Both sides had clean format compliance and solid grounding without fake precision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B4.1's answer to A's sharpest pressure question ("which capability becomes impossible?"). B honestly conceded nothing low-end becomes impossible, then reframed the stakes as denial of indigenous mass production of power-constrained high-performance edge AI—an answer that absorbed the concession while preserving the motion's core mechanism, which A never fully broke.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): This was a close, competent exchange, and PRO (Side B) edges it on execution. PRO built a clear asymmetry frame: commercial losses are concentrated and recoverable, while adversary leading-edge capacity compounds. It also gave a concrete mechanism for why advanced fabrication matters to military AI. Prototyping is not mass deployment, and drone and swarm inference is bound by power, mass, yield and volume. Its Q2 exposed a real tension in CON's case: if mature nodes suffice, why do rivals pay sanction and smuggling costs to get advanced tools? CON's answer, "aspiration, not dependence," was thin. PRO also answered A2 directly by noting that states routinely preserve strategic industrial lines through procurement and subsidy, which blunted the "unilateral disarmament by budget constraint" line. CON's best route was persuasive and well articulated: - delay is not denial; - controls spur indigenous substitution; - lost scale and learning-by-doing hurt the allied innovation base. However, CON escalated losing adversary-market sales into a "permanent weakening" without showing why the lost revenue could not be replaced. It also never resolved the tension between calling controls leaky and calling their commercial cost major. Both sides asserted irreversibility somewhat loosely. PRO's case remained more internally coherent and better defended under pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The deciding exchange was PRO's Q2 and the answers that followed. PRO asked why rivals would chase advanced equipment if mature nodes, stolen designs and cloud access suffice. CON replied "aspiration, not dependence," which conceded that advanced compute is valued without explaining away the deployment gap. PRO then pressed that gap with the power, mass and volume constraints of drone onboard inference, which kept its link between equipment controls and military AI alive.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): B won by maintaining the stronger asymmetry: recoverable commercial losses versus irreversible military-AI capability transfer. B answered the main pressure with a concrete mechanism—leading-edge volume, power, yield, and onboard inference constraints—and turned A’s concession that rivals still seek advanced tools into evidence that mature-node workarounds are insufficient. A’s industrial-base and substitution arguments were plausible, but B persuaded that restrictions are targeted, enforce a real chokepoint, and buy a decisive countermeasure window rather than merely taxing the West.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s sharpest moment was using A’s own admission that rivals still seek advanced fabrication equipment: if mature-node chips truly sufficed, the costly sanctions risk would make no sense. B then converted that into the mass-deployment constraint—prototypes are not swarms—which A never fully neutralized.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Both sides ran clean, well-structured cases and both handled the A1/A2 pressure format properly. PRO (B) won the decisive clashes: the uranium-enrichment analogy effectively answered CON's "knowledge can't be embargoed" point by separating principles from industrial capacity, and the prototype-vs-mass-deployment distinction (power, mass, yield, volume constraints on onboard inference) directly blunted CON's mature-node substitution argument. PRO also gave a more concrete answer to the industrial-base pressure (targeted policy, allied demand, states routinely preserving strategic lines), while CON's core response—that lost scale forfeits the next generation—remained largely asserted without showing why allied demand and procurement cannot substitute. CON's best moment was reframing controls as "subsidizing the rival's catch-up," but PRO's concession-forcing observation that rivals still pursue advanced tools despite substitutes was never fully neutralized ("aspiration not dependence" was a parry, not a win). PRO's asymmetry framing—recoverable commercial loss vs. irreversible strategic transfer—made the debate easier to decide and survived the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B4.1's answer to CON's pressure on whether delay is "militarily decisive": PRO's distinction between what adversaries can prototype on mature nodes versus what they can mass-deploy given power, mass, and volume budgets gave a concrete mechanism for why denial matters, and CON's rejoinder in A4.5 that "many battlefield AI tasks tolerate older silicon" never matched that specificity.
