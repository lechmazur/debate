### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): PRO opened with a strong rehabilitation and equal-access case: petition-based sealing favors affluent youth, and exposed records drive economic exclusion. CON's opening was the more effective move, though. It conceded those harms and separated two things the motion bundles together: the child's identity and the court's reasoning. CON proposed protecting identity by default from the outset, with no petition and no fee, while keeping de-identified judicial reasoning open to scrutiny. That framing took over most of PRO's equity offense and turned the debate toward the cost of sealing the court's file at an arbitrary birthday. PRO's best line was that redaction leaks and clerical errors are permanent. CON neutralized it in three steps: - The Tuesday/Wednesday scraper point showed that sealing at eighteen does not undo earlier exposure, so the leak problem argues for earlier protection, not sealing at adulthood. - CON conceded that staff "cannot guarantee zero mistakes" and used that concession to support withholding doubtful material up front. - CON argued that a passage that is safe to publish does not become dangerous at eighteen. PRO then conceded that a bona fide inquiry suffices for supervised access. CON converted this cleanly into a discovery problem: oversight needs a visible first order to prompt any inquiry in the first place. PRO's closing weighing was solid ("oversight survives asking; a future can't survive a scar"). However, it understated how much privacy protection CON's model already provided, which made PRO's residual harm narrower than PRO framed it. CON's concrete double-counted-corroboration example made its stakes vivid without fake precision. CON made the decision easier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's B2.1–B2.2. A scraper copying a file on Tuesday does not delete it when the teen turns eighteen on Wednesday, and a judge's explanation does not become more identifying at adulthood. This reframed PRO's leak argument as support for early identity protection rather than for sealing at adulthood. PRO never fully recovered from it.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): A won a close, high-quality debate by accepting the need for automatic identity protection without petitions, then showing that adulthood supplies no reason to withdraw judicial material that reveals official reasoning but not a child’s identity. B’s irreversible-leakage and administrative-error concerns remained serious, and supervised access substantially answered the claim that sealing would eliminate oversight. But A narrowed the publication proposal to standalone, pre-vetted passages rather than bulk docket uploads and exposed the remaining weakness in B’s position: access after a bona fide inquiry facilitates investigation without preserving the public discovery that often prompts the inquiry. B’s closing largely restated the risk tradeoff, while A’s final distinction between permission to investigate and a reason to investigate cleanly resolved the central clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: the corroboration example answered how redacted records can reveal judicial error, while the response to B’s supervised-access concession showed why an unseen first order matters. A then turned B’s safe-quotation argument around—if vetted excerpts can safely appear in journalism, comparable nonidentifying passages can remain publicly available without sealing the entire file.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by turning the debate from age-based sealing to identity protection versus court accountability. Its redacted-corroboration example showed how nonidentifying records can expose judicial error, and it answered A’s redaction pressure with a plausible withhold-before-disclosure model. A’s privacy and reidentification concerns were strong, but B more cleanly neutralized them by conceding no zero-error guarantee, narrowing publication, and arguing sealing at eighteen is both too late for scraped data and unnecessary for safely public reasoning. B also kept the discovery problem live: supervised inspection helps after a question exists, but default sealing can hide the first clue that prompts scrutiny.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s pressure about redaction: it admitted staff cannot guarantee zero mistakes, argued against uploading whole dockets, and used the two-independent-accounts versus repeated-witness example to show meaningful oversight can survive identity protection.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): This was a close, high-quality debate. PRO built a clean framing: sealing is not erasure, so the burden should fall on whoever wants disclosure. It also pressed two real points well: an apprenticeship lost at nineteen cannot be recovered by a later petition, and a family's ability to pay restitution says nothing about safety. CON won by keeping the debate on the motion's weakest case, a teenager adjudicated just before eighteen for a serious offense. That pressure produced PRO's A1 answer that the docket would be sealed at eighteen even during active probation. CON then turned that admission into the centerpiece of its case. CON also blunted PRO's flagship example. Under CON's earned-sealing rule, the window-breaker would already have years of clean time by nineteen and would be sealed anyway. CON's good-faith compliance answer neutralized the payment-inequity point. PRO's workarounds were plausible but had to be built during the debate, which let CON frame them as institutions a sealing rule cannot create. These included authorized caregiving checks and outside judges approving name searches for reporters. PRO honestly conceded the roommate gap and pointed out that archived copies cut both ways. That was effective, but it did not dislodge CON's core inversion: the birthday trigger delivers fast secrecy to the freshest, least-tested cases. PRO's response amounted to a judge-made exception at disposition, which CON had already argued judges cannot reliably foresee.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q1 forced PRO to choose between conceding that conduct, not age, governs and sealing active probationers' recent burglaries. PRO candidly answered "seal the named docket at eighteen" (A4.1). CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing then weaponized that answer as the case's core inversion: the birthday trigger hides the newest risks first, while earned sealing already protects the reformed window-breaker.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): A won a close, high-quality debate by making the birthday trigger’s overbreadth the decisive comparison: it can seal recent serious offenses before any demonstrated reform, while conduct-based sealing can protect compliant youths earlier. B offered the stronger principled account of unequal petition access, irreversible dissemination, and the state’s burden, but its safeguards increasingly depended on speculative institutions and case-by-case access that unaware roommates or reporters could not readily obtain.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first pressure question forced B to concede sealing a recent burglary docket at eighteen even during active probation. A then made that concession the debate’s central inversion; B answered honestly and weighed the roommate’s lost warning against mass exposure, but never fully restored a practical safeguard for that case.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B won by making the birthday trigger’s hardest cases decisive. It forced Side A to admit that a recent serious offense could seal at eighteen even during active probation unless a narrow exception applied, then used that concession to show ordinary roommates, employers, and watchdogs would lack practical access. Side A’s principled burden-shifting and deidentified-accountability model were strong, but Side B more convincingly showed that earned sealing better ties relief to conduct while preserving public-safety and oversight access.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s pressure question on a seventeen-year-old adjudicated shortly before adulthood forced Side A’s admission that the docket would seal at eighteen absent a narrow pattern finding; Side B then turned that into the decisive point that presumptive sealing abandons ordinary third parties who cannot access sealed files.
