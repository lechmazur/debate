### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON built a consistent, disciplined framework distinguishing design-bans from enforceable outcome standards, and used it to repeatedly show that PRO's case either smuggled in unstated policy machinery (tariffs, baseline standards, transition support) not contained in the motion, or relied on that same machinery to do the actual protective work while crediting the ban itself. PRO's opening was strong (moral framing, health/environment mechanisms, EU/California examples), and its pressure question about outcome-regulation's historical failure to eliminate crates was the sharpest moment for PRO. But CON's answer—that non-enforcement defeats bans just as it defeats standards, and that PRO's own concession of needing baseline standards shows the ban is not doing independent protective work—successfully blunted that thrust and was reinforced cleanly in the closing ("that concession decides the motion"). PRO never supplied a working definition or threshold for "modest" price increases despite being pressed twice on it, leaving a live, unanswered hole in its own conditional (the motion's qualifier). CON's opening was clipped by the word-limit format note, a real but minor execution cost, since its core contentions were already stated before the cut. Overall CON's tighter comparative framing, sharper rebuttals, and more disciplined handling of uncertainty gave it the cleaner path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 reply to PRO's "floor" claim—arguing that baseline welfare standards, not the design ban, do the real protective work, and that PRO's displacement/price fixes require policy tools outside the motion—set up the framework CON used successfully through the rest of the debate, including the closing's "that concession decides the motion" line.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): B won the decisive clash: categorical design bans impose certain, regressive grocery costs while not uniquely guaranteeing welfare, health, or environmental gains once enforceable outcome standards are available. A’s vivid cruelty case and historical-enforcement pressure were real, but B repeatedly forced the concessions that those standards do the protective work, that “modest” stayed undefined, and that tariffs/transition tools were salvage add-ons rather than parts of the motion. That left A defending certain harms of confinement against a less clean justification for this particular policy package.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s standards-vs-ban sequence (esp. B2.4/B4.1 and B5.2): once A conceded baseline welfare rules are necessary, the design ban either added little beyond them or depended on the same enforcement A said had already failed.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B wins by exposing a fatal logical flaw in Side A's core regulatory mechanism. Side A argued that categorical design bans are necessary because outcome-based standards historically fail, yet simultaneously conceded that alternative cage-free systems require strict baseline welfare standards to prevent worse outcomes (like aggression or crushing). Side B brilliantly capitalized on this contradiction in the later rounds, arguing that if a government possesses the enforcement capacity to mandate those baseline standards for alternative systems, it already has the capacity to enforce outcome-based bans on severe confinement directly. If it lacks that capacity, a categorical design ban will simply be ignored. Side A's rhetorical flourish ("no crate means no crate") failed to resolve this structural paradox. Furthermore, Side B won the economic clash by correctly noting that Side A's macro-level health and environmental savings do not offset the micro-level regressive burden of food inflation at the grocery checkout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it dismantled Side A's defense of design bans by proving that PRO's conceded need for baseline welfare standards either makes the design ban redundant (if enforced) or useless (if unenforced).

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but PRO (B) more effectively controlled the central clash. B's recurring "enrichment dilemma" — that mandated space either restores the animal's basic movement (in which case it is simply a cheaper route to the same compliance the motion demands, not a genuine alternative) or it doesn't (in which case it fails to address the harm) — was never cleanly resolved by CON (A), who repeatedly asserted "CON wins on efficiency" without explaining why achieving the same functional outcome through different means isn't just the motion's own goal by another name. B also handled the affordability clash more precisely: it conceded modest costs exist but tied them to concrete mitigants (phased deadlines, targeted assistance, import equivalence standards) and correctly noted that unverifiable claims about a "permanent" EU price shock don't establish that the increase exceeds "modest." A's repeated appeal to the EU egg-price example, while plausible background knowledge, was used with more confidence than its evidentiary weight justified, and A never squarely answered B's Pressure Question about what price threshold would justify indefinite confinement, instead shifting the burden back to PRO. B's Q1/A1 and Q2/A2 formatting was followed correctly and used to sharpen rather than dodge the clash, while A's answers, though formatted correctly, were more evasive on substance. B's closing crystallized the comparison more cleanly: weigh certain ongoing bodily restriction against manageable, mitigable economic risk. A's closing largely repeated the EU anecdote and the "smarter policy" framing without resolving the enrichment dilemma. B's opening being clipped is a real execution cost, but the substantive case was still established before the cut, and B's subsequent rounds more than compensated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's repeated "enrichment dilemma" framing (B2.5, reinforced in B4.5 and crystallized in B5.2) was the most decisive exchange: it trapped CON's central alternative into either conceding compliance with the motion or failing to address the targeted harm, and CON never found a clean escape from that bind.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): A kept the motion’s limited target and “modest cost” frame intact, forced B into the enrichment dilemma, and answered pressure on assistance and imports with workable policy design. B raised real regressive and farm-disruption risks but never cleanly showed that keeping the worst confinement is preferable to manageable mitigation, and its either-way enrichment claim only worked on one horn.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s enrichment dilemma (rebuttal 1 through closing): if mandated space restores wing-spread/turn-around, B has described compliance with the motion’s target; if not, enrichment fails the central harm—so incrementalism is no excuse to keep extreme systems lawful.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A built a much stronger and logically coherent case, decisively winning the clash over alternatives. Side A successfully trapped Side B on the issue of "enrichment": if enrichment allows basic movement, it is no longer the extreme confinement targeted by the motion; if it does not, it fails to solve the core harm. Side B fell into this trap in Rebuttal 2, bizarrely claiming they still win on efficiency even if their alternative fails to solve the central harm. Side A capitalized on this logical misstep in the closing. Furthermore, Side A effectively handled the economic arguments by pointing out that the motion stipulates "modest" increases and that administrative lag in assistance is a design problem, not a justification for permanent cruelty. Side B's arguments were competent but ultimately undermined by their own contradictory weighing of the enrichment alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's "enrichment" alternative, exposing the logical flaw in Side B's Rebuttal 2 where B claimed to win on efficiency even if enrichment failed to restore basic animal movement.
