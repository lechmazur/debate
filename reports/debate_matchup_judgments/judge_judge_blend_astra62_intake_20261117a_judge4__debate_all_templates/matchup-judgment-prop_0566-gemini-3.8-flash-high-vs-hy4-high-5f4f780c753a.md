### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) adapted better across the rounds and narrowed the dispute to the cases that decide it. It pushed PRO to define "sharply limit," and PRO's answer, ending lineage and wealth as operative criteria, let CON attack the motion as overbroad. That attack centered on tuition-dependent colleges, where PRO's MIT/Caltech/UC examples do not apply. CON also landed the "visible tie vs. invisibly laundered privilege" comparison. It drew on PRO's own admission that the qualified pool is huge to argue that no applicant naturally owns the marginal seat. Its A1 answer was concrete and directly met PRO's auction challenge: a fixed tie-breaker, an academic floor, no weighting by the size of the gift, and recusals. PRO had a real moral core. Its A2 distinction holds that grades and essays measure the applicant's own developed competence, while ancestry measures the parents. That point was genuinely good. However, PRO repeated the MIT/Caltech and "velvet rope" lines without development. It never engaged the tuition-dependent-college point or the worry that influence would migrate into "fit" and interviews. It cited nonexistent reference IDs ([C1.5], [C2.4]). Its closing was largely a recap that ignored CON's auction answer. CON's partial endorsement of capped preferences left some definitional vulnerability. PRO only lightly exploited it with the "concession" charge, which CON rebutted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1, CON's answer to PRO's Q1 on what stops a transparent auction. CON specified a published fixed tie-breaker that is not weighted by gift size, sits behind an independent academic floor, and is paired with donor recusals. This defused PRO's strongest principled attack, the slippery slope to selling seats. PRO never replied to it in Rebuttal 2 or the closing.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): A made the stronger comparative case, directly answering both pressure questions while B explicitly chose elimination without adequately weighing regulated preferences’ potential benefits for aid and capacity. B convincingly distinguished applicant competence from parental status, but its closing repeated that principle rather than addressing A’s institutional-benefit rationale. A’s insufficiently established predictions of funding losses, and its partial overlap with “sharp limits,” keep the victory competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s fixed, published preference—with no greater advantage for larger gifts and no admission guarantee—substantially answered B’s highest-bidder challenge. B’s closing did not engage those safeguards, leaving its characterization of bounded preferences as an auction overstated.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by making the live choice sharper and answering the hardest pressure. It converted the zero-sum claim into a tie-breaker dispute, then answered the auction objection with a real limit: an academic floor, a published non-price-weighted preference, no gift guarantee, and recusal. Side B kept a strong student-versus-parents distinction and a memorable legitimacy frame, but treated corruption as self-proving, repeated that frame instead of neutralizing the capacity tradeoff, and never showed why those constraints still amount to an open auction. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins clearly. It identified the missing link in CON's case: universities can value money and loyalty without paying for either through admissions advantages. It then pressed a clean dilemma. If the preference is too small to change outcomes, limits cost nothing. If it does change outcomes, a stronger applicant is displaced by lineage or wealth. CON never escaped this. Its answer that the preference is a "pragmatic tie-breaker" effectively conceded that the deciding factor sits outside the applicant's own work. On the main CON route, the chain from fewer legacy and donor preferences to fewer gifts to cuts in need-based aid, PRO demanded a non-speculative mechanism. CON offered only asserted "donor psychology" and never showed that naming, scholarships and cultivation would fail to sustain giving. PRO answered both pressure questions directly with A1/A2. CON's answers were also labeled but largely reasserted its framing. CON's closing was mostly recap built on "moral purity" and "starving the engine" language, without new weighing. PRO's weighing was crisper and better adapted across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing (B5.1–B5.2) turned CON's own "donor psychology and capital mobility" answer [A4.2] against it. If gifts continue under recognition and cultivation, there is no aid crisis. If capital leaves unless a child gets an admissions plus, the gift was conditional on access all along, which is exactly what the motion targets. This reinforced the earlier dilemma [B2.4/B4.6] that CON never squarely answered.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): B established the cleaner comparison: a direct advantage based on family status versus a contingent fundraising benefit. Crucially, B distinguished limiting admissions preferences from severing alumni relationships. A supplied a plausible donor-mobility mechanism, but repeatedly inflated it into indispensable funding and inevitable aid cuts without establishing necessity or magnitude. Both sides answered the pressure questions in the required format. B did not fully resolve the hypothetical tradeoff if aid genuinely declined, which keeps this a competitive rather than dominant win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest rebuttal was that academic qualification does not settle allocation among qualified applicants: a modest preference can still determine who loses the final seat. A answered with collective institutional benefits, but did not sufficiently establish why those benefits required admissions leverage rather than other forms of recognition and cultivation.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by forcing a cleaner decision rule. It showed that a preference cannot be both too small to matter and indispensable to fundraising, and that the seat—not gratitude, naming, or cultivation—was the disputed price. Side A never escaped that dilemma: its donor-psychology chain stayed asserted, and calling applicant comparisons “hair-splitting” conceded that lineage or a check, not the student’s own work, decides the last seat. B answered the low-income pressure directly without accepting a false causal premise; A’s cross-subsidy case remained the weaker link.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s reinforcement of the dilemma in Rebuttal 2 was decisive: if the plus does not change outcomes, sharp limits cost nothing; if it does, CON is selling the last seat among qualified applicants, and no non-speculative path from lost tip to aid cuts was supplied.
