### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the more durable path to victory. Its core claim was that competitive pressure on nonparticipants survives any safety improvement, so licensing cannot cure the central harm. CON pressed autonomy and the "proves too much" analogy (tutoring, orthodontics) effectively. PRO answered with a stated line: persistent genetic-functioning changes combined with positional pressure, for non-medical ends. PRO also answered honestly, conceding in A1 that a ban restricts adult options while explaining that leakage is an enforcement limit, not a contradiction. CON's best moment was its R2 answer offering employment law as a remedy for coercion. PRO's closing rebutted it decisively: enhanced strength is genuine job-related ability, so anti-discrimination rules do not protect people who refuse. CON's closing simply relabeled this as a "hiring practice" and asserted that prohibition leaves the pressure "fully intact." That ignored PRO's live point that a ban limits normalization and exposure. On total harm, CON asserted exposure×risk but never showed that prohibition fails to reduce users. Its "universal availability" claim also shifted to "price reduction" under pressure. PRO was more disciplined and more comparative throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing response to CON's employment-law safeguard was the decisive exchange. Because enhanced strength is genuine job-related ability, employers can lawfully select enhanced applicants without demanding edits or inspecting records. That left CON's anti-coercion remedy unable to protect refusers. CON's reply, recasting this as a mere hiring practice, did not neutralize the point.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): B wins because PRO resolved the decisive third-party-pressure clash more cleanly. CON's proposed employment-law safeguard collapses once enhanced strength is treated as actual job-related ability: an employer need not demand or detect editing, only prefer the stronger applicant, so refusers still face the benchmark. PRO also answered CON's black-market turn by comparing total exposure and risk, showing legalization can make refusal more costly even if individual transactions are safer. CON's autonomy argument remained live but was not enough to outweigh that unresolved coercion harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal that enhanced strength is actual ability, so CON's anti-discrimination/privacy remedy does not protect refusal—the employer can simply select the stronger applicant without requiring or inspecting genetic status—was the most decisive exchange.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by separating formal consent from meaningful freedom to refuse and by sustaining a clear comparison: a normalized market expands positional pressure and exposure, while prohibition only needs to deny mainstream legitimacy. Side A defended regulated transactions and autonomy, but never squarely answered how privacy or hiring rules help when enhanced strength itself counts as job-related ability, nor proved illicit use would create equal total harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing rebuttal was decisive: A's employment-law safeguard protects secrecy, not refusal, because an employer can simply select the stronger applicant without demanding an edit or inspecting records.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins by framing the debate early and holding that frame: the choice is a categorical ban versus stringent regulation. Under that frame, each of PRO's risk-based harms (irreversibility, heritability, coercion, black markets) supports a targeted restriction rather than a total ban. CON also caught a factual slip. PRO said edits are "passed down" right after accepting the somatic/heritable distinction. CON's pressure questions then produced the key concession. PRO said it would ban even a non-heritable edit no riskier than cosmetic surgery, and it conceded that cosmetic licensing need not declare unaltered bodies deficient. That moved PRO's whole case onto an expressive or symbolic harm: the claim that a license certifies the baseline as deficient. CON showed that this harm was asserted rather than established. The features PRO cited (naming a target, specifying a result, altering living tissue) also apply to surgery. PRO's fallback to "inheritable biology" did not fit the non-heritable cases under discussion. PRO answered honestly and stayed coherent, which is to its credit. Its closing mostly restated that regulators "cannot" be neutral without demonstrating why. CON's closing was the cleaner way to decide the debate: PRO's ban adds a restriction beyond protection that it never justified.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's Q1/Q2 followed by A4.4–A4.5. PRO admitted it would ban even an edit as safe as cosmetic surgery, and it conceded that surgery licensing need not condemn unaltered bodies. CON then showed that PRO's attempt to distinguish genetic licensing collapsed, because surgery shares the cited features and the edits at issue were non-heritable. That left PRO's ban resting on an unproven symbolic claim.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) gave the cleaner path to victory by shifting the debate from a vague safety dispute to the precise question of whether even a safe, non-heritable adult edit must be banned. PRO ultimately conceded it would prohibit even that, so its case had to rest on the claim that licensing itself declares unenhanced bodies deficient. A attacked that symbolic claim effectively: PRO admitted cosmetic surgery licensing need not carry that meaning, yet surgery also names a target, specifies a result, and alters living biology. PRO never adequately explained why genetic technique alone creates the alleged clinical deficit, especially once heritability was excluded. Risk and coercion concerns supported regulation rather than strict prohibition. B was rhetorically sharp but became increasingly reliant on an asserted social meaning rather than a demonstrated harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal turned PRO's concession into a decisive burden: once PRO admitted it would ban a non-heritable edit no riskier than accepted cosmetic surgery, safety and inheritance concerns no longer justified prohibition. A then used PRO's own concession about cosmetic surgery to undercut the remaining symbolic-deficit argument, exposing a contradiction PRO's closing never repaired.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A sustained the decisive distinction between stringent regulation and strict prohibition. After Side B conceded it would ban even a safe, non-heritable edit, its case rested on licensing supposedly declaring bodies deficient; Side A showed that claim conflicts with Side B's concession on cosmetic surgery and relies on inheritable harms excluded from the example.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it answered both pressure questions directly, then used Side B's answers to show safety and inheritance no longer justified the ban, leaving only an unsupported symbolic distinction between genetic and surgical licensing.
