### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): A (PRO) wins a competitive debate. Its harm-asymmetry frame held up throughout: a checkpointed, forecast-driven training pause delays work, while curtailing physical industry spoils batches and costs workers wages. B's strongest route was "knowing versus guessing," the claim that technology-neutral bidding reveals which load is cheapest to curtail on a given day. That route was substantially blunted by A's layering answer. Cheap, delayable factory flexibility already responds to wholesale prices and voluntary demand response, so the mandate only governs who yields once acute scarcity remains. B never squarely engaged that layering point. A also answered B's classification question concretely with a meter-level firm allowance in interconnection tariffs. A's wage-externality point exposed a real limit of bid signals. B's "double-count" reply was shaky, and A exploited it in closing by noting that lost margin excludes payroll already saved. B's wage-conditioned demand-response proposal partly answered this, though it arrived late. On the technical side, B was correct that in-flight work between checkpoints is lost, but A had already shown that cost to be minor. B's relocation argument was weakened by A's point that firms fleeing to grids prone to unscheduled blackouts would contradict B's own stress on how costly interruptions are. B was disciplined and made a coherent market-design case. However, A did more to neutralize B's live routes than B did to A's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Two exchanges did the most work for A. First, A2 in A4.2 and A5.2 argued that cheap factory flexibility already exits via prices and voluntary demand response before the mandate triggers. Second, A argued that bids omit workers' lost wages because firm margin is net of payroll. Together these undercut B's core claim that a category mandate overrides better market information. B's double-count reply in B4.2 misread the externality, and its wage-conditioning fix in B4.3 was a partial patch rather than a refutation.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B built the cleaner path by showing peak curtailment is foreseeable and checkpointable, softening Side A’s restart-harm story, and by exposing a real market failure: factory bids do not internalize furloughed workers’ lost wages. Side A’s knowing-versus-guessing and neutral demand-response case remained live, but B’s meter-floor classification and damage-asymmetry arguments answered the main pressures more directly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s exploitation of A’s “clean checkpoint” concession, paired with the point that a factory’s bid excludes workers’ erased wages, was the most decisive exchange.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): B won the decisive clash: at a residual peak, after ordinary prices have already cleared cheap flexibility, willingness to pay still tracks deep pockets and omitted worker losses rather than social harm. A never closed that wedge; paying the factory or adding wage conditions still lets software rents buy priority, which is what the motion rejects. A stayed live on checkpoint spacing, within-category variation, and “knowing versus guessing,” and B slightly overclaimed a costless pause, but those points did not outweigh physical irreversibility, ratepayer peaker costs, and adaptation by the more flexible load.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s sequencing answer—that cheap factory flexibility already yields to price before any mandate binds—paired with the closing correction that factory margin nets out saved payroll, so the bid prices workers’ lost wages at zero.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): PRO opened with a clean, intuitive case: training checkpoints, so the most deferrable load should wait. CON reframed the motion accurately. The real comparison is AI versus other discretionary loads, not AI versus hospitals. Within that comparison, CON argued that the same megawatts are shed either way, so the question is only who sheds them, and priced interruptible tariffs pick the cheapest volunteers. PRO's best adaptations were the meter-level cap and counting diesel toward facility load. Its "certainty" pivot also kept it competitive. But CON landed three things PRO never fully neutralized. First, binding interruptible contracts with breaker control provide guaranteed relief, with AI kept as a backstop before households. Second, A4.2's fixed pre-season reduction sits in tension with A4.3's smaller cut on inference-heavy days. Third, the ratemaking point that barring firm service forfeits the firm charges that fund grid upgrades. PRO's closing even conceded that firm charges fund wires. CON's mechanical claims about synchronous training were somewhat confident, but they were plausible. Overall CON made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.3 was the most decisive exchange. It exposed the tension between PRO's fixed "set reduction" and its smaller "that day" cut for inference-heavy sites. That forced PRO into a dilemma: lose the dependable megawatt block it relied on for certainty, or throttle live services. PRO's closing never resolved this.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): A built the cleaner path by turning B's certainty standard against it. Pre-contracted, breaker-controlled interruptible loads provide guaranteed peak relief, with AI curtailment as a backstop before residential harm, so priced priority does not gamble on voluntary bids. A also exposed a live contradiction in B's mandate: the cut cannot be both a fixed plannable block and smaller on inference-heavy days. B never resolved that tension or A's point that demoting AI to interruptible status forfeits firm demand charges and shifts grid costs to households.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive, especially A4.1 and A4.3: it showed interruptible relief can be firm rather than voluntary, then caught B between a set pre-season reduction and smaller day-of cuts for inference-heavy campuses.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that binding interruptible contracts plus an existing emergency shed order already secure peak relief before homes are touched, while a training-only mandate cannot simultaneously guarantee a plannable megawatt block and protect live inference on shared campuses. Side B’s certainty case and anti-buyout intuition were clear, but its later compliance design left a live contradiction between fixed pre-season cuts and day-of mix adjustments that A pressed and B never squared.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 contradiction: a training cut cannot be both a pre-set plannable reduction and smaller whenever daytime inference surges, so B cannot keep both certainty and the promise to spare live services.
