### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): PRO set a clear criterion early: liability should follow preventable control and access to evidence. PRO paired it with a bounded remedy, a rebuttable presumption limited to sidewalks and clearly restricted zones, which blunted CON's overbreadth attacks. PRO then adapted well across rounds. It distinguished rental cars and bike shops through per-ride geofencing, remote throttling, and telemetry. It answered the accountability worry by framing the operator as an added defendant rather than a substitute. It also turned CON's own premise that transient riders are rarely pursued into proof that rider accountability is already thin. CON's strongest material was the over-throttling and market-exit risk, plus its litigation-incentive point about abandoning riders as targets. That material stayed somewhat live but was mostly asserted rather than developed. CON's answer to PRO's Q1, on who should bear the uncertainty, sidestepped the burden question. Its Q2 answer, that mandatory fleet insurance attaches to the vehicle regardless of rider identity, was confidently asserted without grounding. PRO also turned it effectively: the answer makes the operator the gateway to recovery while excusing it from prevention duties. CON's '10 to 15 feet' GPS figure and its absolute claims ('never chase,' 'always') read as brittle precision. Overall, PRO made the decisive clash clearer and resolved more of the live pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing turn on CON's insurance answer was the most decisive exchange. CON had argued that coverage attaches to the fleet vehicle rather than to a named rider. PRO reframed this as an admission that the operator already sits at the center of claims and holds the decisive data. That reframing made the burden-shifting presumption look natural. It also exposed CON's 'coverage without fault' model as excusing the operator from prevention duties.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A built a coherent, proportionate case around a rebuttable presumption tied to ongoing operational control and asymmetric access to trip data, while cleanly answering Side B's strongest challenges—distinguishing rental cars and bike shops through per-ride telemetry and geofencing, and weighing residual GPS and litigation uncertainty against externalizing physical injury onto unconsenting pedestrians. Side B raised credible early concerns about downtown GPS drift and hindsight tort chill, but faltered in the second half by repeating refuted claims verbatim and staking its core defense on an unsupported, internally contradictory claim that mandatory fleet insurance already guarantees pedestrian recovery even in unidentified hit-and-run sidewalk crashes—which Side A neatly exposed in closing as putting the operator at the center of claims while excusing it from prevention.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A4.3], Side A decisively turned Side B's moral-hazard argument ([B2.3], [B3.2]): by pointing out that Side B's own premise—that claimants rarely recover from transient, uninsured riders—proved rider financial accountability is already illusory in the status quo, whereas holding operators presumptively liable gives the company and its insurer both the incentive and the exclusive account logs needed to ban offenders or seek contribution. Side B ignored this turn and repeated its flawed point about plaintiffs' attorneys chasing riders for contribution in [B4.3] and [B5.2].
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): A won by defining a narrower rebuttable presumption tied to operator control and data, answering the overbreadth and accountability objections, and turning B's insurance argument into proof that the operator is already the practical gateway to recovery. B remained clear on rider choice, geofence uncertainty, and access costs, but leaned on broad insurance claims and did not fully answer why uncertainty should not fall on the evidence-holding operator.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal and closing used B's fleet-insurance answer to show the operator controls identification, logs, and coverage, then pressed that compliance uncertainty should burden the operator rather than an injured pedestrian.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): PRO opened with a solid control-and-incentives case: firms set geofences, speed governors and fleet density, so they should share costs. CON built a clear fault-and-causation frame and steadily narrowed the clash to one question: what triggers the extra liability? CON's pressure questions forced the decisive moment. PRO answered honestly. A1 conceded that a compliant operator with working safeguards should not be primarily liable. A2 defined the standard as "ordinary, reasonable care" when a company failure contributes to the injury. CON then turned this into a clean dilemma. Either PRO is relabeling existing negligence, which is not "more" responsibility, or PRO is imposing a no-fault, location-based surcharge on assets. PRO's closing never escaped that dilemma. It did not explain what concretely changes beyond current negligence law, such as a new statutory duty or a burden-shifting presumption. Instead it reasserted the incentive story and called it the "only" mechanism. PRO also leaned on unsupported sweeping claims, such as firms treating safeguards as "marketing gimmicks" and devices "routinely" running at full speed on sidewalks. CON's claims were more disciplined. CON also partially conceded PRO's strongest ground: firms that skip safeguards should pay, and pedestrian compensation can come through rider insurance and deposits. That concession neutralized PRO's compensation argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4.4–B4.5) was the most decisive exchange. It seized PRO's A1/A2 answers, showing that PRO conceded compliant firms should not be primarily liable and that PRO's own standard was ordinary negligence. It then argued that PRO had either abandoned the motion's call for "more responsibility" or needed an unjustified location-based trigger. PRO never answered this squarely.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side A opened with a persuasive real-world intuition about real-time fleet control and uncompensated pedestrian injuries, and landed a sharp early rebuttal against the passive car-rental analogy in [A2.1]. However, Side B took decisive control in the middle and late rounds. Using pointed pressure questions, Side B forced Side A to concede in [A4.1]-[A4.2] that compliant operators should not be primarily liable for rider evasion and that its proposed rule is ordinary reasonable care when corporate failure contributes, then locked Side A into a fatal fork between relabeling fault-based negligence (which still requires proving breach and leaves victims of compliant-device evasion unpaid) and imposing no-fault location liability. Side B also cleanly refuted Side A's overstatement that walking-speed caps prevent serious injury ([B2.5]) and advanced targeted alternatives—audited city safety mandates paired with rider insurance or deposits at unlock ([B2.2], [B4.7])—that Side A dismissed without substantive refutation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pressure questions ([B3.1]-[B3.2]) and follow-up in [B4.4] and [B5.1]-[B5.4] formed the decisive exchange: by asking whether a compliant operator with audited safeguards would still owe damages, Side B drew out Side A's [A4.1]-[A4.2] concession to an "ordinary, reasonable care" standard, then exposed the contradiction with Side A's Closing complaint ([A5.1]) against requiring proof of a specific company failure.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B won by crystallizing the decisive legal clash: PRO’s “ordinary reasonable care” standard, after conceding that a fully compliant operator would not be primarily liable, looked like existing negligence law rather than the “more responsibility” the motion required. B cleanly forced the dilemma that PRO either offered relabeled negligence or an unjustified location-based surcharge, while A never fully articulated a nonredundant legal trigger. A’s control, incentive, and victim-compensation case was strong, but B’s rebuttals and closing made the burden problem clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 was the decisive exchange: it answered A1/A2 directly, accepted fault-based liability, then used A’s own concession about compliant operators to argue that PRO had retreated from “more responsibility” into ordinary negligence.
