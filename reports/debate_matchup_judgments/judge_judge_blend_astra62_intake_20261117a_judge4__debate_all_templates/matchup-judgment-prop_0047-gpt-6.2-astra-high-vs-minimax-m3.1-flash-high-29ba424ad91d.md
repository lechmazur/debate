### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides executed well and honored format (direct A1/A2 answers, pointed pressure questions, mostly disciplined evidence). A won the decisive clashes: (1) the "tax turnover, not price" mechanism plus capped-price basic programs materially blunted B's distributional attack, and A conceded the residual price burden honestly rather than dodging; (2) A's pressure Q1 exposed that B's counterplan (disposal fees, producer responsibility) cannot reach upstream waste and speculative overproduction, and B's rebuttal only partially repaired this — A's closing ("a fee set to cover disposal... not what it costs a community upstream") finished the job; (3) A offered a concrete administrability fix (eight-week replacement threshold, affiliate-consolidated liability) where B mostly restated difficulty. B's strongest live route — that families outside programs pay more and the subsidy gap is unproven — was real but partially blunted by the fact that the motion itself accepts price rises, and B never weighed why uncertain gains lose to equally uncertain alternative-policy gains. B's clipped closing crystallized well but arrived at a verdict that had largely been made. A made the debate easier to decide and landed the more memorable frame ("The parent needs an affordable shirt. The retailer does not need an affordable mistake" — which B had to quote to attack).
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over A's pressure Q1: B conceded its producer-responsibility rule missed sold-then-discarded garments (B4.1-B4.2), and A's closing then showed a disposal fee either lands on households or reaches the price tag anyway without pricing upstream waste — a concession-and-finishing sequence that broke B's counterplan.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): A won a close, high-quality debate by giving the tax a workable target—rapid design turnover rather than inexpensive staples—and answering both pressure questions directly while candidly conceding residual price harm. B’s distributional and administrative objections remained serious, but its second rebuttal did not squarely answer why untaxed repeat imports could not preserve staple competition. A’s closing then exposed weaknesses in B’s preferred disposal-fee alternative, and B’s clipped closing largely reiterated uncertainty rather than neutralizing A’s mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s disposal-fee rebuttal was decisive: charging households shifts costs to the bin, charging retailers can pass costs into prices, and either approach may miss upstream resource waste from garments sold but quickly discarded. This directly damaged B’s claim that its alternative protected affordability while addressing the same harms.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by giving the proposition a workable shape: taxing rapid replacement designs rather than all cheap clothing, applying the charge to imports, exempting repeat staples, and using revenue to cap prices for subsidized basics. That narrowed the distributional harm and made the tradeoff in the motion easier to defend. Side A raised strong administrative and affordability objections, but its cleaner alternatives were partially undercut by B’s point that disposal or producer-responsibility fees do not address upstream speculative production and community costs.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B’s clearance-shirt pressure and follow-up: A moved from rejected inventory to disposal fees, but B showed that sold-then-discarded garments escape a narrow waste rule and that disposal pricing still misses the production-stage incentive to over-order, preserving PRO’s core rationale for a turnover tax.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B (CON) won a clear but competitive debate. B's core analytical challenge never got a clean answer: if fast fashion's harms are measurable, harm-based charges already rise with volume, waste, and emissions, so a separate "heavy tax" on the fashion model buys nothing; if the harms are not measurable, A's "bundle" tax cannot be verified either. A's concession that truly equal-harm lines should face equal taxation gave away the specificity of the motion. A's best surviving ground — that harm pricing alone leaves the transition to local capacity unfunded — was met by B's mismatch argument: a public benefit like supply security should be financed from the general budget, not from a levy on clothing buyers, and a tax designed to shrink its own base is a poor anchor for multiyear mill investment. A's only counter ("political reversal") was thin. Both sides answered pressure questions honestly with proper A1/A2 format, but B's answers were sharper and turned A's own questions into offense. A's execution was solid, with good structure and a reasonable rebate/allowance defense, but B made the debate easier to decide and controlled the decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.2-B4.4: B's answer that the relevant displaced supplier is the post-levy lowest bidder, not yesterday's dirty factory, plus the point that expected charges enter bids before orders are placed, dismantled A's claim that harm pricing only works "after the fact" and exposed that the fashion-model premium remains unjustified when harms are equal.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B consistently separated three issues A kept merging: pricing environmental harm, justifying local-mill subsidies, and deciding who should finance them. The equal-harm comparison, post-levy displacement test, and shrinking-tax-base critique left A defending conditional grants and rebates without adequately justifying the motion’s heavy fast-fashion levy as their funding source. A answered B’s pressure questions directly and offered sensible safeguards, but its own questions were misdirected at its own case, and its closing asserted rather than resolved the funding problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it required grants to be assessed against the supplier displaced after the levy, then showed why a tax designed to shrink its own base cannot reliably guarantee both household relief and multiyear mill investment.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): A won by keeping the debate focused on targeting: if harms can be measured, price them directly, and if local capacity is a public benefit, fund it progressively. B answered pressure honestly but could not overcome the equal-harm/additionality attacks or the tension between shrinking tax revenue and stable subsidies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s response to B’s grant-first concession: cutting factory grants first is humane but exposes that the subsidy base is unstable, especially when the tax is meant to shrink the very sales funding it.
