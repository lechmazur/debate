### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by holding the motion to a pilot, not a future permanent four-day week, and by answering Side B's structural-rest case more directly than that case answered durability, crises, or pressure symmetry. The floor-is-not-a-ceiling distinction and the point that a provisional Friday recreates commitment-signaling stayed live. Side B kept a coherent discretion-versus-structure theory, but its "successful pilots get locked in" reply conceded the guarantee and the closing mostly restated the opening instead of resolving the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A turned Side B's "successful pilots get locked in" and "crises belong to other leave" lines into concessions that the pilot itself cannot secure retention or cover the resignations that matter.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): CON won the central comparisons: a mandatory floor answers low unlimited-PTO uptake without imposing a ceiling, the same cultural pressure could undermine a Friday off, and PRO never adequately defended defending a provisional pilot rather than a permanent benefit. CON also preserved the crisis-flexibility and frontline-coverage attacks, while PRO's closing repeated the structural-rest claim without resolving durability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal and closing most decisively reframed the pilot itself as a probationary favor workers must earn through output, turning PRO's guilt-and-commitment argument against the motion and exposing “successful pilots get locked in” as reliance on a future policy.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by turning the debate toward the specific words of the motion: a pilot is provisional, and retention often hinges on durable, flexible leave during crises. B repeatedly used A’s own concessions—that crisis care belongs to other leave and that successful pilots may later be locked in—to show that A’s strongest benefits either belong to a future policy or to adjacent leave policies. A’s core argument that structural, collective rest beats discretionary unlimited PTO remained plausible, but B neutralized enough of it by arguing that a floor can create a protected minimum while preserving flexibility, and that pressure can undermine a written Friday too. B’s pressure questions exposed real burdens, B answered A’s questions directly, and B’s closing gave the cleaner voting issue: workers can count on a permanent floored entitlement more than a provisional Friday.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal was decisive: it took A’s concession that the four-day week is only a baseline and not crisis leave, then framed retention as being decided during durable, high-stakes leave needs rather than only routine weekly rest.
