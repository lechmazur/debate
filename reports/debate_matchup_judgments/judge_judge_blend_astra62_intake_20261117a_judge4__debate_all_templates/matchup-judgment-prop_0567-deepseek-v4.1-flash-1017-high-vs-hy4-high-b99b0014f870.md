### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): CON wins because it built a fork that PRO never escaped. PRO's opening justified cameras as a way to extend the public gallery. Under privacy pressure, PRO then shifted toward court-controlled, record-only footage. CON pressed this from B2.1 onward: if footage is restricted, the wider-gallery rationale disappears; if it is released, downstream control fails and the witness chill remains. PRO's reply that these are mere "distribution settings" was its best move. CON answered it directly: distribution is the case, and the witness's knowledge that a portable recording may exist is itself the chill. That made the harm causal to the camera, not an accident of later broadcasting. CON also answered PRO's Q1 cleanly with a concrete standard: necessity under compulsion. That rebutted the charge that CON's exceptional-case concession was arbitrary. PRO's safeguards answer to Q2 was reasonable but stayed vulnerable to CON's critique that sealing comes too late and that broad standing orders would swallow routine testimony. PRO's Rebuttal 2 was clipped, which cost it the end of its weighing. Its late appeal to demeanor and tone was thin and was rebutted. PRO's closing asserted that CON never identified a camera-caused harm, but CON had already done so. Overall CON narrowed the clash more effectively and tied it to "most" proceedings involving compelled private people.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B2.1 and B4.2–B4.4 against A4.1 and A5.1. CON exposed the tension between PRO's public-window justification and its record-only safeguards. CON then showed that the "distribution settings" reply either sacrifices public scrutiny or preserves the portable-image chill. PRO restated the distinction but never resolved the fork.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B more convincingly weighed expanded scrutiny against exposure imposed on compelled witnesses, while challenging A’s equation of camera restrictions with secrecy. A’s separation of recording and release was a genuine response; B overstated matters by suggesting controlled access offers no additional transparency. Nevertheless, A did not adequately explain how accessible footage avoids downstream misuse or why narrower recording benefits justify permission in most proceedings. B answered the pressure questions more directly and better defended placing the burden on camera proponents rather than ordinary witnesses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest rebuttal forced A to reconcile its wider-gallery justification with its reliance on restricted footage. A supplied distribution settings but did not fully resolve the tradeoff between meaningful public access and protection against portable exposure and witness chilling.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): CON won the central fork: PRO's opening promised a wider public gallery, but its safeguards retreated to court-controlled or record-only footage. If sealed, transcripts and appeals already provide accountability; if released, courts cannot control permanent, portable exposure and witness chill. PRO's recording-versus-publication distinction never resolved that timing and distribution problem, especially for ordinary compelled witnesses, while CON gave a coherent necessity test for exceptional public-interest trials.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 fork was decisive: PRO could not simultaneously claim public scrutiny from release and privacy from restricting release, and its suspend-or-seal safeguards remained prediction-dependent and retroactive.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined and sharp. PRO wins narrowly because it held the more defensible comparison throughout. It argued that the baseline already includes public exposure through open galleries, names in the press, sketches, and transcripts, so CON has to show that the camera adds a decisive marginal harm, not simply that harm is possible. PRO also answered both pressure questions directly. Its A1 put the protective burden on the court rather than the witness. Its A2 honestly conceded that a leaked copy cannot be erased, then moved prevention to the pre-release stage through no-download rules, no public archive, delay, redaction, and contempt. CON's strongest route was the timing argument: a witness who expects a camera may testify less candidly before any safeguard can act. It framed this memorably. However, that chilling claim stayed largely asserted rather than developed. CON also kept saying that "most" puts the burden on the vulnerable to opt out, even after PRO explicitly assigned that burden to the judge, and it kept listing jurors as chilled despite PRO's standing exclusion of jurors from the feed. CON's "use, not headcount" answer to the double-standard question was clever and partly successful. Even so, PRO's closing turn was not well answered: CON's fallback would allow cameras only in notorious cases, exactly where performance and replay pressures are greatest, while ordinary cases get no governed record. The result is a close but real PRO edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 and the closing turn decided the debate. PRO conceded that copies cannot be recalled but shifted control to pre-release distribution rules. It then noted that CON's narrow-exception fallback would put cameras exactly in the high-notoriety trials where CON's own distortion harms peak. CON's reply ("refusing irreversible exposure") reasserted its frame rather than resolving that contradiction.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): B more convincingly distinguished expanded access from meaningful accountability and explained why full footage and judicial controls cannot reliably contain permanent audiovisual amplification. A established genuine access benefits and proactive safeguards, but insufficiently answered unpredictable exposure affecting ordinary witnesses—the key challenge to permitting cameras in most trials. B’s repeated invocation of juror exposure despite A’s explicit exclusion, and its incomplete treatment of advance screening, keep this competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s “use, not headcount” answer directly defeated A’s strongest pressure question: a short clip can endanger a witness without generating informed scrutiny. Coupled with the point that available full footage does not control which excerpt circulates, this substantially weakened A’s claim that the official feed remedies clipping.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): CON's timing and asymmetry case survived: chilling and permanent clipping begin before screening, delay, or stop powers can work, while the accountability gain in ordinary trials remains speculative. PRO offered plausible safeguards and a strong baseline argument about already-public trials, but conceded post-release copies cannot be retrieved and never fully protected ordinary witnesses outside its automatic exclusions. CON therefore showed why a case-by-case exception is defensible but a default for most trials is not.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 distinction between use and headcount decisively answered PRO's alleged double standard: a small clip audience can irreversibly harm a witness, while accountability requires scrutiny of the whole proceeding rather than fragment views.
