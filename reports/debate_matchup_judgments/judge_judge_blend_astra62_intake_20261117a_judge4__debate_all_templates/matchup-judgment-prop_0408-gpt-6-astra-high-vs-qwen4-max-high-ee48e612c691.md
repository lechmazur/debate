### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side B won a tight, high-quality exchange. B's central move — separating the instructional mandate from the diploma gate — was the sharpest framing in the debate, and B sharpened it every round ("the enforceable part remains the withheld diploma"; "don't make her diploma the enforcement mechanism"). B answered A's pressure questions directly in the required format, resolved the algebra double-standard charge with the infrastructure/reuse distinction, and effectively exploited A's weaker example (the "working senior before her first paycheck"). A fought well, especially on the fairness floor, the design fixes (within existing credits, tutoring), and the closing completion-versus-exposure distinction, which was A's best answer to the gate attack. But A's example of a student the gate uniquely helps was weak, and A never fully explained what the completion requirement adds for the marginal struggling student beyond risking her credential. B's closing claim that A's question "stands unanswered" was a slight mischaracterization since A did answer it, and B's point-of-use alternative was never fully specified as a system — real costs, but not enough to offset B's cleaner comparative case. Both sides showed good epistemic discipline, using hedged background knowledge rather than fake citations.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4): answering A's pressure questions directly, B conceded that instruction could be compulsory at the point of use without conditioning graduation, then weaponized A's own admission that "withholding a diploma does not itself teach anything" — turning A's design response into the core of B's case, and closing with the algebra infrastructure/reuse answer that defused A's double-standard question.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B built the more coherent, motion-faithful case and won the decisive late exchanges. Side A wrote with impressive punch and raised real concerns about behavioral fade-out and graduation barriers, but relied on shifting, asymmetric alternatives: first assuming universal point-of-decision counseling without a clear delivery mandate, then pivoting at the end of Rebuttal 2 to mandatory school enrollment with funded teachers, which abandoned the motion's elective-or-family baseline and contradicted Side A's earlier teacher-supply and opportunity-cost critiques. Side B answered Side A's pressure questions directly, demonstrated with the gross-wage rent example how point-of-use counseling arrives too late (which Side A misread and later inaccurately claimed went unanswered), and persuasively showed why requiring supported completion rather than mere enrollment ensures schools and students actually carry the instruction through.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Asked in Q2 to name one student helped by a graduation requirement whom signing-time counseling misses, Side B cited a working senior committing to rent from gross wages before receiving her first paycheck ([B4.2]); when Side A answered that payroll onboarding reaches her "before her second rent payment" ([A4.5]), Side B cleanly closed the trap in [B5.3] by noting she had already committed to the first.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides executed cleanly with disciplined grounding and direct A1/A2 answers. Side A had the sharper rhetoric and a genuinely strong marginal-benefit defense (non-substitutability of the floor: "the lender does not average your strengths"), plus a good reframe that a required math grade is itself a graduation veto we call a floor. But Side B held the tighter burden framing all debate: PRO must justify the added graduation condition, not the benefits of instruction generally, since B's integration plan concedes assessment, ownership, and remediation. B's B4.4 undercut A's non-substitutability claim by noting course grades average too and that separate competency checks are an assessment design available to either side, and A's late incentive-based answer was thin. Crucially, B's closing crystallized an unanswered point: withholding the diploma does not withhold the loan application — the failing student signs anyway, now without the credential — which blunted A's "only one gets repaired next summer" weighing. A never squarely resolved the harm of the sanction itself. Narrow win to B on burden control and the surviving sanction critique, despite A's superior style.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's pressure Q1 (does failing the loan unit lower the math grade that already gates the diploma?) was the best trap, but Side B's B4.4 answer — that a standalone course grade can average skills just like math, and separate competency checks are a design both sides can adopt — was the most decisive exchange, dismantling A's "non-substitutable floor" argument right before A's closing.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): In a high-level debate, Side B won a clear but competitive decision by systematically exposing the institutional flaws in Side A's integration counterplan. Side A competed well by isolating the marginal graduation barrier, defending timetable savings via replaced math exercises, catching intra-course grade averaging, and pressing the dilemma of withholding diplomas from students who still fail after support. However, Side B forced Side A to progressively bolt on assessments, departmental ownership, mandatory remediation, and separate competency checks while removing the graduation enforcement that actually makes schools and seniors treat mastery as non-optional. Side B also exposed Side A's double standard on remediation staffing costs, answered the summer-wage objection with evening and online recovery routes (which Side A dropped), and convincingly showed that financial competence deserves at least the same certifiable graduation floor schools already give geometry.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In Rebuttal 2 ([B4.1]-[B4.6]), Side B answered Side A's assessed-integration model and pressure questions simultaneously: showing that embedding finance in math lets strong algebra average away loan illiteracy ("Your geometry grade does not lower your APR"), noting that un-gated remediation becomes optional in practice, and catching Side A pricing PRO's evening/online/summer recovery while treating its own mandatory remediation as free.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side A consistently forced the decisive comparison: assessed financial instruction inside existing requirements versus that instruction plus an independent graduation veto. Side B showed why financial competence matters and why it should not disappear into averages, but Side A showed detection, ownership, remediation, and even competency checks do not require withholding a diploma, and that withholding one does not prevent the student from signing a harmful loan.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: a standalone course grade can also average strong budgeting against weak borrowing, so non-substitutability is an assessment design available to integration rather than a unique benefit of a separate graduation barrier.
