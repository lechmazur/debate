### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A won the decisive clashes. B's strongest attack—that "genuinely available" is an unmeetable, veto-granting standard—was answered crisply by A's distinction between objective unavailability and personal preference, sharpened by the seizure-alert dog example that B ultimately conceded was "a problem to fix at the shelter." A's closing effectively trapped B: to make enforcement sound humane, B had to promise no trashing of belongings, no fines or arrests as first steps, and connection to actually existing supports—conceding the proposition's practical core. B's other live route (cumulative density hazards beyond individual codes) was met with hazard-code abatement, rapid interim measures, and the buffer-zone-saturation turn, and B's late "designated sites" answer to where people go was turned by A's point that herding tents onto one lot recreates the density B fears. Both sides were well-grounded with plausible examples and no fake sourcing; A's rebuttals were more direct and progressive, while B's later speeches drifted evasive and introduced new mechanisms late.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing (A5.1–A5.2) exposing B4.2's concession: B could only defend sweeps by promising shelter slots, protected belongings, and service connections—precisely the conditions the proposition requires—while dismissing the shelter's seizure-dog ban as a problem to fix later, which A framed as licensing cruelty whenever cities fail to provide those supports.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A kept the motion’s “only when” condition in focus and showed that enforcement without open beds, storage, and services merely relocates hazards and criminalizes survival, while existing safety codes and interim placements can address acute risks. Side B never fully answered where people may lawfully go tonight when capacity is absent, and its later defense of pre-announced sweeps still presupposed available supports and designated sites the challenged laws do not require.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal and closing, which rejected both a preschool veto and a preference veto, then used B’s own “already available” language to show that defensible clearance still depends on real alternatives.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the decisive clash about what anti-encampment enforcement actually authorizes when no real alternative exists. It answered A’s emergency-harm pressure with existing codes and rapidly deployable motel/storage/outreach options, clarified “genuinely available” as objective access rather than individual preference, and kept pressing A on where displaced people would go and how storage losses would be avoided. Side A’s targeted-enforcement and designated-site model was plausible, but it increasingly relied on available supports and never squarely rebutted B’s core criminalization-of-survival and storage objections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal and closing turn: after A pressed about immediate preschool hazards, B said existing codes can remove acute dangers and motels/storage/outreach can be provided quickly, then used A’s later promise of “already available” supports and no trashing/arrest as a concession that justification depends on the very pillars PRO requires.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but Side B won the decisive clash. PRO's rebuttal 2 (A4.1) conceded that cities may clear obstructions when people are directed to a "nearby, usable, legal spot to stay that night." B ruthlessly exploited this in B4.3-B4.4: a legal public spot is not "shelter, storage, and service options," so PRO's own concession defeats the proposition's "only when" standard as written. PRO's closing (A5.1) quietly substituted "a lawful place to move to" for the motion's actual standard—shelter, storage, and services—without ever squaring that gap, and never answered B's paralysis argument (B4.5) about scarce crisis placements. B also introduced a genuinely useful reframe (B2.5): non-punitive, civil, no-fine time-and-place rules that regulate hazards without criminalizing poverty, which blunted PRO's status-punishment core. PRO's best surviving route—the "string enough narrow zones together into a de facto total ban" point—was real, and B's answer (that this condemns only abusive bans, not narrow ones) was adequate but left some residual real-world concern. PRO's pressure questions were good, and both sides answered pressure directly with proper A1/A2 format. B's execution costs: the invented reference IDs [C1.4, C2.5] in B4.1, and an idealized picture of how narrow ordinances actually operate. Still, B did more to narrow the dispute and gave the cleaner reason to vote: the "only when" precondition, as written, either paralyzes urgent hazard management or collapses into PRO's weaker "somewhere lawful to exist" standard. That is a clear but competitive win for CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.3-B4.4): capturing PRO's concession that clearing an obstruction is fine if people are directed to a "nearby, usable, legal spot," then showing such a spot is not shelter/storage/services—turning PRO's own flexibility against the proposition's "only when" wording. PRO's closing never resolved this contradiction, making it the debate's decisive exchange.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B won by pinning the motion's strict "only when" wording. Side A's answers allowed narrow obstruction-clearing if a nearby lawful spot existed, which is not the required shelter, storage, and services, so those anti-encampment rules can be justified before the full package. Side A made a strong fairness case about displacement and status punishment, but never closed that gap or showed why even non-punitive buffer zones that leave other public space open must wait.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal, which treated Side A's A1/A2 concessions as fatal to "only when": a legal spot outside a buffer is not shelter-storage-services, so either narrow laws are justified without them or cities must leave ramps, hospitals, and brush occupied.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by making the proposition’s “only when” language the decisive burden and showing a plausible category of narrow, non-punitive anti-encampment rules that could be justified before full shelter, storage, and services exist. Side B’s moral case was strong, but its rebuttal conceded that cities could clear immediate obstructions if people were directed to a usable legal spot, and it never fully answered A’s point that such a spot need not satisfy the proposition’s full shelter/storage/service condition. A turned that concession into the cleaner path to victory, despite a minor reference-id slip.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was most decisive: it used B’s pressure-answer concession that targeted clearing can be acceptable if there is a nearby legal spot, then argued that this collapses the absolute “only when” standard because that legal spot is not necessarily shelter, storage, or services.
