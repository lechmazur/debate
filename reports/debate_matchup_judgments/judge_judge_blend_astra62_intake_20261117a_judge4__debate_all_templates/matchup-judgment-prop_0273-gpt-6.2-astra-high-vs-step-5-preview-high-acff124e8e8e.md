### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built the cleaner path to victory. The core clash was whether a mandated inclusive first-screen total creates a "default bundle" that buries stripped fares. B neutralized this decisively: the shopper's own selections (bags, seating needs) generate the total, and B's closing crystallized that the backpacker and parent simply run different searches — the same flight can rank first in one and fifth in the other. A kept asserting a "regulator's guessed itinerary" even after B had shown the inputs come from the user, making later CON speeches repetitive rather than responsive. A's best pressure — that adjacency cannot always be priced — was answered honestly by B's "adjacency not assured" label and the no-zero rule, converting A's false-precision charge into a workable distinction. A's fallback ("fees can be checked in seconds") conceded B's central harm: knowing which fare to check is the point of a comparison. Both sides complied with Q/A format and avoided fake sourcing; B's "fifth fare" test and closing line ("the right to reject an expensive trip at checkout is no substitute for the chance to find the cheaper one") made the stakes memorable. A was competent and rhetorically sharp but never broke B's shortlist-formation argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's pressure questions on seating (what dollar figure enters the total when adjacency is unassured?) were the sharpest CON moment, but Side B's A1 answer — show the bag-inclusive fare labeled "adjacency not assured" rather than inventing a seat price — resolved the false-precision attack and let PRO's comparison framework survive intact.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by framing the core harm around shortlist distortion rather than checkout surprise and defending a clean, shopper-driven mechanism. Because travelers specify bags and seating alongside passenger counts before comparing results, Side B neutralized Side A's repeated claim that regulators would impose one guessed bundle on everyone—a point where Side A badly stumbled in [A4.1] by claiming a backpacker's search would be governed by a parent's bundle. Side A gained real traction pressing the awkwardness of ranking flights when seat adjacency is unassured or seat inventory shifts, but Side B answered those mechanics directly in [B4.1]-[B4.5] and convincingly weighed occasional live-inventory updates against Side A's fee-page rule, which systematically misranks better-value inclusive fares even when prices are static.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B3.2], Side B asked how a parent under Side A's fee-page rule could know the fifth-listed fare is actually cheapest with a bag without rebuilding the ranking by hand. Side A conceded in [A4.2] that the parent cannot rebuild the ranking, arguing that checking the fifth fare takes seconds—allowing Side B to deliver the decisive blow in [B5.1]-[B5.2]: checking a fare may take seconds, but knowing which fare deserves inspection is the entire point of a price comparison.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): B won by making the motion concrete: shoppers specify bags and seating needs, then the first comparison screen shows the requested total. A’s seating and default-bundle pressure was real, but B’s labels, availability checks, and separate-search reply were workable enough, while A’s fee-page alternative never fully solved the problem of knowing which fare deserves inspection.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won the decisive clash. Its single, consistently developed example—parents whose bag count depends on the second bag's price—showed that any mandated "practical total" benchmark can misrank options (the $470 benchmark makes the $450 flight look $20 cheaper when it is $50 dearer for the trip these parents would actually take). PRO never squared this; its replies leaned on the assertion that "real families check bags," which is plausible but does not answer whose definition of practical governs or how a fixed benchmark avoids distorting price-sensitive comparisons. CON also exposed a real tension in B4.1, where the $400 fare is conceded complete for some parents yet branded bait a sentence later. PRO's best live route—the first screen shapes choices and bait has sorting power—was blunted by CON's counterproposal to show inclusions and itemized charges on the first screen plus one-click total recalculation, with the sharp reframe that "it is the labels, not the designation of $470 as practical, that make comparison honest." Both sides followed format cleanly (Q1/Q2 and A1/A2); PRO's execution was solid but its rebuttals repeated the "conditions of the trip" framing more than they advanced it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3–A4.5: CON pinned PRO's concession that the $400 fare is "complete" for the very parents it describes, then showed that the $70 difference appears only because someone other than the parents decided they would bring a second bag—and that the practical-total benchmark hands the rival airline an unearned price advantage on the first screen.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won decisively by taking hidden checkout fees off the table—advocating upfront display of inclusions, add-on prices, and one-click recalculation on the search screen—and attacking the exact flaw in mandating a single "full practical cost" before comparison. Using a tight $400/$450/$470 example, Side A proved that family choices like taking a second suitcase often depend on the price differences discovered during comparison. Side B wrote memorably about first-screen sorting bias, but never specified which bundle the law must mandate before user input, conceded in [B4.1] that the $400 one-bag paired-seating fare was complete for price-sensitive parents before calling it "bait" a sentence later, and had its sorting-bias impact turned against itself when Side A showed that a $470 benchmark makes a flight that is $50 cheaper in practice look $20 more expensive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A2.3]-[A2.5] and [A4.3], Side A dismantled Side B's claim that showing $470 against $450 reveals the $50 saving from packing light (pointing out it actually makes the $400 option look $20 pricier), distinguished bag counts from exogenous departure-time sorting ("a flight leaves at the same hour however the family packs"), and exposed Side B's contradiction in admitting the $400 fare was "complete" in [B4.1] while still labeling it an incomplete fragment.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by making the decisive clash about which comparison method actually serves families. A’s concrete $400/$450 example showed that a mandated “practical” total can misrank offers when bag counts depend on price, while transparent inclusion labels and one-click totals preserve comparison without imposing an arbitrary family bundle. Side B’s first-screen honesty point was strong, but it never fully solved how the required practical total would be chosen when families differ or respond to fees.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal cleanly answered the pressure questions and turned B’s concession into a liability: if the $400 one-bag fare is complete for some families, calling it bait merely because a platform assumes an extra bag shows the danger of the mandated benchmark.
