### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): This was a close, high-quality debate, and PRO wins narrowly. CON's best route was that a no-AI default lets ordinary drafts count as presumed student practice. It argued that PRO's safeguards (independent checks and protected skills) are "teacher authorization by another name," so the permissive baseline adds nothing. That line was sharp and memorable, and "a receipt for skipped practice" landed well. However, PRO repeatedly exposed that this presumption rests on a rule rather than on evidence. CON itself conceded that essays can conceal AI use under either policy. PRO's symmetry argument was never squarely answered: CON's teacher-authorized exceptions face the same questions about whose reasoning it is and how much help was given. If bounded tasks and independent checks make CON's exceptions workable, they make PRO's default workable too. PRO also held two points CON did not fully neutralize: - **Honesty incentive:** under a ban, honest disclosure becomes an admission of misconduct. - **Permission is not credit:** grades follow demonstrated skill, which dissolved CON's "prove inadequate contribution" burden. CON's reply of a "clean remedy" only applies when concealed use is detected, which weakens its evidential advantage. CON's point that independent performance is usually the objective in school was decent but only partially pressed. PRO's answers to the pressure questions were direct, and its stated-learning-objective threshold was coherent. Both sides showed good epistemic discipline and used no fake precision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4.6 symmetry argument was the most decisive exchange. CON's own teacher-authorized exceptions face exactly the same authorship and learning questions, so CON cannot treat independent checks as adequate under its policy but disqualifying under PRO's. CON's B4.4 counter ("teacher authorization by another name") was strong. Still, it did not explain why a blanket prohibition improves the evidence those checks supply.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued at a high, philosophically disciplined level, but B won the decisive clash over what a permissive default actually adds. A's own concession that citation "does not certify understanding" [A2.2] became a recurring liability that B exploited: once A admitted that unaided checks, protected practice, and stated learning objectives must do the evidentiary work, B showed that the permissive baseline is "teacher authorization by another name" [B4.4] that merely normalizes routine auditing of disclosed assistance. A's best weapon—the symmetry argument that safeguards work equally under both defaults [A4.6]—was genuinely strong and never fully dissolved, and A's honesty-route point (bans incentivize concealment) went largely unaddressed by B, which is why the margin stays narrow. But B's weighing was cleaner and better grounded in how assessment actually functions: defaults determine what submitted work counts as evidence of, and B resolved that question in favor of student authorship with a coherent limiting principle and memorable framing ("citation is a receipt for skipped practice," "start with the skill, not the tool"). A never gave an equally crisp reason why silence in a syllabus should favor tool availability over student practice. Format compliance was excellent on both sides, with direct A1/A2 answers and pointed pressure questions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 [B4.3-B4.4] was the debate's pivot: answering A's symmetry argument, B conceded citation proves nothing under either policy, then showed that A's own formulation—"permission whenever the learning requirements leave room"—requires the teacher to know in advance which skills need protection, collapsing the permissive default into B's explicit-exception model while leaving teachers to audit routine disclosed assistance. A's closing symmetry counter [A5.2-5.3] blunted but did not reverse this, leaving B's burden-and-evidence frame standing.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that a default ban does not produce better evidence of learning than permission plus transparency and targeted independent-work rules, while making honest disclosure costly and blocking non-undermining uses unless pre-cleared. Side A's burden and presumption arguments were real but answered: credit tracks demonstrated objectives, the same checks Side A accepts for exceptions work without a blanket prohibition, and a ban supplies a rule rather than proof of authorship. Side A's claim that the motion wins only if citation itself proves learning overstated what Side B needed to establish.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal, especially the symmetry point that safeguards Side A treats as workable for teacher-authorized exceptions cannot be disqualifying under a permission default that likewise withholds credit and can require independent demonstration.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined, grounded, and responsive, so this is a close call. PRO built a strong case around visibility: disclosure turns AI use into assessable evidence, bans push use underground, and detection-driven enforcement is unreliable and inequitable. CON narrowed the debate more effectively to one clean question: what happens when students comply and the teacher has not specified a boundary? CON separated integrity (honest disclosure) from instructional value (the practice actually performed), and that distinction held up throughout. CON also neutralized PRO's exclusivity claim by noting that disclosure and process evidence can coexist with a restrictive default, which leaves "permission" rather than "visibility" as the real point of difference. Under pressure, PRO's A1 answer said the process "must begin with the student's own provisional thesis." CON turned this well, arguing that a restriction, not transparency, is what protects learning in that answer. PRO's counter that compliance under a ban is an "invisible negative" was sharp and kept the debate close. However, CON's "before credit is not before lost practice" point was never fully answered; PRO's reply that it "cuts both ways" was asserted rather than shown. PRO's equity and accessibility arguments faded after CON's category-permissions response and were never pressed in later rounds. The result is a narrow but real edge to CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's B4.3–B4.4 response to PRO's A1. CON reframed PRO's "student-owned start" condition as a concession that a restriction protects learning. It then added that failing the objective "before credit" is not the same as preserving the lost practice. PRO's closing tried to turn this back with "cuts both ways" and the visibility argument, but did not fully restore its claim that a permissive default protects practice in the gaps.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly without fabricated sourcing, but CON controlled the decisive clash. CON's dilemma in B4.3/B5.3 — that PRO's "student-owned start" requirement is itself a restriction, so either restrictions protect learning or transparency merely documents its replacement — went only weakly answered. PRO's best remaining claim, that disclosure makes failure visible, was blunted by CON's concession-in-strategy at B4.1: process evidence, disclosure for authorized use, and classroom first attempts are all available under a restrictive default too, so transparency is not exclusive to permission. CON also neutralized PRO's workload and detector arguments by showing PRO needs the same checks. PRO's equity argument from the opening was never developed under pressure and faded. CON's "before credit is not before lost practice" framing survived PRO's "cuts both ways" reply. PRO executed well and kept the evidentiary comparison alive, but CON made the gap-governing question the cleaner voting issue.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3-B4.4: CON caught PRO's answer to the pressure question as a tacit concession — the "student-owned provisional thesis and outline" condition is a restriction, not citation — and paired it with the timing point that diagnosing a bypassed objective before credit does not restore the lost practice. This forced PRO onto remediation ground for the rest of the debate.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by keeping the clash on compliant behavior: permissible-by-default lets students honestly outsource thesis formation and drafting, then disclose the loss, while independent-by-default plus category permissions preserves the practice itself. B’s student-owned-start move conceded a real restriction and did not answer the gap or the “before credit is not before lost practice” objection; A also showed visibility tools are not exclusive to B’s default.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 turned B’s “process must begin with the student’s own thesis” into a concession that a restriction, not citation, protects origination, then showed disclosed outsourcing still forfeits the exercise before any remedial credit decision.
