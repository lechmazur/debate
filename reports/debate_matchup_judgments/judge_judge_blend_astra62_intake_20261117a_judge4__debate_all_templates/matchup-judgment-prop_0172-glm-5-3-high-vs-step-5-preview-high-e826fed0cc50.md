### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): This was a close, competitive debate. PRO built a strong precautionary case: animal review already handles uncertain sentience, and existing tissue governance protects donors and labs rather than the entity itself. Its "who decides?" pressure was effective. CON, however, gave the cleaner decision mechanism. It argued that animal frameworks presuppose a welfare subject, that organoids lack organismal integration, and it pressed a dilemma: either PRO means animal-specific protocols, which do not translate to a dish, or it means generic research ethics, which already exist and are not "the same frameworks." PRO's split between framework and implementation layer was a real answer. Its fly-versus-primate example helped, but it partly conceded that the animal-specific content drops away. That left the dilemma substantially live. PRO's closing also overstated CON's concession, since CON never agreed to welfare-focused review. PRO's jab that CON's marker was "invented mid-debate" was undercut because PRO also defined its own learning marker under pressure. Execution costs were roughly even. PRO's opening was clipped. Both sides cited nonexistent reference IDs: PRO used [C2.5, C2.6], and CON used [C4.1/P4.1]. CON's crystallization was sharper, so it takes a narrow edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's Rebuttal 2 response to PRO's A1. PRO had answered CON's Q1 by keeping prospective review, harm-benefit analysis, and stopping rules while dismissing housing and veterinary care as "mouse-tier" details. CON turned this into a dilemma: what remains is either generic ethics that already governs organoids, which makes the motion redundant, or welfare-specific content, which is meaningless for a cell culture. PRO's closing reply, that redundancy satisfies the motion, only partly answered this, because the motion requires the animal-experimentation framework specifically.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): B wins the central distinction between ethical frameworks and species-specific protocols. It explained how proportionate welfare review could transfer without importing cages or veterinary routines; A repeatedly insisted those details were indispensable rather than defeating that distinction. A identified existing oversight but did not adequately answer B’s distinction between protecting donors and assessing tissue welfare. B’s case was imperfect: its regulatory generalizations were sweeping, its closing overstated A’s concessions, and learning’s significance as a welfare threshold remained underdeveloped. These weaknesses keep the result competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal directly answered A’s protocol challenge with prospective harm-benefit review and stopping conditions, distinguishing the framework from mouse-specific implementation. A’s subsequent claim that B had supplied no transferable protocol—and had discarded humane endpoints—failed to engage that answer.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a high-level exchange by tethering the debate to what actually distinguishes animal experimentation frameworks and locking Side B into an inescapable dilemma. While Side B smartly separated system-level learning from synaptic plasticity and noted that donor-consent and biosafety rules do not evaluate the entity itself, Side B conceded that proof-of-concept organoids are almost certainly not yet sentient and retreated under cross-examination from concrete animal protocols to broad principles like prospective review, harm-benefit analysis, and stopping rules. Side A proved that keeping an embodied animal-welfare mandate is incoherent for unintegrated cell cultures while stripping those protocols away leaves generic governance that human-tissue and stem cell committees can already provide without diluting animal-welfare resources. Side B's closing claim that existing stem cell review satisfies the proposition effectively gave away the motion's requirement to trigger animal frameworks, and its claim that Side A invented the organismal-integration standard mid-debate ignored Side A's opening criteria.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In Turn 8, Side A cleanly neutralized Side B's sensor-and-effector organoid question by distinguishing machine-coupled telemetry from embodied behavioral agency ("A camera does not see; it records"), while simultaneously turning Side B's Turn 7 dismissal of "mouse-tier" protocols into proof that Side B had replaced animal ethics frameworks with generic research governance.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): CON wins because it held the motion to its own wording and kept narrowing the clash around one question: whether PRO was defending the "same" animal framework or a new one. PRO's "same architecture, scaled clauses" reframe was reasonable, and it scored real points with the tiered-cephalopod example and the transplant blind spot. But CON's pressure question forced PRO to name the surviving clauses. The answer showed that "replacement" had been redefined, and that analgesia and endpoints had become newly invented "capacity monitoring." CON added that the surviving clauses are generic tissue ethics that already apply, which squeezed PRO from both sides: either the machinery doesn't fit, or it already exists. PRO's A2 conceded that a hippocampal slice could qualify, and CON used this to argue that PRO's trigger is too vague to apply. PRO also never really defended rudimentary learning as a proxy for sentience beyond invoking uncertainty. CON bears real costs: two of its turns were clipped, and its closing loosely called its own transplant answer "PRO's concession." These costs keep the margin moderate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's Q1 asking PRO to "name one" surviving clause, followed by B4.5. CON showed that PRO's answer either repurposed animal-specific terms such as "replacement" or listed generic research-ethics duties already covered by tissue oversight. This turned PRO's "same architecture" defense into evidence for CON's claim that PRO was proposing a new framework under the old one's name.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): B wins the central interpretation clash: applying the same ethical framework need not mean imposing identical species-specific protocols. It defended concrete, scalable obligations and precautionary scrutiny before sentience is established. A's learning-versus-experience objection remained important, but its insistence that any adaptation creates a different framework did not defeat that case. B's underdeveloped learning threshold and unfair repetition that A requires visible distress limit the margin. A's clipped turns also cost execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B answered the clause challenge with harm–benefit review, replacement by less capacity-bearing models, refinement, and stopping criteria. A then characterized replacement as merely reducing organoid numbers, overlooking B's explicit substitution requirement. This weakened A's closing claim that no meaningful animal-review obligations survived.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a clear but competitive debate by prevailing on both the moral threshold and institutional fit. Substantively, Side A dismantled Side B's link between rudimentary learning and morally relevant experience, using ex vivo hippocampal slices and markers of valence (nociception, stress physiology, homeostatic trade-offs) to show that adaptive network encoding alone gives no plausible basis for suffering or welfare harm. Institutionally, although Side B smartly defended animal review as a tiered architecture using cephalopods and embryos, Side A pressed further to show that stripping away pain, distress endpoints, housing, and animal-replacement—and substituting newly invented "capacity monitoring and stopping criteria"—amounts to building a new bespoke framework rather than triggering the same one. Side A also cleanly turned the chimeric-transplant challenge via host-animal welfare, maintaining a clear edge despite two minor word-limit clips.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's pressure-to-rebuttal sequence from [A3.1]–[A3.2] into [A4.3]–[A4.6]: by forcing Side B in [B4.1]–[B4.3] to name which concrete animal-review clauses survive for a petri dish and what separates a learning organoid from a hippocampal slice on an electrode array, Side A exposed both that Side B's "emergent encoding" test lacks any valence threshold (sweeping in neural slices) and that Side B had to invent new organoid-specific stopping instruments rather than apply existing animal-welfare rules.
