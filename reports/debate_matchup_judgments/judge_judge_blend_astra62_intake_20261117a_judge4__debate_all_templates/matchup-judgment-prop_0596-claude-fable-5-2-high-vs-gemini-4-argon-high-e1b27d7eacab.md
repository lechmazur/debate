### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B wins a clear but competitive debate. A's present-access and moral case was vivid, especially the turn that walking away leaves patients uncured, but B built the cleaner route: gene editing is not an HIV pill; a compulsory license can be futile where infrastructure is missing and harmful where capable payers exist because it forces know-how transfer and caps the rare success funding one-time-cure R&D. B also turned A's delivery-cost admission into a concession that poor patients gain little, and offered voluntary outcomes-based contracting as the existing remedy. A's repeated clipping and expansion from patent backstop to know-how seizure weakened execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's resolution of A's "useless or devastating" charge was decisive: the pathway can fail the global poor while gutting wealthy-market revenue, so there is no contradiction. B then used A's own delivery-floor concession to show the license neither makes poor-country treatment affordable nor preserves R&D returns.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a close, high-quality exchange. B's opening dilemma (a license cannot be both useless and devastating) forced A into a geographic split, which B then exploited with the India/Brazil wedge and A's own concession that the pathway works where hospitals and manufacturers exist. B's A1 answer (the gap between delivery cost and the ~$2M list price is the monopoly margin, with cost-plus-royalty as the floor) and the pre-emptive line that CON never showed investment requires an unlimited price, only a return, held up under A's late contradiction push. A's strongest material (delivery complexity, per-patient development-risk math, safety of forced know-how transfer, and the sharp closing contradiction between a risk-reflecting royalty and a far-below-list price) was real and landed last, but B had narrowed the dispute to present patients versus a forecast and exposed that CON's only remedy for failed negotiation was walking away, leaving the sick untreated. B's repeated execution cost of clipped, over-limit speeches (turns 1, 3, 7, 9) keeps this from being a solid win; both sides stayed grounded with plausible background knowledge and no fake sourcing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal 2: answering A's marginal-cost pressure by arguing the enormous delivery floor means a licensed price can never be rock-bottom (cost plus royalty is the floor), converting A's own premise into support, then crystallizing that CON conceded the pathway works wherever infrastructure exists, reducing the whole debate to whether a paid royalty instead of an unlimited price ends investment.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): B established the cleaner route to victory: licensing need not solve infrastructure everywhere to improve access where manufacturing and clinical capacity exist, while royalties, negotiation failure, and independent review answer much of the confiscation objection. A powerfully separated poor and wealthy markets and kept the R&D risk live, but offered only walking away or voluntary contracts when bargaining fails, and asserted rather than demonstrated that affordable compensation must necessarily destroy investment. B’s repeated clipping and occasional overstatement keep the margin competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A first neutralized B’s alleged contradiction by distinguishing infrastructure-poor countries from capable wealthy markets. B then turned that distinction into a concession that licensing can work where capacity exists, and its closing exposure of “walking away” as leaving patients untreated was the decisive counter.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B wins because the core solvency challenge was not overcome. PRO needed to show that a compelled license would actually deliver treatment and lower prices without collapsing innovation. B convincingly argued that the binding bottleneck is not the patent but tacit manufacturing know-how, vector supply, quality data, and clinical capacity. PRO's later moves to add regulatory dossiers and hospital point-of-care manufacturing weakened its consistency, since PRO had admitted these therapies cannot be copied like pills. B also exploited the royalty dilemma effectively: a royalty large enough to cover failed trials keeps prices high, while a lower royalty chills rare-disease investment. PRO's moral and fiscal urgency was real, but it did not establish that the proposed pathway produces the claimed access.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal that "the patent isn't what stops the doctor" was the most decisive exchange. It used PRO's own opening concession about complex viral vectors and proprietary manufacturing to show that a license offers legal permission without the validated process, vectors, or data needed to treat patients. This directly undercut PRO's closing image of a legal wall standing between patients and ready hospitals.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a close, high-quality debate. B's central line — that CON's preferred alternative of public capacity investment is legally paralyzed by exclusive patents once a firm abandons a market — ran through every round and was never fully neutralized. A's counter that "the patent isn't what stops the doctor; the firm takes the process, vectors, and data" was plausible, but B answered with a concrete mechanism (academic hospitals pioneered cellular manufacturing; pathway pairs patent licenses with regulatory dossiers) that A mostly met with assertion. B also handled pressure more cleanly: the royalty question (cost-plus vs. risk-adjusted) got a coherent reconciliation in B4.1 (sticker prices are monopoly rents above risk-adjusted costs), and the no-filing scenario got a direct answer in B4.2. A landed real hits — the B1.4 vs. B4.2/B5.3 "cannot be copied like pills" tension and the portfolio-funding dilemma — but B's answers, while sometimes strained, kept those routes alive. Critically, A had three turns clipped for exceeding the word limit (opening, both rebuttals), a recurring execution cost that repeatedly cut off A's weighing right at its climax, while B used every turn fully. B's closings also exploited A's own concessions ("damage control," doctors "can't" treat) more effectively than A crystallized the contradiction hunt. Rhetorically both were sharp ("ransom note" vs. "permission slip"), but B made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3's "legal paralysis" turn: taking A's concession that firms walk away and that doctors "can't" treat patients afterward, then showing that A's own alternative — building public hospital capacity without compulsion — leaves those beds legally unusable under the departed firm's exclusive patent. A's A4.1 response ("the patent isn't what stops the doctor... the licence leaves a permission slip") was A's best moment, but the exchange overall favored B because A conceded the outcome PRO needed while disputing only the mechanism.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): A won the decisive feasibility and incentives clash: a licence supplies legal permission, not necessarily validated manufacturing processes, vectors, comparability data, or treatment capacity, while risk-adjusted royalties still face the portfolio-funding dilemma. B landed strong blows on market abandonment and the legal paralysis of public capacity without licensing, but its answers increasingly depended on assertive claims about hospital replication and foreign regulatory reliance. A’s repeated clipping was an execution cost, but its core rebuttals and closing remained intact and sharper.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal answered B’s legal-wall pressure by distinguishing a patent licence from a usable living medicine, then pressed the unresolved choice between covering failed-program costs and delivering dramatically lower prices. B never fully neutralized that combined feasibility-and-investment objection.
