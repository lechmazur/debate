### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO wins because two of its routes to victory were never neutralized. The first is tactile guidance before contact: smooth glass cannot guide a hand without the eyes. The second is single-point failure: a dead or frozen screen disables every virtual control at once. CON's best move was the performance-based reframe, built on invariant touch zones and a measured test. That reframe had real originality. However, CON kept recasting the motion as a rigid regulatory "mandate" or "dashboard constitution" when the motion is a normative claim about what automakers should do. Under pressure, CON also made damaging concessions. Its emergency solutions were all physical: a stalk, a roof switch, a steering-wheel control, and a hardwired hazard override. It accepted a "brief confirming glance" instead of eyes-up operation. It admitted a "manual backup" may be needed when faults occur. PRO's A1/A2 answers were direct and drew a usable line: eyes-up functions needed in motion stay physical, while parked setup and comfort extras can go onscreen. PRO's closing then cleanly converted CON's concessions into the claim that touch offers zero reward for added risk. CON's scope-instability attack had some bite, but it did not overcome the blackout and glance-time comparison. Both sides cited nonexistent "C" reference IDs, a minor execution cost for each.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's A2.5 observation that every emergency fix CON offered was a physical control. PRO reinforced it with the blackout Q2, which forced CON to rely on hardwired hazards, automatic defog, and an admitted manual backup. That let PRO argue that CON's own fail-operable standard effectively requires physical buttons for core functions.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a clear but competitive debate. B's opening built a coherent physics-based case (no pre-contact tactile guidance, glance cost, single-point screen failure, gloves/glare) and then relentlessly tracked A's accumulating concessions: hardwired hazard overrides, "frozen" invariant core slots, and a "manual backup" on fault detection that B correctly noted must be physical on a dead screen. By the closing, A's position had largely converged toward physical redundancy for critical functions while still resisting the button mandate. A fought back well on line-drawing (wipers-on-stalks, seat heat vs. defrost) and framed the debate as "outcomes not substrates," but this partly swapped the motion as written, and B's A1 answer in Rebuttal 2 supplied a workable eyes-up-in-motion criterion that blunted the scope attack. Both sides used a few garbled reference IDs; neither used fake citations or brittle precision. A's shorter Rebuttal 2 left some pressure (glance-time, blackout recovery) answered only thinly. B made the decisive clash clearer and gave the cleaner reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 turn on [A1.4]: A's own proposed emergency solutions (stalk, roof switch, steering-wheel control) are all physical hardware, quietly conceding that mechanical controls are what work when seconds count. B then compounded this in Rebuttal 2 and Closing by showing A had also conceded invariant frozen slots and a physical manual backup, collapsing A's adaptive-performance framing from within.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A wins on the motion as written. A showed that keeping compact physical controls for eyes-up core functions does not ban screens, stalks, or software, while B's own fixes—hardwired hazard overrides, invariant slots, and a manual blackout backup—quietly restore physical controls and still concede a confirming glance. B's performance-standard reframe and scope pressure stayed live, but the hypothesized equal touch system never answered pre-contact aiming or single-screen failure as cleanly as A's comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's first rebuttal was decisive: B's emergency answers (stalk, roof switch, steering control) were themselves physical controls, and later A seized B's "brief confirming glance" concession while showing a dead screen still needs a physical backup.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): PRO wins a competitive debate on its central reliability claim. Physical controls give tactile confirmation and still work when the touch layer, rendering, or capacitive input degrades. That claim stayed live through the whole exchange. CON ran the more aggressive rebuttal game. It pressed hard on PRO narrowing its scope from all visibility, traction, and signaling functions to a few core controls. It also capitalized on PRO's line that "a screen and a switch are both visual." Those were real hits. CON's affirmative mechanism, however, rested on overconfident and largely unsupported claims: - that gloves and damp fingers "register reliably"; - that a bezel-braced tap is "deterministic"; - that screens are necessarily higher-mounted than switches; - that physical controls signal only through "tiny LEDs." PRO correctly flagged the "tiny LEDs" description as a misreading of its proposal. CON's "same body-control computer" point also sidesteps PRO's actual claim, which was about display and touch failure, not wiring. PRO's prioritization answer is a defensible reading of the motion's "core functions": keep the urgent actions physical and let secondary modes live elsewhere. CON's claim that fan control was pushed onscreen partly overstated what PRO actually said. Both sides cited nonexistent P-prefixed reference IDs, a minor and symmetric cost. Overall, PRO's case is better grounded and closer to the motion. CON's case leaned on best-case screen performance in exactly the degraded conditions at issue.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1/B3.1 and B4.4 pressure on scope was the sharpest exchange. Having first demanded hardware for "anything that affects visibility, traction, or emergency signaling," PRO had to explain where rear defrost and fan modes go. PRO's A4.1/A4.2 answer drew a main-path vs. secondary-adjustment distinction, illustrated with "a backup camera" not making reverse "the main gear." That answer largely held, which kept PRO's reliability frame intact.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by converting Side B's scope retreat into a live contradiction with the motion. B opened demanding physical controls for anything affecting visibility and traction, then shrank to three or four controls to escape the clutter critique, leaving rear defrost, fan, and vent modes onscreen — a real tension with a motion that names climate control as core. A also pinned down B's concession that muscle memory is not the criterion and that a screen and switch are both visual under stress, undercutting B's original eyes-off-road story. B's strongest surviving route — deterministic fallback under degraded conditions — was met with concrete counters (bezel bracing, isolated control bars, damp-finger/glove registration, and the point that electronic switches ride the same body computers), and B's "menu-dependent vs deterministic" reply never fully resolved the shared-electronics objection. Both sides used plausible background knowledge without fake sourcing; both followed the Q/A format. A's rebuttals were sharper, more adaptive, and made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal (A4.3–A4.4): it weaponized B's concession that "a screen and a switch are both visual" to win the eyes-off-road criterion, then showed that B's narrowed hardware still pushes real climate functions (rear defrost, fan, airflow) through the touchscreen, exposing that B trusts screens exactly in the storms it invoked.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side A won narrowly because the live safety comparison—tactile, fixed controls for urgent hazard, defrost, and primary climate actions remain more reliable when glare, moisture, gloves, or touch/rendering faults degrade performance—was not squarely neutralized. Side B landed sharper hits on scope shrinkage, cross-brand muscle memory, and the overclaim that buttons sit fully outside electronics, and sketched a plausible high-bezel alternative, but those points still left a visual precise-touch dependency and rested on confident assertions that modern panels reliably defeat the very failure modes the motion prioritizes. Side A’s primary-versus-secondary distinction adequately fit “core” and “mainly,” so the safer fail-last interface remained the cleaner vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal best exploited Side A’s concession that stressed use is visual for both and the narrowing away from a broad physical rule, but it was not decisive: Side A still showed that only the physical control keeps working when capacitive contact or screen rendering fails.
