### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): PRO built the more complete causal case. A deep slump shrinks the denominator and erodes the tax base, so withholding support moves the deficit rather than cancelling it. Expansion protects the capacity that services debt. PRO also answered CON's pressure questions directly with clear A1/A2 labels: published triggers make withdrawal as automatic as activation, and the baseline is the prior permanent rule. CON's best route was the semantic dilemma: if safeguards differ at high debt, PRO concedes the "even if" clause; if they do not, the safeguards are decorative. The trap is clever, but it rests on reading "even if" as "ignore debt entirely." PRO's reply that the motion denies a debt veto on activation, not prudent design, is the more natural reading of the proposition. CON also drifted. Its opening called for "restraint in the automatic instruments," but by Rebuttal 2 it said only that debt should remain "relevant" to scale. That drift blurred the clash and edged toward PRO's position. CON's asymmetry argument (contractual interest versus forecast output gains) was solid but never squarely neutralized PRO's tax-base point. Three clipped CON turns, each cut mid-argument, were a real execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over "even if." CON argued (B2.1, B4.1) that PRO's call for tighter design conceded the clause. PRO answered (A4.4, A5.1) that elevated debt changes calibration, not activation, and that "even if" denies a veto, not safeguards. This held PRO's ground and exposed that CON had softened from restraint to mere "relevance."
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): B won the central interpretive and policy clashes. Expansion despite elevated debt is compatible with caps, triggers, and debt-sensitive calibration; A repeatedly asserted that the motion forbids these safeguards without establishing why. B directly answered the ratchet pressure and explained why avoided damage to taxable capacity matters even without self-financing stimulus. A raised legitimate refinancing risks, and B overstated how safely interest costs can be bounded, but A never developed the stronger comparative case. A’s repeated clipping added an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s distinction between calibration and activation defeated A’s claimed concession. A answered that different safeguards violate the motion while identical safeguards are decorative, but neither follows: bounded support can still expand during a downturn despite elevated debt.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): A built the stronger substantive case: temporary, trigger-based expansion can protect output, receipts, and future debt-servicing capacity, while elevated debt informs design without vetoing activation. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): This is a close debate, and PRO takes it narrowly on the motion as written. PRO built a coherent case: - the denominator logic of the debt ratio, - self-reversing, targeted flows, - high multipliers when monetary policy is constrained. PRO also stated a clear limiting principle: stabilizers run unless the state can no longer fund itself. CON's stock-versus-flow point was the strongest challenge, and CON's rebuttals were cleaner and more precise. The key weakness is CON's evolving cap: - By B2.2 and B4.1, normal UI and means-tested caseload growth still expand. - Only discretionary-looking top-ups and expanded eligibility are capped. That effectively grants that automatic stabilizers should expand despite elevated debt, which is the motion. CON's case then rests on reading "even if" as "unbounded." That is a stretch, and PRO's unfundability limit partly blunts it. CON also never fully answered the counterfactual question of why capping lowers the post-slump debt ratio rather than raising it through lost output. Its answer was that the numerator is certain while the denominator is uncertain. PRO has real costs of its own: - Two clipped turns. - Repeating the claim that the cap cuts core support for the newly jobless, after CON clearly said indexing and caseload growth protect each claimant's core benefit. - Never sharply exploiting CON's concession. These keep the margin thin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was over the limiting principle. CON's Q1 asked what debt level would ever justify a cap. PRO's A1 answered with unfundability and argued that CON's "core runs, top-ups capped" design had retreated from opposing automatic expansion. CON's reply, that a funding-crisis trigger comes too late, was sharp. But it left CON defending caps on mostly non-automatic additions rather than on stabilizers themselves.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): B wins narrowly on the motion: elevated debt alone is not shown to outweigh the demand and tax-base benefits of letting existing stabilizers expand. A's later policy expressly permits falling revenues and rising indexed caseload payments, shifting its opposition toward extra generosity that B's opening did not require. A lands strong objections about persistent liabilities and waiting until funding fails. B also repeatedly misdescribes A's cap as cutting core benefits, substantially narrowing its win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B correctly answers A's demand for guaranteed future taxable GDP: neither policy guarantees sustainability, so the comparison must include output and capacity lost through restraint. However, A's clarification that ordinary caseload payments remain uncapped successfully defeats B's repeated claim that its policy reduces core benefits per claimant.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate. A’s denominator-and-hysteresis case was strong, but B answered both pressure questions directly, distinguished preserving ordinary indexed stabilizers from capping additional borrowing, and exposed A’s market-closure threshold as an implausibly late limiting principle. A too often treated that narrower proposal as a cut to core support, while both clipped rebuttals cost execution. B’s certainty about numerator savings and focus on top-ups did not fully settle the macroeconomic counterfactual, keeping the margin moderate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered A1/A2, clarified the ceiling-versus-cut distinction, and countered A’s output test by emphasizing future primary surpluses, debt service, and the dangers of waiting until funding markets close.
