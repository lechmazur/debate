### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B wins by isolating the marginal effect of the motion early and holding that frame through every speech. B's frame was that effects-based law already covers physically destructive attacks, so the motion's only real change is to unlock lethal force for reversible disruptions. A's first rebuttal effectively conceded this by describing the gap as "temporary blackouts, disrupted logistics, corrupted hospital data." B then built a two-horned dilemma. Against nuclear peers, the military threat is either a hollow bluff or a path to catastrophe. Against weaker states, it hands aggressors a pretext. A never broke the dilemma. In A1, A retreated to cyber, economic and diplomatic tools that states already hold, and in closing A conceded "you don't have to" strike. That undercut A's own claim that the motion adds deterrence. On the pretext point, A's A2 answer relied on existing safeguards and the "defending state's own intelligence." B turned this into a contrast between verifiable craters and curated network logs. A's strongest material, the moral-equivalence and impunity framing, stayed largely assertive. A repeated that sanctions "fail" and that the status quo is a "green light," citing B's admissions that B had not actually made. A also had grounding issues. Its claim that the Ukraine attack left people "freezing in winter" was contested by B's restoration-within-hours point, and A did not defend it with anything new. A's closing added "water poisoned" without prior support, which B neatly redirected into its effects-based frame. B's answers to both pressure questions were direct and concrete, covering resilience measures, active disruption, and reversibility as a hedge against attribution error.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3/B5.2: B had asked what military strike A would launch against a nuclear peer. A named only tools already available without armed-attack status and later conceded "you don't have to." B showed this left the motion's added military option as either an empty bluff or an escalation trigger, which collapsed A's deterrence route.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A (CON) built the cleaner path to victory. The decisive trap was set early: effects-based law already covers physically destructive cyberattacks, so the proposition's real content is reclassifying reversible, non-destructive disruptions as armed attacks. Side B repeatedly retreated under pressure—when asked what military strike works against a nuclear peer, B4.1 answered with cyber, economic, and diplomatic tools A correctly noted states already possess, leaving armed-attack status adding only paper standing and unlocked lethal force. A's "paper standing deters nobody" and the reversibility asymmetry (misfired server takedown vs. misfired missile) went unanswered. B's strongest counter—the status quo is "permission" and inaction is war by attrition—was blunted by A's concrete alternative toolkit and the Ukraine restoration example. B followed the A1/A2 format and gave consistent weighing, but never resolved what the rule concretely adds against its main adversaries, and its "water poisoned" example fell into A's already-covered-by-effects-law trap. A's arguments progressed, sharpened, and crystallized the choice across every round.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3, where Side A caught B4.1's contradiction head-on: pressed for a usable military option against a nuclear peer, B named none and retreated to tools that require no armed-attack status, leaving only "legal standing" that a nuclear rival would ignore after absorbing the first kinetic strike. This collapsed B's deterrence story and was reinforced effectively in both closings.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A wins by isolating what equivalence actually adds: lethal military options for reversible, non-destructive disruption already addressable by cyber and economic tools. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): PRO wins clearly. It built a coherent case on three points: an asymmetry trap, cross-domain deterrence, and a framing of the motion as a bounded legal option rather than a mandate. It then adapted to each objection CON raised. CON's strongest route was the attribution problem and the risk of misattribution. PRO repeatedly neutralized it in three ways: - Under PRO's standard, force is lawful only with high-confidence attribution. - The naval mine and covert sabotage analogy shows that hard-to-trace attacks have never removed the right of self-defense. - PRO's Q2 pressed CON to say why military options should be barred even when attribution is unmistakable. CON answered by saying mines leave physical evidence. That was partly responsive, but it never engaged the unmistakable-attribution case. CON's pre-emption spiral argument was asserted twice, but it was not developed beyond the claim that it is "equally plausible." PRO's counter was more convincing: an attacker that chose cyber to avoid war would not launch a full war to protect one launch node. CON also repeatedly attacked an inflated version of the motion: "every blackout," "cruise missiles over ransomware," and "requires" military response. This misdescribed PRO's bounded proposal. CON's best answer was that the necessity requirement means non-kinetic tools come first. PRO had already absorbed that point by allowing kinetic force only when non-kinetic tools fail. PRO's closing crystallized the choice sharply around legal sanctuary for attackers. CON's closing largely repeated attribution concerns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was on air-gapped attackers. PRO's Q1 asked how slow sanctions stop immediate civilian deaths when an air-gapped military unit cannot be reached by counter-hacking. CON answered with "emergency medical response" and diplomatic pressure. PRO's closing turned this against CON: these tools manage casualties while the attack continues from legal sanctuary. This exposed that CON's status quo offers no remedy for an ongoing, clearly attributed attack.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built the more decisive case and answered the hardest pressure more honestly. B's asymmetry/sanctuary trap (air-gapped attackers, sanctions that work only "over time") went through the whole debate largely unanswered, and B capitalized on A's weak A4.1 reply of "emergency medical response and diplomacy" against an ongoing lethal attack. B also defused A's core attribution objection by noting international law has never had a pre-strike referee and that covert physical sabotage (unmarked mines) already works the same way, and dismantled the pre-emption spiral with the "deterrence works before the keystroke" logic. A's attribution and escalation-safety-valve points remained live and were well crystallized in closing ("no proportionality clause unrings that bell"), but B's framing—option not mandate, proportionality scaling with gravity—held up. Both sides were grounded, used no fake sourcing, and followed format cleanly, including proper A1/A2 answers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1-B4.3): by contrasting the historically referee-less nature of self-defense with B's bounded proportional responses, and then hammering that A's alternative is slow-motion diplomacy against an ongoing air-gapped attack, B turned A's own "manageable asymmetry" concession into the decisive clash of the debate.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO won by making equivalence conditional on high-confidence attribution, necessity, and proportionality, which answered CON's mandate and minor-incident objections. CON never solved the confirmed, air-gapped attacker case, while PRO turned the status quo's slow, asymmetric tools into an invitation to further attacks. CON's escalation warning remained plausible but more speculative than PRO's deterrence and failure-mode comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was B's pressure-question exchange: CON answered an air-gapped hospital attack with diplomacy, triage, and non-kinetic tools, allowing B to expose that as managing casualties from a legally immune launch site rather than stopping the attack.
