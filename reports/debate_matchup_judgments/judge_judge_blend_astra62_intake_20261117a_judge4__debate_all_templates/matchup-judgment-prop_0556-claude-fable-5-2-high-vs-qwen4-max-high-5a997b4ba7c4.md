### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side A won a high-level, competitive debate by proving that explicit, time-limited opt-in sets a necessary protective floor without precluding additional safeguards. Side B ran a clever structural critique of consent—scoring real points that historical home addresses do not perish when consent expires and that app-delivered prompts strain regulatory enforcement—but had to abandon its opening broker-observability argument after Side A's sharp rebuttal and ultimately relied on an unpassed use-restriction counterplan as though the motion forbade use bans. Side A convincingly showed that use restrictions can sit on top of an opt-in rule, that plain opt-in removes the vast majority who say no from the shelf agencies and unforeseen buyers shop from, and that having no consent gate at all leaves survivors strictly more exposed in the baseline world.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Pressure Questions [A3.1-A3.2] and closing follow-through [A5.2-A5.5]: by forcing Side B to say in [B4.1] that selling nightly trails to private buyers should be "prohibited outright" via use restrictions, Side A exposed that B had conceded unconsented sales are wrong ("the motion with the yes removed") while failing to show why use restrictions cannot sit on top of an opt-in floor that keeps non-consenters out of the dataset entirely.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side A more convincingly showed that time-limited broker opt-in fails the very people Side B invoked: consent is coercible by whoever holds the phone, historical patterns do not expire, reconstruction remains possible from consenters, and an explicit “yes” supplies the voluntary-sharing defense that currently makes warrantless government purchases contestable. Direct use and buyer restrictions can bar harmful sales of individual trails without those drawbacks. Side B’s “ask first” principle, shrinkage claim, and insistence that explicitness rules will police dark patterns were engaging but did not neutralize those live failures or the fact that Side A’s alternative denies the stranger the sale outright.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal, which answered that use restrictions prohibit private sales of an individual’s nightly trail whether or not anyone consented, so the ban travels with the data and cannot be clicked through by an abuser, then showed that Side B’s expiry and “explicit” answers leave historical inferences and prompt-policing as the real, unresolved mechanism.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): CON showed the consent gate can be opened by an abuser, bundled prompt, or surrounding devices, while copies and historical inferences persist after expiry. Its use-and-buyer restrictions protect non-signers regardless of who taps, and PRO's answers ultimately depended on idealized enforcement of “explicit” consent and prompt design.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: expiring consent does not erase home, shelter, or clinic patterns inferred from historical data, and prohibitions traveling with the data protect a survivor even when someone else controls the phone.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): In an exceptionally high-level debate, Side B won by proving that PRO's consent mechanism fails to solve PRO's own headline harms and isolating how little marginal protection the motion actually supplies. Side B Forced Side A to concede that stopping stalkers and insurers requires substantive use limits and recalling already-sold trails requires deletion duties—protections outside the motion itself. Because brokers have no screen on a user's phone, profit-seeking apps write the prompts, turning scheduled opt-ins into click-through obstacles. Side A kept the race competitive by exposing the breadth of "fraud teams" in Side B's licensed-buyer model and defending upstream refusal for attentive users, but Side A undercut itself by conceding "an unread yes" in Rebuttal 1 and then contradicting that concession in Closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's closing claim that "the person who never reads a prompt is never sold" [A5.2] in [B5.1-B5.2]: Side B pointed out that apps paid per opt-in place prompts directly in front of the user's forecast, quoting Side A's own earlier admission of an "unread yes" [A2.4] to show that inattention yields "a sale with her signature on it" rather than protection.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side A won by forcing a concrete alternative—licensed, named buyers under use bans, audits, and deletion—and then showing that the motion’s distinctive consent rule does not uniquely stop dangerous sales. A squarely answered the pressure to name a permitted sale, exposed the tension between B’s “unread yes” concession and the later claim that inattention means no sale, and weighed buyer-purpose gates as acting at the same moment while depending less on click-through vigilance. B kept a strong normative case that individuals should decide whether ordinary movements are sold, but never fully neutralized the practical click-through problem or the costs to representative public-interest data.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 and closing: after naming the vetted-buyer sale B demanded, A showed that stalker/insurer bans and deletion were doing the real work, while an unread opt-in mainly supplies a seller’s alibi and thinner evacuation data.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by exposing the central contradiction in Side B's case: B condemned an unread tap as nonexistent consent, then relied on another tap. B's autonomy argument about ordinary movements remained powerful, but A answered it with a pre-transfer buyer-and-purpose gate that protects people without depending on vigilance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it directly answered both pressure questions, named the vetted sale it would permit, and reframed timing by showing a licensed-buyer gate also acts before transfer but tests who is buying and why rather than whether a box was ticked.
