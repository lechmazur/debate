### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B wins a competitive debate by framing it as a choice between a categorical ban and strict regulation, and then pressing harder on the comparative question. A opened strongly. Its core points were the invalid-proxy argument, cultural and disability variance, and the lack of real consent. Its "audited horoscope" line was memorable. A's closing also found a real opening in B's ground-truth answer: if the validation target is whether a caller calms down, that is behavior that can be measured without emotion inference. But B consistently attacked A's live routes: - **Composure contradiction.** A says composure and empathy are unobservable internal states, yet lets human supervisors judge those same qualities. - **Neutral signals backfire.** The "neutral operational signals" A offered as substitutes (repeated speech, silence, volume) penalize stutters, the very harm A cited. - **Ban scope.** A's functional-inference ban leaves raw acoustic scoring unregulated whenever vendors drop the emotion claim. B's guardrails cover all algorithmic scoring of voice and expression regardless of label. A's answer on human bias, that it is "legally challengeable," was thin. B rebutted it directly: a rejected applicant has no score or record with which to prove discrimination. B leaned somewhat optimistically on the idea that validation will screen out biased models. Still, B's weighing was more developed and better adapted across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1/B2.3 was the most damaging exchange. B exposed the tension in A's position: A calls calm and empathy unobservable internal states, yet lets supervisors judge them. B then showed that A's own "neutral" substitute signals (repeated speech, silence) would penalize stutters, turning A's disability harm against prohibition. A never fully recovered from this, though A's closing turn on "calming down is behavior" partially clawed back ground.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won the comparative-policy clash by explaining why contextual sentiment tools might improve evaluation under mandatory safeguards, while directly challenging the safety of A’s proposed substitutes. B also answered both pressure questions with concrete validation criteria and mechanism-based regulation. A’s distinction between internal feelings and observable performance remained meaningful, but its categorical case relied too heavily on asserting that validation cannot help. B overstated how reliably audits would eliminate bias, keeping the win competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest rebuttal was that A’s supposedly neutral repetition and silence metrics could themselves flag stutters. Combined with the argument that auditable records can make discrimination easier to challenge, this materially weakened A’s comparison between prohibited emotion systems and the alternatives it would preserve.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): A won a clear but competitive debate by exposing the tension in B’s case: B permitted human judgments of composure and empathy from the same cues, while its approved acoustic metrics could reproduce disability harms without an audit trail. A also preserved a concrete benefit—contextual call triage—that raw outcomes cannot reliably replace. B’s validity, scale, and false-confidence objections remained forceful, and its ground-truth question was excellent, but its later speeches largely reiterated those points rather than neutralizing A’s accountability comparison. A’s confidence that validation would reliably filter bias was somewhat idealized, keeping the margin modest.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first rebuttal was decisive: it turned B’s human-supervisor exception and “neutral” acoustic substitutes against prohibition, then reframed auditability as evidence workers can actually use. B pressed back well on ground truth and scalable laundering, but never fully answered the comparative loss of contextual triage and documented review.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): PRO wins a competitive debate. It built a clear dilemma: if emotion data is validated against observable outcomes, it is redundant because employers can judge those outcomes directly. If it adds something beyond outcomes, it penalizes workers for involuntary physiology rather than performance. PRO also answered CON's pressure questions directly. It drew a non-arbitrary line between real-time fatigue alarms and personnel evaluation of moods. It also rejected the human "vibe check" baseline in favor of structured rubrics and work samples. CON's strongest route was the comparative baseline: banning the auditable tool leaves unreviewable human hunches in charge. That point stayed partly live. However, PRO's structured-work-sample alternative blunted it. CON's nurse example then gave PRO a concrete turn, showing that even the best-case tool reassigns a worker who met every target. CON's group-level audit answer was reasonable but never fully addressed undisclosed disabilities. Its closing largely restated the human-baseline point rather than resolving the redundancy dilemma.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing seized on CON's Rebuttal 2 example of two nurses who both meet outcomes, one flagged for vocal distress and coached or reassigned. PRO recast it as CON's own best case penalizing competent workers for involuntary physiology. That converted CON's attempt to show non-redundancy into proof of PRO's mental-privacy harm. CON's reply, that a rule could forbid "automatic consequences," did not fully undo the turn, because CON had itself endorsed reassignment as a use.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): A wins on the practical comparison and privacy stakes. It offers structured assessments instead of either scanners or subjective “fit,” distinguishes fatigue alarms from the motion, and explains how emotion scores can discipline successful workers. B’s outcome-validation and predictive-warning arguments weaken A’s categorical scientific claims; A also unfairly describes the nurse example as solely score-driven. Nevertheless, B insufficiently answers the intrusion and implementation objections, repeatedly relying on a human-hunch baseline A explicitly rejects.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A directly answers the alleged inconsistency between banning algorithms and tolerating biased interviewers: reject both emotion scanning and human “vibe” checks in favor of substantive rubrics. This undermines B’s central comparison, yet B’s closing incorrectly maintains that A cannot condemn the corresponding human bias.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): B built the stronger categorical case by combining invalid measurement, discrimination, mental privacy, and workable alternatives. A offered a serious targeted-regulation defense and a useful predictive-warning scenario, but never fully answered B’s argument that even accurate emotion inference illegitimately polices workers’ inner states. B also neutralized A’s fatigue example and exposed weaknesses in outcome validation and disability audits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions, distinguished safety alerts from personnel evaluation, and turned A’s outcome-validation defense against itself: actual outcomes or work samples can assess performance, while biometric scores add redundancy, anchoring, and poorly detectable disability bias.
