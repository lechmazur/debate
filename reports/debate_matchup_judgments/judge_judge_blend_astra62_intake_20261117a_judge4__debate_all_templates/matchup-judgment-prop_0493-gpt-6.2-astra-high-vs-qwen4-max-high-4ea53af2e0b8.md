### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins a close but clear decision. It framed the debate early and kept that frame throughout: the motion regulates ownership, but the harms PRO describes come from volume, timing and location. It then exploited PRO's concessions. In [B2.3] PRO said to keep caps on public pumps too. In [B4.3] PRO admitted that the same pad and the same pumping rate leave the neighbor's well just as dry. CON used both to argue that the ban's only remaining value is council discretion. That turned PRO's own opening distrust of officials ([B1.4]) back against it, since the utility would now be the seller, the employer and the referee at once. PRO's best line is a real structural asymmetry. A supplier can simply decline the next order, while a regulator must claw back access it has already granted. PRO also pressed hard on what a town would be paying for in a buyback. But this line depends on trusting a council under budget pressure, and CON pinned that tension down repeatedly. CON has some vulnerabilities. Its claim that licenses are freely revocable sits uneasily with its praise of buybacks, and its claim that bottling is a "rounding error" was asserted with some confidence. PRO never converted either weakness into a decisive point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's A3 Q2 and A4.4. CON forced PRO to concede that moving the pump to public hands leaves the neighbor's well equally dry. CON then showed that the single litre PRO claimed to save depends on a deficit-pressured council choosing reserve margin over revenue. That is the same optimism about official behavior that PRO's opening had rejected.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): A won a close, high-quality debate by keeping the central comparison focused on actual water protection rather than ownership alone. Its enforceable local triggers, adjustable permits, and exposure of the utility’s combined seller-regulator conflict answered more of the motion’s practical burden. B’s distinction between declining a new order and stopping an already authorized withdrawal remained a genuine advantage, but depended heavily on discretionary public decisions and did not establish why the broader ban outperformed A’s targeted regime.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal answered B’s pre-trigger falling-well example with localized interference limits, then turned B’s remaining discretion argument against the public utility itself. The closing sharpened this into the strongest comparison: persuasion can produce an enforceable permit condition under A’s regime, while B ultimately asks residents to trust the office booking the sale.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): B won by establishing the decisive default difference: without a corporate claim each shipment needs affirmative public approval, while a permitted bottler pumps until a trigger, discretionary revocation, or buyback stops it. A landed strong points on volume controls and public-seller discretion, but B answered that caps apply under either regime and a mistaken sale is discrete whereas a mistaken grant authorizes continuing withdrawals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's trigger-discretion-buyback dilemma in Rebuttal 2 and closing: if no permit condition is breached, for-cause revocation does not protect residents seeking more margin, leaving A dependent on the discretion or payment A criticized.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): This was a close debate, and CON wins narrowly on direct engagement. PRO had the more memorable framing. "Stored time," the irreversibility asymmetry, and "the emergency is the alibi" all land well. PRO's pressure questions also earned two real concessions: CON admitted a ban is easier to police and that public bottling is possible. CON's questions were equally productive. PRO conceded that monitoring costs are the same under either regime and that public operation saves no water in a given season. That shrank PRO's live case to one claim: a for-profit extractor is an adversary that will capture the safe-yield reading over time. CON answered that remaining claim with specific mechanisms: - the authority selects the monitor; - the valve stays closed by default unless fresh readings support the draw; - statutory suspension triggers cannot be waived locally; - grants are nontransferable, with no renewal right; - no pumping is allowed during an appeal. PRO's later speeches mostly restated the adversary concern rather than showing why those mechanisms fail. PRO also leaned on mischaracterizations that CON corrected cleanly: "deed," "continuously, forever," and relabeling CON's model as "public bottling." CON also held its distinction between ownership, trading, and extraction, which kept the overbreadth of the motion's extraction clause live. PRO's weighing — avoided machinery costs versus an irreplaceable aquifer — is strong rhetorically. However, it assumes the controls fail, and CON contested that assumption more concretely.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.4–A4.5 decided the debate. CON showed that county control of the valve does not turn private sale into "public bottling," so PRO cannot defend the extraction ban by attacking a deed nobody proposed. CON then answered the adversary-incentive point directly: what matters is whether pressing for a generous reading actually gets the firm water. Because the authority picks the monitor and the valve defaults closed, it does not.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): A won a close, high-quality debate by more consistently answering the central comparison: why even a demonstrably sustainable, publicly controlled extraction must be banned. B offered the stronger rhetoric and a serious enforcement and political-economy case, but often treated private sale as necessarily perpetual or irreversible depletion. A directly addressed those concerns with default-closed valves, independent monitoring, nontransferable permits, and no pumping during appeals, while also identifying a concrete benefit private operation can provide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: after B conceded that public operation saves no additional water in the same season, A showed that public bottling retains the same measurement burden, clarified that privately sold contracted extraction is still covered by the motion, and explained the standing-cost advantage without relying on a deed or permanent water right.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): CON won the central comparison: a town that cannot prove a safe, publicly controlled draw should refuse it, but PRO never justified banning even a short, nontransferable, interruptible draw in a well-documented basin. PRO's enforceability and capture arguments were powerful, yet CON answered with a default-closed valve, authority-selected monitoring, mandatory suspension, and no pumping during appeal. PRO also conceded that public bottling of the same volume saves no water that season, leaving long-term adversary risk against a concrete local-choice and emergency-supply benefit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it conceded a ban is easier to police while limiting that cost to towns seeking a draw, directly answered what private operation adds by covering standby bottling costs, and correctly insisted the motion bans private extraction itself, not merely private title.
