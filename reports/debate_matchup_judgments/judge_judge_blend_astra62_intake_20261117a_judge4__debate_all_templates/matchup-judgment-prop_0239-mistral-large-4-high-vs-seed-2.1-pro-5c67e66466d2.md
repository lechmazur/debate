### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): CON built a coherent libertarian position: valid consent means informed, uncoerced agreement from everyone whose rights are affected, and nothing else remains wrong. PRO's circularity charge did the decisive work. CON's claim that informedness and voluntariness are constitutive of consent is defensible. Its extension to "assent of all rights-affected third parties" is not. Neither is its reliance on a prior rights framework to draw the "materially affected" boundary. Both effectively import independent moral criteria, so they undercut "consent alone." PRO's Q2 forced this into the open. CON's A2 answer leaned on "pre-existing rights to bodily security, property, civic standing," which concedes a rights test beyond the parties' yes. CON's handling of the duel was also strained: it first invented diffuse social harms, then bit the bullet on an isolated duel. That bullet is coherent but leaves PRO's inalienability point (servitude, killing) standing for many readers. PRO had execution costs: misreferenced IDs ([C1.2], [P2.2]), a thin A2 answer, and a closing that mostly repeated the circularity point. Its desperate-worker exploitation line was also underdeveloped after the opening. On balance, PRO made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 and the Rebuttal 2 follow-up on the "materially affected" boundary were the decisive exchange. CON answered that the line tracks pre-existing rights violations. That answer coheres, but it confirms PRO's point that a separate rights and harm judgment, not the parties' consent, is doing the ethical work in cases like the polluted river.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins the definitional clash. Side A's core defense expanded "consent" to include informedness, voluntariness, revocability, all-affected-party assent, and absence of rights violations. Side B persuasively exposed this as circular: those are separate moral constraints, not features of bare consent. Side A answered pressure directly but ultimately bit the bullet that a fully isolated no-third-party duel is ethical, which left the substantive wrong of alienating fundamental protections unresolved. B gave the cleaner reason to vote: consent transmits permission but does not by itself make the object or terms ethical.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's most decisive exchange was the circularity charge reinforced by the duel/servitude answer: once consent is defined to include every safeguard needed for an ethical transaction, "consent alone is sufficient" becomes tautological.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): A won a close, conceptually sharp debate. B effectively exposed weaknesses in A’s original examples and gave direct answers on third-party rights and the isolated duel. But B’s sufficiency claim ultimately depended on “consent” already incorporating information, voluntariness, affected-party assent, and an external rights-and-harm test. A kept the motion focused on whether consent itself supplies those further moral criteria, and its inalienability and autonomy argument left a live reason that agreement cannot ethicalize every object or term. A’s repetitive close and stray reference labels prevented a larger margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A’s circularity charge against B’s enriched definition. B’s constitutive-consent defense and rights-boundary answer were strong, but they also showed that independent rights and harm judgments—not consent alone—were doing the decisive ethical work.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side B (PRO) opened with three strong, concrete examples: the coerced worker, the downstream town, and the predatory loan. Its "definitional theft" charge was a live and serious route to victory throughout. Side A (CON) adapted better across rounds. A's key counter was that B uses the thin, signature sense of consent to build its examples, then measures "consent alone" against the thick sense. B never directly answered that. A also made the point that a landowner cannot waive rights he does not hold, which neutralized the third-party example cleanly. In the pressure-answer round, A gave direct, concrete replies: the fair contractor deal between unequal parties, and the limits on future persons holding a veto. B's A1 partly conceded that a fully consented town deal could be acceptable, then piled on extra conditions and pivoted to real-world deals. B's A2 restated the dignity point without resolving A's charge that it works as an external veto. B's second rebuttal and closing largely repeated the definitional-theft charge without advancing it. A's closing crystallized a clean dilemma: either defective consent fails, which is a tautology, or genuine consent is sufficient. B's framing kept the debate competitive, but A did more to narrow and resolve the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's [A2.1]-[A2.3] answered B's definitional-theft charge directly. It argued that B cannot use a bare "yes" to generate its examples while dismissing CON's richer sense of consent. It added that the landowner cannot consent away water he does not own. B never squarely neutralized this two-definitions dilemma; it only reasserted the theft charge.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A wins a close but clear definitional clash. PRO's examples ultimately reduce to missing or defective consent under A's plausible account of genuine consent, so they do not supply a clean case where valid consent is still unethical. A also answered the main pressure better: it distinguished unequal bargaining power from actual coercion, and identified future persons from abstract future-interest vetoes. PRO's dignity and structural-fairness objections remained external moral vetoes rather than a decisive independent criterion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was A's turn on PRO's definitional slide: PRO needed the thin "signed yes" to generate its examples but then demanded a thicker standard to refute consent. A's answer to Q1 also forced PRO to concede the idealized downstream-consent case would not show a separate wrong.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by forcing A to defend its examples under a coherent account of genuine consent. A’s definitional-theft objection remained live, especially concerning fairness and future autonomy, but A never produced a compelling residual wrong after voluntariness, capacity, information, and affected parties’ consent were granted. B answered pressure more directly, while A increasingly repeated its central objection and relied on an abstract dignity claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it distinguished unequal means from coercion and identifiable future harm from an abstract veto for future persons. Combined with B’s earlier exposure of A’s shifting between signatures and genuine consent, this left A’s kidney-and-dignity argument looking more like an external moral veto than a demonstrated defect in consent.
