### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides argued with real sophistication, but A kept closer fidelity to the motion and built a cleaner, more defensible line: sponsorship is fine as a labeled, bounded slot, but wrong once it becomes heavy enough that a typical user cannot reach a cheaper nearby option without "undoing" the interface. A answered the pressure questions directly and consistently, and its closing image ("sponsorship may buy a place on the screen; it may not become the screen") crystallized the clash well. B's rebuttals were analytically sharp—especially the symmetry point (organic defaults can also entrench incumbents) and the critique that "paid majority of the screen" doesn't track actual discoverability—but B never fully replaced this with a better operational standard, and its final reframe ("the motion condemns the wrong variable") edges toward substituting a different question (general comparison-tool access) for the one actually on the table. B's Rebuttal 1 was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost that blunted its full argument before the judge saw it. A's unanswered weak spot (not fully rebutting B's "majority vs. discoverability" counting critique) keeps this from being a dominant win, but on balance A gave the more stable, motion-faithful path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 point that a "paid majority of the first screen" does not track real discoverability (three paid results beside one clearly priced independent could pass the test; an all-organic screen could still fail it) was the sharpest single hit, exposing a real gap in A's operational test that A's closing did not squarely repair.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): A won by defending the motion's limited threshold rather than a cheapest-first ranking: sponsorship may remain visible, but it should not consume the discovery path. B raised strong objections about cold-start visibility, filters, and ambiguous thresholds, yet repeatedly treated A's case as condemning ordinary filtering or all paid prominence. A clearly permitted labeled placements and multifactor organic rankings while explaining why paid saturation uniquely undermines the user's search. B's first rebuttal being clipped was also a modest execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal most decisively answered B's cold-start tradeoff: a labeled sponsored module can still help newcomers interrupt review incumbency while nearby organic alternatives remain visible. This preserved sponsorship's benefit without conceding that advertisers may dominate the whole screen, and it made B's predicted fee increases look comparatively speculative.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B wins by systematically dismantling Side A's definition of "struggle" and exposing the flaws in A's threshold for abuse. B's demonstration that organic ranking also buries options, and that filtering is standard search behavior rather than "undoing design," effectively neutralized A's core mechanisms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5, where B dismantles A's "paid majority" test by pointing out that three sponsored results and one clearly visible independent satisfy A's metric for abuse without actually hiding the bargain.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly within a genuinely contested frame, but B built the more durable structural attack. B's pressure questions exploited A's own concessions (that no restaurant need rank first, and that reliable sorts would "answer the concern") to show the motion either does no work beyond existing filters or else imposes an unadministrable "struggle" standard that effectively mandates surfacing restaurants users never explicitly sought. A's answer in Rebuttal 2 ("practical discoverability," "platform decides in the first instance") restated the standard without resolving how it differs from ordinary ranking discretion platforms already exercise, leaving B's "who decides, and by what count" pressure substantially live into the closing. A's moral framing ("sell attention, not a map that hides the destination") was vivid and consistently maintained, and A's point that CON never proved the fee-pass-through mechanism was fair, but it didn't overcome B's deeper problem: the proposition's trigger term ("struggle") remained undefined and B repeatedly forced A to concede this without ever naming a workable line. A also suffered repeated word-limit clipping (opening, rebuttal 1, and closing), an execution cost that weakened the delivery of otherwise solid arguments. B's closing crystallized the clash efficiently ("a results mandate... a dearer map") while A's closing, though strong, was again cut off before completing its final point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Questions (B3.1/B3.2) combined with A's Answers in Rebuttal 2 (A4.1/A4.2) was the pivotal exchange: B forced A to either admit the standard reduces to existing sorts/filters or concede it has no administrable stopping point, and A's "practical discoverability" answer never escaped that bind, leaving the core administrability objection unresolved through closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A better honored the motion's limited threshold: sponsorship remains allowed, but not when it makes relevant affordable options genuinely difficult to discover. A repeatedly distinguished discoverability from guaranteed first place, undercutting B's claim that the proposal necessarily creates a results mandate. B raised real concerns about vagueness and lost advertising revenue, but the predicted shift to higher fees was asserted more than demonstrated, while its defense lacked a persuasive stopping point before users simply abandon the app. A's repeated clipping was an execution cost, keeping the win competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange was its answer that prominence cannot be commercially valuable because it steers users yet simultaneously be dismissed as inconsequential friction for diners. This exposed tension in B's case and supported A's distinction between bounded advertising and paid obstruction.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully exposing a contradiction in Side B's defense of the user experience. Side B argued that ads are valuable because they steer orders, yet simultaneously claimed that users can easily bypass them using filters. Side A exploited this by pointing out that if defaults and prime placements didn't genuinely obstruct organic discovery, advertisers wouldn't pay a premium for them, meaning the app cannot blame users for 'failing to hunt.' While Side B made a strong economic argument about fee trade-offs, Side A decisively won the clash on app discovery mechanics.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.2) and Closing (A5.1) brilliantly pointed out that prominence cannot be both commercially powerful for steering orders and consumer-neutral as 'mere friction.'
