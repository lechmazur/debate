### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): A wins the central case: deliberate commercial cloning of a named person's voice should require consent because the taking is the harm, and ex post lawsuits arrive too late. B's strongest objections were largely neutralized: the Lincoln example fails because no recording exists, incidental resemblance does not "imitate" an identifiable person, and provenance gives platforms an objective compliance mechanism. B's answer to A's decisive pressure question effectively conceded that harmful deliberate cloning is already prohibited, reducing the dispute to timing and burden; B never justified forcing the victim to discover and litigate rather than requiring the profiting company to ask first. B retains real speech and compliance concerns, but they were less developed and often aimed at a broader rule than the one A defended.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's exploitation of B's statement that harmful deliberate cloning is "already prohibited" was decisive: it turned B's own defense into a concession that the issue is only whether the company asks before or the victim sues after, and B never supplied a principle for selling the voice without asking.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built a much cleaner, better-adapted path to victory despite incurring minor execution costs from having three turns clipped at the final phrase. Side A persuasively established why ex post litigation fails working voice actors and private citizens—who must first discover secret clones and fund lawsuits across patchwork state laws after jobs are already lost—and caught Side B conceding in [B4.2] that deliberate commercial voice cloning is already wrongful, reducing the central dispute to whether companies ask before selling or victims sue afterward. Side B raised credible initial concerns about UGC platform screening, commercial AI satire, and accidental vocal similarity, but stalled out in the second half by repeating assertions about subjective similarity and long-dead historical figures without engaging Side A's answers on model provenance and Abraham Lincoln predating sound recording.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A4.1]-[A4.5], Side A dismantled both of Side B's pressure questions at once: pointing out that an "AI Abraham Lincoln" cannot be a voice clone because Lincoln died before sound recording existed (and where no rights-holder exists, no consent is needed), while shifting the accidental-resemblance test from subjective ear-matching to training and marketing provenance held by the seller.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): B built a convincing ex-ante case: deliberate commercial cloning appropriates identity, while after-the-fact litigation discovers the injury too late and burdens the victim. A offered plausible opening concerns but later overstated the motion as covering every resemblance, misdescribed B’s Anne Frank response, and largely repeated pre-screening and compliance claims without answering B’s distinction. B’s closing then converted A’s existing-law answer into a concession that the real dispute was ask-first versus sue-later.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B wins by exposing that A could not defend the motion as written. Under pressure, A retreated from the claim that a monetized satirical podcast needs consent, answering "No" to the president example. That concession contradicted A's earlier broad consent position and narrowed the rule to selective commercial exploitation. B then showed A's surviving examples—ads, fitness apps, celebrity navigation voices—fall within a narrower anti-appropriation/deception rule, while the consent requirement still chills lawful speech through platform over-removal. A's closing also mischaracterized B as allowing labeled exploitation, which B had already disclaimed. B's complaint-based alternative and default-position framing were clearer and more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 was most decisive: it used A's own "No" answer to show the proposition as written had been abandoned, then turned A's remaining exploitation examples into cases already covered by a narrower publicity/appropriation rule, framing the choice as between targeting wrongs and requiring pre-clearance that platforms would resolve by deleting lawful speech.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B wins solidly by exposing the overbreadth of the proposition as written and dominating the institutional comparison, despite minor execution costs from three clipped endings. Side B showed early that requiring affirmative consent before any commercial distribution sweeps in paid satire, news, and documentaries, handing powerful figures a veto over mockery. Side A first conceded in [A2.1] that monetized comedy podcasts should require consent, then reversed course under questioning in [A4.1] to claim fair use exempts them. Side B still won the resulting comparison: fair use is an after-the-fact defense that liability-averse platforms will not litigate before deleting speech, and ex-ante voice screening across uploads is unworkable without a universal voice registry—points Side A never answered. Although Side A smartly isolated non-deceptive commercial exploitation (labeled ads, fitness apps, navigation voices) in [A4.2]-[A4.4], Side B cleanly absorbed those cases under targeted commercial-appropriation rules enforced on complaint, and caught Side A attacking a strawman in Closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's trap across [B2.1]-[B2.3], [B3.1], and [B4.4]-[B4.8]: Side B pinned Side A on its contradiction over whether paid satire requires a politician's consent, and then showed that even if fair-use exceptions survive on paper, an ex-ante platform consent default forces risk-averse platforms to delete lawful satire upon receiving a legal threat.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): A built the cleaner path to victory by defending the motion as written: commercial consent gives satire targets a veto, while deception and commercial appropriation can be prohibited directly.
