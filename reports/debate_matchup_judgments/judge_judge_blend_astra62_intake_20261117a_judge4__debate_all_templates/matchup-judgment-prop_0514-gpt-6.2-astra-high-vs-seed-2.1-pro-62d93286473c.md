### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO built a precise, adaptable case: favor tested portability while still permitting integrated suites under one prime contractor, and let a demonstrated care advantage override that preference. That framing neutralized CON's core "fragmentation and finger-pointing" harm, because PRO showed that open interfaces do not require multiple vendors or help desks and that responsibility comes from the contract, not from standards compliance. CON kept asserting that daily harm outweighs "rare" lock-in. It never squarely answered the prime-contractor point and repeated the finger-pointing claim afterward. CON's strongest live line was that heavy portability scoring diverts engineering effort from safety features. PRO answered it adequately with market consultation and an explicit override for demonstrated clinical advantage. In Rebuttal 2, CON proposed a full migration test as an exit condition, which concedes that portability matters and needs testing. PRO then turned that concession by arguing that testing at exit comes too late to change the award. PRO's answers to the pressure questions were direct and clearly labeled, and its closing crystallized the decision cleanly. CON's closing largely recapped its earlier framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 in Rebuttal 2 held that compliance does not settle responsibility, the contract does: the prime contractor must restore the medication-order flow regardless of whose software failed. That dismantled CON's central finger-pointing harm. PRO's closing then turned CON's proposed exit-only migration test against it: "A financial penalty can settle who pays for a failed migration. It cannot tell a pharmacist which dose is current."
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A won a high-level debate by dismantling Side B's binary between daily frontline safety and switching portability while exposing a fatal flaw in Side B's contractual alternative. Side A showed that buyers can demand rigorous clinical workflow checks and single-prime-contractor SLA accountability while still favoring open specifications and pre-tested data handovers, leaving Side B's repeated finger-pointing charge unanswered after [A4.3]-[A4.4]. Although Side B made a strong dynamic case that heavily scoring portability diverts vendor R&D away from unscripted workflow edge cases, it undermined its defense of single-vendor lock-in by arguing that complex records are too messy for pre-award export tests while claiming termination migration clauses and financial penalties solve exit risk. Side A cleanly proved that waiting until exit to test portability leaves clinicians and educators holding broken records when it is too late to choose another bid.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): CON (A) wins with a sharper, more disciplined case. It never claimed portability is worthless. Instead it argued that portability should be priced into the bid comparison rather than given a standing preference that double-counts it. A's central distinction was that a rival being able to read the data is not the same as a rival being able to take over the job. A illustrated this concretely: the custody-shielded address on downloaded report cards, and a prescription hold that the releasing system must actually honor. That distinction exposed the gap in PRO's leverage story. PRO (B) relied on repeated assertions of "ransom," "predatory lock-in," and "false choice" rather than engaging the mechanism. Its opening examples ("dozens of districts," "six-figure fees") were vague and somewhat fake-precise. Its key A1 answer, that open standards let a third party build the missing hold function, was directly dismantled. PRO's A2 appeal to "democratic control" over unforeseen needs was met by A's alternative of demonstrating a representative change at bid time. PRO's closing argued that short-staffed buyers cannot enforce contracts. A had already turned this point: staff shortages make the integration burden of a portable bid more costly, not less. A also answered PRO's pressure questions honestly, conceding that contracts do not eliminate lock-in while explaining why that concession does not establish PRO's preference.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3 versus B4.1. PRO claimed open standards give immediate leverage because a developer could build the missing prescription-hold function. A responded that a developer can build a "hold" button but cannot make the prescribing system honor it without authorization and enforcement. A read-only open specification provides neither. This showed that PRO's leverage claim assumed the very capability that needed verifying.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won solidly by distinguishing data/interface openness from end-to-end workflow accountability and isolating what a standing procurement preference actually does. Side A raised real concerns about vendor lock-in, hold-up leverage, and unforeseen mandates, but repeatedly assumed that open standards automatically yield safe, plug-and-play modular replacement. Side B showed that open formats do not resolve concurrency, cross-module testing, or liability after upgrades, and turned Side A's staff-shortage argument against it: an incumbent knows a short-staffed clinic or district cannot safely integrate and warrant multi-vendor replacements on its own. Once buyers evaluate and credit verified switching options alongside ongoing operational burdens, an extra standing preference double-counts paper portability at the expense of dependable execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B4.3], answering Side A's claim [A4.1] that open interfaces let a clinic or third-party developer build a missing prescription-hold feature for immediate leverage, Side B observed that "a developer can build a button marked 'hold'; it cannot make the prescribing system honor that button," cleanly exposing the gap between readable open specifications and enforceable operational control.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A built the clearer route to victory by distinguishing readable data from a rival’s demonstrated ability to take over a safety-critical workflow, and by showing that genuine portability can be credited without a standing preference that double-counts it. Side B’s buyer-capacity and future-flexibility concerns remained relevant, but its answers repeatedly assumed that open standards themselves permit incremental replacement. A also answered pressure more directly and honestly, while B relied on sweeping lock-in claims and weakly grounded opening examples.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s prescription-hold exchange was decisive: a third party can build a “hold” button, but an open read interface neither authorizes the instruction nor makes the prescribing system honor it. This exposed the unsupported step in B’s claim of immediate, tangible leverage.
