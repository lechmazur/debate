### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) controlled the decisive clash. Its opening reframed the mandate as regulating an ad rather than a decision, and its first rebuttal exposed that PRO's discipline mechanism ran through coworker comparison — a different policy. A then landed the sharpest trap of the debate: PRO could not cite Colorado exclusion as proof of "bite" while booking excluded workers as beneficiaries, and PRO never fully subtracted that cost. When pressed by B's excellent Q1, A's answer (firms exit where exit is cheap, post hollow bands where it isn't) turned the apparent inconsistency into a distributional argument: costs on cushionless workers, gains on well-placed ones. A also won the magnitude fight, showing PRO's $3,000-$5,000 compounding was asserted while assuming the entire within-job residue was entry base with zero substitution into level and bonus. B's best route — the floor concession plus the "bands can't anchor nothing yet cap offers" consistency charge — was real but A's closing blunted it: the floor is a limit the discriminator sets itself. Both sides answered pressure questions directly with clear A1/A2 labels. B was strong and coherent, but A made the debate easier to decide and imposed more costs on B's live route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.3: the Colorado contradiction — "bite and lost matches are the same event" — which B tried to escape via remote-only exit and doomed-interview offsets, but A's A4.1/A4.6 split-behavior and distributional counter closed the loop.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B wins by better resolving the central comparison. A establishes plausible information and matching benefits, but never adequately explains why an employer-chosen floor substantially constrains discriminatory placement, and overextends its illustrative savings across every hire. B directly challenges that scaling and explains how hollow bands and costly exclusions can coexist. A's closing treats the floor concession as decisive without answering those limitations. B's unsupported claims about who bears exclusion costs, and its incomplete accounting of matching benefits, keep this competitive.

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side B won by demonstrating that a job-posting mandate regulates an advertisement rather than compensation decisions, leaving employers free to choose band widths, set the floor, re-level requisitions,shift pay into bonuses or equity, or exclude remote jurisdictions. Side A argued well on backfills and doomed interviews, but had to retreat from its opening claim on the source of pay gaps, stacked unsupported assumptions in its lifetime-dollar weighing, and rested its closing case on stopping below-floor offers without overcoming B's point that employers set the floor themselves and can routinely place discounted hires at the minimum.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B4.1]-[B4.2], Side B directly answered Side A's sharpest pressure question [A3.1]—why firms excluded Colorado instead of just posting meaningless wide bands—by showing selection across exit costs: firms excluded applicants where exit was cheap (remote roles) and posted $60,000-to-$190,000 bands where exit was impossible (local hiring), turning A's contradiction charge into proof that the rule destroys matches where it bites and goes hollow where workers stay.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) controlled the decisive frame: a dilemma in which bands either bind (cutting lowball offers where the gap lives) or don't bind (preserving the stretch-hire flexibility A says is lost). A's escape — bands sticky at the ceiling via incumbent pressure but slippery at the floor because employers set floors low and reclassify levels — was coherent and well-executed, and A's closing crystallized it sharply. But B's Rebuttal 2 symmetry attack and closing inversion (using A's own admission that floor violations are the easiest to enforce) left B with the cleaner residual path: the floor trims the bottom tail, base pay drives percentage-based bonuses, and the "destroyed match" is recoverable by level reassignment that A itself admits is unpoliced. A's substitution-to-referrals story was asserted but never substantiated despite B's direct pressure on posting volume. Both sides were format-compliant, well-grounded, and rhetorically strong; B simply did more comparative weighing and made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The floor/ceiling asymmetry exchange: A argued bands bind only at edges (sticky ceiling via incumbents, low self-set floors), and B countered that enforcement pressure is symmetric — the mechanism A invoked to make the ceiling rigid is the same one that raises low offers — then closed by inverting A's own concession that floor violations are the cheapest to police. A's closing response ("the public floor only bites against a number the employer chose for itself") was strong, but B's relabeling rebuttal to the destroyed-match story did more damage to A's efficiency route than A's clustering rebuttal did to B's equity route.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B won the central mechanism clash: an enforceable, employer-chosen floor need not correct unequal offers within a broad band. B also distinguished legal flexibility from public incumbent-pay pressures, answering A's proposed escape through relabeling. A established useful screening and information benefits, but its claimed durable equity gains depended on an insufficiently defended leap from floor enforcement to pay-gap compression. B's matching losses remained plausible rather than well-sized, making this a competitive victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest response was that symmetry in enforcement does not imply symmetry in effects: employers can set floors below discriminatory offers while public ceilings still create incumbent-pay pressures. Its closing directly answered A's relabeling argument by explaining why changing the job level would not necessarily remove those pressures.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won a high-level debate by winning the decisive clash over how posted bands behave at the floor versus the ceiling. Side A opened with a sharp case on prior-salary anchoring, compounding gains, and lost price-discrimination being a transfer, and landed a strong Rebuttal 1 dilemma asking how bands could be simultaneously too wide to compress pay and too rigid to allow stretch hires. However, after Side B's Q1 forced Side A to explain how parity occurs inside wide ranges, Side A staked its mechanism on symmetric enforcement at the posted floor. Side B cleanly dismantled that symmetry in Rebuttal 2 and Closing: employers choose the floor and level classification beforehand, allowing lowball offers to sit legally above a defensively low floor or in a lower level, whereas ceilings bind through current employees who see the public cap and demand raises if a firm stretches or bumps a role up for an exceptional candidate. Side B also squarely answered Side A's state job-board question with credible marginal distortions (remote-state exclusions, generic postings, and referral/agency substitution), while Side A's Closing misconstrued Side B's floor point and overlooked downward level sorting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing counter ([B4.4]-[B4.5], [B5.1]-[B5.6]) to Side A's symmetric-enforcement claim ([A4.4]-[A4.6], [A5.1]), showing that employers neutralize regulatory floor enforcement upstream by setting low floors and level assignments themselves, while internal incumbents enforce the ceiling whenever a firm tries to stretch for a role-expanding hire.
