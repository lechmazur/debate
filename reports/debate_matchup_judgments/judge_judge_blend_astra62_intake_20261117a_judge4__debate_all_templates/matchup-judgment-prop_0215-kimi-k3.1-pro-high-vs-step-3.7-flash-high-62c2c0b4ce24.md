### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): A built its case directly on the motion's own conditional language ("central role"), framing responsibility as proportional and survivor-directed rather than absolute or collective. The decisive moment came in Rebuttal 1, where A exposed that B's "state is primarily responsible" argument "proves too little": it justifies a larger state share, not a zero share for implicated churches. B never squarely answered this logical gap; instead B kept pivoting to implementation risks (litigation over "central role," states gaming contributions, successor-entity diffusion, coercion). These are real practical concerns, but they attack how the policy might be executed, not whether institutions with a genuinely voluntary central role owe anything—the core normative claim in the resolution. A's pressure questions forced this distinction into the open, and its answers (staged/negotiated assessments, protected minimum state obligations, audited totals) were generic but responsive, while B's answers mostly reiterated that hard cases exist without engaging the clear-case scenario A kept isolating. B's closing continued to argue "total resources could shrink" without ever conceding or refuting that the clearly central, voluntary, continuous institution should pay something. That asymmetry—A consistently narrowing to the clean case B could not neutralize—gives A the cleaner, more resolved path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 point that B's "government is primarily responsible" argument justifies a bigger state share but not a zero share for implicated churches ("proves too little") was the pivotal exchange; B never directly rebutted this framing and instead shifted to implementation-risk arguments for the rest of the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by effectively arguing that the proposition is conditional, meaning Side B's examples of coercion and lack of continuity are reasons to adjust liability in specific cases, not reasons to grant blanket immunity to institutions that voluntarily committed abuses. Side A also successfully defused Side B's 'crowding out' argument by framing state evasion as a reason to regulate the state, rather than an excuse for church impunity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal decisively neutralized B's strongest points by explaining that a conditional rule is not refuted by showing that its condition sometimes excludes liability.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B wins decisively by dismantling Side A's primary pragmatic argument. Side A argued that requiring church contributions would allow the state to reduce its own payouts, leaving survivors with less. Side B effectively trapped Side A by pointing out that the risk of future state misconduct is a reason to constrain the state, not a reason to grant blanket immunity to responsible churches. Side A never recovered from this logical flaw, merely repeating that the motion lacks a binding mechanism to stop the state. Side B also handled the vagueness and successor entity concerns well by clarifying that conditional rules require assessment, not blanket exemptions, and that hard cases do not defeat the core principle of institutional responsibility. Side B's rhetorical framing was sharp, particularly the line about giving the wrong party the benefit of the doubt.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to Side A's crowding out argument that state payouts would shrink. Side B correctly identified that using the risk of state evasion to excuse past institutional misconduct gives the wrong party the benefit of the doubt, completely neutralizing Side A's main offensive line.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): PRO opened strongly with a concrete, emotionally forceful case grounded in the Canadian residential school example, establishing a clear moral baseline: institutions that operated the abuse should fund repair. But CON's central structural objection—that "central role" conflates moral condemnation with legal apportionment, and that the real fight is over which specific legal entity (order, diocese, or state) owns the duty and the assets—was never fully answered. CON's pressure questions forced PRO to narrow its position (only the "operational" entity, only its assets, with enforceable deadlines and survivor-led allocation), and CON's final rebuttal and closing sharply exposed that this narrowed, workable version already requires exactly the entity-identification and evidentiary inquiry PRO had dismissed as a mere "stalling tactic." PRO's closing reasserted its obstruction narrative but never resolved this internal tension, while CON's closing crystallized it cleanly: "identifying that operator...is the very inquiry PRO calls an intolerable barrier." CON also kept its grounding more epistemically disciplined, relying on general legal reasoning rather than repeated unverified figures. The debate tightened around this liability-vs-quantum distinction, and CON's handling of it was the more decisive, unresolved point by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4.4–B4.6), showing that PRO's own "workable" fund (legislated operator identification, enforceable deadlines, survivor-led allocation) silently reintroduces the entity-tracing inquiry PRO had just called an unjustifiable barrier—an internal contradiction PRO's closing never addressed.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing a fatal contradiction in Side A's case: Side A argued that a categorical rule was necessary to bypass complex, obstruction-prone legal inquiries, but then clarified that its proposed fund would surgically target only the assets of the specific operational order. Side B brilliantly pointed out that identifying the correct operational entity and tracing its assets is the exact legal inquiry Side A claimed was an intolerable barrier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Turn 8, where Side B points out that Side A's promise to target only the operational order's assets assumes away the hard cases of mixed liability and relies on the very institutional investigation Side A is trying to avoid.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by exposing the mechanical and legal contradictions in Side B's proposal. Side A effectively demonstrated that Side B's "central role" threshold either risks unfair liability by association or requires the exact same granular entity identification and asset tracing that Side B criticized as an intolerable barrier. Side A's alternative—combining state guarantees for prompt survivor repair with anti-obstruction legal tools like adverse inferences—provided a more coherent and legally sound path to justice without the categorical overreach of the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled Side B's narrowed definition of liability by pointing out that "operational control" is precisely what is disputed among orders, dioceses, and the state, and that assuming the order is solely responsible without examination simply assumes away the hard cases. Side A also sharply noted that Side B's added safeguards require the exact same statutory design and entity identification they claimed was a barrier.
