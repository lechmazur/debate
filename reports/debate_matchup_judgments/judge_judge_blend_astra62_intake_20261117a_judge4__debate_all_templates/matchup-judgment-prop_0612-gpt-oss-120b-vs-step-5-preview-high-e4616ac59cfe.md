### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): CON wins a competitive debate by narrowing the clash to the "limit" half of the motion. Its central claim is that a nationality-based cap targets the source of money rather than the actual harm, which is coercive or concealed conditions. CON supported this with concrete reasons the cap fails both ways. It over-includes, because it would break benign bilateral partnerships. It under-includes, because a state can route money through proxies, foundations, or the uncapped remainder. CON also offered a coherent conduct-based alternative: universal disclosure, contract review, audits, and sanctions. It answered both pressure questions directly. Its main flaw was the "German lab with its own federal ministry" example, which describes domestic funding rather than foreign funding and so blunts part of the over-inclusion point. PRO had a clear causal story, built on Confucius Institutes and the risk of a single state dominating a lab's budget. Its best point was that foreign states hold non-financial leverage such as visa threats. However, PRO never answered the proxy-evasion objection. It contradicted itself by citing a consortium with 25-30% shares per state while claiming projects "comfortably" stay under a 5% per-state cap. Its closing restated "only a cap guarantees independence" without resolving these holes. PRO also lost three speeches to clipping, which is a real execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO's enforcement question. CON argued that a cap is not a detection mechanism: it reads no contract and can be evaded through foundations or proxies. It then paired this with the over- and under-inclusion framing. PRO's subsequent speeches never squarely answered the proxy point. That left CON's "false security" route to victory standing.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B won solidly by proving that a foreign-funding cap targets donor nationality rather than coercive conduct, making it both over-inclusive against specialized cross-border research (such as U.S. NIH partnerships) and under-inclusive against covert contract clauses, sub-cap grants, and proxy foundations. Although Side B carelessly included a lab funded by "its own federal ministry" among its foreign-partnership examples, Side A suffered far heavier logical and execution costs: shifting inconsistently between university-wide and lab-level 5% caps (contradicting its own 20–30% consortium example), conflating domestic defense contracts with private corporations, asking a backwards Q1 in Turn 5 that argued against its own motion, mislabeling reference IDs, and getting clipped in three speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pressure question [B3.1] and follow-up [B4.2]-[B4.3] decisively exposed the gap in Side A's cap: Side B honestly noted that no rule guarantees catching covert language, showed that a 5% ceiling reads no contracts and stops no proxy funding, and forced Side A in [A4.1] to rely on the very disclosure-and-audit mechanisms Side B advocated.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): B won narrowly by making the cleaner structural case: disclosure and COI rules can expose problems, but they do not stop one foreign government from becoming the dominant funder of a lab or center. B’s per-government cap directly addressed that concentration risk while preserving multinational collaboration. A’s conduct-over-nationality critique was sharp and exposed proxy/evasion limits, but it never fully answered why universal disclosure and COI review would neutralize decisive financial dependence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s clarification that the cap limits each foreign government’s share, not total foreign funding, blunted A’s consortium harm; A’s strongest reply was that caps do not read contracts or stop proxies, but that did not fully remove B’s anti-dominance point.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins clearly. It built a simple, coherent case around one asymmetry: legitimate collaboration survives disclosure, while capture depends on concealment. It applied that asymmetry consistently across the rounds. PRO answered both pressure questions directly. On risk, it proposed objective, published statutory markers. It also corrected CON's misdescription that PRO relies on internal ethics committees. PRO then exposed a real tension in CON's case: CON warns against state mandates but relies on statutory ethics bodies as its own remedy. CON raised a live concern about state politicization and the chilling effect of registries. However, its grounding was weak. It invoked an apparently invented "Foreign Influence Transparency Act" with specific consequences, made a dubious semiconductor-lab claim, and asserted unverified foreign "registries" causing freezes. Its second rebuttal was clipped, which cut off its financial weighing. CON's diplomatic-flashpoint answer was creative, but PRO neutralized it by noting that the leverage exists before disclosure. CON's closing largely restated earlier points without engaging that response.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's B4.3–B4.4 answer to CON's Q2. PRO showed that CON had misstated PRO's position on internal committees. It then turned CON's own remedy against it: CON condemns state mandates while depending on state-created statutory protections. That left CON's "state control" objection as a difference of degree rather than of principle.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A won solidly through sharper comparative weighing, stronger epistemic discipline, and cleaner execution across every round. Side B mounted a serious counter-case that public registries can turn benign collaborations into domestic geopolitical flashpoints and proposed statutorily insulated internal ethics boards instead. However, Side A met the discretion critique head-on in [A4.1] with auditable statutory markers (ministry affiliation, attached speech or curricular conditions, donor control over content) and showed why internal boards still fail to cap dependency or resist diplomatic clout. Side B damaged its own credibility by misattributing its own internal-committee position to PRO in [B3.2], citing dubious enacted legislation in [B2.2], conceding foreign funding's implicit reciprocity in [B4.1], and repeating earlier points into a word-limit clip in [B4.6] without engaging [A4.1].
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's one-two sequence in [A4.1]-[A4.3] and [A5.1]: A defused B's strongest critique on vague risk caps by supplying objective contractual criteria, exposed B's [B3.2] role-reversal blunder on internal ethics committees, and then turned B's [B4.1] admission that foreign state grants carry an implicit expectation of reciprocity into proof that disclosure exposes leverage rather than creating it.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B built the cleaner decisive clash: disclosure exposes pre-existing leverage, published risk criteria constrain state discretion better than opaque internal review, and limits cap dependency.
