### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): In a high-level debate from both sides, Side A won by proving that even with non-waivable statutory minimums and public budgets, someone still decides how a school splits campus dollars above the floor between shared protections and star premiums. Side B effectively pressed the tensions of cross-sport heterogeneity and annual roster turnover, forcing Side A to refine its bargaining structure with baseline sport protections and annual allocation reopeners. However, Side A neutralized Side B's central warning—that collective bargaining trades away baseline protections—by forcing the concession that statutory floors can bind unions and schools alike, while exposing that Side B's reliance on publicly set pool partitions still pre-caps star contracts and leaves the athletic department to draw the budget lines unilaterally.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Pressure Questions [A3.1-A3.2] and follow-through in [A4.5-A4.6] and [A5.1-A5.2] turned the debate: forcing Side B to concede in [B4.1] that statutory minimums can bind a union neutralized CON's tradable-rights critique, while [B4.2]'s reliance on unilaterally set school pool partitions undercut CON's critique of pre-capped premium pools and proved that transparency alone gives athletes no say in setting the split.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that formal exclusive bargaining is a poor fit for divided, high-turnover college rosters and that Side B’s own safeguards (binding external floors, sport assent, annual reopening) either fragment the exclusive representative or leave the least-leveraged athlete dependent on a vote she can lose. Side B made a sympathetic case for collective voice over unilateral departmental splits, but its hybrid never cleanly delivered durable campus-wide settlement without recreating the coercion and rigidity Side A identified. The clearer path to the motion as written was therefore the case against replacing floors plus individual choice with tradable majority bargains.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal, which answered both pressure questions directly and then showed that Side B’s assent rules, annual revisions, and non-tradable floors either move real protection outside bargaining or surrender the exclusive settlement Side B had promised.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): B won because it kept the decisive clash on who chooses the allocation above enforceable minimums. A sharply argued that non-tradable floors protect vulnerable athletes better than majoritarian bargaining, but never fully answered how athletes get a voice over budget lines above those floors. B’s closing crystallized the choice: compliance can enforce a split but cannot give athletes a say in drawing it.

B used that concession to say both systems can keep the same floor, but only PRO gives athletes a vote over above-floor allocations, which is where the real revenue-sharing choice lives.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A won a high-level exchange by proving that once schools directly share revenue, the central problem is who authors baseline terms across an adversarial payer-payee divide. Side B mounted a clever, original critique of collective bargaining—showing that negotiated roster caps and conference pay ceilings give schools planning certainty precisely by blocking willing coaches and unrepresented recruits from making deals—which successfully blunted Side A's secondary claim about institutional stability. However, rather than defending ad hoc NIL and compliance systems, Side B relied on a conference-imposed floor plus independent adjudication and never solved Side A's central critique: an adjudicator can police a late check, but cannot force self-interested conferences to write generous pay shares, hour limits, or post-eligibility medical definitions in the first place, nor do non-star athletes like swimmers have individual leverage to bargain above a stingy floor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side B argued in [B1.4] and [B2.3] that conference-mandated contract floors and independent adjudicators could protect athletes without union trade-offs, Side A's [A3.1] pressure question asked how athletes could revise inadequate conference-authored terms rather than merely enforce them. Side B's improvised reply in [B4.1]—letting athletes petition for a conference rules vote and block reductions—left initial generosity in the hands of the paying conferences, allowing Side A in [A4.3] and [A5.2] to crystallize why enforcement without co-authorship fails.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by making the decisive comparison concrete: direct payment justifies enforceable floors, coverage, and independent enforcement, but not a bargaining system whose school-stability benefit comes from letting today’s roster bind tomorrow’s willing coach and recruit. Side B never neutralized that cost. Its concession that an excluded recruit has no grievance under a lawful cap, plus the claim that later renegotiation or fair-representation duties suffice, left the lost season and non-voter problem intact. B’s authorship frame was strong but less precise than A’s floor-versus-ceiling weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 distinction: when a school will not fund a spot, neither system creates one; when the coach has the money and wants the recruit, only the bargained cap turns that mutually wanted offer into a non-grievable violation, and next year’s renegotiation cannot restore this season.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won narrowly by making the decisive clash about authorship rather than mere enforcement. B answered the mandate objection with election-based representation, treated compliance as conflicted, and explained why conference floors plus adjudication protect athletes without giving them continuous negotiating power. Side A’s cap and excluded-recruit argument was sharp and stayed live, especially the point that planning certainty may come from ceilings that bind future recruits, but B’s comparison to unilateral cuts and its emphasis that bargaining makes limits time-limited, renegotiable, and contestable blunted that attack enough.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive: it answered the pressure questions directly, conceded the lawful-cap grievance point but compared it to unilateral cuts, and reframed the debate around who authors the terms rather than who merely polices them.
