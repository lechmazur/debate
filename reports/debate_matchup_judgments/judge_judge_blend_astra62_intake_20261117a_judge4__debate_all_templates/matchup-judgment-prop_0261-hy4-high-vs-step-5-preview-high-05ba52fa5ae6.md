### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins with a coherent, bounded case. Its core claims are that adhesion "non-transferable" terms create a death-triggered forfeiture, that "buy" and "your library" language invites reliance, and that inheritance law already overrides restrictions that defeat property incidents. PRO also answered CON's main objections directly. On privacy, it proposed a library-only transfer with no passwords, messages or payment history. On next of kin, it deferred to ordinary estate and probate rules, with pauses allowed for disputed claims. On scope, it argued that the same private-use entitlement applies per asset type, with no duty of permanence. CON's strongest material was the license/lease framing and the "service tail" cost argument. The service-tail point is real: an heir's lifetime extends the vendor's obligation. But CON's scope attack contradicted itself. It first accused PRO of narrowing the motion, then accused PRO of stretching it. Its closing "decisive concession" was weak: shutdown risk applying to heirs does not defeat a motion about passing assets to next of kin, not guaranteeing permanence. CON also conceded that labeling does not restore a library after death, which left PRO's forfeiture harm standing. PRO made the clash easier to decide and weighed more clearly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2 did the most damage. It reframed "one verified migration" as a method rather than a scope limit, covering software seats and in-game items with the same bounded rights. It routed next-of-kin conflicts through existing estate law. It then pressed CON's unanswered questions: CON had no price benefit beyond the vendor's cancellation advantage, and labeling warns too late to restore a library. CON's reply admitted labeling cannot resurrect a library and fell back on a contradictory scope attack.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B wins a competitive, high-level debate by establishing the cleaner comparative framework on contract structure, scope, and market trade-offs. Side A effectively neutralized CON's privacy and probate-chaos objections via clean entitlement-only migration and exposed CON's closing overstatement on making vendors insurers against shutdown. However, Side B persuasively distinguished inert physical books from ongoing server-dependent licenses, showed that mandating inheritance across the motion's full scope (including software seats, in-game items, and live-service assets) imposes real classification, account-splitting, and multi-generational service-tail costs, and severed PRO's deceptive-"buy"-button critique via point-of-sale labeling. Side A left unanswered why buyers wanting durability cannot choose physical or DRM-free options, ignored the risk that mandating inheritance on purchases pushes vendors into pure subscriptions, and prematurely faulted CON in Turn 7 for not yet answering Turn 5's questions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B4.1]-[B4.4], Side B directly answered Side A's sharp pressure questions ([A3.1]-[A3.2]) by showing that even single-heir replacement creates an indefinite multi-generational service and compliance tail across heterogeneous digital assets, while clear point-of-sale labeling cures surprise deception without outlawing cheaper lifetime licenses.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by defending a bounded reading of the motion—transfer of the same private-use entitlement, with no added copy, resale, copyright, or duty to outlive a service shutdown—and by weighing modest verification costs against death-triggered forfeiture after “buy/your library” reliance. Side B landed real pressure on contract duration, heterogeneous assets, and disclosure, but overstated A’s position as an insurance-of-permanence mandate and never fully neutralized the core injustice that the seller keeps the price while the paid library vanishes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 answers on scope and next-of-kin, then turns B’s own permanence concession into a limit rather than a collapse: the heir takes only what still exists, so the live choice is forfeiture versus bounded passage.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON wins a competitive debate on sharper conceptual framing and better adaptation across rounds. PRO opened with vivid stakes and a memorable shelf/server analogy. Its line "a server should not be a forfeiture clause" stayed a live harm claim throughout. CON, however, consistently pressed one distinction: a book passes as an owned thing, while a non-transferable licence can reach an heir only if the vendor creates and polices new access for that heir. CON then used PRO's own safeguards against it. Proof of death, provider-run transfer, allowed fees, the expired-remainder exception, specific performance, and the withholding of annotations, playlists, saved games and metadata became evidence that PRO's guarantee is a curated, compulsory re-license rather than succession. That argument is partly definitional. Even so, CON tied it to concrete cost and privacy mechanisms, and PRO's answers to those mechanisms were procedural assurances rather than refutations. PRO answered the pressure questions directly. In its closing, though, it mischaracterized CON as demanding "zero vendor cooperation" and cited nonexistent reference IDs [C4.1, C4.2], both real execution costs. PRO's family-photo example also sat awkwardly within a motion about purchased assets. Overall, CON narrowed and resolved the clash more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turned PRO's privacy concession ([B4.3]: withholding annotations, playlists and saved games, and redacting shared photos) together with its specific-performance answer ([B4.1]). CON argued that what passes is a court-compelled, vendor-curated substitute rather than the library as bought. This exchange undercut PRO's core "it's just succession" framing.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B (CON) wins a clear, competitive debate. Side A framed consumer expectations memorably around the "shelf vs. server" analogy and credibly argued that relying on voluntary legacy settings creates forfeiture by default for unsophisticated buyers. However, Side B prevailed on three decisive fronts. First, Side B explained how single-user non-transferability enables cheaper personal pricing (such as student software seats and discounted courses) and how mandating death-transfer infrastructure imposes verification, migration, and policing costs that push vendors toward higher prices or exempt subscriptions—a mechanism Side B detailed in [B2.5]-[B2.6] and Side A dropped for the rest of the debate. Second, Side B pressed the privacy risks of passing intimate libraries and third-party shared photos to statutory next of kin upon silence, forcing Side A into awkward curation and redaction patches. Third, Side B synthesized Side A's accumulated concessions (allowing fees, awkwardly saying "cross-life rights can be prohibited" in [A2.6], excepting expired remainders, withholding notes/saves/metadata, and redacting shared photos) to show that Side A was no longer passing the library as bought, but mandating a vendor-curated substitute. Side B also maintained cleaner execution, while Side A cited nonexistent IDs ([C4.1, C4.2]) in Closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B4.3]-[B4.6] (building on [B2.4]), Side B turned Side A's own defensive guardrails—allowing verification fees, prohibiting "cross-life rights" [A2.6], excepting expired remainders [A4.2], withholding annotations, playlists, saved games, and metadata, and redacting shared photos [A4.3]—directly against Side A's core thesis, showing that the more process Side A added to fix cost and privacy flaws, the less its rule resembled inheriting a physical shelf and the more it became a compulsory, vendor-curated re-licensing substitute.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by keeping the live clash on altered entitlement rather than mere inconvenience. PRO’s own later concessions—expired remainders, withheld annotations and metadata, fees, and compelled vendor performance—showed the “guarantee” manufactures a narrower, court-backed substitute instead of passing a right the decedent already held. B answered the pressure questions cleanly and exposed A’s closing mischaracterization and nonexistent citations, leaving compulsory re-licensing as the clearer reason to reject the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4’s weighing that PRO’s privacy carve-outs, fee allowances, and specific-performance remedy convert the proposal from succession into a curated, vendor-administered re-license.
