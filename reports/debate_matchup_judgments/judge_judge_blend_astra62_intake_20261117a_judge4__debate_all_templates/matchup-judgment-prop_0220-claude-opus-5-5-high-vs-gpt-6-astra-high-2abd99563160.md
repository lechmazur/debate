- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A won the decisive clashes. A answered both pressure questions directly in A1/A2 format and forced two honest concessions from B: that deductions deliver nothing to non-responders, and that B has not shown debt offsets outweigh enrollment gains. But B's case increasingly rested on "can" and "could" possibilities rather than a positive comparison, never sized the responsive-saver group, and only answered the relabeling evidence with assertion. B's closing crystallized its frame but did not neutralize A's weighing. A made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.7) was decisive: A directly answered both pressure questions, conceded imperfect evidence honestly, then narrowed CON's case to a small triple-qualified group while showing the procrastinator group was larger and more valuable — a frame B's B4.1 pushback ("some procrastinators, not your much larger group") could not undo given A's participation evidence.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A built the much stronger comparative path to victory on net new wealth, targeting, and marginal security value. Side A showed not only that automatic enrollment moves participation from roughly half to most workers with sticky wealth gains, but also that expanding tax deductions mostly subsidizes existing saving and account reshuffling while delivering the smallest benefit to lower-bracket workers. Side B mounted a smart, disciplined critique around revolving debt, liquidity sequencing, distress-triggered opt-outs, and middle-income adequacy—and Side A had a minor sequencing slip in [A4.7] claiming Side B had not answered Q1 before Side B's answer turn—but Side B's candid concessions in [B4.1] and [B4.2] left its case resting on qualitative possibilities against Side A's demonstrated comparative scale.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: When Side B pressed Side A in [B3.1]-[B3.2] to prove net wealth superiority after borrowing and to weigh existing middle-income savers against new low-rate enrollees, Side A answered in [A4.1]-[A4.6] by pairing empirical evidence on deduction reshuffling and modest secured-debt offsets with sharp inframarginal-cost and diminishing-marginal-utility weighing, then sealed the clash in [A5.1] by showing Side B's own "affordability" frame favors higher tax brackets least in need of help.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A wins because it keeps the comparison on the margin that decides long-run security: non-participants for whom larger deductions deliver nothing, versus existing savers whose tax-driven gains are mostly inframarginal and poorly targeted. ### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Both sides were grounded and disciplined, with no fake sourcing. B built the clearer comparative frame ("defaults raise headcounts; deductions raise wealth"), skillfully aggregated A's own concessions (uncovered workers, debt offsets, anchoring) into a narrowed PRO case, and answered pressure directly with the strong pre-tax asymmetry ($100 invested for $75 of take-home pay). A's closing mounted the best single strike, noting B's example shows the existing deduction rather than the increment from increasing it, but B's closing recovery (each sacrificed dollar buys more) blunted it. A was honest and mechanically consistent but increasingly defensive, and never pressed hard enough on the plausible size of the procrastinating-but-solvent group. B made the debate easier to decide with sharper clash creation and more memorable weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answer A1 (B4.1): responding to A's pressure question about whether tax windfalls are consumed, B showed the subsidy is embedded in the pre-tax contribution structure and compounds inside the account automatically — directly neutralizing A's balance-sheet symmetry argument, with A's closing counter (A5.2) only partially dismantling it.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins a sharp, closely contested debate through superior economic precision and cleaner answers on the decisive behavioral clash. Side A kept the comparison anchored on the actual policy margin: existing tax advantages already apply under automatic enrollment, so increasing deductions merely sweetens a reward that still requires taxable income and affirmative action—a tax-liability limitation Side B never answered, despite championing low-coverage gig and cash-strapped workers. Side A also dismantled Side B's claim that deduction tax savings compound automatically inside accounts without behavioral change, while giving persuasive answers on why immediate cash squeezes prompt opt-outs more readily than distant retirement prompts enrollment and why solvent procrastinators still face real retirement insecurity. Side B kept the round competitive with punchy rhetoric and effective pressure on coverage gaps and credit-card debt offsets, narrowing Side A's margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's setup in [A3.1] and takedown in [A5.2]-[A5.3] of Side B's [B4.1] accounting claim: Side A showed that contributing $100 at a $75 paycheck cost puts $25 into take-home pay rather than extra money inside the account, meaning increased deductions do not automatically compound unless retained or paired with an active choice to contribute more—while reminding the reader that existing pre-tax advantages are available under automatic enrollment too.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by turning Side A’s concessions on coverage gaps, debt-financed contributions, and anchoring into a narrowed residual case, then showing deductions reach more households, improve the return on every contributed dollar, and do not force anyone into a worse balance sheet. Side A’s inertia mechanism and closing correction of the tax-accounting example were sharp, but they did not restore a comparative advantage large enough to outweigh those leaks.
