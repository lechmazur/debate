### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Both sides were competent and stayed on the motion. PRO built a clear cost-internalization frame: platforms profit while the public absorbs emergency and welfare costs. It also adapted well by proposing a portable multi-platform pool to answer the administration objection. CON, however, pressed harder on the mechanism question that decided the debate: what a ten-hour contribution actually buys, and what happens to the marginal shift. CON's dilemma was sharp. Either a small prorated contribution buys only partial premium support, or taxpayers backstop the gap, in which case proration becomes a public entitlement. PRO never squarely resolved this. Its claims that "ten hours buys ten hours of protection" and that a proportional contribution leaves the shift's net value intact were asserted rather than defended. PRO's anti-rationing rule was persuasively countered: platforms can lengthen minimum shifts, batch assignments, or exit thin routes without formally capping hours. PRO also left CON's duplication point about full-time workers largely unanswered. CON had weaknesses of its own. Its threshold argument fits awkwardly with proration, which removes benefit cliffs. Its own alternative also shifts costs to taxpayers, as PRO noted. Still, CON's later speeches narrowed the clash more effectively, while PRO's closing mostly restated the internalization frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A4.1/A4.5 against B4.1/B4.3. PRO argued that a public pool absorbs fixed overhead and that anti-rationing rules protect short shifts. CON replied that pooling "fixes paperwork, not arithmetic": a small contribution either buys only partial premium support or needs a taxpayer backstop. CON added that no rule can make an unprofitable short shift profitable, so platforms can exclude workers without breaking the letter of the mandate. PRO did not answer either point effectively.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Both sides executed cleanly and followed format, including proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 structure. The decisive clash was whether prorated benefits are arithmetically workable and whether mandates cause hour rationing. Side B's public multi-platform pool was a reasonable answer to fixed overhead, but Side A's follow-up—that low-hour contributions either buy unusable partial premium support or require a taxpayer backstop, making proration symbolic or a new entitlement—was pointed and never fully resolved. On rationing, Side B's enforcement answer (ban caps, audit schedules) was materially undermined by Side A's realistic escape-hatch response: lengthening minimums, batching assignments, and tightening acceptance evade the letter while producing exclusion, and "cutting a shift sacrifices revenue" does not answer threshold behavior. Side A also landed the duplication problem for workers with other jobs, which Side B never squarely answered, and resolved Side B's timing attack on tax credits via advance payment. Side B retained a live route on cost internalization—the socialized ER-care externality was its strongest persisting point—but its core mechanisms were more damaged than Side A's alternative. Slight edge to Side A on rebuttal quality and unresolved burdens.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 [A4.5], showing that Side B's anti-rationing rule (remit per hour, ban caps, audit) cannot make an unprofitable short shift profitable, since platforms can lengthen minimums, batch assignments, or exit thin routes—this defused Side B's main answer to the mandate's employment effects and set up Side A's closing.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): A wins the economic tradeoff. B's portable pool substantially answered the administrative objection, but B then confused preserving revenue with preserving profitability and never adequately weighed potential lost earnings against benefit gains. A explained why cap bans and audits cannot prevent other access-reducing adjustments, while defending protection independent of platform hours. A overstated benefit indivisibility and did not establish the scale of job losses, keeping the win competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest response was that banning shift caps cannot make an unprofitable shift profitable: platforms can change assignments or withdraw routes instead. This directly damaged B's claim that sacrificing trip revenue makes rationing irrational, which B repeated rather than repaired in closing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO built a clear cost-internalization frame and adapted well under pressure. Its strongest move was clarifying that proration means a scaling funding duty paid into portable accounts tied to engaged or dispatched time. That answered CON's binary-coverage and attribution questions directly. It also exposed a weakness in CON's "threshold" and hour-cap story: under true proration there is no cliff to cap below. CON did raise legitimate points. Its incidence argument (benefits deducted from pay, harming supplemental earners) and its point about uncovered idle time were real pressure. However, CON's second rebuttal cited nonexistent reference IDs (C1.1, C2.1, C1.2, C2.3), which is an execution and grounding cost. It also asserted that funding "codifies dependence on a single platform" despite PRO's portable, multi-source design. CON never squarely answered the adverse-selection critique of its opt-in alternative. It shifted to "government-backed portable" benefits and then reverted to "opt-in" in its closing. PRO's answer on incidence ("workers already pay") was somewhat assertive, but on balance PRO left the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive exchange. It reframed proration as a scaling funding duty into portable health and retirement accounts and defined attributable hours as engaged or dispatched time. Together these neutralized CON's two pressure questions and weakened the hour-cap and reclassification line. CON's reply pressed the unpaid-waiting gap but leaned on nonexistent references and did not engage the portable-account mechanism.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won the decisive clashes. B answered both pressure questions directly in A1/A2 format with coherent mechanisms: a portable, hours-scaled funding duty avoids the binary-coverage problem, and an engaged/dispatched-time rule resolves multi-apping attribution. B's "if platforms cap hours, that proves the hours carry real costs" flip repeatedly absorbed CON's strongest threat, and the closing crystallized the who-absorbs-the-cost weighing cleanly. Side A (CON) argued well—especially on incidence and the binary nature of health insurance—but its core reclassification claim remained assertion-heavy after B's statutory carve-out answer, its alternative (portable benefits funded "outside the platform-hour relationship") was never given a funding source and stood vulnerable to B's adverse-selection attack, and Rebuttal 2 cited nonexistent reference IDs ([C1.1], [C2.1], [C1.2], [C2.3]), a real grounding and execution cost. B left fewer unanswered burdens and made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The binary-health-proration exchange: A's Q1 forced B to explain how insurance can be prorated at all; B's A1 answer (accruing portable contributions toward an eligibility threshold rather than fractional coverage) defused the hour-cliff dilemma, while A's counter that this is "the same mandate with different branding" reasserted rather than refuted, leaving B's design standing.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): B wins by distinguishing prorated funding from fractional insurance coverage and providing a concrete engaged-time allocation rule. A never established why these obligations necessarily produce reclassification, rigid shifts, or threshold-based hour caps; its later speeches largely repeated those predictions despite portability. A’s strongest objection—reduced cash pay for workers prioritizing immediate income or already holding coverage—remained incompletely answered, limiting the margin. Nevertheless, B answered implementation pressure more directly and preserved the stronger case for baseline protection. A’s nonexistent reference IDs also weakened its grounding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B answered the multi-app objection by assigning contributions to engaged or dispatched time rather than simultaneous passive logins. A identified uncovered waiting time but did not show why that defeated the allocation rule. Its accompanying claim that benefits create dependence on a single platform also failed to address B’s portable, pooled funding proposal.
