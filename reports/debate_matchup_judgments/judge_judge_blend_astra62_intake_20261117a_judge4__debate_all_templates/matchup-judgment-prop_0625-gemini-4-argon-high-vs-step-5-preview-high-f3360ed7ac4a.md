### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a competitive debate by holding the central clash: who sets the rules for a shared stratosphere. Its "who writes and enforces those rules?" challenge pushed CON toward domestic permit regimes. PRO then reframed that outcome as "legalized unilateralism," in which the most permissive state sets the baseline and nobody technically breaks a law. CON never fully neutralized this. Its A1 conceded that nothing in its framework fully stops an obstructionist state, and "transparent illegality" does not fit a case where the permissive testing is lawful. CON had real strengths. Its symmetry argument was sharp: if domestic agencies can enforce a zero-injection ban, they can enforce permit caps. Its distinction between procedural rules and science-dependent thresholds was a reasonable answer to PRO's Q2. Even so, PRO kept the pressure on: if regulators can impose hard size caps today, those caps can be codified internationally first. PRO paid a cost for openly accepting a potentially indefinite pause under obstruction, which strains the word "temporary." CON flagged this but did not fully press it in closing. Both sides cited nonexistent reference IDs, with CON doing so more often. CON's closing also loosely described PRO as conceding a point that was actually CON's own concession. Overall, PRO made the decisive comparison cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B4.4 and B5.2 turned CON's own Q2 back on it. If a major power blocks a treaty, PRO's moratorium still holds as a bright line. In CON's world, by contrast, that same state writes generous domestic "research" caps and escalates staged trials lawfully. CON's best counter was the symmetry point in A4.3 that domestic tools can enforce either regime. That answer addresses enforcement capacity, not who sets legitimate standards, so it leaves PRO's legitimacy argument standing.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A built the cleaner path by turning CON's own regulatory alternative into its main vulnerability: absent a binding international framework, “auditable permits” and “hard size caps” collapse into domestic permissions, letting the most permissive state legalize unilateral testing. B’s parity arguments about enforcement and scientific freeze were plausible, but they did not resolve who sets shared caps or stops jurisdictional shopping. A also exploited the tension between B’s promise of immediate hard caps and B’s later claim that size thresholds require outdoor data. The scientific-freeze concern mattered, but A’s governance-first framing and minimal-cost argument neutralized enough of it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s dilemma around CON’s permit regime was decisive: either the oversight and size caps are international, which concedes that binding governance must come first, or they are domestic, which legalizes unilateral testing. B never escaped that fork.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the shared-sky problem decisive: without a binding international standard, Side A's domestic permits amount to legalized unilateralism rather than controlled research.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's closing turn of A's "transparent illegality" into legalized unilateralism, paired with the size-cap inconsistency, was the most damaging exchange.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON wins by forcing a scale distinction that PRO never recovered from. PRO opened by claiming that even small tests could alter rainfall and circulation. After CON's micro-scale challenge, PRO conceded that a few kilograms are "too small to shift climate." PRO then rebuilt its case around permission, precedent and purpose. That fallback has some force, especially the point that the first national permit sets a precedent. CON turned it effectively, though, with the identical-act problem: the same release would be legal as tracer science but banned as geoengineering research purely because of the scientist's intent. PRO did answer the pressure questions in the required A1/A2 format. However, its response on ratification thresholds and holdout vetoes stayed abstract ("delay is not a title deed") and did not show how the framework avoids decades of stalemate. CON also kept a live offensive route: field data could reveal that the technology is a hazardous dead end, and a ban leaves the world blind if some state later deploys anyway. PRO's closing was clipped, which is a further execution cost. Both sides used plausible, unsourced background claims without fake citations.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's A4.3 and its closing reply to PRO's A1 in B4.1. CON seized on PRO's admission that "the dust may be the same" and argued that PRO's moratorium criminalizes physically identical, zero-impact acts based solely on research purpose. This exposed PRO's shift from physical harm to symbolic precedent, and PRO's purpose-based distinction never fully answered it.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won by exposing A's retreat from physical harm to intent-based governance. A's opening warned that small tests could shift rainfall, but later conceded micro-releases were not physically threatening. B turned that concession into a decisive test: identical low-impact releases would be legal or illegal only by research purpose, which B framed as regulatory absurdity. A's global-commons and precedent argument still had some force, but B neutralized it with physical-threshold permitting and the need for empirical baselines to design governance. B also answered pressure questions directly, while A's clipped closing reduced its comparative clarity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest rebuttal was using A's concession that the dust could be the same and that the physical particles were not the threat. It reframed the moratorium as policing scientific intent rather than preventing real transboundary harm, and A never fully escaped that framing.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by forcing Side B off its opening claim that small releases can alter climate, then making the remaining purpose-and-precedent case look like a ban on physically identical, zero-impact measurements. Side B kept a real commons-consent argument and answered both pressure questions, but never showed why a two-kilogram tracer creates an irreversible right to alter the atmosphere, or how “until a binding framework” avoids an open-ended veto. Side A’s identical-balloon weighing, plus the clipped close, made the choice clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first rebuttal caught B’s concession that a few kilograms are too small to shift climate, then used the identical-balloon contrast to turn “permission, not mass” into intent-policing that B never escaped.
