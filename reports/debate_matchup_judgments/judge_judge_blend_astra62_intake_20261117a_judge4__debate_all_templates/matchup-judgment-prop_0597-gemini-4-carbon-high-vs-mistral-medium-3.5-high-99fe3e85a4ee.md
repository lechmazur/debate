### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO wins solidly. It built a clear case on families who cannot avoid transmitting disease, the restoration of ordinary alleles, the monogenic boundary, and regulated permission versus ineffective bans. It then adapted well across rounds. PRO's pressure questions exposed real tensions in CON's position: why fetal therapy counts as ethical while germline repair counts as a rights violation, and why CON's multi-generational trial standard would not also condemn the alternatives CON itself endorses. PRO's A1/A2 answers were direct and concrete: it separated validation of the editing process from validation of the restored sequence, and it proposed closed treaty schedules and delisting to contain expansion. CON's opening was clipped. Its later speeches largely repeated the consent, mirage, slippery-slope, and 2018 China points without adapting. CON never squarely answered the argument that the edit restores an existing allele, which undercuts its claim that the result is an "artificial genetic line." Its pressure questions were thin and mostly restated its opening. Its closing was an assertion of an absolute principle rather than weighing. Both sides made minor reference errors: CON cited [P2.3-P2.4] and PRO cited [C4.3, C4.5]. These do not change the outcome.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 and Rebuttal 2 turned CON's safety standard against CON's own alternatives. If an edit is unsafe without multi-generational human trials, then donor gametes and embryo adoption, which create untested gene combinations, would also fail that test. CON replied that those methods use "unmodified" material. That reply left PRO's point standing: a corrected gene is the ordinary human allele already carried across billions of genetic backgrounds.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): B established an unmet reproductive need and offered concrete conditional safeguards, while directly challenging A’s absolute consent principle. A largely repeated the natural/artificial distinction without explaining why it outweighs preventing severe inherited disease, demanded certainty rather than defending an appropriate safety threshold, and failed to answer B’s closed-list safeguards. B overstated what preclinical testing can establish and what rogue experiments prove about prohibition; both sides also used invalid reference IDs. Those weaknesses limit the margin, but A’s repetitive responses and clipped opening leave B clearly ahead.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B argued that nobody consents to inherited disease and that donor conception also deliberately shapes descendants’ genomes. A answered by labeling editing “artificial,” but never established why that distinction creates an absolute rights violation while its preferred alternatives remain permissible.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): A built the clearer winning path: it tied the motion to concrete families, answered consent with autonomy-from-disease, separated process verification from the restored allele, and forced B’s impossible safety standard onto IVF/donor alternatives. B’s intergenerational consent principle remained live but became repetitive, and it never fully solved why absolute prohibition beats enforceable conditional rules, especially after A used the China example against blanket bans. B’s clipped opening was also an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it answered the safety pressure by distinguishing verification of editing quality from the ordinary restored allele, then showed B’s multi-generational-trial burden would also condemn the donor-gamete and IVF alternatives B defended.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by establishing early that embryo selection (PGD) already prevents transmission for nearly all affected families, and it pressed that redundancy point consistently. PRO's case shrank to rare homozygous couples who want a child genetically related to both parents. CON reframed that remaining benefit memorably as a parental preference rather than a medical rescue. PRO's closing then explicitly made this its single decisive reason, which validated CON's framing. CON also landed the safety catch-22: multigenerational human data requires creating edited human lineages first. PRO's reply of "phased early human trials" effectively conceded that point. CON also exposed an inconsistency in PRO's boundary for "serious" disease: early-onset criteria versus the adult-onset Huntington's example and editing healthy carriers. PRO kept repeating "moral failure" rhetoric and "guaranteed disease" weighing that CON had already neutralized. PRO also misstated what PGD achieves for Huntington's. Its speeches did not adapt across rounds. Both sides cited some nonexistent reference IDs, which is a minor cost on each side.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A2.2 and A4.2. CON showed that a PGD-selected embryo lacks the Huntington's mutation entirely, and that the real alternative is a healthy child via PGD or donor gametes, not "guaranteed disease." This collapsed PRO's central risk-versus-certainty weighing and forced PRO to rest its closing on dual genetic relatedness alone.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): A wins by dismantling B's disease-versus-editing comparison and isolating the strongest remaining benefit: dual genetic relatedness where embryo selection cannot help. A candidly concedes that exception; B never adequately defends its importance against residual inherited risk, repeatedly returning to inevitable suffering despite available alternatives. A overstates prohibition's enforceability and its validation objection is weakened by the motion's safety condition. Both also misuse reference IDs. Nevertheless, B's errors about embryo selection and its inadequate response on descendant consent leave A with the substantially stronger comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A directly corrects B's claim that embryo selection cannot end Huntington's transmission: selecting an embryo without the mutation also excludes its subsequent inheritance. That rebuttal removes B's flagship comparison and forces its case toward exceptional couples, where B asserts rather than establishes why preserving both genetic connections outweighs A's objections.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won by reframing A’s broad disease-prevention case into a much narrower claim about rare homozygous couples wanting dual genetic relatedness, then showing that PGD and donor gametes already prevent serious disease without germline risk. B also kept pressure on the safety catch-22 and on the lack of a credible international enforcement mechanism. A had a live rare-case benefit but never adequately explained why that benefit outweighed irreversible lineage risk or how permission would hold definitional lines across states.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttal sequence exposing that A’s urgent suffering case had narrowed to avoiding donor gametes, then using that concession to undermine A’s risk-versus-disease weighing.
