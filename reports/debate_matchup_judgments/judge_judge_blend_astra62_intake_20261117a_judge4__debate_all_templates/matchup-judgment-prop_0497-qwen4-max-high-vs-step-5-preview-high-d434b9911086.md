### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins on a burden it set early and kept live: name a mechanism that reliably captures software's hidden components without an obligation to deposit. CON (A) raised real problems: - Live services lack a stable edition. - Holding source code under embargo creates leak risk. - Deciding what counts as "culturally significant" is hard. - Archives would need emulation capacity. PRO answered each of these directly: - **Editions:** deposit milestone builds, an "edition" model. - **Operation:** custody plus on-demand emulation, not a duty to run services forever. - **Selection:** broad default intake with appraisal later, as in web archiving. PRO then reframed all of these as costs of doing deposit well, not reasons to leave the material outside the archive. The decisive moment came in Rebuttal 2. CON conceded there is no consent-free mechanism for a private studio's server code. It then proposed compulsory licensing in insolvency, which PRO convincingly recast as the same compulsion arriving after the material is already lost. CON's argument that public release is safer than restricted custody was somewhat plausible but did not resolve the inconsistency PRO exposed. CON's closing mostly restated the snapshot and edition objection. PRO's closing crystallized the comparison cleanly: a recoverable cost (money and backlog) versus a permanent loss (the work itself).
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response to Ruffle and Flash was the sharpest. It argued that an emulator cannot conjure files nobody saved, so the example shows accession must come before engineering ("a projector without film"). That turned CON's best example around. It set up the later exchange where CON admitted there is no consent-free route to private server code. PRO then exploited CON's insolvency licensing proposal as "obligation scheduled after deletion."
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a high-level debate by establishing an asymmetric burden Side A never overcame: without mandated accession of underlying binaries and build documentation while a studio is intact, future emulators have nothing to run and shuttered digital culture is permanently lost. Side A raised incisive objections regarding software mutability and the security risks of holding proprietary pipelines in state vaults, but weakened its case by repeating a refuted claim that archives would have to operate live services perpetually and fumbling B's second pressure question on leak risk. Side B cleanly answered Side A's operational pressure via milestone builds and on-demand researcher emulation, decisively won the Flash/Ruffle clash, and turned Side A's late pivot to insolvency licensing into a concession that state compulsion is necessary—just scheduled too late under Side A's model.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Turn 3 inversion of Side A's Flash/Ruffle example ([B2.1]-[B2.2]), reinforced by the Q1-to-Closing insolvency trap ([B3.1], [B5.1]-[B5.2]). After Side A argued that Ruffle proved engineering rather than deposit saves software, Side B pointed out that Ruffle only worked because Flash files sat on public web servers volunteers could copy, making emulation without accession "a projector without film." Side B then pressed that exact gap in Q1, forcing Side A to concede that voluntarism cannot reach private server code and to propose compulsory licensing in insolvency—which Side B exposed in Closing as the same compulsion arriving only after catastrophe.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): A won a clear but competitive debate by identifying deposit’s irreplaceable function: securing private, perishable components before voluntary or engineering-based preservation can reach them. B’s versioning, security, and live-service objections were substantial, and its targeted rescue tools narrowed the dispute, but they did not provide comparable coverage or show why incomplete custody is worse than permanent loss. A’s irreversibility weighing therefore survived more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s framing of emulation as the machine and deposit as the supply line, followed by B’s admission that no consent-free alternative reaches a private studio’s server code, decisively exposed the gap in B’s case. B’s blanket-versus-targeted distinction limited but did not erase that damage.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins a competitive debate by setting the terms of the clash and holding them throughout. Its three opening problems all stayed live: software works die from missing dependencies rather than missing permissions, "culturally significant" is unworkable as a legal trigger, and the duty lands on whoever holds the least of the work. PRO's best line was timing. Deposit captures material before liquidation, and engineers cannot preserve what was never collected. That point is real and partially answers CON's "fund the engineers" alternative, which is weaker than CON claims once backends are already gone. However, PRO took on two avoidable costs. First, its answer to Q1 shifted toward broad deposit of all published works with significance triaged later, which CON fairly framed as defending a broader statute than the motion states. Second, it conceded that "if a studio dissolves first, nobody can be compelled." PRO also cited nonexistent reference IDs (C2.7, C4.4, C4.5, P4.2) and attributed a "censorship" claim CON never made, which weakens its grounding. CON's "duty is not capability" framing and its reel-versus-missing-organ comparison made the decision easier to reach.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.3–A2.4: CON answers PRO's film analogy by distinguishing licensed music as a permission problem ("the reel still projects") from a missing server as a missing organ. It also shows that "preserve the deposited object" redefines the work downward from the designed experience PRO opened with. PRO never fully recovered from this, and its timing answer only partly offset it.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins a high-level exchange by proving that legal compulsion cannot solve the technical or statutory problems of software preservation. Side B's strongest point was that publication-time deposit acts as a deadline to capture raw builds and specifications before studios liquidate. However, Side A broke the books-and-films analogy by showing that software fails from external dependencies and third-party property rather than mere permission limits, and that release-day filing cannot compel offline emulation stubs that do not yet exist or capture live-service evolution. Side A also trapped Side B on the motion's "culturally significant" trigger: once Side B conceded significance cannot be known at publication and proposed broad deposit of published works for later appraisal, Side A exposed this as universal deposit in disguise, while showing that tiering duties onto app stores captures only compiled clients rather than source or server layers. Side A also maintained cleaner reference-ID and citation accuracy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A2.1]-[A2.3] and [A4.1]-[A4.2], Side A dismantled Side B's film-music analogy and "server stubs" fix ([B2.1]-[B2.2]): licensed music in a film is a permission problem where the reel still projects, whereas a missing server is a missing organ, third-party dependencies belong to unreached licensors, and a statute cannot compel the existence of an emulation stub that no studio has written at release—showing that runnable preservation comes from funded engineering rather than a filing mandate.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B made the decisive clashes clearer: what compulsion adds beyond funded preservation, and whether A’s broad-deposit safeguard still fits the motion’s significance trigger. A’s early-capture argument remained genuinely valuable and prevented a wider margin, but A’s pressure questions mischaracterized B’s claims, and A’s answers exposed scope and dependency difficulties that B exploited more effectively. B occasionally overstated the mandate-versus-funding dichotomy, but consistently gave the cleaner comparative reason to vote CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure questions forced A to reconcile broad deposit with cultural significance and identify who must produce server stubs after dissolution. B’s second rebuttal then used those answers to distinguish a filing deadline from actual preservation capability, while A’s closing recovery—that the deadline captures evidence before loss—came too late to fully neutralize the exchange.
