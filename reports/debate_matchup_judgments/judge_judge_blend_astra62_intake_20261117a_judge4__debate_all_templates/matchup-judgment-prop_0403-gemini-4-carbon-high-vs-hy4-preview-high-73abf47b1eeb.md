### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO wins a clear but competitive debate. It built one consistent causal story and kept it through every round. Monopoly leagues create scarcity and play cities against each other. Short-term officials therefore sign weak exit terms. A non-waivable approval rule is what removes the waiver from the bargaining table. CON's most damaging moment was its own concession that officials are "outgunned." PRO's Q1 exploited this well, and CON's answer shifted ground. It moved from negotiated lease clauses to a statutory repayment bond, subsidy bans, and antitrust repeal. The statutory-bond answer was reasonable, but it was largely a counter-policy rather than a defense of its original contract-only model. PRO also landed points CON never fully neutralized: - Liquidated damages fixed decades in advance become "cheap buyout coupons" as franchise values surge. - Unlike a manufacturer's vacated factory, a closed cartel bars any replacement team. CON's best material was strong: - The "perpetual tribute" and holdup framing. - The closing turn that PRO's own "bounded by the move's value to the owner" amounts to a 100% exit tax. That exit-tax turn arrived late and still left the core value clash open. PRO says taxpayers bought civic presence; CON says they only lent money. PRO argued its side of that clash more persuasively, and its account of why contracts alone fail survived the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Q1 and A4.3. PRO used CON's "outgunned" concession to show that negotiated non-relocation clauses cannot be secured against cartel leverage. That forced CON to retreat to statutory mandates and subsidy bans. CON's best counter was B5.3, which turned PRO's "bounded by the owner's windfall" into a 100% exit-tax charge. It was sharp but came too late to fully reverse the clash.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): A narrowly wins the distinction between protecting public investment and granting indefinite relocation control. B convincingly exposed the weakness of negotiated leases, but A’s mandatory repayment-guarantee response addressed that bargaining problem without an open-ended veto. B established that communities value more than debt repayment, yet never adequately justified their continuing power to block departure after financial obligations and the agreed lease were satisfied. Its reliance on voters’ financial self-interest did not resolve A’s holdout objection. A’s late antitrust claim earns no credit; the bounded-remedy comparison carries the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first pressure question demanded a limit on compensation beyond debt and infrastructure. B answered that the owner could stay and voters would prefer a payout. A’s closing exposed the weakness: those incentives permit capturing the relocation windfall rather than establish a fair compensation limit. This materially undermined B’s claim that approval merely sets a fair exit price.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): B won a close, high-quality debate by preserving the clearest distinction: repayment of bounded public risk versus an open-ended political claim over a private franchise’s future location and value. A sharply exposed B’s initial reliance on outgunned local negotiators and the tension in B’s capitalization argument, but B answered with uniform mandatory guarantees and directly distinguished finite debt from perpetual control. A never supplied an objective limit on community demands; economic self-interest was a weaker safeguard, and A’s claim that subsidies purchase lasting civic presence or franchise upside remained more asserted than established.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answers to the pressure questions and subsequent closing were decisive: a non-waivable statutory repayment guarantee addressed the race-to-the-bottom, while A’s “owner can always stay” bound allowed B to characterize the veto as potentially capturing the entire relocation windfall rather than merely making taxpayers whole.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): CON wins on the decisive clash. It identified early a dilemma at the heart of PRO's model: "community approval" is either a real veto that voters can exercise at no cost to themselves, making it a de facto exit ban, or it is an objective buyout that is not really community approval. CON pressed that dilemma through every speech. The Pressure Questions forced PRO to choose. PRO chose "voters cannot say no." That concession hollowed out the binding-community-approval element of the motion and left PRO defending a pre-written repayment formula. CON then showed that this formula looks like its own statutory repayment alternative with extra, vaguely defined payouts added on. PRO's second answer also cost it. Admitting that a fully performed lease yields zero undercut its "subsidy as capitalization" frame. CON exploited that tension sharply in the closing. PRO's opening framing was strong: bait-and-switch, relationship-specific investment, and the asymmetric exit threat. Its answers were honest and direct. But its closing claim that CON "concedes the decisive point" overstates the overlap, because CON's alternative deliberately omits community approval. PRO also never fully reconciled its capitalization logic with the zero-after-lease concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing turned PRO's A1 answer ("voters cannot say no... the community must approve") into a concession that PRO's process is a statutory buyout rather than community approval. CON paired this with the contradiction between PRO's appreciated-value argument and its admission that a completed lease owes zero.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): A wins the institutional comparison. B directly answered the pressure questions and substantially defused indefinite captivity, but its guaranteed-exit formula left unclear what binding community approval adds beyond enforceable repayment. A’s upfront statutory remedy answered stranded debt, while B’s closing continued attacking delayed litigation instead. B retained a plausible argument for compensating broader public investment, but did not sufficiently justify its additional liabilities or explain why owner resistance undermines repayment statutes without also undermining its own process. A made the remaining choice clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal exposed the unequal comparison: a legislature capable of mandating B’s approval-and-compensation process could also mandate repayment before departure. Coupled with B’s concession that compliant teams must receive approval, this weakened both its institutional advantage and its earlier team-retention rationale.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won by exposing and repeatedly pressing the central instability in A’s case: either the community can reject an objectively adequate make-whole offer, creating the threatened hostage veto, or approval is automatic and the policy is essentially a repayment statute rather than binding community approval. A answered the pressure questions directly and usefully narrowed the rule’s duration, but those concessions weakened its original San Diego and permanent-capitalization claims. A retained a plausible case for compensation beyond stranded debt, yet never explained why its approval process was necessary instead of B’s cleaner upfront repayment rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal decisively quoted A’s admissions that voters cannot say no, the community must approve, and a completed lease yields zero liability, then used them to collapse A’s distinction between community approval and an automatic buyout.
