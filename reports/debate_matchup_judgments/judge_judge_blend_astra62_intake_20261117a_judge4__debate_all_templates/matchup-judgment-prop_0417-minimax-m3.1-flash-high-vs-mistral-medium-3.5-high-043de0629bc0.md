### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): PRO wins on a clearer causal story and better adaptation. Its core mechanism holds up through the exchange: the risky moment is the gap between venue closing and the last safe way home, and late transit fills that gap without lengthening drinking hours. CON's root-cause argument, that less drinking time means fewer impaired people, is plausible and was its best route. But CON rested almost entirely on two suspiciously exact statistics, Cardiff's 11% and Stockholm's 15%. It repeated them in every speech without developing them. PRO correctly pointed out that ED visits are a different outcome and that a transit-crime rise is not a policy comparison. CON's claim that staggered closings already disperse crowds cut against its own case, and PRO used that well. PRO also answered A1/A2 directly: coverage need not be universal to cut drunk driving, and venue hours can stay fixed so late trains do not mean longer drinking. CON's second rebuttal and closing largely restated its opening rather than engaging those answers. PRO's clipped opening is a minor execution cost and does not offset these advantages.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4.4–A4.5 was the decisive exchange. It separated late transit from late venue hours, which defused the "safety net / longer drinking" claim. It also showed that staggering cannot remove the mismatch between the final closing and the last train. CON never answered either point directly.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B built the clearer comparative path. Side A’s earlier-closing mechanism was plausible, but its unverified Cardiff emergency-visit figure did not establish superior reductions in both disorder and drunk driving, while Stockholm did not compare the policies. Side B directly answered the coverage and staggering objections, separated transit hours from venue hours, and explained why managed egress addresses harms that earlier closing leaves unresolved. Side A largely repeated its root-cause assertion and overclaimed its evidence. Side B’s clipped opening and own lack of comparative outcome evidence prevent a larger margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal decisively answered both pressure questions: staggered closings still leave a gap between final departures and usable transport, and transit need not cover everyone to prevent some impaired driving. Side A’s corresponding answers merely restated Cardiff, existing staggering, and Stockholm without supplying the requested comparison.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by building the cleaner comparative case: late transit directly supplies a sober, supervised way home at the moment venues empty, while earlier closing mostly changes timing and may compress drinking rather than remove the final dangerous egress gap. B also handled the key pressure better, explaining that transit need not have universal coverage to reduce drunk-driving risk at population level and that staggered closings still leave a last-wave transport mismatch. Side A’s root-cause intuition was understandable, but it leaned heavily on exact Cardiff and Stockholm claims that were not verifiable in this closed-book setting and never convincingly tied them to superiority over transit on both disorder and drunk driving. B’s clipped opening is a modest execution cost, but B still made the decisive clash clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 was most decisive: it answered A1/A2 directly, distinguished useful coverage from universal coverage, and showed that staggered venue closings do not eliminate the final departure gap that transit extensions address.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): CON wins clearly because it kept the debate focused on the comparative claim the motion requires, while PRO kept repeating one mechanism instead of defending it. PRO built everything on a "transport vacuum" and "compressed crowd" story. CON damaged both supports. First, a closing time does not create a gap in transit service. Second, compression is conditional, since venues can stagger last entry, stop alcohol service early, and release patrons in waves. PRO's evidence was also weak. It made vague claims about London, Berlin and Sydney, and listing Sydney is odd given its well-known restrictive closing policies. Its claim in Rebuttal 2 that cities "consistently document" surges carried unwarranted certainty and had no grounding. PRO's rebuttals and closing largely recycled the "root cause" line almost word for word. CON offered a distinct exposure mechanism: earlier closing cuts drinking time and street presence before the trip home matters. PRO never squarely engaged that mechanism. CON did have flaws. In its closing it misattributed staggered releases to PRO. Its "inconsistent framing" point muddled who had raised the idea that transit enables longer drinking sessions. Its own case also leaned on mechanism without evidence. Even so, CON's demand for a head-to-head comparison and its symmetric treatment of correlational evidence went effectively unanswered. PRO's attempt to shift the burden in A2 did not establish that transit extensions are superior.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's B2.1/B4.1 response to PRO's midnight-gap claim. CON argued that patrons drinking before midnight face the same missing transit whether venues close at midnight or 2 a.m., so the gap is not an effect of closing time. That cut PRO's central claim that earlier closing "leaves the vacuum" or worsens it. PRO never rebuilt the link.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): A won by keeping the debate on the proposition’s comparative burden. Its exposure-versus-trip-home distinction explained why transit could be beneficial without outperforming earlier closing, while its causality objections applied evenly to both policies. B’s transport-vacuum mechanism was initially forceful, but it became repetitive and increasingly overclaimed: city correlations were treated as comparative proof, compression was asserted rather than established, and disorder received little direct support. B also shifted the burden instead of supplying a head-to-case comparison. A had some weaknesses, including an under-evidenced affirmative case for closing and a confusing second pressure question, but it answered the central clash much more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s rebuttals decisively separated a safer journey home from lower total nightlife harm, showed that earlier closing does not itself create the transport gap, and explained why the Night Tube examples established neither causation nor superiority. B answered mainly by restating the vacuum theory and demanding counter-evidence.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): A won by keeping the comparative burden on PRO and by giving the cleaner total-harm story: transit may make the trip home safer, but earlier closing can reduce drinking time, intoxication, and late-night street exposure. B’s transport-vacuum frame was rhetorically strong, but it leaned on broad, unsupported city claims and never fully answered the exposure problem or the demand for head-to-head superiority.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2, especially the A2 section, cleanly rejected B’s attempt to shift the burden and reduced B’s transit examples to plausible but non-comparative evidence of benefit rather than proof of superiority.
