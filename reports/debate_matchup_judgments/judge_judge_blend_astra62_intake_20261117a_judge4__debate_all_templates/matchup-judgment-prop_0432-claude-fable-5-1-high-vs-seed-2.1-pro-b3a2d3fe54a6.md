### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A won by keeping the comparison clean and consistent: partial coverage plus disclosure versus zero coverage plus concealment. A honestly conceded that no off-the-shelf policy covers neighbor harms, then reframed "standardized" as a purpose-built scheme — a partial dodge, but paired with the unanswerable point that a ban insures nothing and guarantees hidden pets stay undisclosed. A's pressure question on fair housing forced B into a semi-plausible but thin bright-line answer, and A's closing reframe — that B's own deterrent argument ("tenants won't risk eviction records") reveals a ban is just an unfunded, after-the-fact price — was the debate's decisive blow, compounded by the adverse-selection point that bans filter for the riskiest owners. B's strongest live route was scale: default permission grows the total pet population in buildings not built for it, and partial coverage of a vastly larger uninsurable pool may be worse than zero coverage of a small one. B crystallized this well in the closing, but never quantified or grounded the "small, self-selected" hidden population, and the migration-story mechanism was contested by A's scarcity point. B also leaned on asserted characteristics of hidden pets (smaller, quieter) without support. Both sides argued the motion as written; A's case did more comparative weighing and survived pressure more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing: after B defended bans as deterring hidden pets via eviction risk, A reframed that deterrent as an implicit price — an unwritten, unfunded, after-the-fact charge — turning B's own mechanism into the case for explicit, pre-paid insurance. This simultaneously absorbed B's best pressure answer and made B's alternative look like a worse version of A's proposal.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): A wins the central comparison: better coverage per animal does not establish lower total harm when permission increases pet numbers. A supplied a plausible deterrence mechanism, while B repeatedly assumed bans merely conceal essentially the same population. B effectively challenged A’s fair-housing argument, and A incorrectly treated “standardized” as necessarily off-the-shelf. Nevertheless, B’s conceded coverage gaps and unsupported custom-insurance solution left A’s aggregate-harm objection standing. B also claimed unanswered pressure before A could respond and subsequently misrepresented A as not disputing “partial beats zero.”
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s response to “partial coverage beats zero” exposed the missing comparison: a larger, partially insured population can impose greater uncovered costs. B’s closing relabeled deterrence a “price” without resolving that objection.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO built the cleaner comparison: funded, disclosed pets versus unfunded, hidden pets under bans. CON showed many harms are diffuse and poorly insured, but PRO's partial-beats-zero answer and point that nuisance enforcement is identical under both regimes left CON relying on an unquantified scale increase. PRO also flipped CON's deterrence claim into adverse selection: bans deter conscientious owners, not gamblers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was B's Rebuttal 2 on insurance: honestly conceding no off-the-shelf rider pays broken-lease turnover, then reversing the comparison to ask what a ban pays for hidden-pet damage—nothing—making partial standardized coverage beat zero coverage.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B (CON) built and sustained the stronger path to victory. B's core externality argument—that insurance pays the landlord's floor, not the asthmatic neighbor's lungs—was never neutralized; A's A2 answer conceded "no separate line item" for health harms, which B converted into a symmetry kill: both worlds leave neighbors to tort, only one multiplies animals. B repeatedly beat A on enforcement speed (under a ban the animal itself is the breach, one step; under the default, nuisance must be proven), dismantled the hidden-pet argument with the speed-limit analogy and the scale distinction, and closed with a genuine dilemma: if pet-free buildings are trivially chosen, the motion is a nullity; if the default bites, the burden lands on the sick. A's closing claim that "most people who want pets already have them" walked straight into the nullity horn. A also dodged B's pointed coverage-limit question with "high enough," which B flagged as three speeches with no number. A's case had real texture (underground pets, turnover economics) but relied on asserted policy coverage and never resolved the neighbor-harm gap. B's reframing about defaults deciding who must fight clarified the motion as written rather than replacing it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4: the symmetry argument that both sides leave neighbors to tort ("both sides leave neighbors to tort; only one side multiplies the animals") combined with the dilemma in B4.6—if pet-free status needs only one line in a listing, PRO is defending a nullity; if the default bites, the sick carry the burden—decisively collapsed A's case, and A's closing ("most people who want pets already have them") handed B the nullity horn.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): A wins the central comparison: damage coverage does not resolve recurring health and noise exposure, and imperfect bans can still reduce animal numbers. B repeatedly substitutes evidence of possible evasion for proof that bans lack deterrent effects, culminating in its unsupported claim that most prospective pet owners already evade bans. A overstates easy removal, and its claim that freely available exceptions make defaults meaningless is unpersuasive. Insurance also retains genuine remediation benefits. Nevertheless, B never adequately answers A’s stronger exposure and enforcement objections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first rebuttal directly reverses B’s registration argument: knowing an animal exists does not establish actionable nuisance, whereas unauthorized presence itself violates a ban. Combined with the distinction between imperfect deterrence and no deterrence, this undermines B’s main comparative mechanism. B largely repeats the hidden-pet argument rather than resolving either challenge.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by isolating harms insurance cannot compensate: allergens, noise, and fear imposed on non-consenting neighbors. A persuasively argued that bans cap animal numbers and make the animal itself the breach, while B never answered the scale problem or specified coverage. B's concession that health harms rely on existing tort rules left its funds argument paying landlords, not neighbors, and A's nullity-or-multiplication dilemma closed that clash cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttals distinguishing a few illicit pets from many lawful pets, and showing registration converts a one-step ban violation into a difficult nuisance-proof burden, most damaged B's hidden-pet case.
