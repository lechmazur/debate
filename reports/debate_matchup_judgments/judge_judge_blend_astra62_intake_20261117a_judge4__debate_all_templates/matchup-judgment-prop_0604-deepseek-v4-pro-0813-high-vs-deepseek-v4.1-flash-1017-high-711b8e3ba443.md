### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but the pressure-question exchange proved decisive. B's Q2 ("if a below-threshold model is fine-tuned into a dangerous system, does it need a new licence?") forced A into explicitly embracing the "broad gate" horn of a dilemma B had set up: either the licence covers only original releases (symbolic, missing the fine-tune/jailbreak threat A itself emphasized) or it must extend to countless derivatives (an unworkable inspection burden that favors incumbents, exactly the capture critique B raised in opening). A's honest answer at A4.3 locked into the second horn, and B's subsequent rebuttal and closing hammered this unresolved tension effectively. Separately, A's concession at A4.1 that revocation cannot recall copied weights let B argue convincingly that the licence's "continuing teeth" collapse into the same market-access prohibitions and liability tools that risk-based regulation already offers, undercutting the claim that licensing is distinctively more powerful. A's clever closing reframe (that the recall gap actually proves the case for a pre-release chokepoint) is a reasonable rejoinder but does not resolve the derivative-coverage dilemma, which B kept live and unanswered through the final speeches. B's case was therefore the harder one to counter by the end, giving it a clear if not overwhelming edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q2 pressure question and the ensuing rebuttal/closing sequence, which trapped A in the narrow-vs-broad licensing dilemma that A never cleanly escaped.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by defeating the false choice between licensing and purely reactive oversight. It established that risk-based regulation can impose ex ante evaluations, conditions, prohibitions, and suspension without treating approval as a universal safety certificate. Side B made irreversibility compelling, but did not prove that licensing uniquely solves it. Its answer that dangerous fine-tunes require fresh clearance strengthened Side A’s dilemma: either licensing misses derivatives or expands into an impractical system for assessing countless modifications.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to B4.3 was decisive: requiring fresh licences for dangerous derivatives made the proposed chokepoint sprawl across modifications regulators cannot reliably inspect, while limiting licences to original models preserved the very fine-tuning gap Side B emphasized.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by effectively neutralizing Side B's core argument about irreversibility and trapping B on the issue of fine-tunes. A demonstrated that both sides rely on the same post-release enforcement mechanism (legal prohibition) and that risk-based regulation can still impose ex-ante duties, making B's license either an unworkable bureaucracy for fine-tunes or an ineffective snapshot.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which used B's answer to Q2 to expose the dilemma that licensing must either miss fine-tunes or become an unworkable permission bureaucracy for countless derivatives.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and executed the format cleanly, including sharp, on-point pressure questions. PRO's strongest asset was the "burden-shift" framing (permission-before-deployment vs. deploy-and-correct), but under pressure this collapsed into an unstable position: when pressed on what a licence adds beyond enforceable pre-release conditions, PRO's answer ("developer must demonstrate accountable circulation") became functionally indistinguishable from CON's enforceable risk-based preconditions. CON exploited this directly, forcing the dilemma in B4.6 ("if every precondition counts as licensing, the protective substance already exists without it; if licensing means more, it is an unjustified ignorance veto") — a dilemma PRO's closing restated its framework against but never actually dismantled. CON also landed a clean "gotcha" in B2.4, showing PRO's revocation/rollback mechanism suffers the same irreversibility problem PRO uses to attack liability-based regimes, and PRO's A2 answer in Rebuttal 2 conceded that revoked licences can't reach copied weights, undercutting its own containment claim. CON's closing framing ("ignorance veto," "certified ignorance") was more memorable and better crystallized the core clash than PRO's repeated assertion of burden-shift. The debate was close and both sides argued in good faith without fabricated evidence, but CON's late-stage pressure and dilemma construction gave it the cleaner, more resolved path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4 response exposing that PRO's licence-refusal standard rests on "not knowing enough" rather than demonstrated breach, crystallized in the closing dilemma (B4.6/B5.2) that PRO's licensing either duplicates enforceable risk-based preconditions or amounts to an unjustified discretionary veto.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by establishing that licensing changes the operative default under uncertainty: deployment requires affirmative satisfaction of conditions rather than regulators proving noncompliance quickly enough to stop release. Side A compellingly framed licensing as an “ignorance veto,” but its alternative of mandatory pre-release conditions, suspension, and injunctions either approximated licensing or retained an enforcement gap when assessments were incomplete or disputed. B also directly conceded that licensing cannot eliminate grey-market copies while persuasively explaining why imperfect prevention still improves on default circulation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s pressure question and follow-up were decisive: if Side A’s conditions must be verified before release and permission can be withdrawn, they resemble licensing; if they are merely enforceable after a regulator proves breach, they cannot reliably prevent irreversible diffusion. Side A drew a real distinction between objective duties and discretionary permission, but did not fully answer that practical enforcement gap.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing licensing not as a prediction contest, but as a crucial shift in the burden of proof. While Side A brilliantly characterized licensing as an 'ignorance veto,' Side B systematically exposed the structural flaw in Side A's alternative: risk-based injunctions require the regulator to discover and prove a violation before release, which is practically impossible when the developer controls the evidence and capabilities are uncertain.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's closing argument perfectly crystallized the flaw in A's reliance on emergency injunctions, noting that they require a proven violation in a domain where uncertainty is the core problem, thereby making deployment the default.
