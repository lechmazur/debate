Overall, A made the final comparison easier to resolve.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins a clear but competitive debate because it kept forcing PRO onto the defensive. - **Openings:** CON opened with the Phalanx/Iron Dome problem and established it as the central definitional clash. PRO's munitions-versus-people distinction was a reasonable answer to it. - **The rubber-stamp inversion:** CON then made its best move. A lieutenant approving two hundred nominations a minute satisfies a nominal human-in-the-loop ban, while a tightly bounded, tested system is outlawed. - **PRO's answer:** PRO responded well in A1. It defined meaningful control through target-specific information, realistic time, a genuine veto and a reviewable record. That definition is legitimately within the motion, so CON's claim that "this is just regulation" overreaches somewhat. - **Unanswered points:** - CON's anti-radiation missile example directly undercut PRO's "human must select that specific vehicle" criterion. PRO never answered it. - CON's closing point also went unaddressed: a one-second human glance on a degraded feed sees no more of surrender or coercion than the classifier does. - **CON's costs:** CON paid a real price on verification. Its opening argued that autonomy cannot be inspected, then in A2 it listed robust behavioral verification methods. That undercuts its own case that a ban is unenforceable. - **PRO's strengths:** PRO's moral frame was coherent and consistently held: a human must decide at the moment a person is selected to die. Net, CON delivered more damaging rebuttals and left more live pressure standing. PRO's closing restated its frame rather than resolving CON's counterexamples.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came in A2.4–A2.5. CON argued that the rubber-stamping lieutenant is compliant under a human-in-the-loop ban but illegal under CON's deployment-reasonableness rule, framing the ban as licensing the very theater PRO condemns. PRO's A1 rescued much of the ground by defining meaningful control. CON then followed up with "theater with better paperwork" and the anti-radiation missile counterexample, which left PRO's line looking hard to administer.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Both sides debated the motion as written and executed the format cleanly, including direct A1/A2 answers. But B consistently made the clash sharper and landed the two points A never fully neutralized: (1) the anti-radiation missile / radar van example, showing that weapons already in the field engage crewed targets without a human selecting that specific target, so PRO's munitions-versus-people line is harder to draw than claimed; and (2) the decisive inversion that PRO's own test of meaningful control — target-specific information, realistic time, genuine veto, reviewable records — is a case-by-case performance standard adjudicated from logs and exercises, which is regulation, not a categorical ban. A's closing ("defining meaningful control enforces a ban rather than replaces it") asserted coherence but did not resolve the tension. B also handled pressure better overall: answering the bound-breadth question with a concrete engineering criterion, withdrawing the unverifiability point honestly, and turning A's own audit evidence against the ban. A's best material — the front-loaded-control inversion, the commander accountability dilemma, and the surrendering-fighter scenario — was strong but was met each time with a credible counter (artillery analogy, "theater with better paperwork," degraded feed showing no more than the classifier). A's escalation/proliferation pillar from the opening was also dropped and never pressed. B made the debate easier to decide and gave the cleaner reason to vote, winning clearly but competitively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal: after A supplied a four-part observable test for meaningful control, B pounced that this concedes the debate — a per-deployment performance standard is regulation, not a ban — then compounded it with the anti-radiation missile example and the closing image that PRO's line "tracks whose hand is nearest the paperwork" rather than who exercises judgment. A's assertion that defining control "enforces a ban" never adequately answered this.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): A narrowly wins by better resolving what meaningful control requires and why changing battlefield circumstances matter. B effectively challenged the accountability vacuum and offered serious bounded-use counterexamples. However, its later case repeatedly treated rubber-stamping as compliant after A expressly excluded it, and wrongly equated case-specific enforcement with abandoning a ban. A supplied an observable control standard, while B did not convincingly explain how preset bounds address surrender or changing civilian presence at engagement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's direct answer to the rubber-stamping challenge—target-specific information, realistic assessment time, genuine veto, and reviewable records—undercut B's claim that the ban licenses nominal approval. B largely repeated that claim rather than defeating the clarified standard.
