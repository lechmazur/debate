### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined, quote-anchored, and free of fake sourcing, but Side A controlled the decisive clash. A's core move—separating the benefits of a lingua franca from the harms of dominance—was never fully neutralized. B repeatedly answered attacks on dominance as if they were attacks on any shared code ("dismantling," "tearing down," "burn the bridge"), even after A explicitly endorsed funded multilingual coordination in A2.6 and again in the closing. That persistent strawman is a real cost. A's pressure questions exposed the tension between B's "investment, not linguistics" and "multilingual journals are slower," and A's Rebuttal 2 answered B's questions directly with the "consumption vs. renewal" framing and the sharp weighing of thin coordination gains against generational, reproductive capacity loss. B's best material—the counterfactual of Latin/French/empire and "a bridge prices distance, it doesn't create it"—was genuinely strong on causation, and B answered both pressure questions cleanly, but B never disentangled its defense of a shared second language from the motion's actual target: dominance and its compounding prestige costs. A made the debate easier to decide and conceded what needed conceding; B's late speeches leaned on a mischaracterized demolition framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over B2.3/B2.6 versus A4.2-A4.5: A's pressure questions trapped B between claiming local capacity is "investment, not linguistics" and dismissing multilingual journals as "slower," and A's Rebuttal 2 exploited it—showing that "investment" never explains why money flows to the English channel, and that translated guidelines deliver consumption, not renewal, absent local peer review and terminology bodies.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO sustained a clearer mechanism: dominance makes English compulsory passage, producing asymmetric bilingual costs, institutional domain loss, and cumulative prestige compounding. CON defended a useful lingua franca and coordination benefits, but its own remedies—multilingual publication, funded local journals, terminology bodies, and career credit—conceded that dominant defaults are insufficient. CON's causation reply that English merely prices distance did not neutralize PRO's account of how that pricing directs investment away from local knowledge systems.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 and Closing were decisive: the ladder concedes hierarchy, individuals may be bilingual while institutions remain monolingual, and CON's bilingual institution-building defends diluting dominance rather than showing dominance itself is net good.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the focus on dominance rather than a generic lingua franca and by showing a cumulative reproductive harm: English defaults can drain prestige, funding, and institutional capacity from non-anglophone knowledge systems. B gave a strong network-effects and investment-based answer, but did not fully rebut the claim that dominance compounds those costs, and its bilingual institution-building remedy allowed A to argue that CON was effectively conceding the need to dilute dominance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 best answered B’s pressure by separating individual access from institutional ownership: a Kenyan researcher may publish in English and machine translation may spread content, but that does not create local peer review, terminology, textbooks, or markets. That kept A’s structural harm alive.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side A built a vivid, coherent case around compulsory translation, asymmetric irreversibility, and demotion of source languages to "museum tongues," with memorable framing (taxidermy, tenancy, tollbooth). But Side B consistently met the clash on the motion's own terms: it reframed the comparison as net harm versus a feasible alternative (fragmentation with narrower funnels), defused A's metaphors one by one (taxidermy proves too much; tenancy confuses use with ownership; tolls are governable), and answered both pressure questions directly and honestly—conceding unfair tolls while insisting repairs preserve source-language primacy. B's closing crystallization ("to be findable in English is not to exist only in English") directly falsified A's "compulsory translation for existence" conversion and left A's alternative looking like additive repairs without a better hub. A's strongest live route—irreversibility—was countered by B's flip that obscurity, not translation, is the truly unrecoverable loss. A answered pressure questions in proper format but its answers narrowed its own claim into a reciprocal/compulsory distinction that B immediately exploited as a concession. Slight edge to B on comparative weighing and burden control; A retains an edge on rhetorical vividness.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answer A2 ("the community should decide... an English gloss acts as a pointer") plus the immediate exploitation of A's concession that reciprocal exchange can preserve knowledge systems—reframing the whole clash as governance and reach rather than contact, which A never fully reversed.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B wins the comparative test for net harm. A powerfully shows unfair tolls, flattening, and prestige loss, but B repeatedly shows the feasible alternative is fragmentation with smaller audiences and more local gatekeepers, while A's regional journals and translation reforms can supplement rather than replace a shared hub. B also answers the pressure questions directly and leaves A's claim that visibility requires cultural demotion unproven.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal that lossy translation can preserve leverage while obscurity guarantees loss, reinforced when A concedes reciprocal exchange can preserve knowledge systems, shifting the clash to governance rather than English dominance itself.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B won because it made the decisive comparative question clearer: not whether English imposes unfair costs, but whether dethroning it would better serve non-Anglophone knowledge systems. B answered the feasibility pressure with a governable shared discovery layer, original-language primacy, and local institutions, while A’s distributed-network alternative never showed how it would avoid fragmentation or give small-language work more reach and defense. A’s rhetoric was powerful, but B’s rebuttals turned the taxidermy and tenancy claims into purity and control problems and kept the vote on net reach versus obscurity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s taxidermy and compulsory-translation claims was decisive: it showed A’s survival test risked condemning all cross-language contact, then used A’s reciprocal-exchange concession to narrow the clash to governance and reach, where B had the stronger comparative story.
