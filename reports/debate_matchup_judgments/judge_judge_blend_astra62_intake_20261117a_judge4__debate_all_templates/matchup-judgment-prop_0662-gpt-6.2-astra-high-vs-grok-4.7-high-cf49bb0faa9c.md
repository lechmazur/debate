### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A won the decisive clash. A's core move—separating the base-period/eligibility function from separation verification—broke B's central "optional sales, not lost wages" frame. A showed the hotel closure supplies the separation while earnings records only test attachment, and then pressed the killer question: what does exclusion accomplish that a proof-of-loss requirement does not? B's recurring answer ("restaurant deliveries were covered wages, app payouts were not") was circular against a motion precisely about what should count, and A flagged that circularity preemptively in the closing. B's best live routes—the expense-measurement problem and the surveillance tension—received workable answers from A (standard allowance plus documented-actuals option; judge the offer, not the acceptance rate; deny rainy-day-dip claims but retain credit), and B's pension rebuttal ("prevents a false service history") was thin against A's point that a worker who financed the system would be recorded as doing no covered work. B's rhetoric was sharp and consistent, but the clipped Rebuttal 1 is a small execution cost, and B never escaped the definitional trap on the main clash. Margin is clear but competitive because B's implementation critiques on expense guessing and contribution incidence survived partially unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Pressure Questions exchange. A's Q1 (why count past restaurant but not app deliveries when neither clerk lost the delivery work?) forced B into the circular "covered wages" answer, and B's Q1/Q2 on suitability and separation elicited A's most constructive move of the debate: "Deny that claim; retain the earnings credit"—conceding weak claims while preserving the earnings record, which left B arguing only that the credit itself is illegitimate, a premise A had already shown to be the very thing in dispute.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A won a high-level exchange by cleanly separating whether past work counts toward monetary eligibility from whether a claimant proved an involuntary loss of work. Through the two-hotel-clerks comparison, Side A showed that excluding platform earnings denies unemployment insurance after an undisputed layoff—and denies pension credits that require no layoff at all. Side B pressed real administrative challenges regarding mixed-use expense verification and policing slowdowns without surveillance, and incurred a minor execution cost from a clipped first rebuttal. However, Side A defused the slowdown critique by conceding that mere payout dips should be denied while retaining verified earnings credits, convincingly argued that an imperfect expense deduction beats counting zero, and exposed Side B's reliance on prior "covered wages" as circular.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A3.1], Side A asked why past restaurant deliveries should count after a hotel closure while past app deliveries do not when both clerks keep delivering for the same net pay. When Side B answered in [B4.1] that restaurant deliveries count "because they were covered wages" and app deliveries do not "because they were not," Side A decisively dismantled the circularity in [A5.1-A5.5], showing Side B was merely restating the rule under dispute rather than justifying why proven work should be erased.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): A built the cleaner path to victory by separating earnings credit from proof of involuntary loss: unsupported slowdown claims could be denied without erasing verified work history, while pension credit required no separation test at all. ### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): This was a tight, high-quality exchange. A built a clean administrability case (the earnings trail exists; use it as a floor, not a ceiling) and answered both pressure questions directly with proper A1/A2 labels. But B's parity framework — the two cleaners — was the most original and persistent line of the debate, and B sharpened it each round rather than repeating: from "insure a payment channel" to the guarantee-vs-permission asymmetry, to the decisive observation that a cleaner "closer" to the threshold still receives nothing, to the closing catch on A's word "once," exposing that A's fix quietly depends on coverage beyond the motion's mandate. A's claim that agencies "can already take her invoices" was asserted rather than grounded and was never firmly defended, leaving B's distortion critique live. A's strongest remaining point — that the universal fix "never arrives" — was partially answered by B's counter-plan to require platform evidence and credit net income across the business now. B made the debate easier to decide and controlled the decisive clash by a narrow margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.4/B5.1: B dismantled A's "floor, not ceiling" answer by showing that a guaranteed credit for app bookings plus mere permission for direct bookings still leaves a mixed earner below the threshold with nothing — and that A's own closing ("once her invoice is taken on those terms") conceded that the mandate alone does not protect her.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by dismantling both the administrative premise and the equity logic of a platform-specific eligibility mandate. Side B demonstrated that standardized platform records capture gross payouts rather than net labor income after off-platform capital and vehicle costs, and cannot distinguish a lack of offers from voluntarily declined trips even with an "open-to-offers" field. Once Side A conceded that common net-income and worker-contribution rules are needed anyway, Side A lost its rationale for mandating credit for app bookings while leaving direct invoices merely optional—an asymmetry Side B showed leaves mixed-stream workers below the threshold with nothing or forces them to route direct clients through apps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling in [B4.4] and [B5.1] of Side A's "floor, not a ceiling" defense ([A4.1]-[A4.2], [A5.4]): Side B pointed out that getting a mixed app-and-invoice cleaner "closer" to a threshold still pays zero benefits, and that guaranteeing credit for app bookings while merely permitting credit for direct invoices distorts who qualifies—and pressures workers to route direct customers through an app—without needing a ceiling.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B made the stronger comparative case by separating useful standardized evidence from a platform-specific entitlement. Its common net-income and contribution rule addressed mixed earners without making protection depend on booking channels, and it answered concerns about expenses, involuntary loss, and implementation without delaying coverage. A presented a compelling fairness and administrability case and answered both pressure questions directly, but too often characterized B as scoring platform earnings at zero despite B’s proposed mandatory credit. A consequently left the narrower platform-specific mandate less justified than B’s nondiscriminatory alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s use of A’s concessions “closer” and “once” was decisive: guaranteeing credit for app earnings while merely permitting credit for direct bookings can still leave a mixed earner below the threshold. B then showed that mandatory credit across all insured net income resolves that distortion without ignoring platform statements or waiting for identical reporting machinery.
