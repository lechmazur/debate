### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides were sharp, but PRO kept closer to the motion as written: whether countries should permit commitments even when unit prices stay high. PRO's strongest live route was feasibility. A single country that demands low unit prices risks foreign reference pricing that erodes revenues abroad. Without global coordination, that country would have to fund the whole return itself. CON's reply called this a "transition problem" and suggested confidentiality, exemptions or joint funding. That answer was plausible, but it conceded that today's uncoordinated world is the operative one, and PRO pressed that hard. CON's line that developers "still need revenue abroad" also tended to support PRO's case for keeping unit prices intact. PRO used that in closing to argue that a high-price commitment is proportional rather than requiring a global buyout. CON won the stewardship sub-clash with an elegant dilemma: the price is either cancelled by reimbursement or applied too late as a punishment. PRO's answer about prospective budget deterrence was only partial. Still, CON mostly argued that a better design exists. It never showed that forbidding high-price commitments beats permitting them where that better design isn't available. CON's clipped opening was also a modest execution cost. On balance, PRO made the decision easier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Q1/A2 on reference pricing and single-country free-riding against CON's A1 response. CON's best counter was that high prices obtain no foreign contributions. But CON's concession that developers depend on foreign revenue, plus its "transition problem" framing, left PRO's feasibility argument standing. That mattered more for a motion about permission than CON's otherwise strong "redundant or harmful" stewardship dilemma.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B more persuasively showed that forbidding high unit prices would make national AMCs unworkable via reference pricing and free-riding, forcing one country to fund global returns or watch developers walk away, while conditional reimbursement can cover justified use and high prices can deter off-protocol prescribing. Side A’s delinked alternatives and redundancy claims did not fully close that practical gap, and the clipped opening further weakened the initial case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 directly answered why a single low-price availability payment fails under reference pricing and why denied reimbursement for off-protocol use supplies a hospital-level stewardship incentive that free drugs would remove.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO won the practical feasibility clash: a low unit price in one country's commitment could trigger reference-pricing losses abroad, making developers walk away unless that country funded the entire global return. CON's delinked alternative was cleaner in principle, but its answers about coordination, exemptions, and negotiated rewards remained more aspirational than operational for a single country acting now. PRO also gave high prices a distinct stewardship role through denied reimbursement for off-protocol use, while the motion only required permitting—not preferring—high prices.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was B's Rebuttal 2 answer on reference pricing: it broke CON's claim of an identical reward without high prices by showing a fair-share availability payment plus a forced low price could destroy far more foreign revenue than the national commitment replaces.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): This was a close, high-quality exchange, and A wins narrowly. A's opening was clipped, which is a real execution cost. Even so, A framed the motion faithfully as permission versus prohibition. A then showed that permission already contains every near-cost, delinked option CON prefers, while a ban only removes the option of accepting a costly but valuable mixed-price deal. B built a sharp and original case. Its core was a commitment-device argument: forbidding high unit prices strengthens governments' bargaining position. B also pressed hard on why a developer would refuse an equal-value fixed payout, and showed that course caps fail at both ends. That pressure was real. However, B's case ultimately rested on an asserted premise: that developers will always accept a raised fixed commitment. It also relied on overclaims such as "no rational developer concedes near-cost supply." A kept exposing that B's route fails whenever a firm simply declines to develop. A's answers to B's pressure questions on bedside gating and volume incentives were reasonable but not fully decisive. Overall, A's burden framing made the debate somewhat easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive clash came in Rebuttal 2. A's A4.4 argued that "No from a developer is not a concession," pointing out that before development there is no cure to hold hostage, only a decision whether to fund a risky candidate. B's B4.1/B4.4 reply was to raise the fixed payment and claim that permission crowds out near-cost deals. A's closing then crystallized the point that "raise the offer is not a signed contract," which left B's bargaining theory looking like an unverified wager.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B wins because the motion is about permission, not mandatory acceptance. A made a strong case that mixed high-price deals can double-pay, revive volume pressure, delay sepsis care, and crowd out near-cost terms, but B kept showing those are reasons to reject or condition offers. A never established that every needed developer will accept a raised fixed payout, so prohibition can leave only an imaginary cheap vial. Permission still preserves A's preferred delinked model whenever it is actually available.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer that "equal value" is an accounting assumption, not a signed contract: if the capped offer is refused, prohibition produces no drug, while permission still lets a country compare a costly treatment with absence.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A showed countries can offer any worthwhile total reward through staged fixed commitments with near-cost vials, making high unit prices useful mainly for volume incentives or price anchoring. Its bargaining argument also answered PRO's claim that permission merely preserves options: a legal high-price fallback lets patent holders hold out against delinked deals. PRO's no-drug risk remained plausible but did not explain why an equal or greater, less demand-risky fixed payout would be rejected except for rent-seeking.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 cap dilemma was decisive: below a reimbursable-course cap, steep per-dose margins reward pushing use; above it, outbreaks revive rationing or firms must supply near cost anyway, undercutting the need for high unit prices.
