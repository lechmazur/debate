### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B won by consistently distinguishing a necessary public performance floor from a complete or sufficient regulatory regime. Side A compellingly defended local validation, but repeatedly assumed the motion required one static national leaderboard, which neither the text nor B’s case required. A’s eventual alternative of regulator-set, published, audited local testing also approached B’s benchmark requirement while never explaining why public disclosure without an adequacy threshold was enough. B directly answered the fairness-metric and threshold-setting pressures and better defended ex ante protection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive response was that “only if” creates a necessary condition, not automatic permission after a national pass. This undercut A’s claims that a nationally passing but locally failing model would be waved through and that local validation was displaced. B then sharpened the distinction between prescribing a testing protocol and requiring performance good enough to pass.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO held the necessary-vs-sufficient line: "only if" is a floor that accommodates local validation and monitoring rather than replacing them. CON's most memorable charge – that a nationally passing but locally failing tool is "fully permitted" under the motion – misreads the logic, while PRO's harm-based floor and public contestability answers the fairness-metric and gaming worries. CON wins points on the freeze-until-thresholds-exist cost, but PRO's ex ante protection of the first patient versus post-hoc clawback proved more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 exposing reporting-vs-meeting and getting PRO to concede that national averages conceal local failure, then PRO's Rebuttal 2 reframing as harm-based, group-specific missed-emergency floors and necessary-condition logic.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A won by rigorously holding Side B to the exact text of the motion ("only if" and "meet"). While Side B attempted to soften the motion into a general argument for public transparency and baseline standards, Side A successfully penalized this by distinguishing between "reporting" and "meeting" a pass/fail threshold. Side A's "mirror case" argument in Rebuttal 2—that a tool passing a flawed national benchmark but failing locally would still be permitted under the motion, whereas local validation would catch it—was highly decisive. Side A also effectively capitalized on Side B's concession that deployment must wait until non-existent regulatory thresholds are established, framing it as a freeze on beneficial innovation while exempting the unmeasured human baseline. Side B argued well on principle but lost the technical clash over the motion's mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, specifically the "mirror case" argument (A4.1-A4.2), which demonstrated that Side B's national benchmark gate would actually permit tools that fail locally, thereby turning Side B's "additional safeguard" framing against them.
