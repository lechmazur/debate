### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO wins because it built the more complete comparative case and kept adapting it as the clash narrowed. It set up two harms early (the displaced tenant and the excluded commuter) and wrote protections into the by-right rules. It then took CON's pressure head-on. On A2 it conceded plainly that by-right gives up the power to refuse a compliant project. It justified that tradeoff: the city makes its final judgment when it adopts the rule, while a discretionary backstop deters projects and favors identifiable over statistical victims. That candor made PRO's weighing credible. CON had a clear, memorable frame: certain, identified, uncompensable loss versus speculative gain, and the city's duty not to authorize harm. However, it leaned on repetition ("permission slip," "uncertainty is not the enemy" three times). It never seriously engaged PRO's point that denial does not remove the transit premium or the bidders competing for existing units. Its answer, vacancy taxes and rent stabilization, was asserted rather than developed. CON's closing also flattened PRO's position into "bulldoze the rest," ignoring PRO's required nearby temporary home and right of return. Overall, PRO made the tradeoff easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.4–B4.5 was the most decisive exchange. PRO turned CON's grandmother example into a dilemma: a backstop limited to failing projects does nothing for her, since her project passed. A backstop that also reaches compliant projects builds in a hearing bias toward losses that can be named over families who cannot yet testify. This pre-empted CON's "speculative stranger" argument (A4.3–A4.4), which CON then repeated rather than answered.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Both sides argued at a high level with clean format compliance and no fake sourcing. The decisive clash was B's "discretionary backstop" versus A's conditions-vs-final-vote distinction. A cornered B with a genuine dilemma (A4.4): if the backstop only triggers on non-compliant projects, it does nothing for the grandmother whose project passed; if it triggers on compliant projects too, hearings structurally privilege identifiable losses over the unseen families denied homes. B's answer — that weighing a certain identified injury against speculative gains is a "category error" — is a real philosophical position, but B only restated it rather than dismantling A's rejoinder that the two-bus cleaner's care ties are no less real for being unnamed, and that denial does not remove the transit premium. B's alternatives (vacancy taxes, rent stabilization) protect tenants without adding supply, a gap A exploited in closing. B's closing leaned heavily on labeling A's honest concessions as defeat, but A had already integrated those concessions into a weighing framework. A made the debate easier to decide and answered more of the live pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.4–A4.5: A's dilemma on B's discretionary backstop, paired with the observation that a hearing can always capture the grandmother's loss but never the school pickups a never-proposed home could have enabled — this neutralized B's central mechanism and set up A's closing.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): A won by accepting the motion’s tradeoff more cleanly: it paired by-right approval with enforceable tenant protections, conceded residual disruption, and weighed that against the less visible displacement caused by withholding supply. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won by sharply narrowing the dispute. Its parking lot versus occupied courtyard contrast kept by-right approval for non-displacing sites and isolated the real disagreement to occupied clearance. It also blunted PRO's "exclusion is displacement" point by insisting PRO be credited only with the additional homes, or the earlier homes, that by-right clearance delivers on risky sites, rather than with every unit along the corridor. When PRO called CON's condition "discretionary delay," CON answered convincingly that an objective, published rehousing condition is not a neighbor veto. PRO's later speeches then drifted toward CON's position. It accepted protections priced into the deal and conceded that non-viable parcels "adjust or wait." CON exploited that concession cleanly in its closing. PRO's strongest material, on sprawl, the transit investment and exclusion as lagged displacement, was mostly asserted rather than weighed against CON's marginal-supply framing. PRO's answer to A2 ("demolition sooner prevents more forced moves") was also asserted without support. CON's own answers were honest and direct, including admitting it could not name a percentage. Its rhetoric was memorable and grounded: "defer clearance, not protection" and "who has a roof while they wait."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange runs from A4.4–A4.5 into the closings. CON asked PRO which additional demolition its rule actually permits if PRO's code also requires rehousing before clearance, warning that PRO "cannot count apartments made viable by omitting a protection while taking credit for enforcing it." PRO's closing replied that a parcel unable to bear the protection "adjusts or waits." CON turned this immediately: in that case PRO's own rule withholds permission in exactly the case that divides them, so the gap between the sides collapses in CON's favor.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides argued the motion as written with disciplined, mechanism-based cases and no fake sourcing. A's strongest routes were the exclusion-equals-displacement reframe and the charge that B's protections amount to "discretionary delay wearing a safety vest." But B neutralized the veto charge early ([B2.1], [B4.1]): the rehousing condition is objective, uniform, and involves no neighbor consent, and B honestly bit the bullet on accepting delay when owners decline the discounted offer. B then landed the decisive convergence trap ([B4.4]-[B4.5]): if A's by-right carries full code-level protections, it permits no more clearance than B's rule; the only marginal homes A can claim come from weaker or merely compensatory protection. A's concession that unviable parcels "adjust or waits" ([A5.2]) let B's closing land cleanly: the real dispute reduces to guaranteed rehousing versus a check, and "the tenant still has to find the home the builder could not." A's supply-side weighing ("infill alone cannot absorb demand") stayed an assertion; B's timing asymmetry—tenants need homes the day construction starts, while supply benefits arrive later—blunted it. B made the debate easier to decide and held the live route on the displacement clause.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal convergence trap: "which additional demolition does your rule actually permit? You cannot count apartments made viable by omitting a protection while taking credit for enforcing it" ([B4.4]-[B4.5]), which forced A into the priced-compensation position B then dismantled in closing.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by making the decisive clash clearer: it separated low-displacement by-right sites from occupied-site clearance, then forced A to defend how by-right creates actual interim homes and enforceable returns, not just prices or checks. A’s scarcity and exclusion arguments were strong, but B’s marginal-supply weighing and “who has a roof while waiting” frame gave the cleaner reason to vote CON on the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal turned A’s “price, not veto” answer into a concession: if the owner refuses the lower offer, the project still waits, and B showed why a discount or moving check is not a home. The line about not counting apartments made viable by omitting protections while claiming credit for enforcing them was decisive.
