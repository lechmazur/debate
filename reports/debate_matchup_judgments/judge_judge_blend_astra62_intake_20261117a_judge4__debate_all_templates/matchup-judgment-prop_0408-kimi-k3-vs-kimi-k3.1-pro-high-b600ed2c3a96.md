### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but A's pressure questions exposed a genuine gap in B's case: B's "targeted decision-point support" alternative never named a funded, universal mechanism reaching students before their first major financial decision (the student loan at 17-18). B's answer (school counselors "if the school has one," federal loan entrance counseling run by lenders at the moment of signing, workshops "if budget season spares it") conceded exactly the conditional, patchy delivery A predicted, while A's mandate at least creates an enforceable floor that can be improved. A's framing—harms of degree (quality, fading retention) versus harms of kind (zero baseline)—was repeated effectively across rebuttals and closing, and B never found a clean answer to it beyond asserting both alternatives "require funding and can fail," which is true but doesn't rebut the asymmetry A built. B's strongest material point—that a mandate has guaranteed opportunity costs (displaced instructional time) while benefits are speculative—was real and somewhat under-pressed by A, who mostly reasserted "imperfect guarantee beats perfect absence" rather than naming what's actually displaced. But B also couldn't name a specific casualty when pressed (Q2), retreating to "no single national casualty exists," which reads as an evasion under the pressure-question framework. On balance, A's control of the key comparison (reach/floor vs. targeted-but-contingent help) and cleaner answers to pressure questions give it a clear, non-dominant edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Questions (Q1/Q2 in Turn 5) forcing B to name a funded universal provider and a specific displaced subject—B's answers in Turn 6 and Turn 8 (A1/A2) remained conditional and diffuse, which A then exploited decisively in Turn 7's "zero compounds" framing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): B won by keeping the live clash on what is owed before first adult contracts and by forcing a cleaner comparison: an imperfect enforceable floor versus conditional alternatives that still leave a zero floor for the uninformed. A’s threshold, scarcity, quality, and displacement points were real and disciplined, but never fully neutralized B’s reach/selection argument or named a universal pre-signature provider; B’s pressure and Rebuttal 2 made that failure decisive. A remains competitive on mandate costs and “attendance ≠ protection,” yet B’s harm-of-kind weighing and crystallization of the chain-of-ifs were easier for a third party to vote on.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1–B4.5: mandate creates an enforceable floor; CON’s decision-point plan names neither provider nor displaced requirement, so costs money without the guarantee.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): PRO kept the burden on universal reach: electives select for already-informed, family teaches unequally, largest loan hits at 17. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B built a single, consistent throughline—"coverage versus competence"—and used it to repeatedly trap PRO in its own language, from the early concession that the course "will not make graduates wealthy" to the late admission that it offers merely "a foundation later guidance can build on." B's timing/decay argument and opportunity-cost point about dropout-prone students went only partially answered: A asserted scheduling/support could mitigate dropout pressure but never explained how, while B kept returning to the concrete, certain costs (displaced electives, a barrier for stretched students) against PRO's uncertain, delayed benefits. A's pressure-question answers were honest but conceded ground (no existing requirement guarantees the bundle; failure would mean "reconsidering" rather than defending the mandate), which B exploited cleanly in both the following rebuttal and closing. A's case had real strengths—the algebra/civics analogy and the equity framing—but never neutralized the core decay/opportunity-cost clash, and two clipped speeches (opening and final rebuttal) cost it the chance to fully land its closing argument. B's closing crystallized the clash more sharply and left the sturdier final impression.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R2 response turning A's own hedge ("if the course failed, the mandate should be reconsidered") into "coverage, certified"—paired with the closing's reuse of A's "foundation for later guidance" line to argue the mandate is parasitic on the very infrastructure CON proposed instead.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON won by keeping the decisive clash on what the mandate actually guarantees versus certain costs. PRO's answers on assessment and curriculum process were honest but never neutralized the timing/guarantee weighing or the point that integration-plus-decision-moment tools better match recurrence. Equity stayed live for PRO, yet CON made the comparison easier to decide: a graduation box vs. what schooling reliably delivers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 and closing lock on PRO's own A1 ("performance tasks" / "reconsidered") and foundation retreat, converting coverage-at-18 into the vote reason against certain costs.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): PRO kept a live guarantee route: universal practice and assessment on taxes, contracts, insurance, fraud, budgeting via measurable performance tasks.
