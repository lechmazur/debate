B also never squarely neutralized the charge that its alternative is the motion plus a label. A answered pressure directly in the required A1/A2 format, adapted across rounds, and made the debate easier to decide; B's case remained a critique of drafting specificity rather than a reason to reject accountability. Clear but competitive edge to A.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Both sides were competent and disciplined, but B imposed a persistent dilemma that A never fully broke: either the provider controls deployment (making it a deployer already reachable by negligence, warranty, fraud, and anti-discrimination law, so the motion is redundant) or it does not (in which case deployed behavior is jointly produced by prompts, integrations, and review, and "foreseeable harm plus omitted safeguards" becomes a hindsight inquiry without a stable defect). A offered real counterweights—an upstream duty to test, warn, restrict, and update distinct from deployer choices; safe harbor for prudent providers; negligence-style factors (severity, likelihood, exposure) as the ex ante standard; and the victim-compensation gap. These kept A's route alive, and A's rebuttals adapted well, notably conceding open-source carve-outs and reframing the clash as risk-controller versus after-the-fact responder. But B's rejoinders were sharper: the carve-outs "relocate the defendant," the safe harbor "protects only providers who guessed right," the safeguard list is "a menu, not a standard," and shared causation is precisely why product-liability calibration fails. B's pressure questions exposed the live tension, and B's answers (deployer insolvency is an insurance problem, not a liability-structure problem) were direct. B's closing crystallized the decisive comparison more memorably ("hindsight and a deep pocket"), while A's closing partly recapped. Neither side used fake sourcing or brittle precision. B wins on cleaner clash resolution and stronger weighing, with A's compensation/incentive route only partially neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 [B2.1]-[B2.2]: the observation that PRO's carve-out of providers "controlling deployment risk" describes deployers, meaning the motion adds a new defendant rather than a missing remedy, while the distinctive target—the upstream general-model provider—is exactly where PRO cannot identify a stable defect. A's joint-responsibility answer [A4.2] kept the route alive, but B's closing [B5.3] turned A's "shared causation" concession into the decisive calibration problem.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): CON's dilemma survived: when providers control deployment they are deployers already reachable by existing law, and when they do not, PRO never supplied an ex ante defect or safeguard standard distinct from hindsight negligence. PRO showed upstream capacity and joint responsibility, but not a workable product-liability boundary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and pressure follow-up were decisive: PRO's narrowing to providers controlling deployment risk either duplicates deployer liability or leaves indeterminate upstream liability for unknown integrations.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): B narrowly won by keeping the debate on a workable liability standard: foreseeable risk, supported uses, proportionate safeguards, and safe harbor. A's defect/causal-control attack was sharp, but B adequately answered that shared causation does not erase upstream omissions and that existing remedies can leave compensation gaps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's claim that PRO's standard collapses into deployer liability versus B's Rebuttal 2 answer that provider duties such as testing, warnings, updates, and restrictions remain distinct upstream omissions.
