### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A (PRO) won solidly by establishing a clear, order-of-magnitude break-even framework and successfully defending it against every pushback. Because methane traps roughly 80 times more heat than CO2 over twenty years, CCS at $50 to over $100 per tonne requires methane abatement to exceed several thousand dollars per tonne before CCS draws level. Side B (CON) mounted a smart conceptual challenge focused on the word "tightening," arguing that once easy super-emitters are fixed, tighter rules spend heavily on surveys and tiny intermittent leaks. However, PRO conceded rising marginal costs while dismantling CON's core premise: large leaks are recurring malfunctions (such as stuck tank hatches and unlit flares) caught faster by frequent monitoring, not a static stock cleared once. PRO also turned the fossil-rebound argument via enhanced oil recovery, exposed CON's reliance on cherry-picked CCS sites with pre-existing infrastructure, and cleanly resolved CON's late unit-conversion doubt using the gas-price anchor already established. CON dropped the recurring-malfunction rebuttal entirely and offered no quantitative path across PRO's roughly tenfold buffer. Minor tail-clipping on three of Side A's turns slightly trims the margin but did not leave any substantive burden unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In B2.2 and B3.2, Side B cleverly flipped Side A's super-emitter point, arguing that initial inspections clear big conspicuous leaks cheaply and leave tightened rules to chase small, costly intermittent leaks. In A4.3-A4.5, Side A conceded the upward cost slope but refuted the static-stock premise by explaining that super-emitters are recurring malfunctions—stuck hatches, unlit flares, failed seals—that keep appearing at new sites, making more frequent detection the exact way to catch big plumes, all with a roughly tenfold cost buffer to spare. Side B never answered that mechanism.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by converting methane’s near-term potency into a break-even cost test that Side A never showed tightening would miss. B’s recurring-malfunction answer blunted A’s best point—that later rules only chase expensive leftovers—while A conceded the cited ranges generally stay inside the gap and never showed modular CCS closing it on speed or verified tonnes. Clipped turns cost B some finish, but the comparative case stayed clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal and closing: recurring super-emitters answered the rising-marginal-cost curve, and A’s concession that the stated costs do not clear the eightyfold bar left only an unquantified hunch.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): B won by turning methane’s potency into a clear break-even test: equivalent CCS spending would need to avoid roughly 80 tonnes of CO2 per tonne of methane, and A ultimately accepted that the stated cost ranges generally do not cross that threshold. A’s strongest objections—marginal costs under tighter rules and possible CO2-equivalent double-counting—remained relevant, but A never quantified the necessary cost escalation or established that modular CCS would outperform repairs on speed and delivered benefits. B’s concessions and recurring-large-leak answer preserved the stronger comparative case.

A’s subsequent concession on the break-even figures left mainly an unquantified marginal-cost objection.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B won by anchoring the debate to the motion's exact marginal comparison—tightening beyond existing baseline rules at equivalent spending—and trapping Side A in a decisive dilemma. After Side B showed that dominant oil and gas methane plumes stem from intermittent events, venting, and unlit flares that scheduled surveys miss, Side A first expanded its policy to gas-recovery infrastructure and then retreated under pressure in [A4.3] to narrow component swaps. That retreat preserved speed but left the largest emissions unaddressed, while Side A never convincingly showed what the marginal dollars of an equal budget buy once profitable and already-mandated fixes are exhausted, especially since aging equipment creates recurring leaks. Side B also narrowed the near-term timeline gap by pointing to mature, high-purity CO2 streams in ethanol, ammonia, and gas processing. Both sides paid execution costs—Side A cited nonexistent [C2.x] IDs and inverted its own Q2 in [A3.2], while Side B was clipped at the word limit in four turns, tempering the margin—but Side B's core arguments landed before each clip and controlled the central clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's multi-turn dilemma on intermittent super-emitters ([B2.1]-[B2.4], [B3.2], [B4.4]-[B4.9]): when Side A answered intermittent venting and flaring in [A2.2] by proposing equipment replacement and gas recovery, Side B pointed out that gathering lines and compressors require the same capital permitting and construction timeline Side A criticized in CCS, forcing Side A to drop recovery infrastructure in [A4.3] and fall back on a narrow repair program that misses the largest methane releases.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A more convincingly showed the proposition fails the motion’s own test of tightening at equivalent scale. Incremental LDAR beyond already-profitable or mandated repairs is a shallow pool that misses major intermittent sources unless expanded into slower capital work, a dilemma Side B never escaped. Side B’s retreats from recovery infrastructure and failure to specify last-dollar tonnes left near-term superiority unproven against metered high-purity CCS that can run within a few years.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 dilemma: a narrow crew-and-wrench program is fast but adds few tonnes beyond the baseline, while the broad program needed for big intermittent releases recreates CCS timelines—so neither beats equivalent CCS spending on near-term climate benefit.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): A won by keeping the motion focused on tightening and equivalent spending, then exposing an unresolved dilemma: a narrow methane program is fast but adds limited abatement and misses major intermittent sources, while a broader program requires capture-like capital timelines. B established methane’s speed and plausibility but never adequately showed what the last dollar of a capture-sized budget buys or why it beats high-purity CCS. A’s repeated clipping and B’s generally coherent case keep the margin competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions, used B’s retreat from recovery infrastructure to construct the narrow-versus-broad program dilemma, and showed that B’s “next-worst sources” answer conceded declining marginal abatement.
