### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by keeping the statutory-fit and overbreadth objection live. Its strongest path was that securities-exchange and broker-dealer regulation is built for registered corporate securities, issuer filings, clearinghouses, and leveraged credit intermediaries, not issuerless bearer digital assets and non-custodial protocols. Side B pressed FTX and the bundled-conflict story well, and its observation that CON’s lighter category kept borrowing protections was sharp. But Side A answered the central pressure by showing its category could still require 1:1 segregation, a lending ban, reserve checks, independent audits, and order-flow oversight without full broker-dealer machinery. It also exposed a real tension in Side B’s Treasury-bond analogy: government bonds are still statutory securities, and tailoring issuer rules for commodities risks creating the very distinct category the motion opposes. Side B’s shift from “most platforms” to “most retail money/volume” did not fully neutralize Side A’s literal count-based objection. The debate was competitive, but Side A gave the cleaner route to vote against replacing the existing framework with securities regulation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s rebuttal that PRO’s issuerless-token answer still requires bespoke commodity-like tailoring, not existing securities regulation, was the most decisive exchange. It undercut PRO’s claim that the motion could simply regulate the platform rather than the asset, turning PRO’s Treasury-bond analogy into evidence that the proposed model was a distinct category in disguise.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a close, high-quality debate on the strength of its convergence narrative: every time the FTX problem was pressed, Side A's "light" category absorbed another broker-dealer protection (segregation, lending bans, audits, order-flow inspection), and the residual withheld items — capital cushions and structural separation of the affiliated desk — were exactly the anti-insider constraints that matter. Side A answered the pressure questions directly and mounted a real counter-architecture (bearer assets vs. leveraged credit intermediaries, 24/7 withdrawals as discipline), and its "most platforms are non-custodial" definitional route was never fully refuted, only reframed by B toward where retail savings actually sit — a mild evasion of the motion as written. But B's Treasury/municipal-bond analogy cleanly defused the "tailoring = distinct category" trap, and the closing point that a firewall inside one firm is a policy the firm enforces against itself went unanswered. Both sides argued the motion as written, used plausible background knowledge without fake sourcing, and followed format. B made the decisive clash clearer and held the stronger ground on the protections actually in dispute, while A's definitional and internal-contradiction hits kept it competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (B4): the A1 answer using exempt Treasury/municipal bonds to show intermediary duties can attach without issuer registration, combined with the B4.6-B4.7 narrowing that Side A's regime verifies the vault but never constrains the affiliated desk or capital shortfalls — the frame that "CON accepts the securities model wherever it is cheap and refuses it where conflicted insiders would actually be constrained."
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side A won by turning the debate into a clear test of which regime actually prevents custodial abuse before losses occur. A used pressure questions and rebuttals effectively to show that B’s supposedly light category kept absorbing securities-style safeguards, while still refusing capital requirements, liability-focused examination, and meaningful separation of conflicted desks. B made strong points about issuerless tokens, decentralized protocols, and offshore flight, but never fully answered A’s core claim that reserve checks and firewalls are weaker than enforceable broker-dealer constraints when the platform can hide liabilities or exploit order flow.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered the issuerless-token challenge by focusing on platform duties rather than asset registration, then isolated the remaining gap in B’s model—no capital cushion, no liability-focused examination, and no real constraint on affiliated desks.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): A (CON) wins a competitive debate by turning PRO's key concessions into a cleaner comparative case. PRO forced A to accept most substantive broker-dealer protections, but A showed those protections are portable into a bespoke regime and do not require securities-exchange classification. A then exploited PRO's admissions that every rule could theoretically be copied into a separate statute and that unregistered or teamless tokens would be delisted, arguing the classification adds mainly a registration gate that pushes users offshore. PRO's case was strong on centralized-platform conflicts and off-chain risks, but it did not fully neutralize A's final weighing that delisting leaves retail holders with the same opaque asset and fewer enforceable protections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 [A4.4-A4.5] was the decisive exchange: it used PRO's admission that the protections could be copied into a separate statute and that domestic venues must delist non-registering tokens, then reduced the remaining difference to a gate that sends holders into unregulated offshore markets.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by converting Side B's own concessions into the decision rule. B's rebuttal 2 admitted that every operational protection (custody, capital, surveillance, best execution) is portable to a bespoke statute, leaving issuer registration as the only unique feature of securities classification; A then showed that this gate produces delistings rather than disclosures, pushing trading to offshore venues B conceded users would reach. B's counter — that a fully-loaded bespoke regime is no longer "lightly supervised" and that registration deters token-wrapped equity evasion — remained live but was more semantic and less weighed against the offshore consequence. B's off-chain internal ledger point was strong, but A neutralized it by noting broker-dealers also internalize and hold customer assets under one roof with duties attached. A's pharmacy-analogy reversal, sharper pressure questions, direct A1/A2 answers, and crystallizing closing ("imperfect light beats guaranteed darkness," "governs an emptied room") made the clash easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B4.1's admission that "legislators could theoretically copy every exchange and broker-dealer rule into a separate statute," which A4.4 seized immediately: the protections are portable and only the label and registration gate are distinct — a gate B admitted in B4.2 forces delisting ("yes, domestic venues must delist it"), which A showed yields no disclosure while exporting holders beyond all regulation.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B won by turning the debate into a practical comparison between two regimes and by forcing Side A into two damaging concessions: that many investor protections could be copied into a bespoke statute, and that the unique securities-registration gate would require delisting tokens that still trade elsewhere. B answered pressure questions directly, kept the focus on custody, capital, surveillance, execution, and offshore flight, and gave the cleaner final weighing: a distinct but robust regime keeps more activity inside enforceable rules. A argued forcefully that issuer registration and clearing-like safeguards are essential, but did not fully prove that those protections are unavailable or ineffective in B’s tailored framework.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttal to A’s pressure questions and Turn 8 was decisive: B conceded the need for core protections, identified that A admitted they could be legislated separately, and isolated securities classification as mainly a delisting gate with offshore consequences.
