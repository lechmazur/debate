### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B won decisively by separating the motion's beneficial victim-support half from its counterproductive prohibition half and exposing the operational and economic flaws in Side A's case. Side A argued that pairing a ban with 24/7 crisis aid makes non-payment rational and starves ransomware gangs, but conceded that underground transactions would still occur at higher risk premiums. Side B turned that concession effectively, showing that desperate victims bear black-market premiums, IT restoration is a slow process rather than an instant fix for corrupted backups or impending data leaks, and criminal liability chills incident reporting. Side B sealed the debate with an unanswered dilemma: if state support suffices to make victims refuse, conditioning aid on non-payment with narrow, traced exceptions achieves the goal without a criminal ban; if support falls short in acute crises, prohibition fails dangerously by coercing victims into costly secrecy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B2.1]-[B2.2] and [B4.4], Side B seized on Side A's claim in [A2.1] that bans force criminals to "demand premiums" on underground payments, showing that desperate hospitals and clinics pay those surcharges—taxing victims in crisis while leaving total criminal revenue uncertain—before locking Side A into an unanswered fork in [B4.1]-[B4.2] on whether state support alone already drives non-payment.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by trapping the proposition in a live comparison Side B never closed. If victim support truly makes non-payment rational, a ban is redundant and can be replaced by conditioned aid plus narrow exceptions; if it does not, prohibition merely drives premiums underground and criminalizes the last lawful lever before irreversible deadlines. Side B kept asserting a revenue death spiral and market collapse without showing why volume loss would outweigh secret premiums or why reporting would rise once a report becomes a confession. A's fail-safe versus fail-dangerous weighing made the vote clearer than B's repeated slogan that any payment funds crime.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal and closing pressed the redundancy trap: B had already conceded underground payments, so either support already secures refusal and the ban is unnecessary, or the ban produces hidden premiums and concealment rather than collapse.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B won the central clash: A conceded underground payments would persist but never proved total revenue collapses rather than shifting into victim-paid premiums. B also showed support cannot guarantee timely restoration or prevent irreversible data leaks, making non-payment not reliably rational. B's regulated last-resort alternative, with reporting and recovery aid, provided a safer comparative path that A answered mainly by repeating that any payment funds crime.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 dilemma was decisive: if state support already makes non-payment the default, the ban is redundant; if it does not, prohibition produces secret premiums and removes the victim's last lawful lever.
