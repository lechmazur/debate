### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): This was an exceptionally close, disciplined debate with no fake sourcing and consistent A1/A2 compliance. Both sides built coherent cases: A defended adaptation-led reform with targeted recruitment for residual gaps; B argued essential care shifts need people and that capacity must be built before caregiver retirements hit. B edges it on the decisive clash: the fallacy-of-composition argument (every town hoping to recruit its neighbor's worker cannot form a national plan, so "the sum of targeted offers" is significant immigration) directly undermined A's middle position, and B pre-empted A's final "organizing principle vs. sum" move in the closing. A landed real counters—turning B's own per-yen test toward software and noting immigration meets demand without reducing it—but A's final characterization of B as sequencing recruitment "after" reform slightly misdescribed B's concurrent-reform framing, and A never fully quantified how much labor demand redesign could actually remove. B made the debate marginally easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The B4.5/B5.2–B5.3 sequence versus A5.3–A5.4: B argued that towns facing simultaneous caregiver retirements cannot all solve staffing by recruiting from one another, so the sum of "targeted" admissions is itself significant immigration as a de facto priority ("Calling each offer targeted does not make their sum small"). A's reply—that scale should follow the redesigned system's needs rather than lead it—was coherent but largely definitional, leaving B's aggregate-timing pressure intact.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A consistently forced PRO to distinguish targeted, projected-gap recruitment from making immigration the national organizing priority. A answered the timing and funding tests directly, accepted substantial residual admissions, and showed demand reduction—especially automation freeing existing caregivers—is more durable. PRO proved some care shifts need matched recruits, but not that immigration should lead over adaptation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A’s Rebuttal 2: B’s cost-per-coverage test also justifies care-sector automation, while the remaining night-shift gap establishes targeted immigration, not a national priority defined by admissions growth.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): A won by controlling the burden. It conceded that immigration may be necessary and even substantial for credible residual care gaps, but kept the debate on whether immigration should be the organizing priority. B made the human stakes vivid and landed strong points about irreducible night-shift care and lead times, yet its own cost-per-reliable-coverage test collapsed into project-by-project comparison, allowing A to argue that adaptation and demand reduction should lead while immigration complements them.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s “only one starts with a match” answer sharply challenged A’s automation-trickle-down theory, but A’s Rebuttal 2 and closing defused it by accepting direct recruitment for credible projected gaps while denying that this makes immigration the principal strategy.
