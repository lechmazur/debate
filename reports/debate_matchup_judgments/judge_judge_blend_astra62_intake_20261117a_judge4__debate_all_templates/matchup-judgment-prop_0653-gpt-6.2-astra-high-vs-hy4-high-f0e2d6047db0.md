### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): PRO wins by steadily narrowing the debate to one question: what value does an individual-level prediction add beyond non-individual alternatives, and what happens when safeguards fail? PRO's machinist example and the self-fulfilling-forecast mechanism set up a strong harm story. CON responded with a sophisticated "govern use, not the instrument" regime. But under pressure, CON conceded that individual rankings have no proper role in training selection and that shift-level forecasts "often" suffice for quit risk. It then kept tightening its rules until they nearly matched a ban: no named referrals, no manager access, no material use, mandatory deletion. That left CON defending only a residual "smoke alarm" margin of precision. PRO credited that margin honestly and weighed it against irreversible harm when safeguards fail. PRO also answered the "invisible hunch" line well: authorizing scores does not put a supervisor's impression on the record, and a ban gives auditors a cleaner violation test. CON's "recorded power vs invisible power" framing was memorable and remained its best route. However, PRO showed that the hunch persists under either regime, so the frame did not carry enough weight. Both sides kept good evidentiary discipline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Q1/Q2 and the A1/A2 responses that followed. CON admitted that a score shaping training invitations changes the worker's chance, and that shift forecasts often suffice. This forced CON to defend only a narrow precision gain. PRO's closing then distinguished counting exposure ("workers on back-to-back shifts") from assigning each person odds, which neutralized CON's attempt to turn A4.3 against it.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side A won by systematically exposing the leaks in Side B's regulatory safeguards and winning the marginal weighing on what remained. Side A showed that "not sole basis" rules and forecast-based training invitations still deny opportunities through unverifiable counterfactuals, forcing Side B to concede away individual rankings, invitations, manager access, and named compliance referrals. Once Side B retreated to defending individual scores only as confidential, immediately deleted inputs for unit-wide audits and universal schedule repairs, Side A showed that direct group-level metrics—such as counting workers assigned back-to-back shifts, inviting all qualified trainees, and auditing unit-wide assignment changes—achieve nearly all the preventative value without generating sensitive individual labels that could fail safely only if never leaked. Side A also punctured Side B's "hallway hunch" defense by showing that unlogged supervisor impressions remain untouched under Side B's own manager firewall, while a ban creates a cleaner bright-line violation than policing downstream misuse.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A2.2]-[A2.3] and [A3.1], Side A dismantled Side B's training safeguards in two steps: first showing that human review, file contestability, and a "not sole basis" rule still leave a passed-over machinist unable to disprove an unverifiable forecast about equipment she was never trained to use, and then catching in Q1 that letting scores "invite candidates" [B2.3] still shapes who gets a meaningful chance—forcing Side B in [B2.3], [B4.1], and [B4.6] to abandon individual rankings and selection uses altogether.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): PRO won by showing that the legitimate aims—fair training access, whole-shift repair, and unit-wide retaliation audits—do not require scoring an individual worker's future productivity, quitting, or organizing. CON adapted well and tightened use rules, but those concessions stripped individual prediction of most of its work, leaving only a small timing gain against irreversible misuse and harder-to-prove influence. PRO's closing distinction between counting bad schedules and assigning quit odds made the vote clearer than CON's recorded-versus-invisible-power frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's questions and second rebuttal forced CON to concede that an individual score cannot shape invitations or selections and often adds nothing a shift-level warning cannot do; the closing then separated counting who has bad hours from predicting who will quit.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): PRO wins narrowly. CON gave up most of the substantive ground early. It conceded that individual unionization scoring should be banned outright. It also conceded that managers should be denied lists of likely quitters. Its entire defense against the motion then rested on one tightly controlled hypothetical: a worker-only tutoring forecast. CON argued that case with real precision. Its point that counting errors cannot tell the cashier which course yields the larger gain was clever. So was its argument that inspection is needed under either rule. PRO, however, did three things that held. It forced CON to admit that all five terms of the motion apply to the cashier example. It showed that most of the tutoring value survives a ban, through error feedback, course matching, and letting her sample modules. And it weighed contraband against a lawful product that sits one configuration change from misuse. That left CON defending a narrow carve-out with modest benefits against a ban whose core rationale CON itself largely accepted. CON's closing landed a real blow: the bright line is less bright than PRO claims. But the comparison still favors PRO, because the cost CON identified is small and contained.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.3–B4.4 was the strongest exchange. It argued that a vendor tutor exists under either rule. It also pointed out that PRO's inspectors must still distinguish matching a recorded stall from predicting a future gain. This directly undercut PRO's bright-line and "contraband" enforcement claim. PRO's A4.1 answer was its best reply. It conceded course matching but drew the line at forecasting, which kept most of the tutoring value while preserving the ban, and that limited the damage.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by conceding prohibitions on unionization scoring and manager-facing quitting or productivity lists, then exposing a real overbreadth flaw in the blanket ban: outlawing worker-only AI training tools that estimate which course will improve an individual worker's performance more. Side B refuted Side A's claim that current error-counting suffices (contrasting frequent mistakes already improving against rarer, lane-halting stalls) and neutralized Side A's strongest institutional point (that lawful predictors are one configuration change from diversion) by showing Side A's own allowance for error-matching tutors still permits vendor contracts on practice telemetry while creating a harder inspection problem. Side A had vivid framing and forced Side B from access logs to ephemeral local computation, but hurt its back-half execution by prematurely faulting Side B in Turn 7 for not answering Q1 before Side B's turn to answer, misattributing [B4.4]'s inspection burden in Closing, and conflating what the proposed ban covers with why the worker should lose that tool.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B4.3-B4.5], Side B turned Side A's strong "contraband vs. lawful workflow" argument [A4.5] using Side A's own [A4.1] answer: because Side A still lets firms buy tutors that read practice runs and recommend modules, lawful vendor contracts exist either way, and Side A's rule forces inspectors to audit internal logic to distinguish past-stall matching from predicted time saved, whereas Side B's rule tests whether anything is retained or leaves the terminal.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B more cleanly showed that the motion is overbroad. The worker-only training comparison is covered by the ban, yet it supplies a real choice that error-counting cannot, while targeted no-retention and no-export rules address management misuse. Side A’s bright-line and diversion arguments were strong but left the practical indistinguishability of predictive versus descriptive tutoring under-answered, and the closing treated textual coverage as if it settled the normative question.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 and closing: identical course recommendations do not reveal whether software matched a current stall or predicted a future gain, so Side A’s ban also depends on hard internal inspection while still denying the cashier a comparison she alone would see.
