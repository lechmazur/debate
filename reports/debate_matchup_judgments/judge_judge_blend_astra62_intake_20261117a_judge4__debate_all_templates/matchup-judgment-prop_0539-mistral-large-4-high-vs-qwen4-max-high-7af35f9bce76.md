### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins on comparative weighing. CON (A) built a sharp structural claim: across borders, no single authority reaches the whole transaction, so "enforceable" standards cannot be met and conditional legality only certifies exploitation. That line stayed coherent all the way through. PRO consistently forced the comparison CON avoided. A ban faces the same jurisdictional limits and the same forgeable documents, and it gives up every lever: licences, escrow, recognition conditions, funded counsel and sanctions on brokers who recruit destination-state clients. PRO answered both pressure questions directly. It tied escrow release to documented protocol steps and argued that pre-fixed parentage means refusing recognition need not strand children. CON's A2 answer treated escrow as releasing only on delivery and did not engage PRO's protocol-conditioned release. CON also never showed what its own rule puts in the surrogate's hands. CON's best point remained live: real-time control over medical decisions abroad. But PRO's framing — "which rule leaves the woman with something in her hands" — made the debate easier to decide in its favor. CON also never fully explained why its conclusion differs from the motion's own conditional-legality structure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B2.1–B2.6 and B4.3 turned CON's jurisdiction argument into a comparative liability. A ban inherits the same reach limits with none of the leverage, and CON's Thailand example shows a crackdown stranding families rather than protecting them. CON's forgery answer (illegality refuses to certify) was clever. PRO's closing blunted it: under a licence a false record costs the operator something, while under a ban nothing was ever conditioned on it.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): A offered the stronger comparative case: conditional legality supplies financial, legal, and licensing leverage, while prohibition inherits the jurisdictional problems B identified. B raised substantial concerns about recognition and medical autonomy, but A answered with pre-transfer parentage, accessible funds, and prospective provider incentives. B never established that enforceability requires one all-powerful regulator or that prohibition better protects surrogates. A overstated how completely bans eliminate enforcement tools, limiting the margin, but B's categorical impossibility claim remained less persuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest move was asking what prohibition detects or prevents that regulated verification cannot. B conceded that illegality detects no more, then defended refusing certification rather than demonstrating better protection. Combined with A's distinction between enforceable incentives and constant physical supervision, this undercut B's central route to victory.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): A won by turning enforceability into a comparative institutional argument rather than promising perfect jurisdiction. Its recognition, market-access, escrow, parentage, insurance, and clinic-licensing mechanisms answered B’s central pressure while showing that prohibition inherits the same cross-border and forgery problems but removes affirmative protections. B landed a strong objection concerning medical decisions during pregnancy, but relied too heavily on categorical claims that enforcement is impossible and never established that its ban leaves surrogates or children better protected.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions with sanctions on destination-market revenue, parentage fixed before transfer, and payment and licensing conditions governing treatment. B’s response that delivery-linked escrow cannot control earlier medical decisions was the strongest counter, but it did not fully engage the combined local-clinic and destination-state mechanisms or repair the comparative weakness of prohibition.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins. It identified the central fault line early: cross-border enforceability, and whether the "only when" condition does any protective work. When PRO answered the decertification scenario by saying the surrogate's rights vest statutorily and survive any loss of compliance, CON turned that into a dilemma. Either the condition is not what protects her, or the original objection stands. The pressure questions then forced two damaging admissions. First, protections attach even when the broker never complied. Second, corridors without secured recognition fail the condition and close. CON showed these admissions empty the motion's operative words and reproduce CON's outcome. CON also blunted PRO's main "levers vs no levers" frame. Asset freezes and prosecutions of home-state brokers exist under prohibition, and destination licensing is separate from home-state permission. PRO's case was coherent and well structured, but it largely repeated the leverage and visibility frame without answering the vacuity charge. Its closing assertion that a black market makes inspection impossible came late and was underdeveloped.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive sequence was A2.1–A2.2, carried through A4.3–A4.4. CON took PRO's "protections vest at inception and survive collapse" and the admission that a "corridor fails the condition" when recognition cannot be secured. It argued these leave the legality condition doing no protective work, while effectively conceding prohibition wherever recognition is unavailable. PRO never squarely escaped this dilemma.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): A wins on the proposition’s central distinction: providers’ permission to operate can depend on compliance without women’s and children’s protections doing so. B repeatedly treats those conditions as inseparable, even after A explains the distinction. A also allows closure of unverifiable corridors without requiring prohibition everywhere; B asserts, rather than establishes, that compliant corridors are impossible. B sharply exposes A’s exaggerated claim that prohibition provides no enforcement leverage, but does not eliminate the comparative benefit of a lawful, supervised channel.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest rebuttal establishes that domestic asset freezes and prosecutions remain available under prohibition. However, A’s answer to the decertification challenge preserves unconditional personal protections while sanctioning noncompliant providers. B’s response—that unconditional rights make licensing pointless—does not defeat that distinction.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B won by exposing a dilemma A never fully escaped: either surrogate and child protections survive noncompliance, making them independent of the motion’s legality condition, or they lapse and punish the vulnerable parties. B also showed that asset freezes, prosecutions, and destination licensing do not uniquely require conditional commercial legality, while A’s parentage mechanism ultimately depended on foreign recognition or corridor closure. A offered a plausible supervised-market alternative and answered the required questions directly, but its later safeguards increasingly detached protection from the proposition’s operative “only when.”
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal crystallized the decisive dilemma around protections vesting at inception; its second rebuttal then showed that A’s enforcement tools survive prohibition and that the pre-birth parentage order either assumes the disputed recognition or closes the corridor.
