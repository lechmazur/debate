### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): PRO wins clearly. It set the key distinction early: weak commitment belongs to silence and vagueness, not to cohabitation as a category. It then used CON's own standard to establish that point. CON's demand to "name intentions and set terms before sharing a home" (B1.6) admits that cohabitation can be responsible. CON's later "neutral rule" of "ordinary for people, explicit about commitment" (B4.3) comes close to conceding the motion outright. CON's best route was the "social defaults" argument: an ordinary label could act as a shield and suppress the commitment conversation. That route was sharp, but it stayed largely speculative. PRO answered it directly by separating permission from approval and by pointing out that stigma creates its own pressure to hide or rush into vows. CON also had internal and execution weaknesses: - **Opening tension:** B1.2 calls cohabitation an "easier exit" and then says the exit is expensive. - **Misattribution:** CON twice credited its own concession to PRO (B4.1, B5.1). - **Faulty references:** CON cited nonexistent IDs ("P2.4"). PRO also answered both pressure questions cleanly with A1:/A2: labels. Its closing narrowed the clash to a clear measure: a voluntary, informed promise versus difficulty of exit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4.5–A4.6 and A5.1 decided the debate. They seized on CON's rule requiring explicit terms before moving in, and later on its "ordinary for people, explicit about commitment" formulation, to show that CON had given up the categorical claim. At most, CON had proven that silent cohabitation can signal weak commitment, which is equally true of silent engagements and inertial marriages. That left CON's warning-label case attached to vagueness rather than to cohabitation itself.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins solidly by maintaining a coherent metric of commitment and trapping Side A (CON) in a fatal categorical retreat. Side A opened well by flipping the apartment-inspection analogy into a household lock-in story and arguing in Rebuttal 1 that an "ordinary" default acts as a shield against explicit marriage talks. However, Side B convincingly showed that social permission for a step lowers shame without endorsing silence, and that stigma creates worse distortions—hiding, premature engagements, and pressured vows—which Side A never squarely answered. Worse for Side A, its attempt in Rebuttal 2 to disavow suspicion in favor of a rule that is "ordinary for people, explicit about commitment" effectively conceded the motion against treating the category as a sign of weak commitment, while Side A also grew repetitive late and twice misattributed its own concession about explicit cohabitation to PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side A argued that ordinariness shields silence [A2.3] yet retreated from suspicion to propose a rule "ordinary for people, explicit about commitment" [A4.3], Side B cleanly separated social permission from couple-level silence [B4.1]-[B4.2] and seized on [A4.3] in Closing [B5.1]-[B5.2] to show Side A had surrendered the categorical claim: the defect is vagueness, which afflicts engagements and marriages too, not cohabitation itself.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that the motion’s social default—treating cohabitation as ordinary and reasonable rather than a possible sign of weak commitment—operates before any conversation and can blunt the very prompt for explicit terms that both sides say matters. Side B effectively separated bad instances from the category and rejected stigma, but never fully neutralized how dropping the cautionary “sign” makes the commitment question easier to defer until exit costs have risen. A’s false-dichotomy rebuttal (neither stigma nor unexamined ordinariness) and closing on cheap-exit timing made the decisive comparison clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal and closing, which answered the concession claim by distinguishing workable individual cases from the motion’s categorical presumption and showed why “ordinary rather than a sign” can itself become a shield against the early commitment question.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Both sides were competent. B (CON) built the cleaner path to victory. PRO's case rested on three supports: cohabitation's high exit costs show commitment, prevalence makes it ordinary, and stigma silences the vulnerable partner. CON neutralized each one. Exit cost is entanglement or friction, not a promised future. Prevalence describes behavior but does not establish reasonableness. And ambiguity, rather than stigma, is what shelters "not yet." PRO's best counter was that CON begs the question by defining commitment as the vow. PRO's stigma-silences-the-vulnerable mechanism was also creative, but it stayed assertive and contested rather than demonstrated. PRO then conceded that "nothing coerces an answer" and that a "sign" can mark a missing structural element. CON exploited both concessions effectively in its second rebuttal and closing. PRO's closing largely restated earlier points, and it borrowed CON's own "before debts, pregnancy… make silence expensive" framing without showing why its mechanism delivers that clarity. CON narrowed the clash more sharply and weighed it more memorably.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal turned PRO's A1 answer against it. "Nothing coerces an answer" was framed as conceding that normalizing move-in licenses "not yet" without breach. CON paired this with the line "Ambiguity, not stigma, shelters 'not yet'": in a normalized setting, "Let's not label this" sounds mature and the marriage-seeking partner sounds premature. That directly inverted PRO's main vulnerable-partner argument.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won solidly by establishing a clear distinction between domestic entanglement (shared rent, bills, and exit friction) and a promised future horizon, while winning the decisive clash over asymmetric vulnerability. Side B overreached in claiming leases impose higher exit costs than marriage, contradicted its own earlier premise on what a "sign" requires, and failed to defend its "routine checkpoint" mechanism once it conceded in A1 that ordinary status coerces no answer—proving Side A's point that normalizing pre-vow cohabitation licenses "not yet" while one partner bears escalating domestic risk.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's refutation of Side B's "routine checkpoint" turn ([A4.1]-[A4.2], [A4.6]), using Side B's own admission ("Nothing coerces an answer" [B4.1]) to show that treating pre-pledge move-ins as ordinary validates "let's not label this" as mature while making a request for marital clarity look premature and pushy.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by keeping the motion’s normative burden in view: calling pre-vow cohabitation an ordinary, reasonable step licenses “not yet,” not a promised future. Side B’s practice-versus-ceremony case and stigma mechanism were coherent, but never repaired the concession that ordinary status coerces no marriage answer. A’s distinction between costly entanglement and a mutual pledge survived, and the closing turned that gap into the cleaner reason to reject the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal and closing weaponized B’s “Nothing coerces an answer,” showing a routine checkpoint is not a promise and that ordinary status validates the lease while leaving the hopeful partner unable to treat “not yet” as a breach.
