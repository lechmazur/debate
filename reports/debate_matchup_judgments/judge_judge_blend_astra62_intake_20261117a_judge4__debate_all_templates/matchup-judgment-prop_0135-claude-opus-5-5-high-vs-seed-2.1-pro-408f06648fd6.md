### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): A won by keeping the debate focused on the motion's actual conditional. B's strongest argument was that targeted rules are better than blunt caps, but A turned that into a dilemma: if those rules fix the harms, the cap condition never triggers; if they don't, B has no answer for residents still absorbing net losses. A also showed that timed routing and peak pricing are themselves forms of metered arrivals, meaning B's cap-versus-alternative contrast largely collapsed. B raised real concerns about mismeasurement and regressivity, but A answered with institutional guardrails and equitable allocation, while B never directly explained why residents should keep paying a losing bill.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's most decisive rebuttal exposed that B's preferred fixes either prevent the cap from triggering or operate as caps under another name: shore power does not widen medieval streets, while timed routing and peak pricing necessarily meter arrivals.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A masterfully exploited the motion's conditional trigger: every CON benefit (return-visitor pipeline, earmarked fees, goodwill) belongs on the revenue side of the scale, so if real, the cap never fires. A's pressure question forced CON to finally name alternatives (shore power, timed routing, peak fees, rental caps), and A's closing sealed the trap: if those tools work, costs stop outweighing gains; if they don't, CON has nothing to offer residents still losing. A also landed that timed routing and peak pricing are themselves de facto arrival metering, and that a medieval street's capacity is a hard physical limit no fee can widen. CON's regressivity point was adequately answered (lotteries, timed entry, no auction required), and the fixed-cost argument was blunted by A's sharp Q2 about why residents must absorb passengers to fund visitor-scaled infrastructure. A used required Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 formats cleanly, progressed across rounds, and crystallized the clash better. B relied on somewhat overstated confidence about earmarked fee flows and repeated the "blunt tool" framing without fresh weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 identification that CON was "attacking an accountant, not the motion" — plus the dilemma that CON cannot both claim fees fix everything and that the cap would wrongly bite — set up the closing trap that CON's preferred targeted tools either prevent the trigger or become caps by another name.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): B (CON) built the cleaner path to victory. A's strongest case treated the cap as a backstop only after targeted tools fail, but B forced the damaging admission that the motion would allow capping before cheaper tools were tried. This undercut A's claim that capped cities had no remaining tourist upside. B repeatedly compared the cap unfavorably to levies and targeted management: same volume reduction, but with revenue and services preserved. A's responses relying on lobbying and delay were plausible but never neutralized B's argument that caps were stickier, permit-scarce, and regulation by convenience.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's turn on A's Q1 answer was decisive: after A admitted a city could cap without installing shore power or staggering arrivals, B reframed the net harm as missing management rather than worthless visitors, defeating A's no-remaining-upside claim.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B (CON) won the decisive clash. CON's core comparison—caps vs. targeted pricing tools—was sustained across every round and sharpened over time. The pivotal exchange came when CON's pressure question forced PRO to admit a city that never tried shore power or levies could cap anyway; CON then used that "Yes" to collapse PRO's "no remaining tourist upside to forfeit" claim, arguing the trigger measures mismanagement, not visitor worth. CON also dominated the instrument comparison: a high levy cuts volume like a cap but leaves money for clinics and housing, while a cap creates scarce, grandfathered permits that are politically sticky. PRO's best surviving points—the unmeasurability rebuttal, physical street capacity, and levy capture—were each answered: cities already make comparable marginal decisions, a cap doesn't widen a medieval lane either, and capture is worst where reversal is hardest (caps, not annually-adjusted fees). PRO's answers to pressure were direct but substantively thin, and its closing leaned on a hypothetical of perfect levy calibration being captured, which CON had already addressed. Both sides argued the motion as written with clean, citation-free reasoning; B simply made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5–B4.7: CON seized on PRO's "Yes" answer to show that a net-losing city with no shore power or staggered berths is a city with missing management, not worthless visitors—turning PRO's own admission into a refutation of PRO's forfeiture argument, then sealing it with "The cap throws that gain away and calls the loss prudence."
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by making the decisive clash about instrument choice: even when harms outweigh revenue, targeted rules and a sufficiently high levy can reduce arrivals while leaving money for mitigation, whereas a cap reduces arrivals without compensation and risks sticky permit capture. A also turned B’s direct “Yes” answer into a damaging admission that cities could cap before trying cheaper measures, framing net harm as a management failure rather than proof that the visitors are worthless. B gave a clear normative backstop case and handled format well, but did not sufficiently answer why a cap beats a well-designed levy when both reduce volume.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal used B’s “Yes” answer to show caps could precede shore power, staggering, or levies, then delivered the key comparison: a high levy can cut crowds like a cap but still funds clinics, transit, and housing.
