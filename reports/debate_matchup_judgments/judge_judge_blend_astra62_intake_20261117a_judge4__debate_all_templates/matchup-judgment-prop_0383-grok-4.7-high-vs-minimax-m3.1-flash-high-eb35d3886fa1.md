### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins a competitive debate. Its case stayed consistent from opening to closing. It argued that wastefulness depends on local context, that pricing internalizes hidden costs without deleting functional options, and that a phaseout faces a fork. If the policy pauses wherever substitutes fail, the accepted inconvenience does little; if it does not pause, it retires packaging before the substitute has earned the switch. PRO (B) responded reasonably with targeted categories, narrow exemptions, and a "retire the worst once credible alternatives exist" stopping rule. It also made good points that fees cannot create return routes and that unnecessary cutlery has no defense. However, PRO's growing flexibility let CON portray the policy as either hollow or reckless. PRO never cleanly explained why inconvenience still does environmental work where substitutes cut harm but cost more. PRO's opening was also clipped, an execution cost. PRO's closing landed some blows on the limits of fees, but CON had already framed the fee-versus-deletion comparison more sharply and answered the pressure questions more directly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A2.4/A4.6 against B2.4/B4.1. CON seized on PRO's admission that where reuse fails, phaseout merely "hopes" the replacement is least-bad. CON then turned PRO's pause-and-revise flexibility into a dilemma: withdraw the policy in hard cases or retire functional packaging prematurely. PRO's answer, ranking categories and acting only where reliable substitutes exist, was sensible but did not fully defuse the fork.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): B built the clearer path to victory by repeatedly forcing A to distinguish a genuinely binding phaseout from a context-dependent exemption regime. A offered a plausible targeted policy and answered both pressure questions directly, but never fully explained why scalable fees were insufficient or which items must still disappear when substitutes perform worse. B’s claim that fees can capture the relevant costs was somewhat underargued, keeping the result competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal decisively exposed A’s claim that phaseout prevents a worse outcome where reuse fails: deleting the disposal option does not establish that the replacement has lower net harm. B then converted that gap into the sharper choice between locally assessed, adjustable fees and category deletion based merely on a substitute’s existence.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): A wins narrowly by defending a targeted, phased phaseout that fits the motion: rank the worst categories, allow narrow exemptions, and retire items once credible substitutes exist. B’s fee-and-context alternative was sharp and exposed implementation risk, but it did not fully answer why pricing can internalize persistent litter, cleanup, and ecological harms or why no worst categories can be removed when workable alternatives are available.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure and second rebuttal forced A to confront the pause-versus-deletion fork; A’s reply that phaseouts apply where reliable substitutes exist kept a live targeted route, though the exchange remained the debate’s closest clash.
