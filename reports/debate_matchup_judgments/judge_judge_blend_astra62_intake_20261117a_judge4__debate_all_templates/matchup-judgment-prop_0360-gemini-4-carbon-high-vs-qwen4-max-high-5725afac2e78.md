### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): CON won the decisive clash over whether price or class is a sound proxy for harm. From the opening ("a decibel meter cannot read a badge"), CON argued that both harms are measurable outputs. It then showed that every trigger PRO named (active valves, launch control, track modes, power-to-weight) is a hardware feature that can be regulated on any car. When PRO answered Q1 by admitting the $30,000 hot hatch enters "for its launch control and crackling exhaust," CON turned that into a concession: the operative criterion is hardware, so the word "luxury" adds nothing protective. PRO's strongest remaining route was deterrence: flat fines are trivial to wealthy drivers, and income cannot be audited at the curb. CON neutralized this in Rebuttal 2 and the closing. It noted that a raised floor or escalating penalties fall only on people who actually offend, so ordinary commuters who never offend pay nothing. PRO also leaned on contestable factual assertions, for example that family EVs do not make quick launches and that slow flagships "barely exist." It misdescribed CON as having dropped income-scaled fines, which CON corrected cleanly. PRO never answered the asymmetry question: which pedestrian is protected when only the expensive car of two identical launches gets the stricter treatment? CON made the debate easy to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.1–B4.5 sequence was decisive. It seized PRO's A1 admission that the hot hatch is covered because of its hardware. It then showed that capability justifies rules written on capability, while wealth justifies penalty design, so "everything protective in their proposal survives striking the word 'luxury.'" PRO's closing never repaired this split.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side B won a clean but competitive debate. B's core move — harms are measurable outputs, so write rules on hardware and conduct rather than on class — survived every exchange. B's pressure questions were sharp, and B4.5 ("capability justifies rules on capability; wealth justifies penalty scaling; everything protective survives striking the word 'luxury'") was the debate's cleanest crystallization. A answered pressure honestly (A4.1/A4.2) and kept a live route via deterrence — wealthy buyers price flat fines into spectacle — plus practical triage, but B's counter that penalty design (raised floors, income scaling, escalation) fixes deterrence without a class surcharge largely neutralized it, and B5.2 deftly dismantled A's "crushed commuters" bind. A's defense of the luxury tier leaned on contestable empirical claims ("slow six-figure flagships barely exist," base EVs never launch hard), and the identical-launch asymmetry B kept forcing was never fully resolved. Both sides followed format well; B simply made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B3.1/B4.1 versus A4.1: B forced the hot-hatch question to expose whether the tier tracked hardware or price, and while A's answer (performance trims enter via hardware, luxury flagships via mass/torque/spend) kept the tier alive, B's follow-up in B4.5 — splitting capability rules from penalty design so that "everything protective survives striking the word 'luxury'" — reduced the tier's unique output to a surcharge on the invoice, a frame A's closing never escaped.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): PRO was the more inventive and quotable side. It made three strong moves: framing the debate around scarce enforcement, turning CON's deterrence point (penalty times probability), and arguing that siting hotspots by spike volume would fall disproportionately on this class. That progression, however, steadily shed the motion's actual comparator. In stages, PRO: - dropped luxury as a category ("capability, not chrome"); - exempted quiet luxury EVs from noise scrutiny; - folded modified ordinary commuters into the target group; - conceded that cameras should be sited strictly by spike volume; - said officer screening is "conduct underway, not bodywork." CON consistently and accurately named this as circularity. If loud ordinary cars count as "performance" and quiet luxury cars do not, then conduct, not vehicle class, concentrates the harm. On that reading, the motion's call for tighter enforcement of luxury and performance cars than of ordinary vehicles goes unsupported. PRO's best surviving route was the sub-limit launch: an officer needs a "prior," and capability supplies it. CON blunted this route in two ways: - **Visibility:** PRO's stated priors (a cabin sport button, the hidden power of a silent EV) are invisible from the curb. - **Uniform alternative:** an officer watching the stop line catches any car that lunges, whatever its badge. PRO's closing claim that hotspot enforcement *is* tighter enforcement of the class relies on disparate impact rather than class-based tightening. It also rests on an unsupported assertion that complaints cluster around valved exhausts, whereas CON asserted that aftermarket mods and motorcycles dominate. Neither side's empirics were verifiable. Overall, CON left the cleaner decision rule, with PRO stronger on rhetoric but weaker in fidelity to the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came in CON's B4.1 and B4.3. CON exposed a tension in A4.2: PRO promised "full acceleration attention" on silent EVs while claiming officers screen by revving and blipping, which silent cars cannot do. CON then lined up PRO's concessions (strict spike-based siting, conduct-not-bodywork screening, recasting ordinary vehicles as "used ordinarily") to show that PRO's defense had collapsed into conduct-based enforcement rather than class-based enforcement.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side A opened well with the capability-not-chrome frame and the "externality sold as an option" line, and landed sharp moves like the expected-cost deterrence rebuttal ([A2.4]) and the cabin-insulation point ([A2.5]). But Side B steadily dismantled the class-based core of the motion: it caught A's silent-EV contradiction ([B4.1] — promising "full acceleration attention" on cars that cannot rev or blip), exposed the circularity of A redefining loud ordinary commuters into the target class while exempting muffled luxury sedans, and cleanly answered A's siting argument by distinguishing spatial hotspot enforcement from differential treatment by vehicle category — on any monitored block, treatment is identical for every violator. A's best remaining route, the sub-limit launch needing an officer's "prior," was met with a workable uniform alternative (stop-line officers watching the line itself) and undermined by B's observation that A's stated priors (a cabin sport button, hidden EV power) are invisible from the curb. A's closing claim that B was "arguing for the motion while insisting it not be named" was rhetorically memorable, but B5.1 adequately defused it. B made the debate easier to decide and held the motion's actual comparison; A progressively conceded the class line while relabeling the drift as victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1's contradiction catch: Side A promised "full acceleration attention" on silent EVs with five hundred horsepower, then in the same answer said officers pre-screen by "revving" and "blipping" — silent cars can do neither. This forced A's pre-screening into either profiling bodywork or watching the stop line uniformly, collapsing the capability-prior route across the motion's acceleration half.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): A won the central comparison: targeting harmful locations and conduct does not itself justify tighter scrutiny of particular vehicle classes. B accepted spike-based camera placement and conduct-based screening, but never adequately established what additional class-based enforcement contributes. Redefining the comparator as ordinary vehicles used ordinarily further weakened B's concentration claim. B correctly exposed the limitation of speed cameras, but A answered with category-neutral intersection officers. Both sides overstated some prevalence claims, yet A more clearly resolved the policy choice.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest rebuttal connected B's pressure answers: siting cameras strictly by spikes and screening conduct rather than bodywork largely adopted A's alternative. A then exposed the circularity of counting offending ordinary vehicles as performance-car conduct. B's sub-limit acceleration objection was sharp, but did not establish why the same officers needed vehicle-class prioritization.
