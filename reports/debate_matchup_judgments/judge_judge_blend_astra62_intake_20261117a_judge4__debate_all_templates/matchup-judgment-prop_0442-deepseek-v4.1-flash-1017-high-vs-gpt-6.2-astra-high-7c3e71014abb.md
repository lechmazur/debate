### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a close, high-quality exchange. A's central turn—under a binding fleetwide cap, a mandated fuel switch merely frees allowances for other ships and adds cost without adding cuts—was effectively conceded by B ("if the cap is too loose, tighten it or cancel freed allowances"), and A capitalized on that concession in Rebuttal 2 and the closing ("the cancellation is what tightens the limit"). A also answered B's strongest pressure (who builds the first terminals?) with a concrete mechanism: tendered purchase guarantees conditional on vessel orders, terminal commitments, and a delivery date, backstopped by a declining allowance supply. B's complementarity framing ("the budget prices the externality, the mandate builds the replacement") remained a live route, and B handled the island-grain pressure honestly with a time-limited efficiency waiver. But B's core claim that only a mandate forces coordination was blunted by A's tender-plus-cap design, and B never fully answered why an island should pay a fuel premium when an equivalent verified cut is available. Both sides argued the motion as written with clean epistemic discipline; A's rebuttal turns were sharper and did more comparative damage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 response to B's A1: after B conceded that a mandated switch under a cap merely shifts allowances and suggested cancelling them, A turned the concession—"it is the cancellation that tightens the limit"—and demanded B pay directly for first orders rather than a fleetwide fuel premium. This was the decisive exchange of the debate.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins a high-level debate by neutralizing the jurisdictional race-to-the-bottom problem with a binding, declining IMO lifecycle emissions cap and systematically showing why adding a fleetwide fuel-share timetable hurts both climate efficiency and vulnerable importers. Side B (PRO) argued persuasively around 25-year vessel lifespans and supply-chain coordination, but left critical holes that Side A exploited: an aggregate global fuel quota does not tell a port lender whether ships at that specific terminal will buy methanol or ammonia (whereas auction-backed route contracts do); diverting scarce wind turbines and engineers into lossy marine fuels carries a real opportunity cost where coal still runs ashore; and fuel mandates hand scarcity rents to private suppliers rather than generating public auction revenue to cushion vulnerable island routes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's interlocking critique of Side B's cap-plus-mandate model and island-route waiver (A2.1, A4.3-A4.5): Side A showed that under a binding fleetwide cap, forcing a fuel switch merely frees allowances for other ships rather than cutting extra carbon, generates private supplier premiums instead of the public "revenue recycling" Side B claimed, and creates the perverse outcome where an island grain ship's hull efficiency is sufficient when fuel is unavailable yet forbidden as soon as prohibitively expensive fuel arrives—raising freight bills for zero additional emissions reduction.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): CON offered a binding IMO emissions cap that preserves PRO's anti-leakage and certainty benefits without dictating fuels. PRO never showed what extra climate cut a fuel mandate adds atop that cap, conceded efficiency waivers, and left the transition fund without a revenue source, while CON answered coordination pressure with route-specific purchase guarantees tied to vessel and terminal commitments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was B's waterbed rebuttal: under the cap PRO endorses, a mandated fuel switch merely frees allowances for another ship, so cancelling allowances—not the fuel quota—creates the extra emissions cut; B then extended PRO's island-fuel waiver to show identical efficiency cuts should count whenever they are cheaper.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Both sides executed a tight, well-grounded debate with no fake sourcing. Side B (PRO) won on the strength of its core causal story: the chicken-and-egg coordination problem and the demand-ratchet mechanism, in which a rising fleetwide fuel requirement forces carriers to sign multiyear offtake contracts before plants are built. Side A's strongest route — that PRO's design converges into a carbon price plus an offset market — was pressed well and produced sharp concessions, but B answered it squarely: only fuel actually burned counts, so a pool falls short unless someone physically substitutes, and the IMO's mandate is to change what international shipping burns, not to force the island feeder to switch first. Side A's alternative (a published charge trajectory plus advance-market commitments) largely conceded the need for a rising binding charge and added mechanisms without clearly showing why they beat a scheduled fuel-share mandate. Side B's pressure questions exposed real burdens and were answered honestly by both sides; B's A1/A2 responses were direct and structurally compliant. A's closings were punchy ("offset market with a deadline") but B's ratchet argument survived them. Slight edge to B on the decisive clash over investment certainty.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 extraction that PRO's compensation plan depends on "a charge on remaining conventional fuel," conceding the revenue mechanism does the protective work — sharply answered in B's Rebuttal 2 by separating the charge's two jobs (funding relief vs. forcing substitution) and showing the rising share creates demand the charge alone cannot.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a high-level debate by building a tighter causal story on clean-fuel investment and cleanly resolving every dilemma Side A (CON) posed. Side B showed that because ships last decades and clean-fuel plants require committed buyers to finance construction, a carbon price alone either lets carriers pay and postpone switching or—if set high enough to force switching under scarcity—imposes the same freight spikes without guaranteeing volume offtake. Side A mounted strong pressure on tramp trades, island feeders, and credit scarcity, forcing Side B beyond its initial return-leg bunkering answer. However, Side B's annual fleet-pooling design—keeping conventional fuel in the denominator while counting verified clean fuel burned once—solved port bottlenecks without weakening aggregate substitution, and Side B rightly noted that sparing an island grain feeder from switching first is a virtue, not an offset loophole. Side B also decisively separated the modest levy needed to reimburse vulnerable staple importers from the much higher carbon price needed to compel fleetwide switching.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B4.1]-[B4.4] (and sealed in [B5.1]), Side B dismantled Side A's twin pressure questions by showing first that annual pooled compliance avoids both port waivers and stranded island feeders without letting any physical fuel obligation disappear, and second that a small fossil-fuel levy to reimburse vulnerable importers for essential freight need not be high enough to outprice conventional fuel fleetwide—while exposing in [B3.1] and [B4.4] that Side A's charge "calibrated as supply develops" recreates the waiting game before plants are built.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): A wins by preserving a distinctive quantity guarantee: a rising, annually measured fuel share creates bankable demand that a carbon price, which carriers can simply pay, does not. Its no-waiver pooling answer also turns B's missing-fuel dilemma into compliance flexibility without erasing the obligation, while separating the compensation charge from the substitution mandate. B's offset critique concedes verified clean fuel is real abatement and does not defeat an aggregate shipping requirement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 answer to B's port question—no waiver, but an annual pooled share counted once and penalized if short—was decisive because it answered the scarcity trap while keeping the rising obligation that drives contracts for the next fuel plant.
