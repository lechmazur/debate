### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): This was a close, high-quality exchange. PRO built a careful case around the arcade example and a narrow explicit duty. Its strongest point was that making the duty explicit spares pedestrians from arguing from scratch that operators owed them care at all. It handled CON's pressure questions honestly: a reasonable response discharges the duty, and relocation claims fail when the rider would have entered anyway. Those honest answers, however, narrowed PRO's proposal until it closely resembled ordinary negligence. CON exploited this consistently with its dilemma: the duty is redundant when fair and unfair when it adds anything. In B4.2–B4.6, CON tracked PRO's concessions point by point and turned them into evidence of redundancy. CON's weak spot was that it never firmly established that existing law clearly imposes this duty on operators for rider-created hazards; "already examinable" was asserted more than shown. Its hindsight worry also lost force once PRO conceded the reasonable-response standard. Even so, CON made the debate easier to decide. It kept one clear frame through every speech, while PRO's case for meaningfully "more" responsibility rested mainly on the value of settling the duty question. That rationale was real but thin by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange ran from CON's B4.2–B4.6 into the closings. CON used PRO's A1 and A2 concessions (a reasonable response discharges the duty; causation must be proven case by case) to argue that the proposal "adds nothing beyond negligence law" on fair terms. PRO's counter in A4.4–A4.5 and A5.1 was that an explicit duty settles the threshold question and guides operators ahead of time. That counter was its best answer, but it did not fully escape the redundancy horn of CON's dilemma.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly without fake sourcing, but Side B's "redundant when fair, unfair when additional" dilemma was pressed consistently from the opening through the closing and never fully broken. B correctly identified that A's Rebuttal 2 concessions (reasonable response discharges the duty; plaintiff must identify an unreasonably omitted precaution; causation keyed to actual prevention) collapse the explicit duty back into ordinary negligence. A's best live route — that an explicit duty gives ex ante clarity and lowers the pedestrian's burden of establishing that fleet operators owe care at all — was raised but underdeveloped, and B's answer (the same fact-specific judgment remains, plus a trigger inviting hindsight) blunted it enough. A's two-riders causal test and honest A1/A2 answers were strong, and B's answers to pressure questions were equally direct, so the win comes from B making the decisive clash easier to decide and forcing the more damaging concessions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2: by quoting A's own concessions that a reasonable response discharges the duty and that breach requires an unreasonably omitted precaution plus probable prevention, B showed A's proposal had been narrowed to ordinary negligence — the turning point of the debate.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing that Side A’s “more responsibility” either collapses into ordinary negligence or becomes unfair hindsight liability. B’s redundant-when-fair, unfair-when-additional frame remained the cleaner voting rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal and closing were decisive: once A conceded that a reasonable response discharges the duty and that relocation fails against a determined rider, the proposed expansion added no principle beyond negligence while still inviting courts to start from the injury.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): CON wins mainly through the redundancy clash. PRO built a careful, negligence-based case: responsibility should follow control, with no strict liability. That moderation let CON argue that ordinary negligence already does everything PRO wants. CON's two-collision opening framed causation sharply. Its first rebuttal kept asking which omission cannot support liability now. In the pressure round, CON asked what legal obstacle PRO's rule removes, and PRO answered "None." PRO's remaining distinctive claim was that operators could "fragment notice" account by account. CON neutralized it by conceding that pattern evidence at a pickup point can support liability by inference, with no need for rider-specific notice. PRO, in turn, conceded that a rider arriving from elsewhere creates no claim against the company. After those exchanges, PRO had shown how to prove corporate negligence, not why responsibility should expand. PRO never substantiated its claim that responsibility "in practice stops with the rider." Its insurance-versus-prevention point was answered: CON said damages for negligent deployment remain the incentive to prevent harm, and rider coverage only fills the compensation gap. PRO was articulate and disciplined, but CON made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's Q1 and PRO's answer to it. CON asked what legal obstacle PRO's rule removes when a pedestrian proves the operator negligently rented to a known repeat offender. PRO answered "None." CON then used that answer in B4.1 and its closing to show the motion adds no new liability beyond existing negligence. CON's concession in B4.3 that pattern evidence suffices without account-specific notice reinforced the point by removing PRO's last distinctive claim.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by executing a disciplined redundancy strategy. The two-collision opening framed the decisive test: what does the motion add beyond ordinary negligence? A never supplied a case where the proposed rule changes the verdict. A conceded "None" on the repeat-offender question, conceded the far-end rider has no claim, and B then conceded that pattern/location evidence is already admissible without account-specific notice—closing every remaining gap A tried to claim. A's residual route ("responsibility in practice stops with the rider") remained an assertion about practice that B challenged and A never substantiated with a concrete barred claim. B also handled the insurance clash cleanly: coverage pays where the operator acted reasonably, while damages for negligent deployment already supply the prevention incentive. A was honest, well-structured, and gave direct A1/A2 answers, but honesty cost it the burden of showing what "more responsibility" changes. B's closing question—"Which pedestrian gets a different verdict?"—crystallized why B made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 [B4.1]: catching A's concession that no legal obstacle exists in the proved repeat-offender case, then converting it into the decisive frame that A's rule would let "a foreseeable pattern stand in for proof" of causal contribution—paired with B's own concession on pattern-based location evidence, which neutralized A's remaining escape route.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B wins because it forced the live clash onto whether “more” legal responsibility changes any verdict. Side A’s own limits—causation, feasibility, and no liability for the unrelated far-end rider—matched ordinary negligence, and A never identified a legal obstacle its rule removes. Insurance was cleanly confined to the compensation gap where the operator already acted reasonably. A stayed coherent and morally clear, but could not show the motion requires an expansion rather than proper application of existing duties.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal and closing pinned A’s “None” concession: pattern evidence already supports negligence when it explains this collision, while a reason to move a pickup is not itself a reason to pay for an unconnected crash.
