### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): PRO wins. PRO built the cleaner path to victory: statutory law can override post-mortem non-transferability clauses, and asset reassignment is distinct from account takeover. That distinction neutralized CON's privacy and fraud arguments early. PRO also answered both pressure questions directly. A1 explained that what passes is a reassigned entitlement that statute authorizes. A2 explained that "next of kin" sets who holds the right, while ordinary probate mechanics and safe harbors govern how they prove it, including disputed claims. CON's best ideas were the category-error framing, metadata privacy, and server dependency, and they were genuinely interesting. However, CON mostly reasserted them rather than adapting. Its central closing claim, that no mechanism can transfer access without either the data dossier or an unauthorized copy, is asserted rather than supported. It is undercut by the gift and entitlement architecture PRO pointed to. It also repeatedly appeals to what "the license forbids," which is circular when the motion itself proposes a legal override of those terms. PRO's shares analogy made the insolvency objection a bounded limitation rather than a reason to permit confiscation by operating storefronts, and CON's attempt to distinguish shares did not engage that point. CON's prediction that platforms would retreat to subscriptions was answered with the analogy to physical goods and never developed further.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2.2 reframe was the most decisive exchange: "account takeover vs. asset transfer," migrating entitlements to the heir's own account. It defused CON's privacy and fraud lines at once. CON's later reply that gift codes are "born clean" never showed why a reassigned entitlement must carry telemetry, so the reframe stood through the closings.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A built the cleaner path to victory by reframing the debate from account takeover to paid-entitlement transfer. B raised serious license, privacy, fraud, and insolvency objections, but A answered them directly: platforms already issue clean entitlements without behavioral telemetry, executors already manage contested estates through probate safe harbors, and inheritance need not guarantee solvency—only prevent active platforms from confiscating paid assets. B's strongest later claim, that no third mechanism exists between a privacy-invasive account transfer and an unauthorized clean copy, was too brittle and conclusive. A's statutory reassignment of a single permission more plausibly resolved that pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 effectively dismantled B's clean-copy-vs-new-license dilemma by explaining that statutory succession can reassign the paid permission to an heir's account without transferring telemetry, passwords, or creating an extra active license.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): PRO built the cleaner live route: marketed-as-ownership purchases should not be confiscated via boilerplate. PRO neutralized privacy, fraud, and liability by distinguishing account takeover from reassignment of a single entitlement to an heir's own account under probate verification and safe harbors, and showed future insolvency does not justify confiscation by a still-operating storefront. CON repeated its clean-copy versus surveillance-dossier dilemma and subscription-extinction warning without proving technical impossibility or answering that while-operating point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 answers to B's pressure questions: statutory succession authorizes reassignment, deactivates the deceased account while keeping active licenses at one, and requires a single court-validated claim, directly dissolving CON's reproduction, privacy, and rival-heir objections.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): CON wins because it narrowed the motion to what it actually covers: non-transferable purchased licenses. It then showed that PRO's most emotive impact, irreplaceable family history, comes mostly from user-owned data such as photos and annotations. Those files can be preserved without transferring commercial licenses. PRO never squarely defended why commercial ebooks and games, which remain publicly available, count as irreplaceable. On implementation, PRO said the account passes "to the estate as a single unit." CON answered that estates close and multiple heirs still need division. CON also argued that probate sealing does not help when the executor is the relative who inventories the account. PRO's closing waved this away as a later distribution question without explaining how it works. PRO's "family or the server" framing was memorable but largely repeated. It also assumed the very property status that CON contested. CON's economic backlash argument was somewhat speculative. However, CON turned PRO's own concession that platforms are already drifting toward subscriptions into evidence that buy buttons are fragile, and PRO never answered that turn. Overall, CON adapted across rounds and narrowed the clash, while PRO mostly restated its framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1/B4.1 "decoy" rebuttal was decisive. CON separated user-owned photos and annotations from licensed commercial media. That left PRO's irreplaceable-history impact without a link to the assets the motion actually covers. PRO's Q1 on annotations tried to rescue it, but CON's A1 answered cleanly: annotations are exportable user data and need no license transfer.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B wins because it successfully kept the debate on the actual motion: non-transferable commercial licenses, not user-owned personal files. A’s emotional core relied on family photos and irreplaceable history, but B neutralized that by showing photos, annotations, and reading lists are exportable user data that do not require license transfer. B also exposed A’s “estate as a single unit” solution as unworkable: a family executor would see private purchases before any seal, and multiple heirs would force cloning, shared logins, or unbundling. A’s economic response was largely speculative, while B offered a concrete pricing and servicing mechanism. A’s probate analogy never fully answered that personal licenses are not equivalent to owned physical heirlooms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 was most decisive: it showed personal annotations are exportable user data, so no irreplaceable interest requires transferring commercial licenses, and it turned A’s “single unit” answer against it by showing the account still cannot be privately inventoried or cleanly divided among multiple heirs without unbundling or shared logins.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B wins by separating user-owned photos and annotations from commercial licenses, showing the remaining benefit is only avoiding repurchase. B also exposes that PRO never proves a non-transferable license is owned property, and that PRO's estate-as-single-unit and default opt-out answers worsen multi-heir division and privacy rather than solving them. PRO's family-versus-server framing remains rhetorically strong but repeatedly begs that central question, while its economic response is only that subscription shifts are already occurring.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: annotations are exportable user data requiring no license transfer, a family executor sees sensitive titles before any sealing, and a temporary estate still cannot divide one account among multiple heirs without cloning, sharing, or unbundling.
