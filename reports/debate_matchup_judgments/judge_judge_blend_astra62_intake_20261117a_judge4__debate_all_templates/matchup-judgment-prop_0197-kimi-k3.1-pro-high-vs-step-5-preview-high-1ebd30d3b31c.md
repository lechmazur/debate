### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO built the cleaner path to victory: a cumulative, irreversible externality that a test ban reduces at modest cost, since the ban leaves possession and use intact. CON's main routes were incompleteness (wartime use, legacy debris), verification difficulty, and the claim that public tests provide stabilizing calibration. PRO neutralized incompleteness by holding CON to a "reduce versus eliminate" standard and leveraged CON's own concession in [B2.7] that the environmental gain is real. On verification, PRO answered B's pressure question directly with a conduct-and-target line and noted that the disputed cases create no debris. PRO also turned CON's worst-case-planning claim against the transparency benefit. CON's intent-signaling reply in B4.3 was its best adaptation, but it stayed speculative and never explained why calibration requires destructive intercepts. CON's closing also misdescribed PRO as conceding the gain was "narrow." PRO's opening was clipped, which is a real execution cost, but the core case was already on the table and that cost did not outweigh its rebuttal advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A4.3–A4.4 against CON's "doesn't prevent cascades, wartime use remains" argument. PRO reframed the standard as reducing cumulative risk and used CON's concession that the debris gain is real. It then pointed out that CON's own worst-case-planning claim undercuts the reassurance value of public tests. This left CON's security cost looking speculative next to persistent fragments.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): B built the stronger comparative case: preventing additional fragmentation remains valuable even when weapons, wartime use, and other debris sources persist. A conceded that environmental gain but repeatedly demanded complete cascade prevention rather than showing why the treaty’s costs outweighed its benefits. A’s calibration argument supplied a genuine counterweight, yet its connection to reduced preemption remained underdeveloped. B answered the major pressures more directly and explained why mitigation and removal complement rather than replace prevention.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B directly undermined A’s verification objection by distinguishing observable prohibited interceptions from compliant, non-fragmenting capability development. A’s response merely reiterated that the arsenal could still mature. B also answered A’s late capability-versus-intent distinction: a public test cannot establish peaceful intent or future restraint, weakening the claimed security benefit of preserving tests.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO won by holding the motion to risk reduction rather than total cascade abolition. Side B showed that kinetic tests create conceded, irreversible shared debris, that simulations and component work do not, and that verification need not be perfect to deter avoidable breakups. Side A pressed real gaps—dual-use ambiguity, wartime use, and lost calibration—but never showed that destructive tests are required for reassurance or that those speculative stability costs outweigh persistent fragments. PRO’s weighing stayed cleaner: incomplete prevention is still a concrete gain; preserving tests for uncertain military signaling is not.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it drew a conduct-and-target line for banned intercepts, then used Side A’s own concession and worst-case-planning premise to show that demanding elimination of every cascade pathway, including wartime use, wrongly preserves avoidable peacetime debris.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): CON won by attacking the motion's breadth. It conceded that Fengyun-style tests are dangerous, then argued that a binding debris and altitude rule captures the persistent harm without banning low-risk, rapidly decaying tests. PRO never gave a clean answer to what extra generation-long protection the total ban adds over that rule. Its fallback, that low-altitude fragments still add "probabilistic" risk, undercut its own "certain, transgenerational" weighing, and CON exposed that tension. PRO also lost credibility in Rebuttal 1. It claimed the voluntary commitments predated the 2007 and 2021 tests, which misdescribes CON's point. CON corrected this cleanly: the commitments came after those tests. On verification, PRO's tu quoque was the right attack and partly landed. However, its answer to Q1 defined a prohibited test by "staged," "peacetime," and "outside active hostilities." That reintroduced exactly the contextual adjudication CON had warned about. On deterrence, CON's A2 gave a concrete account of what integrated orbital validation involves, answering PRO's sharpest question. PRO replied mainly by restating that possession suffices. PRO's closing was short and largely recapped its opening. CON's closing crystallized the decisive question.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 did two things. First, it turned PRO's own verification answer: "instrumentation, target preparation, repetition, and active hostilities are contextual judgments, not a bright line." Second, it showed PRO's "probabilistic" low-altitude risk claim was inconsistent with its "certain, transgenerational" weighing. Together these neutralized PRO's bright-line and impact-asymmetry routes.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): A won the crucial comparison between banning all tests and imposing binding debris limits. B established the danger of persistent debris and identified residual risks from low-altitude tests, but inadequately weighed those incremental risks against A’s concrete validation benefits. A also exposed B’s overstated verification simplicity and erroneous chronology of voluntary commitments. Although A overstated how completely rapid decay eliminates long-term risk, its narrower alternative and direct answers provided the stronger overall case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A answered B’s component-testing challenge by explaining why integrated orbital interception tests tracking, geometry, timing, and operator performance together. B’s closing returned to component validation and mere weapon possession without resolving that distinction, leaving A’s principal cost of a universal ban standing.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing the universal ban is overbroad for the motion’s stated aim of stopping long-term cascades. Low-altitude, rapidly decaying tests lack that generation-long harm, while a binding debris rule can target persistent clouds without sacrificing integrated orbital validation. PRO never closed that gap, and its “outside hostilities” line reintroduced the contextual judgments it claimed to escape.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal and closing forced the live comparison: what generation-long harm a compliant low-altitude test uniquely creates that a debris limit would not prevent, while exposing PRO’s shift from “certain” transgenerational harm to merely probabilistic transient risk.
