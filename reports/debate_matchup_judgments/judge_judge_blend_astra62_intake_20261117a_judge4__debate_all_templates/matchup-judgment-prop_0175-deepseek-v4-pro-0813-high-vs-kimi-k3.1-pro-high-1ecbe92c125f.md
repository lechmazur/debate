### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): A built a tight, concrete narrative (irrelevant training hours, incumbent-run boards, redundancy with codes/inspectors) and then relentlessly exploited B's early concession that "a short, targeted course may well be better policy" for braiding — a concession A kept converting into "if safety were primary, the cheaper option would exist; its absence is capture." B's best defense was the logically sound distinction between "a rule that excludes" and "a rule that primarily exists to exclude," plus the sharp "proves too much" riposte to A's redundancy argument and concrete fire-safety finish example. That defense neutralized some of A's overreach but never fully recovered the ground lost from the opening concession, and B's opening itself was clipped for exceeding the word limit — a real execution cost that weakened the foundation of B's case. A's closing crystallized the clash more memorably (persistent benefit flowing to the same insiders who write the rules), while B's closing, though conceptually correct, read more as a restatement of the calibration-vs-purpose distinction than a decisive rebuttal of A's "direction of error" point. Both sides avoided fake citations and stayed within plausible general knowledge, but A's case was simply easier to follow and more damaging in the exchanges that mattered most.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 seizing on B's opening concession ("a short, targeted course may well be better policy") and converting it into a capture argument was the pivotal exchange; B's subsequent "speed limit" analogy and "proves too much" response in Rebuttal 2 was the strongest counter but arrived too late to fully undo the rhetorical damage.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by comparing the burdens of existing licensing regimes with their marginal safety value. The hair-braiding example was especially strong: extensive irrelevant training, incumbent influence, and readily available targeted safeguards made exclusion the better explanation of the rule’s practical operation. Side A persuasively established some genuine risks and defended layered safeguards, particularly for interior design, but mostly showed that proportionate screening could be valuable rather than that the challenged requirements are primarily safety measures. Side A’s clipped opening and Side B’s erroneous C-reference were minor execution costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange turned Side A’s concession that braiding rules should use targeted training into comparative offense: if most mandatory coursework is unrelated to the regulated risk, the existing license operates chiefly as an exclusionary gate. Side A’s inertia explanation was plausible but did not adequately weigh the large entry burden against the small added safety benefit.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins a very close debate by effectively weaponizing Side A's concession about disproportionate requirements. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but A built a clearer, more consistent analytic test (proportionality: does the requirement's content and scope match the specific hazard?) and used it to answer the pressure questions directly and concretely. Crucially, A exploited B's recurring move of defending a hypothetical "short, calibrated" license rather than the actual broad regimes named in the motion (lengthy cosmetology hours, unrelated exams, grandfathering). B's defense—"that's just a defect, not the primary function"—is a reasonable mixed-motive point, but it never squarely explains why the disproportionate, unrelated burdens that dominate real-world licensing in these fields should count as secondary rather than primary, especially once B concedes a minimal safety-focused credential would satisfy the safety rationale. A's closing crystallized this concession-exploitation cleanly, while B's closing largely reasserted "calibration, not cartelization" without fresh rebuttal traction. B scored some unanswered ground on the timing argument for interior design (permits/inspectors not catching every later design change), which A addressed only thinly, keeping the debate competitive rather than lopsided.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 turning B's "short, focused license" concession back on B (A2.1–A2.3), later crystallized in A's Closing (A5.1), was the most decisive exchange: it forced B to defend actual broad regimes rather than an idealized minimal version, a pressure B answered cleverly (by redefining any pre-entry safety demonstration as "licensing") but never fully resolved in its own favor.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B won by distinguishing narrow, risk-matched safeguards from broad occupational gatekeeping. Side A established that some limited licensing can protect safety, but repeatedly treated that concession as defeating a proposition about licensing being primarily protectionist. B persuasively showed that justified hygiene or code-literacy requirements do not explain unrelated training, lengthy experience mandates, or grandfathering, and that narrower regulation preserves safety while reducing exclusion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response that “calibration” is itself the test of primary function was decisive: requirements unexplained by safety still restrict supply and burden entrants, while A's defense relied mainly on replacing the challenged regimes with leaner hypothetical licenses.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing 'calibration' as the exact test for the motion's 'primarily' clause. Side A argued that the bloated requirements are merely 'defects' in a system that is essentially about ex-ante safety. However, Side B convincingly countered that when the vast majority of a requirement is unrelated to safety (the 'defect'), that disproportionate design proves its primary function is incumbent protection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing brilliantly turned Side A's 'calibration' argument on its head: 'calibration is precisely how we identify primary function... strip away what safety cannot explain, and the remaining machinery still restricts supply.'
