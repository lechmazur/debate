### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): CON wins narrowly. PRO's core access/resilience case was compelling, but CON's second rebuttal opened a fatal fork: if the kiosk PRO used as a safe, affordable way to meet the mandate merely dispensed cards, PRO lost cash resilience in outages; if stores had to accept bills directly, armed-robbery risks, cash recyclers, and armored-truck fixed costs returned. CON also distinguished its public, government-funded kiosks from retailer-funded in-store cash machines and pressed the unqualified scope over online and mail-order retailers. PRO's closing effectively framed the choice as guarantee vs hope, but it did not resolve that implementation/cost turn, so CON has the cleaner comparative route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was the kiosk fork: PRO tried to co-opt CON's cash-to-card kiosk as a safe, affordable way to satisfy the mandate, but CON distinguished public kiosks from retailer-funded cash recyclers or card-only kiosks, stripping PRO of both its robbery-cost answer and its outage-resilience benefit.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently and answered pressure with proper A1/A2 structure. B landed the strongest technical moves: the scope attack (mail-order/online retailers cannot take bills), and the fork in Rebuttal 2 (if a card-dispensing kiosk satisfies the mandate, PRO's outage case dies; if stores must handle real cash, the cost/robbery harms return), which exploited A's own kiosk concession. However, A's pressure questions were the sharpest tactical exchange of the debate, forcing B to relocate its kiosks to public hubs, which A then effectively skewered in closing ("hope" vs. a guarantee, and the kiosk card still failing with the network). A's core weighing — a certain, present harm (cash-holders turned away) versus speculative closures — survived the exchange, and B's own concession that mainstream stores already accept cash blunted its cost case. B was repeatedly sloppy with nonexistent reference IDs (C1.6, C2.5, C4.1, C4.4, P2.x), a real grounding cost, while A's only execution cost was a clipped Rebuttal 1. Net: a razor-thin but real edge to A on weighing, rhetoric, and epistemic discipline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Q1 pressure — "if a bill-taking machine is safe and affordable enough to be your solution, why can't it satisfy the mandate?" — forced B to abandon in-store kiosks for public-hub kiosks, and A's closing then showed that solution still fails during outages and requires hoping a kiosk is nearby, turning B's own remedy into support for the mandate.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a close debate by holding the proposition to its own text. The mandate is redundant where major grocers already take cash and costly or unworkable exactly where they refuse it: high-risk shifts, automated stores, and mail-order sellers. Side B's strongest move, treating an affordable bill machine as proof that robbery and cost harms vanish, collapsed once Side A separated public card-loading kiosks from in-store cash acceptance. Side B's later insistence that only bills survive network outages reattached those harms, and "online was never the target" narrowed the motion rather than answering it. Side B kept the better moral frame and a live point about someone already holding cash, but not a cleaner decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's fork: public cash-to-card kiosks are not in-store bill acceptance, so they cannot both erase robbery and overhead costs and preserve outage resilience. Side B's closing choice of actual cash put the safety and fixed-cost harms back on the marginal stores.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B built the cleaner path to victory. CON's opening case was sharp, but its core cost objection collapsed once CON granted that a single cash lane spread across all baskets is modest. PRO successfully reframed the "idle now, costly later" dilemma as fire-code-style prevention, while chains are already piloting cashless formats and self-checkout-only hours. CON's alternatives also weakened because PRO showed prepaid cards themselves depend on cash outlets, and banking reforms still fail in outages, missing ID, or account freezes. CON's strongest point—that cash acceptance only helps people who already hold cash—was real but narrow: that is exactly the excluded population the mandate targets. PRO's rural pharmacy and cashless self-checkout examples remained live, and its "governments can add reforms without removing the cash floor" point undercut CON's better-path framing. Two clipped Side A turns were a minor execution cost, not decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal showing that CON's prepaid-card workaround itself relies on major pharmacies and supermarkets accepting cash, plus its staffed-lane answer to the kiosk accessibility challenge, most damaged CON's claim that targeted alternatives make a cash mandate unnecessary.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued at a high level with no fake sourcing, and the debate stayed on a clean clash. B's best weapons were the dilemma (mandate either idle or expensive) and the kiosk contradiction, and B's closing store-by-store story defused A's sharpest contradiction charge. But A held the more durable route to victory: B conceded one lane spread across baskets is "modest," collapsing the regressive-price scare; A's fire-code/tipping-point story explains why market forces fail the cash minority at the moment of harm; and A repeatedly showed B's alternatives (prepaid cards bought with cash, offline backstops only in "declared emergencies") quietly depend on the very cash acceptance B downplays. B's both rebuttals were also clipped for word-limit violations, a real execution cost on the two most important speeches. A wins a close, competitive debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The contradiction exchange: A's [A5.2] charge that B assumes cashless chains to count costs and cash-accepting chains to deny harm was the debate's sharpest blow, and B's closing [B5.1-B5.2] store-by-store answer was the best defusal — but it relied on market faith that high-demand stores keep cash voluntarily, which A's [A4.4] "final lane cut for margin gains" point had already kept live.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO won because the live comparison ended as modest, compatible cost versus a distinct cash-in-hand constituency that banking reforms do not reach tonight. CON landed a sharp dilemma and correctly limited the till in bank outages and freezes, but the price scare shrank once one lane was conceded as modest, and “reforms can coexist” was never shown to make the floor unnecessary or net harmful. CON’s selective-exit close was clever but did not kill the market-failure case: a small cash minority can be dropped even without a real alternative, while profit will not reliably protect them. Clipped CON turns also left comparative closes unfinished.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Q1 forced CON to grant that one cash lane is only a modest basket-level cost, and the prepaid-card and coexistence points then left CON defending futility rather than a real regressive harm.
