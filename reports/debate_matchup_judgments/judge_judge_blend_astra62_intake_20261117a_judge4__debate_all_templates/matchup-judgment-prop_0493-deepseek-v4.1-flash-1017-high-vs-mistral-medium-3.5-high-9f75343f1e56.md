### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): PRO built a tighter, more coherent causal story (irreversibility of aquifer depletion, financialization through trading, erosion of democratic allocation power) and used it consistently across rounds. Crucially, PRO's rebuttal that infrastructure investment does not require ownership or extraction rights—a city can contract for treatment/bottling services without ceding the resource itself—was never squarely answered by CON; CON's replies (B4.1) mostly reasserted that "ownership provides investment security" without explaining why service contracts or regulated concessions couldn't achieve the same financing, which PRO's pressure question (Q1) specifically targeted. CON's case also contained an internal tension PRO's Q2 exposed: CON argued both that banning ownership would be an "uncompensated taking" of groundwater rights and that those rights are "already constrained by public interest statutes"—weakening the takings claim CON relied on. CON's Central Valley and Flint examples were reasonable background knowledge, but they were reused largely unchanged across rounds rather than developed further, and the repeated "trillion-dollar funding gap" stat became a stand-in for argument rather than a growing case. PRO's closing crystallized the comparative stakes well (reversible regulation vs. irreversible depletion), giving the cleaner decision rule for the judge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2.4/A3.1 line—that infrastructure financing and treatment investment can be secured through public service contracts without transferring ownership or extraction rights—was the most decisive exchange. CON never offered a concrete reason why ownership specifically (rather than a well-structured concession or contract) is necessary for capital to flow, leaving a live gap in CON's central investment-based defense of private ownership.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): PRO won the allocation-right clash. CON's core financing case conflated public water infrastructure with private bottling plants and failed B's pressure Q1 about why ownership is needed when a public-owned source with a service contract could do the same work. PRO's irreversibility and entrenchment weighing—that caps require permanent vigilance against a rights-holder, while a depleted aquifer cannot be refilled—answered Flint, offshoring, and property-rights attacks more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Q1 [B3.1] asking what ownership provides that a public source + service contract cannot, and A1 [A4.1] collapsing into sunk-cost bottling investment that does not fix the public trillion-dollar gap.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by decisively narrowing the clash to the specific harms of corporate ownership and trading of aquifers for exportable bottling, contrasting it with place-bound uses like agriculture. Side B effectively neutralized Side A's "regulation instead" argument by highlighting the structural conflict of forcing the public to perpetually enforce caps against a profit-maximizing rights-holder, and exposed the gap in Side A's claim that corporations must own the water source rather than just use service contracts. Side A's reliance on general infrastructure funding gaps failed to justify the specific transfer of sovereign water rights.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side A's Central Valley example and "under-inclusive" argument by clearly distinguishing between place-bound agricultural extraction and the creation of tradable, exportable water commodities, effectively neutralizing Side A's primary comparative offense.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): CON built a cleaner, more internally consistent case by distinguishing bad permitting from the instrument of prohibition, and by Rebuttal 2 had exposed a genuine contradiction in PRO's position: PRO's own answer to the migration pressure question admitted the ban reaches emergency standby contracts while claiming it "ends" extraction, yet also conceded (in A2.3) that extraction would simply migrate to private land/weaker jurisdictions. CON correctly pressed that this is instrument mismatch—demand and withdrawal persist but become less visible and less governable, while PRO never repaired this contradiction in the closing, instead retreating to the repeated "irreducible conflict" slogan. PRO's Nestlé/Flint examples were vivid and rhetorically effective, but the same two examples were recycled across every speech without engaging CON's reframe that these are permit-design failures, not proof prohibition is necessary. CON's answers to pressure questions (A1/A2 analog via its own regulatory mechanisms: metering, scarcity royalties, automatic cutoffs, revocable permits) were concrete and directly responsive, whereas PRO's answers to CON's pressure questions inadvertently conceded ground (banning emergency contracts) and offered only an assertion ("targets the source itself") without resolving enforcement against private wells or imports. CON's closing crystallized this clash sharply; PRO's closing restated prior claims without closing the leakage gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 point (B2.5) that PRO's leakage answer "collapses" because PRO simultaneously admits migration to uncovered sources and claims the ban "ends" the practice—later reinforced when PRO's own Rebuttal 2 answers confirmed the ban covers emergency contracts and relied on contested source definitions, a contradiction PRO never resolved.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by turning PRO's moral case into an instrument-choice comparison. PRO asserted an irreducible profit vs public conflict but never showed why permits can't channel fiduciary duty. CON detailed revocable permits, metering, caps, drought triggers, and scarcity royalties, then exposed two fatal costs in pressure: B4.1 conceded the ban reaches emergency standby supply, and B4.2's source-based answer left imports and definitional enforcement unanswered, confirming overbreadth + leakage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 plus Closing: using B's own admissions that emergency contracts are banned [B4.1] and that the ban must chase aquifers across land titles [B4.2] to prove instrument mismatch – regulation governs extraction, ban only moves it beyond oversight.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by exposing the mechanical and practical flaws in Side B's proposed ban. Side A successfully argued that banning corporate involvement does not eliminate demand, leading to leakage into less regulated private wells and imports. Furthermore, Side A trapped Side B on emergency supplies, forcing B to concede the ban would disrupt municipal emergency contracts. Side B relied too heavily on the repetitive slogan that "fiduciary duty makes regulation impossible," which Side A effectively countered with concrete regulatory tools and analogies to other regulated industries. Side A's comparative weighing of "accountable permits vs. leaky prohibition" was decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's answers to the pressure questions, highlighting that B's ban both disrupts emergency supplies and relies on unenforceable definitions of aquifers to prevent leakage.
