### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases, but A's pressure questions exposed a structural dilemma that B never fully escaped: B's "good-faith, decision-specific account" either collapses into a disclosure duty that existing accuracy/adverse-action regimes already cover, or becomes an unfalsifiable narrative the applicant cannot test, or (per B's own A2 answer) survives intact for a "sound" decision while the remedy is merely a better-written explanation and costs. A pressed this dilemma hard in Rebuttal 2 and Closing ("launders rationalization, penalizes tested accuracy for poor narration, or produces paperwork"), and B's closing reasserted the procedural-key framing without squarely resolving the cherry-picking/conflicting-rankings problem or explaining what substantive recourse follows beyond "order a better explanation." B's strongest counter—that pre-claim discovery is circular because you need the explanation to even plead inaccuracy/discrimination—was answered plausibly by A (regulatory complaints, discovery tied to alleged violations, audits across protected groups) and never fully refuted in B's closing. On balance A's institutional and operational objections held up better under the specific pressure exchanged, giving it a clear but not dominant edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Questions (Q1 on conflicting post-hoc rankings, Q2 on remedy when a decision is accurate but "unexplainable") forced B into answers that conceded the right either can't pick among conflicting accounts or produces only a paperwork remedy for sound decisions—A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing then converted that concession into the decisive "launders rationalization or produces paperwork" dilemma that B's closing did not fully dismantle.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing that explanation is a limited gateway to testing accuracy and discrimination, not a substitute for that testing. Side B compellingly exposed the dangers of unstable post-hoc narratives and vague judicial standards, but its preferred remedies often require access to the very inputs or grounds that affected people initially lack. A also limited remedies, protected sensitive details, and showed why imperfect explanation can still be better than total opacity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest response was the catch-22 against Side B’s discovery alternative: an applicant may be unable to plead a concrete error or discriminatory mechanism without first learning what data and grounds drove the denial. This made B’s proposed recourse look inaccessible at the moment it was most needed.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A effectively deconstructed the practical utility of a judicially created "right to explanation," demonstrating that post-hoc explanations for complex models often act as unfalsifiable rationalizations rather than genuine proof. A's distinction between judging the "story" versus the "decision" itself, and the argument that actual accuracy and non-discrimination are better tested via audits, inputs, and discovery, decisively undercut B's claim that explanation is the necessary prerequisite for proof.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, particularly A4.2 and A4.3, where A dismantled B's "good-faith" standard by showing it allows operators to select the most flattering post-hoc ranking, leaving the applicant unable to falsify it without the underlying data, thus turning the right into a mere "prose test."

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but CON (Side A) built the more decisive and sustained critique. Its core move—exposing that PRO's "limited" right keeps expanding (data access, retention, correction routes, sanctions, fidelity litigation) until it becomes a full regulatory regime rather than a narrow judicial entitlement—was pressed consistently from Rebuttal 1 through closing, and PRO's own pressure-question answer (A2: "limited means bounded disclosure, not toothless") effectively conceded the point. CON's most damaging blow was the technical critique of PRO's proposed "material fidelity" test in Rebuttal 2: removing a variable like zip code can leave correlated proxies intact, so a notice could pass the test while still masking discrimination. PRO's closing never answered this specific flaw, instead re-asserting the cost-allocation framing. PRO's strongest surviving argument—that without explanation, applicants must "sue blind" and cannot use correction or discrimination claims effectively—was real and never fully neutralized, but CON's institutional-competence point (courts applying vs. inventing cross-sector regulatory machinery) and the unanswered proxy-persistence critique gave CON the cleaner, more complete path to the decision by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point that removing an input like zip code may leave correlated proxies intact, so a stated "principal factor" notice could pass PRO's own fidelity test while still concealing discrimination—a specific technical flaw in PRO's enforcement mechanism that PRO's closing never addressed.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that A’s supposedly limited right depended on record retention, testing, correction procedures, sanctions, and judicial standards for causal fidelity. A persuasively established that opacity harms applicants, but did not adequately justify courts designing a cross-sector regime rather than legislatures imposing targeted access, review, audit, and correction duties.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange exposed the weakness in A’s material-fidelity test: sensitivity checks can miss proxies preserved by correlated variables, while applicant comparisons and internal-record review effectively require the broader discovery and systemic scrutiny that A claimed a limited explanation right could avoid.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A successfully decoupled "explanation" from "accountability," demonstrating that post-hoc explanations of complex models are technically fraught (e.g., proxy variables masking discrimination) and that enforcing such a right requires courts to invent a massive regulatory regime. Side B's moral appeal was strong, but they could not adequately answer Side A's technical critiques regarding model fidelity or the institutional overreach required to make the "limited" right functional.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 [A4.5], which dismantled Side B's "material fidelity" test by explaining how sensitivity checks fail to detect proxy discrimination when correlated variables preserve the score.
