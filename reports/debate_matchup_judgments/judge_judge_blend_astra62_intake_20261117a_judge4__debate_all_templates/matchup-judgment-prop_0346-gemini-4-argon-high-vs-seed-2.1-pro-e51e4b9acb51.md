### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won. It tied its case to the motion and drew a clear line between inspectable work products and behavioral proxies. CON opened strongly. Its free-rider and invisible-coordination concerns made the best case for its side. But CON then retreated step by step. It moved from "constant monitoring" to "core-hours availability" and then said outright that presence signals do not outweigh deliverables. PRO turned that retreat into a concession on the motion. PRO's pressure questions exposed a real tension in CON's case. CON had admitted that activity tracking cannot judge interaction quality, yet still claimed presence signals catch quality failures early. CON's A1 answer narrowed the claim to detecting total absence. That was a reasonable point, but PRO absorbed it: an untouched review is simply a missed output deadline. CON's closing said output-mainly weighting is "backwards." That sits awkwardly with its own A2 concession, and it did not answer PRO's contradiction point. PRO had some execution flaws. It cited nonexistent IDs [P1.5, P4.1], and "logically impossible" overstates the point. These are minor, and PRO's comparative framing was cleaner throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (B4.3–B4.5) did the most damage. It pointed out that a senior engineer who fires off an instant one-word brush-off passes every presence and activity check, so only inspecting the output exposes the failure. It also showed that CON's retreat to core-hours availability is already accommodated under "mainly outputs." Together these cut off CON's main route to victory, and CON never repaired that damage.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B argued more convincingly that output-based evaluation can include collaborative work products and quality, while baseline core-hour availability remains compatible with a mainly output-driven standard. Side A raised a real concern about uncredited daily coordination, but B weakened it by showing presence signals cannot distinguish substantive responsiveness from performative one-word replies, and by leveraging A’s admission that presence signals do not outweigh deliverables. B’s path to victory was clearer after the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s decisive rebuttal exploited A’s concession that activity tracking cannot tell a thoughtful code review from a useless brush-off: an instant one-word reply passes every presence check, so only inspecting the work product catches the failure. A’s later shift toward catching total absence did not fully repair that contradiction.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A won the central definitional and weighing clash. It showed that B's collaborative examples are judged by inspecting work products, not presence proxies, and that B retreated from constant monitoring to core-hours availability that A already allowed as secondary. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): This was close, and CON (Side A) was the sharper rhetorician. Its strongest lines were the "semantic stretch" charge, the "autopsy" framing of reactive management, and the claim that DLP is continuous monitoring. Its case, however, rested on attacking "output-only" evaluation. The motion says "mainly on outputs rather than constant monitoring," and PRO (Side B) consistently held that distinction. In PRO's framing, response to a page or a colleague request within an agreed window is a measurable outcome. Narrow compliance safeguards like DLP are risk controls, not the primary performance-evaluation metric the motion targets. CON's alleged "fatal concessions" therefore partly misdescribe PRO's position. Data-loss-prevention logging is not the same as scoring workers on presence. CON's overwork argument was its weakest link. The claim that activity trackers protect caregivers from burnout is implausible, and PRO's Q2 exposed the ratchet problem. CON's answer, an "objective baseline," was thin. PRO also left a cost on the table. Its closing was repetitive and only asserted, rather than demonstrated, that output evaluation does not wait for catastrophe. That left CON's leading-indicator point partly alive. On balance, PRO's reading of the motion and its gaming critique left the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came after CON's Q1, which used the engineer who pastes proprietary code into a public tool to show that outputs miss misconduct. In B4.1, PRO replied that targeted DLP safeguards are not presence-based evaluation. CON's counter in A4.3 was that DLP is itself continuous monitoring. That counter was clever but conflated security controls with performance evaluation, so PRO's distinction survived as the cleaner reading of the motion.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. CON had sharp attacks, especially that PRO seemed to stretch outputs to include availability and that targeted monitoring still requires continuous activity tracking. But PRO maintained a cleaner route to the motion as written: constant presence and activity signals are unreliable proxies, while genuinely core obligations such as response SLAs, coverage outcomes, and compliance rules can be evaluated as concrete performance results rather than dashboard presence. PRO also persuasively distinguished narrow security safeguards from the broad presence-scoring the motion rejects. CON leaned on output-only catastrophes, but PRO never had to defend output-only in absolute terms because the proposition says mainly outputs, not exclusively outputs. That distinction undercut CON’s best examples enough to make PRO the more convincing side.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON’s claim that PRO’s DLP and coverage admissions conceded continuous monitoring. PRO answered by distinguishing targeted data-loss tools that flag prohibited transfers from broad keystroke and presence scoring, and by explaining that coverage is verified through actual response failures, not green-dot dashboards. That neutralized CON’s strongest process-risk examples.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side A preserved the proposition's key distinction: workers can be evaluated mainly by defined results, response outcomes, quality, and coverage obligations, while narrow compliance safeguards need not become primary presence scoring. Side B landed strong warnings about hidden shortcuts, lagging outputs, and overwork, but often treated any availability metric or targeted data-loss tool as proof that constant activity monitoring must drive evaluation. Because the motion says mainly, B did not overcome A's distinction or show that easily gamed presence signals are a reliable main metric.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 distinction between targeted safeguards that flag prohibited transfers and broad keystroke, screen-time, or idle-status scoring most decisively blunted Side B's compliance example, though B's autopsy attack on coverage verification kept the debate competitive.
