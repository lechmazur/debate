### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won the interpretive and weighing battle. Both sides converged on a division of labor: civilians restore civilian infrastructure, a military core handles classified military missions. PRO pre-empted the definitional fight in its opening by flagging "rely" as the motion's key word, then defended that framing with substance (talent location, legal access, surge shape), and turned CON's own concessions—"civilian operators remain in charge of their own networks" and the admission that civilians physically restore hospitals—into the decisive weighing: the layer that determines whether the public has water and power is the layer a country relies on. CON's remaining route was purely definitional ("if a core remains, the proposition fails"), which PRO blunted with the coast-guard/flood-defense analogy and the observation that CON spent four speeches defending a military core PRO never attacked. PRO also answered the release problem honestly (defending the stricken employer is the mission; surge comes from unaffected sectors), while CON's counter ("restoration and national defense are different measures") was asserted more than earned. Both sides complied with Q/A formats; both had minor clipping. CON was competent and disciplined, but PRO created the sharper comparative pressure and made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 [B4.1]-[B4.5]: answering the pressure questions directly, then quoting CON's own words to show the standing unit "has no release problem only because it was never going in," collapsing the availability objection and narrowing the debate to the division of labor that decided the round.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by owning the institutional mechanics of cyber defense and winning the decisive surge-capacity exchange. Side A established an unrefuted military talent-retention deficit, forced Side B to concede that soldiers do not enter private critical infrastructure while civilians physically restore hospitals and utilities, and weighed effectively around what determines whether a national cyberattack succeeds or fails. Side B mounted smart pushback by distinguishing ordinary private-job restoration from reserve status and showing that Side A's conceded military core performs persistent tracking and counter-operations well beyond "defending the barracks," creating real tension with the motion's "rather than rely on" phrasing. Side A's three clipped endings also carried an execution cost, keeping the margin tight, but Side B's failure to answer Side A's second-rebuttal surge mechanisms or retention argument gave Side A the stronger path to the ballot.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Answering Side B's pressure question on simultaneous attacks and employer non-release [B3.2], Side A [A4.2-A4.4] combined statutory call-up, cleared and intel-connected defense at stricken firms, and surge pools from unaffected sectors, consultancies, universities, and retirees, then flipped reliability by noting the fixed-headcount standing unit "has no release problem only because it was never going in"—an answer Side B largely ignored in [B4.5].
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by making the decisive clash clearer: it treated “rely” as primary reliance, conceded a narrow military core, and repeatedly used CON’s own admissions that civilian operators restore critical infrastructure. ### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won the clash over the motion's actual text. A's central trap—that "rather than rely" requires naming a mission the reserve replaces, and PRO kept conceding intelligence, offensive action, and intrusion hunting to standing units—was pressed consistently across every round and never neutralized. When forced to answer A1, PRO named domestic recovery at hospitals and schools, which A convincingly showed is a reliance on military cyber units that no country ever had. PRO's A2 answer (retirees, academics, independent consultants) was honest and specific but materially shrank the promised surge pool from [B1.4]'s bankers and network engineers, and A exploited that gap effectively ("the surge shrank to a remainder the moment it was asked to name itself"). PRO's division-of-labor reframing was coherent and well-defended, and the emergency-services analogy and "protects the system of institutions" line were genuinely good, but the concession structure meant A controlled the decisive metric: which capability a country cannot lose. A's main cost was three clipped turns (opening, both rebuttals), though each clipped turn had already delivered its key points. PRO also used the required Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 formats cleanly, as did A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.8): answering the pressure questions, A showed that PRO's named "replaced" mission was one military units never performed, and catalogued how the promised surge workforce (bankers, network engineers) dissolved into retirees, academics, and junior staff once asked to name itself—collapsing PRO's core scale advantage while turning PRO's own [B2.1] "backbone" framing into the decisive test.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B won decisively on both the motion's core comparison and operational feasibility. By pressing Side A's concession that standing military units remain "elite and indispensable" for continuous intelligence, pre-incident intrusion hunting, deterrence, and offensive operations, Side B showed that Side A reduced the reserve's replacement role to post-breach recovery at hospitals, schools, and municipalities—a mission countries do not rely on standing military units to perform today. Even on Side A's best remaining argument (that a public reserve prioritizes cross-sector recovery better than private commercial contracts), Side B neutralized the underlying workforce mechanism, forcing Side A off active sector insiders onto a fallback pool whose availability and specialized utility Side B dismantled without a closing response. Side B incurred minor execution costs from clipping the ends of three turns, but Side A clipped its closing and committed a major unforced error in Pressure Questions by asking questions framed against its own side.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's multi-round pursuit of the "double-counted banker" problem ([B1.5]-[B1.6], [B2.5]-[B2.8], [B3.2], and [B4.3]-[B4.8]): after showing that active sector specialists cannot leave their own burning institutions during a simultaneous crisis, Side B forced Side A in [A4.2] to pivot to retirees, academics, independent consultants, and junior noncritical engineers, then systematically exposed why each group is either already booked by clients, outdated, or unqualified to repair critical infrastructure control systems.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by making the decisive clash clearer: it got PRO to concede that standing units keep continuous intelligence, offense, and state-deterrence roles, then framed reserves as post-breach recovery. CON’s pressure questions exposed PRO’s surge-staffing burden, and PRO’s later pool of retirees, consultants, and junior noncritical workers was less persuasive than its original broad surge claim. PRO’s division-of-labor model remained plausible, but it never fully answered which existing military reliance was being replaced or restored the credibility of its reserve workforce. A’s repeated clipping was an execution cost, but not enough to overturn its cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s pressure question on which reservists would actually be available, followed by its second rebuttal attacking B’s named pool as consultants, retirees, and junior staff, materially shrank PRO’s surge argument.
