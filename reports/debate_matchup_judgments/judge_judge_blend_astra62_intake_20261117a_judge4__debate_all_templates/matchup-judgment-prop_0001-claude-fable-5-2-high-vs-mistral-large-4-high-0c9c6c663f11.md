### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won every major substantive clash despite losing margin for getting clipped at the word limit in Turns 2, 4, and 8. On security, A dismantled B's absolutist "master key" framing via software-update signing keys and per-device split-custody escrow; B pivoted to consent (which A exposed as a privacy issue handled by warrants) and ultimately demanded a zero-risk guarantee nothing else in security meets. On policy durability, B never escaped A's fork: repealing a ban requires the same public vote as passing a mandate, so a ban adds no protection against panic while blocking safer designs—including the client-side safety signals B itself relied upon. On child safety, A refuted criminal migration on mainstream apps, neutralized B's investigative alternatives, and flipped B's "sealed letters" analogy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A2.3]-[A2.4], Side A dismantled Side B's attempt to distinguish software-update signing keys via user consent—pointing out that signing keys carry even greater technical reach than decryption while consent is a privacy objection that warrants exist to override—which forced B into defending an asymmetric perfection standard in [B5.1] that A closed the debate on in [A5.1].
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B won by forcing the motion's real choice: not whether bad backdoors are dangerous, but whether a categorical ban is required. B conceded the security risk, then showed a ban is either redundant—countries can keep refusing unsafe designs—or the only barrier to a later safer mechanism. A never escaped that fork. B also damaged the migration and durability claims: mainstream-app grooming does not require obscure networks, and mandates already need public votes, so a statute adds little anti-panic friction. A's concession that no listed alternative finds the unknown groomer made the stipulated investigative cost concrete. A's master-key rhetoric was memorable and the Juniper point was fairly used, but it mostly restated a danger B had already accepted. Repeated clipping hurt B's finish, yet the visible case still gave the cleaner vote against the ban.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's redundancy fork in the second rebuttal and closing: if no end-to-end backdoor can ever be safe, refusal already suffices; if one can, the ban is what blocks it—especially after A conceded no alternative finds the hidden groomer.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B built the stronger comparative case against a categorical ban: lawful-access risk varies with design and can be regulated, while A conceded that some hidden abuse cases have no effective alternative. B also dismantled A’s statutory-durability argument and exposed A’s demand for a perfect security guarantee as an unfair decision standard. A retained a plausible systemic-security concern, but repeatedly converted increased attack surface into inevitable civilization-wide breach without adequate weighing. B’s repeated clipping is an execution cost, but the surviving arguments remained coherent and decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it showed that mandating access already requires public legislation, so A’s ban adds little panic resistance, and turned A’s own client-side safety proposal against the ban’s categorical scope. This followed B’s successful pressure question forcing A to concede that no listed alternative reliably finds the hidden groomer.
