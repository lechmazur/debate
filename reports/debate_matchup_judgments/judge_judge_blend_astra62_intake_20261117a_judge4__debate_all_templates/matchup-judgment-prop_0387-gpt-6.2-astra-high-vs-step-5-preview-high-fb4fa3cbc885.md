- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A (PRO) won a close but real edge. A answered both pressure questions directly and honestly, conceding the match count might fall while giving a weighing story for why that trade is acceptable. Side B's strongest material—the pipeline shrinkage from compliance costs and the "conditional promise vs structural harms" weighing—was well crystallized but rested on asserted mechanisms that were never grounded, and B repeatedly framed A's proposal as "statutory" when A had explicitly built it on league entry conditions. B's "None" concession in A2 confirmed the adaptability contradiction A exposed, and B's late retreat to "uniform entry conditions across tiers" came after the clash had already moved. A also landed the sharper reframes: the difference between back pay awarded later and clearance to play now, and the point that a league can move a round while a starter cannot claim rest. B's closing was effective but largely recapitulated B4 rather than resolving the forfeit/collective-action pressure, which was never squarely answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's pressure questions against B's "statute vs adaptive standard" framing. B's A2 answer began with "None"—conceding no institutional fact distinguishes a league-set recovery window from a league-set salary minimum—then retreated to a narrower uniformity objection. A's closing then landed the cleanest comparative frame of the debate: judge protections by what a player can ask the league to do (award back pay later vs. clear her to play now), which B's "existing remedies" answer never fully neutralized.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won solidly by pairing smart institutional design with surgical rebuttals across every plank of the motion. B also proved why clear contract language and slow wage litigation do not free an unpaid, benched player from a buyout while a rival's offer is live, and why individual players cannot manage their own sleep when skipping an early match forfeits for the entire roster. A raised a credible concern about reduced match volume, but B answered it with admirable honesty and sharper weighing (distinguishing an extra exhausted scrim for five starters from five new pipeline spots), whereas A relied on statutory strawmen and misread [B4.5].
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B2.1] and [B3.2], Side B caught Side A welcoming league-enforced minimum salaries as adaptive self-regulation ([A1.5]) while condemning B's league-set recovery windows as "statutory gaps" ([A2.6]), forcing A to admit in [A4.3] ("None") that the institutional authority is identical while noting B's proposal did not even burden small academies with a uniform wage floor.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won the decisive clash. CON's standing challenge from the opening—identify the abuse that stays lawful once existing employment and child-labor rules are enforced—was never squarely answered. PRO's best attempt (twelve-hour days surviving employment status) was rebutted by B2.4 and B4.5, which cleanly split "coverage disputes" from "inadequate limits" and showed PRO had only proven the former. PRO's pressure question demanding a proactive inspector was answered concretely (DOL Wage and Hour Division can investigate without the child proving employment), neutralizing PRO's core "child must litigate first" framing—yet PRO's closing reasserted that neutralized framing anyway, a recap-heavy close. PRO's benching-log fix for the "optional practice" hard case was genuinely dismantled by B4.3-4.4: the coach writes the log, so paperwork requirements deter honest coaches, not dishonest ones. PRO kept a live point about esports-specific ambiguity and ex ante clarity, and its rhetoric was punchier, but B's analytical precision and honest engagement with pressure did more argumentative work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3-B4.4 dismantling PRO's benching-log proposal: two coaches can both write "stronger performance," so the log gives an inspector a claim to test rather than an answer—what actually exposes the lie is interviewing players and comparing messages, which investigators can already do. This turned PRO's transparency mechanism into a paper shield written by the very party it is meant to constrain.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a solid victory by separating rule stringency from enforcement and concealment, then systematically dismantling every mechanism Side B (PRO) offered to bridge that gap. Having narrowed its case almost entirely to minors, Side B claimed that even employed teenagers face lawful twelve-hour days and must win expensive lawsuits before regulators can act. Side A refuted both premises—showing that general youth-work laws already bar such schedules once employment is established, that agencies like the Wage and Hour Division can investigate sham classifications directly without waiting for private suits, and that even granting automatic coverage and benching logs leaves covert coercion unsolved unless an investigator interviews players and checks messages. Side B repeated its lawsuit framing in closing rather than answering Side A's institutional and evidentiary rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: When Side B proposed in [B4.1] that requiring teams to log mandatory scrims and benching reasons would expose retaliation over "optional" practice, Side A dismantled the mechanism in [A4.3]-[A4.4] by noting that an honest coach picking a better player and a dishonest coach punishing a missed scrim will both write "stronger performance," proving that outside investigation—not a coach-written record—is what actually protects the teenager.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by keeping the decisive distinction clear: concealed employer control and weak enforcement are not proof that existing substantive protections are inadequate. A directly answered both pressure questions, identified an existing investigative authority, and exposed why PRO’s benching log would still require investigation. B presented compelling harms and a useful ex ante framing, but never identified a twelve-hour schedule that would remain lawful once youth employment was established. B’s closing then repeated the claim that children must prove classification before protection despite A’s direct answer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: the “stronger performance” example showed that a dishonest coach could satisfy PRO’s logging requirement while continuing coercion, whereas interviews and message comparisons address the actual evidentiary problem. It also separated a coverage dispute from an inadequate hours limit, leaving PRO’s central legal-gap claim unanswered.
