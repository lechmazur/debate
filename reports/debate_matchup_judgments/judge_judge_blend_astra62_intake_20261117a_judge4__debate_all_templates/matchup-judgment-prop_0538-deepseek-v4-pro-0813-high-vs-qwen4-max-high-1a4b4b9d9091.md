### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a clear but competitive debate. It fixed the interpretation early with "prioritize is not park," and CON never fully escaped that framing. PRO also turned CON's own remedy, cutting internal trade costs, into a partial concession. PRO's answers to CON's profitability test ("a photograph of a moving object") and its closing claim that CON's threshold is "a door that opens only from the inside" exposed that CON's gate depends on the very investments CON declines to prioritize. CON built the strongest opposing route, the net-dollar argument: a factory relying on imported inputs and diesel can drain foreign exchange, while a leaky mine still earns it. CON answered PRO's mine-symmetry question honestly and named a concrete threshold when pressed. CON's late move was clever: if PRO's next dollar goes to roads and power, PRO is defending shared infrastructure rather than manufacturing. But PRO had already seeded the counter in its opening. Colonial infrastructure ran from mine to port, and corridors, power lines and customs unions oriented toward intra-African links are not neutral. Prioritizing them sits squarely within a motion that names intra-African trade explicitly. PRO's A1, citing Dangote cement, the EAC customs union and PAPSS, used plausible real-world grounding without brittle precision. It edged toward widening the debate but directly answered a live question. Overall, PRO framed the decisive clash more memorably and left CON's net-dollar risk looking manageable rather than decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over CON's threshold and the "only defending roads" pivot was decisive. CON argued that PRO's next dollar goes to shared infrastructure that also helps the copper truck. PRO answered that infrastructure direction is itself the priority: mine-to-port versus city-to-city. It added that every term in CON's threshold (reachable buyers, realistic energy, scale) is produced by the very priority CON resists. That reframing neutralized CON's best closing line.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a high-quality exchange. Both sides met burdens and used the required formats honestly. A's sharpest route — that B's own answer routes the next dollar to shared infrastructure, making PRO "defend roads, not manufacturing" — was squarely answered in B's closing: infrastructure is directional (mine-to-port vs. city-to-city corridors, customs unions negotiated for manufacturers), and those directly serve the motion's intra-African trade component. B also landed the two decisive turns: (1) A's profitability threshold is endogenous to the priority A refuses ("a door that opens only from the inside"), and (2) applying A's net-dollar test to the export lane itself (Nigeria shipping crude out and buying refined fuel back) neutralized A's balance-of-payments asymmetry, which A answered only with "leaky but it is cash." A's pressure questions were well-aimed, but B's Rebuttal 2 answered concretely (cement, Dangote, EAC customs union, PAPSS) without fake precision. B's case cohered across rounds — prioritize as direction, not prohibition — while A's "when" attack, though sharp, was blunted by B showing A's "when" depends on the very priority being proposed. B made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2: the direct, grounded answer to A's "name the product and regime" pressure (cement, EAC customs union, PAPSS) combined with the reversal that CON's own net-dollar test condemns the crude-export lane (Nigeria exporting crude, importing refined petrol) and the observation that CON's threshold is a "veto dressed as patience."
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): PRO sustained the key distinction that prioritize does not mean abandoning export earnings, then showed regional demand is fragmented rather than absent. CON's claim that such infrastructure is neutral was undercut by PRO's mine-to-port versus city-to-city distinction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response to CON's profitability filter was decisive: reachable buyers, realistic energy costs, and scale are produced by prior investment, so requiring them beforehand turns sequencing into a permanent veto. CON named a threshold late, but PRO's closing showed that threshold reinforced rather than defeated the priority.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built the stronger case and kept it live through every round. Its core mechanism was that factories need recurring hard currency for machinery, spares and inputs, and that commodity sales to paying buyers supply it. PRO never fully neutralized this. CON also pressed two points well. First, regional demand is itself commodity-financed and therefore correlated with the same shocks ("when copper falls, Zambia stops buying Kenyan tea"). Second, PRO's own clarification that raw exports would continue and fund industry largely collapsed the ranking the motion asks for. PRO's best moment was Q1, asking for a non-circular test of escaping dependence. CON answered it directly with a falsifiable criterion: can the country keep importing machinery, fuel and medicine through a price crash, as measured by reserves, savings and a wider export basket. PRO's answer to the request for a historical example was weak. It argued that no example exists because the system suppressed regional trade, and CON fairly called that unfalsifiable. CON then offered Morocco and Mauritius as cases of upgrading exports to rich markets. PRO's closing merely asserted that these cases "reinforce the trap." It missed the chance to claim them as evidence for the manufacturing half of the motion. PRO did name concrete marginal trade-offs: port slots, foreign-exchange quotas and export incentives. CON turned these effectively, arguing that they delay dollar-earning cargo and squeeze farmgate incomes, which shrinks the rural market PRO said it would serve. Overall, CON made the debate easier to decide and adapted more sharply from round to round.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.1–A2.4 was the decisive exchange. CON seized PRO's statement that raw exports would keep flowing and fund factories, and recast it as conceding "keep the earnings, change the terms." It then exposed PRO's claim to "buy capital goods once" as false, because machinery, spares and inputs create recurring dollar demand. PRO's later attempt to turn this into a "permanent dependence" argument came too late and never restored a clear mechanism by which regional sales earn the needed hard currency.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Both sides were competent and compliant, but Side A (CON) won the decisive clashes. A's rebuttal 1 framed B's position as a concession of CON's own case ("keep the earnings, change the terms") and landed the sharpest technical blow of the debate: intra-African trade recirculates but does not earn hard currency, and capital goods demand is recurrent ("machinery is not once") — B's answer about substituting imports and buying capital goods "once" never fully recovered from A's rejoinder about depreciation, spares, and imported inputs. When A's pressure forced B to name what "prioritise" actually demotes, A attacked the named levers concretely (delaying dollar-earning cargo, squeezing farmgate prices and thus the rural incomes B counts as its market), and B's only reply was a late, brief subsidy reframing in closing. On the definitional clash over "dependence," A offered an operationalizable test (ability to import through a crash via reserves and a widening export basket) with real passing examples (Botswana, Morocco, Mauritius), while B's no-exit circularity argument was A's strongest structural point but ultimately rested on a definition that classifies the continent's actual escapes as failures — which A memorably exposed as "a test that cannot be passed by anything that actually happened is not a test; it is a preference." B's honesty in conceding no economy has made the regional shift, plus the unfalsifiable "system suppressed it" defense, left B without empirical instances. A was also crisper rhetorically ("dependence is not a postcode," "getting richer beats choosing poorer customers") without sacrificing substance. Margin is clear but competitive because B's circularity and subsidy-reframing arguments remained live to the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 was decisive: the concession frame ("routing those earnings toward local production... is my case") combined with the hard-currency recurrence point — intra-African trade "recirculates" dollars rather than earning them, and "buy capital goods once" fails because machinery depreciates and needs spares and imported inputs — put B on the defensive for the rest of the debate, and B never found a full answer.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B won the central clash over sequencing and finance: PRO's concession that raw exports continue and fund factories became B's model, while B showed regional sales recirculate rather than earn the recurring hard currency manufacturing needs. B also answered the dependence test with reserves and diversification and used Botswana, Morocco, and Mauritius to show upgrading succeeded through rich-market sales, whereas A admitted no comparable regional-shift example and relied on suppressed-demand claims B effectively called unfalsifiable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first rebuttal was decisive: it reframed A's 'route the earnings' position as conceding CON's case, then pressed that machinery, spares, and inputs create continuing dollar demand that intra-African trade cannot itself supply.
