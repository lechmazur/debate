### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins by a clear but competitive margin because it kept the debate anchored to the motion's systemic question: does real-time visibility invite micromanagement? PRO built a consistent comparison of daily, widespread costs against a rare benefit: - children lose space to self-correct; - teachers are pulled into managing parents; - the logging burden grows. Its answers to the pressure questions were direct and useful. PRO stated exactly what it would restrict: immediate parent visibility of routine entries, whether or not alerts are pushed. It also gave the self-selection point: anxious parents choose live feeds while relaxed parents choose digests. That point undercut CON's opt-in design fix and was never squarely answered. CON (A) was inventive and elegant. Its distinction between knowing about a lapse and intervening in it was sharp. The forgotten-glasses example made a vivid case for parents holding information that teachers lack. CON also answered the "clerical contradiction" well by denying that access creates any duty to log. However, CON's case rested on one rare, somewhat contrived benefit. Its safeguards also partly concede the motion's premise. Keeping phones away, withdrawing access from intrusive parents, and silencing teacher inboxes are themselves limits on dashboards becoming micromanagement tools. CON's best counter was that digests also feed dinner-table punishment. PRO answered it adequately: summaries convey patterns, not isolated settled moments. PRO's closing slightly overstated A4.4 as an outright admission. Its "candid notes vanish" point was a mild extension rather than a fresh factual angle. Overall, PRO made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over Q2 and A2. CON argued that silent parents only threaten a child when the child expects a second correction, and offered design fixes to prevent that. PRO answered in B4.1 and B4.4 that optional digests fail through self-selection: the hovering parents most likely to correct twice will choose live feeds. This meant CON's mitigations could not contain the micromanagement the motion targets. CON's reply that digests also supply dinner-table occasions was clever. It still did not address who opts into live access.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): A wins because it kept the debate centered on the structural incentives of real-time visibility. B's seeing/directing distinction was sharp, but A showed that live feeds pressure teachers into granular logging and that optional digests fail by self-selection: the parents most likely to micromanage will choose live access. A also outweighed the forgotten-glasses benefit by treating it as a rare contingency that does not justify daily surveillance costs and teacher distraction. B's reliance on ideal parental restraint and design tweaks felt less robust than A's causal account of how the dashboard itself invites micromanagement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's conversion of B's duty-to-log admission was decisive: B conceded live access would be costly if it created a documentation duty, and A showed the glasses scenario implicitly requires logging ordinary recoveries and slips, leaving B caught between quiet delivery and running commentary. A's self-selection point further undercut B's design solution by showing the parents who need restraint least are the ones who would opt out.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won a close, high-quality exchange by preserving the distinction between immediate access, exhaustive logging, and parental intervention. A’s logging dilemma and self-selection critique were serious, but B answered with a plausible lesson-planning note, classroom phone controls, and access withdrawal, while retaining the glasses example as a noninterventionist benefit that delay would destroy. A too often treated live visibility as necessarily producing play-by-play logging and double punishment; its late defensive-writing point did not fully overcome B’s cleaner boundary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s “clerical contradiction” was the strongest attack. B’s response that a teacher can make one useful planning note, without any duty to narrate subsequent recovery, decisively separated recording from visibility and kept the CON case intact.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO wins narrowly by holding the clearer decision rule: share speed only when speed changes what a family can do now. It also keeps that rule tied to the motion's modest ask, which is to limit live behavior dashboards, not to end logging or communication. PRO's first rebuttal neutralized CON's diagnostic case early: timestamps are just as accurate at dismissal, and one-tap logging can be kept while broadcasting is limited. That turned most of CON's pattern-spotting benefits into arguments for good records rather than for real-time feeds. CON fought back well with three points: the private-log negativity turn, the workload fork, and "controlling parents are armed either way." PRO answered each adequately. Positives can become ordinary taps, platforms can group observations automatically, and while no rule guarantees a gentle parent, a school need not supply the script. CON's strongest late move was "at ten the child is in class, so there is no audience." PRO's closing answered it cleanly: the parent still has to decide at ten whether to act without the context that later shows the slip was minor. That is exactly the invitation to micromanage the motion targets. CON's forgotten-glasses example remained a fairly narrow edge case, and PRO addressed it with teacher-initiated check-ins and parent-initiated messages. CON was sharp and well structured but somewhat overclaimed, for example that private logs would inevitably skew negative and that a delay prevents zero interrogations. PRO's case was more modest and better calibrated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B2.1 ("precision about when something happened is no reason to broadcast it as it happens") reframed CON's diagnostic benefits as arguments for record-keeping, not live dashboards. Later, B5.1 ("quiet hours can put the mark in perspective; they cannot unsend the message it prompted") answered CON's strongest "no audience at ten" rebuttal.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won the central clash by reframing when micromanagement can actually reach the child. A’s strongest image was the premature alert that could not be unsent, but B showed that during class the child is not exposed to the feed, reply norms keep parent messages harmless in an inbox, and by pickup the timeline contains later context and positive taps. B also damaged A’s alternative: a hidden log may skew negative because quiet recoveries are less reliably recorded without visible payoff, and preset check-ins cannot know each child’s baseline. A’s closing ‘cannot unsend’ point was vivid but not enough to overcome B’s comparative case that limits mostly cost timely useful context without protecting children from controlling parents.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive rebuttal was turning A’s own fairness worry into a secret-log problem: without live visibility, busy teachers are less likely to record quiet recoveries, leaving a lopsidedly negative record. Paired with the point that actual parental micromanagement happens after school, when a live timeline already provides context, this directly undercut A’s main route to victory.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side A won narrowly by preserving the decisive distinction between retaining useful behavioral records and broadcasting every correction live. Its balanced scheduled updates, targeted check-ins, and parent-initiated messaging answered much of Side B’s workload and early-intervention case, while its closing exposed why later context cannot undo an unnecessary real-time intervention. Side B offered a strong efficiency and proportionality defense, but its claim that micromanagement matters only after dismissal was too narrow, and it did not fully establish why routine corrections must be visible immediately rather than summarized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first rebuttal cleanly separated timestamped recordkeeping from live parental visibility, then challenged the reliability of positive recovery logging. B’s later delayed-digest and teacher-workload fork was the strongest counterpressure, but A’s ordinary scheduled updates and judgment-based check-ins kept its alternative viable.
