### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins clearly. CON's central dilemma held up through the debate: either all-in disclosure with standardized sizes cures the hidden-cost harm, or PRO is simply abolishing a cheaper product that some travelers rationally want. PRO's best response was the "common unit" and residual-gaming point. CON neutralized it by offering standardized sizes plus ranked all-in totals with a bag toggle. That left PRO defending a definitional claim, that a trip without a roller bag is "incomplete," which CON exposed as the real disagreement. On scarcity, PRO's A1 conceded that late boarders are still gate-checked for free. CON turned this into a memorable "entitlement to a queue" funded by everyone's higher fares. CON's rival versus non-rival distinction directly answered PRO's pooling argument. PRO kept a live point on predictability and surprise fees hitting low-income travelers. However, CON's consultant turn and its disclosure alternative reduced PRO's harms to a display problem that does not require the mandate. CON had minor weaknesses: the bare assertion that unbundling expanded access to flying, and the claim that price "compensates" denied passengers. These cost CON a little but did not undo its lead.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.2–A4.4 settled the debate. It took PRO's own A1 admission that late boarders are gate-checked free and recast the baseline as fares rising to fund "an entitlement to a queue" while removing the lever that reduces bag volume. It then separated rival bin space from non-rival safety and crew costs, which dismantled PRO's pooling defense.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that standardized sizes plus first-display all-in ranking of the traveler's actual bag choice solves the transparency problem without forcing a regressive transfer or abolishing light-travel fares. Side B never neutralized that alternative, and its own concessions—that bins still overflow, late boarders are still gate-checked, and priority upsells survive—left the baseline looking like prepaid access to a queue while removing the price signal that reduces bag volume. Side A's rival-good distinction and "entitlement to a queue" framing made that clash decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal turned Side B's own gate-check concession against the mandate: free overflow for late boarders means fares rise to fund an unguaranteed entitlement, while eliminating the fee that discourages extra bags makes the shortage worse, not more predictable.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): A won a close, high-quality debate by supplying a less restrictive alternative—standardized bag sizes plus traveler-selected, all-in totals at first display—and by showing that B never clearly identified a harm surviving it. A also exposed the central weakness in B’s scarcity case: a baseline guarantees an entitlement and an overflow rule, not bin space, while removing fees reduces the incentive to limit bag volume. B argued the deceptive-anchor problem effectively and answered pressure directly, but too often relied on defining a roller-bag trip as the only “complete” trip rather than proving that light travelers should prepay for it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: the personalized all-in display escaped B’s include/exclude dilemma, and the distinction between pooled non-rival costs and rival bin space sharpened the transfer objection. B’s free gate-check answer established predictability, but not that the mandate would avoid more bags competing for the same shortage.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins a competitive debate on the strength of one structural dilemma that Side B (PRO) never escaped. PRO's best weapons were real and well delivered: - **Gate-leverage point:** the same bag costs little online and much more at the boarding door, so the fee reflects leverage rather than scarcity. - **Disclosure is also regulation:** CON's own remedy is a rule, so the choice is between regulations, not between regulation and freedom. - **Competition contradiction:** competition cannot be strong enough to keep honest bundles available yet too weak to protect bag dimensions. Lines like "a cheaper number, not a cheaper product" were memorable. CON's valet-check dilemma (A2.1) was the decisive move. Either the baseline guarantees bin space a full aircraft cannot provide, or it is merely a free checked bag under a carry-on label. PRO's A1 answer conceded that medication, laptops and chargers belong in the personal item every fare already includes. That gutted PRO's opening frame that a carry-on is as essential as a seat, and A4.3 exploited the concession cleanly. CON also answered both pressure questions directly: - It named the light traveler who saves, roughly the fare gap. - It gave an operational rationale for the gate premium: an agent transaction, boarding delays, and gate-check handling. PRO's closing recast that answer as pure leverage, which partly misdescribes CON's cost-and-disruption justification. CON's subsidy argument, that light travelers pay for everyone else's roller, was never squarely neutralized. PRO effectively conceded it with "the backpacker may pay a little more." The leverage harm stays live but is narrower once PRO's own concession reduces the roller to a convenience. CON was also unrebutted that disclosure and visible late fees address the ambush.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1/A4.3: CON's valet-check dilemma forced PRO to concede that the essentials ride in the free personal item, reducing the baseline to free hold transport for clothes. CON then turned that into a subsidy charged to light travelers. PRO's strongest counter was B4.2's competition contradiction, which landed but did not repair the essential-access frame.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side A won by forcing the motion into a choice it could not survive cleanly: either promise impossible bin space or mandate free roller transport that light travelers must subsidize. A answered both pressure questions directly, named a real saver and a pre-purchase fare gap, and offered disclosure plus optional bundles as the less restrictive fix. B’s gate-leverage frame stayed vivid, but the concession that essentials already fit in the personal item stripped the seat analogy, and the closing flattened A’s disruption explanation into pure captivity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s valet-check trap in Rebuttal 1, completed in Rebuttal 2: if hold transport satisfies the right, it is a free-bag mandate rather than in-cabin protection; B’s own answer then conceded medication and laptops already ride in the personal item, leaving a clothes-roller subsidy.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): A won a competitive debate by preserving the clearest comparative defense: light travelers receive a real advance-purchase discount, while total-price disclosure addresses deception without imposing a bundle on everyone. A also exposed the shift from essential in-cabin access to free hold transport and kept the operational-cost and definitional objections alive. B had the more memorable gate-leverage rhetoric, but never squarely showed why disclosure was insufficient, and the closing overstated the light-traveler concession while largely asserting away A’s late-compliance explanation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions: it named backpack-only travelers and the basic-versus-bundled fare gap as genuine savings, then supplied concrete planning, staffing, and boarding-disruption reasons for higher gate charges. That exchange substantially weakened B’s claims that nobody saves and that the price gradient could only reflect captive leverage.
