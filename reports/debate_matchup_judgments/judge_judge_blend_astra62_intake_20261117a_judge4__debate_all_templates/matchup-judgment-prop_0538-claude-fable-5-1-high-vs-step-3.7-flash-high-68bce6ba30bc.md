### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won the decisive clash. B's opening set a clear causal story (commodity trap, existing regional demand for manufactured goods, "prioritize" governing the next dollar not banning exports), which pre-empted A's strawman framing about abandoning raw exports. A's core case was transition risk around forex losses, but it rested on brittle precision (the "2020 copper price drop" causing "15% health budget cuts" — copper actually rallied in 2020, making this look invented). B consistently flipped A's material: Zambia's budget cut proved the trap, logistics costs proved the inheritance, and South Africa/Egypt's dominance proved manufacturing works. A's pressure questions were answered cleanly by B with proper A1/A2 format; more importantly, A4.2 conceded that past policy prioritized ports and the fix is redirecting revenue to regional corridors — B's closing devastatingly quoted this back as CON "signing" the motion. A's final position reduced to sequencing caution with no start condition, which B framed as "end dependence, but never today." A followed format adequately but repeated the Zambia example across nearly every speech with little progression.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing: after A4.2 explained a century of failed processing capacity as "past policy prioritized port throughput over regional corridors," B seized the concession verbatim, showing CON had adopted the motion's own remedy, then exposed A's viability trigger as circular — waiting for regional markets that only materialize once the corridors are prioritized.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): B consistently defended prioritization without abandoning exports. A raised legitimate transition risks but never established that redirecting future investment necessarily collapses existing export earnings; its repeated, unsupported budget figures did not prove that mechanism. A then endorsed funding regional corridors rather than port expansion, substantially conceding B’s policy. Its misdirected pressure questions and contradictory closing further weakened its case. B overstated some historical claims and treated demand too readily as proof of viability, but offered much stronger comparison and rebuttal throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s closing quoted A4.2’s endorsement of redirecting future export revenue toward regional connectivity rather than port expansion. This exposed how A’s proposed alternative substantially supported the motion, crystallizing B’s earlier distinction between maintaining export income and changing investment priorities.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): B built and sustained the clearer case: “prioritize” means directing new investment toward processing and regional corridors, not shutting mines or instantly sacrificing export earnings. B turned A’s Zambia example into evidence of commodity vulnerability and treated logistics barriers as problems the motion addresses. A never adequately explained why viable processing would collapse existing foreign-exchange earnings, relied on brittle specifics, and ultimately conceded that revenue should be redirected from port expansion toward regional connectivity. B’s closing exposed both that concession and A’s circular demand that regional markets become ready before the investments needed to make them ready.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal decisively reversed A’s strongest evidence: Zambia’s price-driven budget crisis demonstrated the danger of dependence, while expensive regional logistics demonstrated the cost of infrastructure built around extraction. B then pressed that advantage until A’s own proposed remedy substantially endorsed the motion.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) controlled the decisive clash from the opening onward: the word "over" implies ranking, yet every concrete success PRO cited (Ivorian paste to Europe, Chilean lithium to Asian battery makers, Korea selling to America, Botswana polishing for Antwerp) was value addition aimed at rich markets. A turned PRO's own examples into concessions, exposed the circularity of "latent demand created by manufacturing jobs," and articulated a clear sequence: commodity revenue funds infrastructure, exports to rich buyers create wages, wages create regional demand. A answered both pressure questions directly and cleanly corrected B's factually shaky Botswana poverty claim. Side B (PRO) kept real lines alive (commodity volatility, resource curse, colonial structure of trade routes) but repeatedly leaned on brittle precision (Nigeria's 30% GDP crash, Botswana's 60% poverty, a late, unverifiable Ghana-Nigeria paste statistic), and B4.2's "complementary" framing fatally undermined the motion's ranking requirement. B's closing failed to resolve the sequencing critique and mostly restated the case. A's progression, direct answers, and memorable framing (chocolate melting in transit, "regional demand is the output of export-led growth, not its input") made the debate easy to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 [A4.4]: catching PRO's admission that the two paths are "complementary" and converting it into a resolution-level concession—if complementary, nothing is prioritized "over" anything, so PRO abandoned the motion it had to defend. This crystallized the whole debate and PRO never recovered.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): A wins the practical sequencing clash: it explains how existing export earnings and affluent customers can finance manufacturing, while B insufficiently establishes viable demand for its regional-first strategy. B also relies heavily on unsupported precision, particularly its Ghana trade figures and Botswana poverty claim, and mischaracterizes A's export-manufacturing alternative as permanent raw-material dependence. The margin is limited by A's own substantial errors: the motion does not deprioritize all rich-market exports, and complementary policies can still be ranked. A wins through its demand and feasibility objections, not those interpretive shortcuts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response to B's “latent demand” argument exposes the central sequencing problem: factory wages cannot simply be assumed to supply the customers needed to make those factories viable. B identifies funding sources but never adequately answers who buys its finished regional products at sufficient scale.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): A kept the motion’s actual prioritization burden central and supplied a coherent sequence: existing exports finance infrastructure, rich-market manufacturing creates wages, and regional demand follows. B’s claims that the paths were complementary and that prioritization did not reject outward exports left the required ranking underdefended, while its financing and demand arguments remained circular. A also answered both pressure questions directly; B relied increasingly on dubious precise statistics and unsupported examples.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal decisively corrected the Botswana premise, showed that B’s processing examples still depended on rich external buyers, and exposed the complementarity concession as incompatible with defending the motion’s “over” requirement.
