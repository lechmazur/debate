### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): A won on the core fairness question. A’s turn of the rental-car analogy was decisive: B borrowed a fault rule while omitting the compulsory insurance and licensing system that makes that rule tolerable. B never supplied a satisfactory payer when the rider is insolvent—its answer became health coverage and “uncompensated loss falls on her,” which concedes the pedestrian absorbs the cost. B’s strongest counter was the exit/shift-to-cars-and-private-scooters chain, including the memorable morgue point, but A credibly reframed that as an unpriced subsidy and speculative; spreading insurance costs and narrower tailoring undercut the doomsday prediction. B also had to concede geofences work in wide plazas, leaving mainly unmapped zones and insolvent riders in dispute, where A’s modest backstop looked more reasonable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s rental-car rebuttal was the most decisive exchange: it showed that B’s comparison works against CON because hired cars operate with compulsory insurance and checked licences, while shared scooters have no comparable backstop.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): PRO wins the central loss-allocation clash. CON honestly admitted an insolvent rider leaves the loss on the pedestrian and denied liability for a known, geofenceable plaza absent a permit, while PRO's control, profit, traceability, and insured-backstop arguments remained a live route to more responsibility. CON's GPS-fault and substitution arguments were strong but did not justify zero added responsibility in clearly restricted zones.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's morgue rebuttal badly damaged PRO's claim that substitution into insured cars leaves the victim better off, but PRO's closing more decisively turned CON's own admissions—victim bears unpaid losses and recovery depends on city permit mapping—into the deciding comparison.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side A built the clearer and more persuasive route to victory by tying “more legal responsibility” to operator control, profit, traceability, risk-spreading, and the compensation gap when riders are insolvent. It exploited Side B’s concessions on feasible geofencing in wide plazas and made B’s permit-dependent liability line look arbitrary. Side B’s GPS-limit and safety-backfire arguments were strong and kept the sidewalk issue live, but its answer that uncompensated losses fall on the victim or general health systems was a decisive fairness weakness that A repeatedly crystallized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s pressure question about a known but unpermitted restricted plaza forced B into a narrow city-map defense, which A turned into the decisive “city clerk” objection; B’s best reply was the GPS/no-fault challenge to post-ride audits, but it did not fully answer the compensation clash.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): A won because it answered B's hardest pressure honestly and turned B's proposed fixes into a live dilemma: per-trip rider insurance still pools costs and uses insurer payouts, while disclosure and permits can leave hit-and-run victims unable to prove which rider is liable. B's strongest moment was exposing that A would make operators pay even after full compliance, but A integrated that into a backstop/compensation and marginal-prevention story rather than letting it collapse its case. B's clipped second rebuttal was a real execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was A's A2 showing that B's per-trip rider insurance recreates the same fare-funded insurer payout and moral-hazard cushion B attacked, while excusing operators from safety incentives.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): CON pinned PRO to paying even after full geofencing, throttling, and bans, then showed that residual payment chiefly spreads costs or encourages retreat rather than deterring controllable conduct. Its narrower alternatives—rider-linked insurance, compelled identification, and enforceable permit duties—answered compensation and enforcement while keeping liability tied to control. PRO exposed real tensions in rider-insurance funding and identification, but never fully justified strict fleet liability as superior to those fixes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pressure question and follow-up were decisive: PRO's admission that a fully compliant operator still pays let CON separate genuine prevention incentives, already reachable through negligence and mandates, from the motion's unique residual payment.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side A won because it maintained a live compensation-and-deterrence route that B never fully neutralized: fleet operators profit from deploying vehicles in restricted zones, can spread risk, and are needed as the liable backstop when victims can identify only the brand, not the rider. Side B’s control principle and compliant-company pressure were strong, but its rider-insurance/disclosure alternatives remained vulnerable to hit-and-run identification problems and to A’s point that rider-funded insurance recreates the same compensation pool without giving the operator a direct safety stake. B’s clipped second rebuttal also cost it some closing clarity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure question forcing A to say that a fully compliant operator still pays was the sharpest exchange, but A recovered by framing that payment as enterprise risk-spreading and a spur beyond static permits, while B could not prove rider-only liability reliably reaches victims.
