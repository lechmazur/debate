### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): PRO (Side A) wins clearly. It anchored the debate in a concrete, memorable stake: an elder's time with a learner cannot be bought back once lost. It then answered each of CON's structural objections with design detail rather than assertion. PRO's strongest moves addressed the gatekeeping worry directly: - Oral applications through trusted local institutions. - No requirement for state recognition of a dialect. - Rationing by "cost of delay" rather than by cultural worth. PRO also kept custody separate from funding and secured CON's admission that no custody decision passes to officials merely because the funding is standing. It exposed a tension in CON's alternative: tax incentives and matching grants also spend public money, but only where private money already flows, which excludes poor communities. CON (Side B) raised legitimate concerns about concentration, politicized appropriations, and dependency. Its "one gate vs. many doors" frame, however, never answered PRO's reframe that a standing fund adds a door alongside charity rather than replacing it. CON kept describing the fund as the "sole caretaker," which overstates PRO's model. The claim that diaspora mobilization would atrophy stayed speculative. Its housing-versus-preservation tradeoff was neutralized by PRO's point that CON's own preferred mechanisms also draw on public resources. CON also showed execution weakness: one rebuttal paragraph was merely a bare reference ID, and later speeches largely repeated the concentration point without adapting to it. PRO built the cleaner path to victory and engaged the pressure more directly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4.3–A4.5 decided the debate. It reframed the comparison as "independent grants alone vs. independent grants alongside a standing fund," which undercut CON's concentration and resilience argument. It also showed an inconsistency: CON credited a college with finding new sponsors when a project grant expires, yet treated the same college as helpless if a standing appropriation ends. CON's reply in B4.4 restated the "one gate" image without answering the additive framing.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A won by holding the ballot to irreversible loss and charity's blind spot for poor communities, then meeting Side B's ranking and austerity pressure without giving up community custody. Side B's control concession and "many doors" frame were sharp, but never solved the case where every private door stays shut for the same community, while work funded this year still survives a later cut.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2: rank by irreversible delay and lack of alternatives, not cultural worth, and treat the standing fund as additive to diaspora support rather than a sole caretaker.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): PRO wins by framing the standing fund as an additional, community-custodied route rather than a replacement for private support. CON's concession that custody need not move to officials undermined its gatekeeping case, while PRO honestly conceded austerity risk and outweighed it with irreversibility: work completed before any cut survives, but an elder's lost time cannot be recovered. CON's centralization and scarcity arguments remained plausible but did not defeat that baseline comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: ranking by cost of delay, conceding shared-cut vulnerability, and exposing CON's inconsistency in treating local colleges as resilient after project grants but helpless after standing support.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): CON (B) won because it turned the debate into a concrete allocation problem and PRO never escaped it. A standing fund that ranks applicants by severity must either renew existing apprenticeships, leaving more urgent communities waiting, or switch funding and break the continuity it promised. That made PRO's "floor" claim look like a right to be considered rather than a guarantee against loss. PRO also leaned on "pooled, dedicated revenue" and "capitalized for decades" without explaining why such a fund is safer from cuts than a fully funded apprenticeship. B had asked exactly that and drew no real answer. B conceded that public money is often needed, which PRO tried to exploit. B handled this cleanly: it compared equally financed commitments and argued that a permanent competition among communities adds a harmful ranking contest without adding security. PRO's later speeches mostly restated "irreversible loss requires permanence" rather than adapting to the specific mechanism B kept pressing. PRO's real asset was the structural underprovision argument, along with the Hawaiian and Indigenous Australian examples. But B's case for dispersed, publicly backed transmission blunted that argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure question about the four unfunded communities, followed by the closing line "that is a right to consideration, not a floor beneath loss." Together these exposed that PRO's central guarantee did not deliver what it claimed. PRO answered by restating "published criteria" and "a place to return to," which conceded the point.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing that a standing fund's promised floor is mainly a recurring right of consideration, not secured teaching, while equally durable public commitments to particular apprentices can be made without a permanent cross-community ranking contest. Side A kept reasserting institutional permanence and statutory obligation, but never closed the gap that scarcity, renewals, and cuts still leave many without teachers, or that targeted commitments can match the needed continuity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal and closing most decisively punctured the floor: a fund can publish reasons, renew two awards, and meet its duty while four elders still receive no teaching afternoons, whereas paying through an apprenticeship and letting the money follow secures transmission without a permanent selector.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B wins by separating permanence of an office from permanence of commitment to an apprentice. It shows A's floor is only a right to reapply, exposes the renew-versus-more-urgent ranking dilemma, and offers targeted, fully funded public posts as an alternative to both private charity and a permanent severity contest. A repeatedly asserts insulated, pooled permanence but never answers why a fund is less cut-prone or better than an equally financed portable commitment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: compare equally well-financed promises, not a capitalized fund against an unfunded handshake, and let support follow the apprentice if a school closes. This neutralized A's strongest pooling and continuity claim.
