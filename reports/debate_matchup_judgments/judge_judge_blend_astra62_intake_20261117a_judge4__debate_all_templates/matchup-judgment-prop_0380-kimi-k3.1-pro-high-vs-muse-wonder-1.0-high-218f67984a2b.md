### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but PRO (B) landed the more damaging exchange: it seized on CON's concession that "eighteen is not a magical birthday" and used it to expose that CON's defense of eighteen (a "reasonable" convention tracking legal majority) relies on the same type of class-based, convention-anchored reasoning CON criticized when PRO used it for sixteen. CON's strongest asset—the "every line excludes someone, so inclusion-as-default proves too much" argument—was real and never fully neutralized, but PRO's closing reframed the dispute cleanly as "both lines are conventional, so choose the one that includes capable, affected citizens at the most stable moment to build a voting habit," which is an easier story for a reader to adjudicate than CON's more abstract "reasonable threshold" defense. PRO also handled the pressure-question exchange with sharper, more concrete answers (anchoring sixteen in convergent legal/work responsibilities) than CON's answers, which mostly restated the "ordinary institutional position" framework. CON's case was damaged further by its opening being clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost that cut off its argument before full development. Overall PRO narrowed and crystallized the clash more effectively in the final rounds, giving the cleaner path to a decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2's turn of CON's own concession ("eighteen is not a magical birthday... a convention is not a justification") against CON's bright-line defense, reinforced by B's Q1 pressure question forcing CON to articulate a concrete capacity gap it could not cleanly supply.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully defending 18 as a coherent proxy for independent citizenship (legal majority/formal independence) and neutralizing Side B's strongest practical argument by turning it into a concession of Side A's main objection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal and closing efficiently turned Side B's 'stability' argument against it, pointing out that the stability of being at home and in school is precisely the lack of independence that justifies the age-18 threshold.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B built a more compelling and original case, particularly through the "stability vs. chaos" habit-formation argument, which highlighted that sixteen is a better time to establish voting routines than the disruptive transition at eighteen. Side A failed to decisively refute this, relying mostly on calling the benefits speculative. Side B also effectively weaponized Side A's concession that eighteen is merely a conventional bright line, forcing Side A to defend the specific merits of eighteen over sixteen or fourteen. While Side A's distinction regarding formal legal independence was a strong defensive maneuver, Side B's overall weighing of the democratic error—excluding capable citizens with the longest stake in the outcome versus including them—was more persuasive and better resolved the core clashes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first and second rebuttals, where they dismantled Side A's "life administration" and "dependence" tests by showing they prove too much and apply to many enfranchised adults, while sharply contrasting the real-world consequences of exclusion against the lack of harm from inclusion.
