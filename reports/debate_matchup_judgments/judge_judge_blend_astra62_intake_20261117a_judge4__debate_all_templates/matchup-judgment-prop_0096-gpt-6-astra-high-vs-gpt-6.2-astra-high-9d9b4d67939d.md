### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A more convincingly isolated the contested asset: generating the actor’s own voice in a new language, not merely authorizing a dubbed release. Its example of a French track voiced by another performer versus by the lead’s synthetic voice kept the distinction vivid, and its distribution-promise analogy put real pressure on B. B’s strongest line was that informed broad authorization is not necessarily less valid than a separate checkbox, but A answered by reframing opt-in as requiring the producer to ask for the voice use, not a second signature, and by treating access costs as a reason the studio wants a grant, not proof the grant exists. A offered the cleaner default rule on the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A’s response to the legacy/access and boilerplate pressure: a French dub in another voice fulfills a dubbing promise, while the same dub in the lead’s voice is an additional commitment; unavailability or cost may explain why the studio wants that grant but cannot create one.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined and well-grounded, but Side A built and defended the cleaner comparative framework. PRO's core distinction — a dubbing grant covers a translated soundtrack, not the creation of new speech in the performer's vocal likeness — survived CON's best attack. CON's strongest route was the "formalism" charge: if an actor demonstrably understands a broad clause covers synthetic dubs, why is that consent invalid? PRO answered honestly (A1) and reframed the rule as burden allocation — the producer who wants the voice must ask for it, rather than the performer having to claw it back from boilerplate — and the closing crystallized this as what the first signature is being asked to authorize. Meanwhile CON materially conceded ground in B4.2 (asking once at signing jeopardizes nothing; no defense of concealment), narrowing the live dispute to legacy films and the informed-consent edge case. On legacy films, PRO's reply that a lost release "is not evidence of a grant," plus the point that synthetic dubbing in another authorized voice remains available, blunted CON's access-cost argument. CON's response to PRO's seller/buyer distribution analogy (commercial distinctness ≠ separate grants) was its weakest moment, leaving PRO's asymmetry point standing. No fake citations or brittle precision on either side; both used plausible mechanisms and examples well.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's distribution analogy in A4.4–A4.5 (a studio selling a French dub in another voice versus in the lead's voice treats these as distinct commitments, so they should not become interchangeable when buying from the performer) against CON's counter in B4.5 that commercial distinctness does not require separate grants. CON's reply identified the right target but never neutralized the asymmetry, while CON's parallel "formalism, not autonomy" charge in B4.3–B4.4 was its most damaging hit and forced PRO's most careful closing work.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B earned a clear but competitive win. A powerfully distinguished synthetic use of vocal likeness from ordinary dubbing and offered a practical, low-cost opt-in for new productions. But B’s pressure question forced A to reject even demonstrably informed contractual authorization unless the voice use was separately singled out. B then showed that A’s “failure to reserve” description no longer fits a performer who knowingly signs a grant covering synthetic dubbing. A accepted possible legacy access losses without adequately identifying the added autonomy gained in that informed case. B’s scope-based defense therefore survived the central exchange more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Q1 followed by B4.3–B4.7 was decisive: knowing acceptance of a broad grant is itself affirmative authorization, so mandating a separately specified voice grant can protect form rather than understanding. A’s distribution analogy showed that the soundtracks differ commercially, but not that one broad grant cannot authorize both.
