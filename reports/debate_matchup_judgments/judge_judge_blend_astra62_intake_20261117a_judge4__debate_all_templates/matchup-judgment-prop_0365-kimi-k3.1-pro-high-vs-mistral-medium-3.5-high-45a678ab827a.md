### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): B built and sustained the decisive attack: CON's market-correction logic is circular, since it treats premium purchases as proof of satisfied demand while never counting moviegoers who gave up, waited, or stayed home. B pressed this with a sharp pressure question ("what evidence could ever demonstrate 'too few'?") and CON's answer essentially conceded the circularity ("their absence proves demand is met"), without noticing it had ceded the point. B also offered an administrable standard for "too few" (sparse/fringe-hour standard access despite demand) and distinguished "profitable" from "maximally profitable," directly rebutting CON's claim that any forgone premium revenue must cause losses or price hikes. CON's case, by contrast, repeated the same market-equilibrium assertion in nearly every speech without adapting to this challenge, and its closing simply reasserted "the market has spoken" rather than engaging the circularity charge that had been raised twice. B's clipped opening is a real execution cost, but B recovered and controlled the central clash in every subsequent round.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question Q1 ("what evidence could ever demonstrate 'too few' screenings if premium sales automatically validate the schedule?") combined with CON's self-defeating answer in Rebuttal 2 ("their absence proves demand is met") was the decisive exchange, exposing the unfalsifiable core of CON's market argument.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by decisively dismantling Side B's free-market defense. Side A correctly points out that theaters have limited peak slots and prioritize maximum revenue per seat over base profitability, completely neutralizing Side B's repeated claim that theaters would schedule standard showings if they were profitable. Side B fails to address the circularity of its market test, which Side A exploits effectively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 completely breaks Side B's core logic by distinguishing between a 'profitable' screening and a 'maximally profitable' one, explaining why theaters displace standard audiences despite existing demand.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): B won by making the decisive clash clearer: A's market-test was circular because schedules that remove usable standard options manufacture premium purchases while silent dropouts leave no queue. B kept the motion narrow (practical peak access, not public-utility quotas), answered pressure cleanly with profitable-vs-maximally-profitable, and weighed ordinary theatrical access against extraction. A repeated revenue-maximization and empty-seat logic without neutralizing that epistemic gap or showing why coexistence must cause losses/hikes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 plus circularity exposure: profitable ≠ maximally profitable; absence of standard peak shows does not prove demand is met when quiet dropouts and schedule-shaped upgrades are invisible.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): The decisive clash was arithmetic: does converting a premium showtime to a standard one actually create more affordable access, or does it merely reshuffle who attends the same finite seats? B never resolved this. B's "brutal math" (one IMAX auditorium replaces three standard screens) is a construction-economics claim misapplied to a scheduling-reallocation motion, and A called this out cleanly as a category error that B never corrected in later rounds. Worse, under direct pressure (A's Q1), B's answer ("same seats serve more people at lower prices... many premium attendees would accept standard pricing") directly contradicts B's own sold-out premise — if seats are already full, converting pricing can't simultaneously satisfy existing premium demand and newly admit excluded standard demand. A caught and named this contradiction explicitly in Rebuttal 2, and B's closing never returned to repair it, instead retreating to the cultural-moment framing without re-engaging the capacity mechanics. A also shifted the threshold question productively, showing B's own trigger ("too few") kept sliding between different empirical conditions (both sold out vs. premium empty/standard sold out), which A exploited to argue for flexible, case-by-case remedies over a blanket scheduling mandate. B's case had vivid, memorable framing (opening weekend as irreplaceable cultural moment, Barbie/Oppenheimer) and real emotional pull, but it rested on an unexamined mechanical assumption that A persistently and successfully attacked. A's opening being clipped is a real execution cost, but the core arguments survived and were reinforced through later rounds, so it only modestly tempers an otherwise clearly superior performance in rebuttal and pressure-question resolution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 response exposing the self-contradiction in B's A1 answer — that the "same seats" cannot be simultaneously sold out, serve more people, and absorb both existing premium demand and newly admitted standard demand — was the most decisive single exchange, since B's closing never repaired this logical gap.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B decisively won the debate by dismantling Side A's underlying arithmetic. Side B successfully pointed out that changing the format of a scheduled showtime does not conjure more seats, meaning forced conversion under peak demand would simply swap one set of frustrated buyers for another. Side B also effectively highlighted Side A's contradictory thresholds regarding sellouts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was highly effective at exposing the logical impossibility in Side A's claim that converting existing seats to standard pricing would simultaneously serve more people and absorb both premium and standard demand.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by making the motion easier to decide: access is seat availability and run-wide supply, not an opening-weekend entitlement, and forced conversion cannot manufacture peak capacity. PRO’s cultural-moment framing was memorable but kept resting on shaky arithmetic (same seats serve more people) and an unstable “too few” trigger, while CON consistently showed reallocation rearranges scarcity, risks revenue that supports standard runs, and is inferior to flexible scheduling. A’s clipped opening was a real cost, yet later clashes still favored A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 capacity/threshold reply: converted seats cannot simultaneously be sold out, expand affordable attendance, and absorb willing premium buyers—so PRO’s “same seats, more people” claim collapses into displacement.
