### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued seriously, but B built the more consistent and resilient case. B's recurring analogy to other poverty-motivated risky choices (mining, enlistment, trials, sterilization) forced A onto the back foot, and B's answer to pressure ("debt does not disappear when government removes this option... she keeps the debt but loses a lawful choice") directly neutralized A's paternalism framing rather than merely restating it. B also handled the awkward concession that net gain isn't guaranteed by reframing it as a falsifiable, accountable policy test, turning a potential weakness into a comparative advantage against prohibition's "guaranteed shortage." A's strongest move — reframing the net-benefit debate as "hypothetical lives vs. irreversible sacrifice" and distinguishing "procurement" from "permission" — was conceptually interesting but never fully escaped the charge of being a relabeling of disapproval of the donor's motive, which B pointed out cleanly in closing. A's case was also hurt by two clipped speeches (opening and Rebuttal 1), a real execution cost that blunted full development of its distinctions on inducement versus reimbursement. Overall B made the clash easier to resolve and landed the more memorable, structurally sound rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answer (A2) that consent requires competence and absence of external coercion, not purity of motive, combined with the closing line that banning the option "leaves the donor in debt, now with one less lawful choice" — this directly dismantled A's paternalism-based objection without conceding the consent point A was trying to win.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): PRO won a competitive debate by keeping the decisive comparison live: regulated, testable compensation versus ongoing shortage deaths, family pressure, and black-market harm. CON’s best frame—state recruitment via poverty for irreversible sacrifice—was sophisticated and well refined after pressure, but it never fully neutralized the consistency burden with other paid high-risk options or showed that “cleaner” deceased-donation reforms close the gap. Clipped CON openings also imposed a real execution cost. PRO’s withdraw-if-no-net-gain concession was flipped by CON, yet still left PRO with the cleaner, decision-ready weighing for the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1–A2.3: applying CON’s poverty-pressure test consistently to roofing, trials, and unpaid irreversible choices, forcing CON onto procurement-wrongfulness rather than invalid consent.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side A wins by building a more resilient and comparative case. A effectively neutralized B's core objection—that the state shouldn't use poverty to recruit for irreversible sacrifice—by drawing sharp, unrefuted analogies to military enlistment and hazardous labor, where the state already uses financial incentives for risks that include irreversible outcomes (death). A also made a highly effective strategic concession in Rebuttal 2, agreeing to withdraw the policy if it failed to produce a net gain in transplants, which neutralized B's empirical doubts and forced B to defend the status quo's indefinite shortages. Rhetorically, A's framing of the ban as demanding "heroism" and protecting the state's "moral comfort" at the expense of dying patients was decisive and memorable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to the pressure question on consent, arguing that consent does not require pure motives and that debt makes an option attractive without rendering the chooser incapable, effectively turning B's "state procurement" objection back onto the state's existing practices in military and hazardous labor recruitment.
