### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): B (PRO) built the stronger comparative case that mass expansion mainly produced credential inflation. A's case for hidden productivity and portable skills was real, but B sharply showed the observable labor-market mechanism: unchanged jobs now use degrees as cheap filters, and employers relax the requirement when labor is tight. A's best answer—removing the requirement is not removing the education—rescued some opportunity, but it remained more speculative and less scalable than B's tollgate and inequality story. A never fully neutralized B's charge that its gains were possible rather than predominant.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's removal test and portability rebuttal were the most decisive. By asking what changes when the degree is dropped, B distinguished queue-position credentials from capability and turned A's access examples into contests whose finish line moved, forcing A into defending uncounted within-job benefits rather than broad new opportunity.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) sustained the burden comparison the motion demands: credential inflation versus genuine opportunity, judged by predominance. B offered a concrete, repeatable test (what happens when the degree requirement is removed), forced real concessions in the pressure round, and closed with crisp weighing: "possibility is not predominance" and the moving finish line from bachelor's to master's to unpaid experience. Side A (CON) fought well — its reversal of the bank test ("removing a degree requirement is not the same as removing the education") and the poaching counterfactual against employer traineeships were the sharpest individual rebuttals of the debate. But A's positive case rested on narrow possibilities (a manufacturer here, a clinic there) and never answered B's structural claim that expansion was mostly general degrees rather than bottleneck skills, leaving A's opportunity gains looking exceptional rather than predominant. B's accessibility rebuttal — a contest she might lose versus a route she cannot afford, where differentiation migrates to purchasable extras — remained the stronger live route after the full exchange. Format compliance, grounding, and epistemic discipline were strong on both sides with no fake sourcing. A clear but competitive win for B, narrowed by A's superior late-race rebuttal work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B4.1's counterfactual test (drop the degree, train in weeks, work unchanged) against A5.1's reversal (removing the requirement is not removing the education; the test measures job entry, not a working life). B landed the frame — reversible filters reveal sorting, not capability — and crystallized it in the closing, while A's reversal blunted but did not overturn B's predominance weighing.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A set the clearest decision rule: if removing a degree requirement leaves the work unchanged and only short training is needed, expansion mainly inflated credentials; if removing it removes the work, expansion mainly created capability. A applied that test cleanly, answered pressure directly, and kept the burden on B to show that capability gains predominated. B offered strong counterexamples and a good point that education can broaden what workers do after hiring, but never showed that those gains outweighed the moving-door, rising-cost dynamic A made central.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it distinguished bottleneck credentials from reversible hiring filters, answered the traineeship question without overclaiming, and turned B’s portability point into a liability by framing universal credentials as a compulsory toll.
