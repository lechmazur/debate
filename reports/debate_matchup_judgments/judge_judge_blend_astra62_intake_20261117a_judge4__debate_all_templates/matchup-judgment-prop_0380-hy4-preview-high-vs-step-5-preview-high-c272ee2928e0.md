### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a close debate because it carried a fuller positive case across every round. That case had four parts: stake, capacity framed as "cool" political judgment rather than hot impulse control, the habit-formation mechanism, and real-world examples in Austria, Scotland, and Brazil. PRO also adapted to the pressure it received. Pushed on why the line should stop short of fourteen, PRO conceded that stake is continuous. It then rested the line on capacity plus responsibility, which was a coherent and honest narrowing rather than a dodge. CON (Side A) built a genuinely strong frame: the ballot commissions coercive power, so it should track the full adult legal package. Its A2 answer was also good, offering a concrete failure criterion (a durable class-based turnout gap among young voters). However, CON never squarely rebutted two PRO claims: - Contracts, juries, and criminal liability are shielded for risk-specific reasons, not for lack of political judgment. - Adult voters with weak information or susceptibility to misinformation are not disenfranchised. Both of CON's main capacity attacks were partly neutralized: - Its school-bias worry was blunted by the secret-ballot and "reach versus neglect" responses. - Its "complex ballot issues" point applies equally to adults. Both sides cited nonexistent reference IDs (CON's "P2.x" labels, PRO's "C1.5, C2.7"), so those execution costs roughly cancel. CON's closing claim that PRO's test has "no principled stop" also misdescribed PRO's later position. PRO's closing line, "taxation without representation", crystallized its stakes more memorably.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was the fourteen-year-old slippery slope (A3.1 answered in B4.1). PRO openly conceded that stake is continuous and moved the line onto capacity and responsibility. CON's later reply called this convergence "a preference, not a line." That charge was rhetorically sharp, but it did not engage PRO's developmental and responsibility distinctions, so PRO's line stayed standing.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): A won because it more successfully reframed the franchise as coercive jurisdiction rather than mere affectedness. PRO's strongest case was that sixteen-year-olds are governed and taxed, but A neutralized much of that by distinguishing being governed from holding the power to commission coercive instruments. PRO also conceded that stake is continuous, leaving its age line resting on a vaguer capacity claim, while A offered a coherent bright-line principle tied to full adult legal responsibility. The school-voting bias objection remained live after the exchange, and A's burden-based standard, requiring proof of equal participation rather than mere no-collapse examples, was cleaner and more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal that the ballot is not a permit but the instrument that commissions officials who tax, imprison, and make war, paired with its later demand for proof of equal participation rather than mere absence of disaster, was the most decisive exchange. PRO's secret-ballot and adult-influence responses did not fully answer the institutional amplification worry.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins a clear, competitive debate by dismantling CON's central "full legal package" standard while defending a coherent capacity-and-responsibility line at sixteen. Side A framed the ballot sharply as commissioning coercive power and pressed strong questions on the fourteen-year-old boundary and school-based influence. However, Side B answered that pressure directly—conceding continuous stake while anchoring sixteen in cold political reasoning and real-world duties, and reframing school voting as universal reach versus status-quo neglect. Meanwhile, Side A never explained what political capacity emerges between sixteen and eighteen, misdescribed Brazil as nonpresidential, and retreated to hypothetical harms against Austria's and Scotland's real-world track records.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B broke Side A's core "full legal package" defense ([A1.1], [A2.5], [A4.1]) across [B2.1] and [B5.2]-[B5.3] by distinguishing protective shields from political voice: withholding contracts and adult criminal liability shields minors from individualized risk, whereas excluding already-taxed, governed sixteen-year-olds from collective elections does not soften state coercion—it only strips it of consent.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON wins on a clear but competitive margin. Its case had a coherent spine: the vote is forward-looking authority over others, and it is tied to legal adulthood and its reciprocal package. CON adapted well across rounds. It used PRO's own concession about psychosocial immaturity, and it pointed out that "no chaos" is a weak evidentiary bar. Its best work was turning PRO's stake metric with the twelve-year-old challenge, which forced PRO to rely on a developmental line close to the one it called arbitrary. CON also had a sharp answer to the bound-by-law argument: prisoners and non-citizens are subject to law without voting, and "you obey a speed limit without setting it." PRO had real strengths. It showed that adults vote without meeting a strict reciprocity checklist. It exposed the oddity of adult criminal liability for minors. It forced CON to admit its line is a status line rather than a cognitive one. But PRO leaned heavily on a repeated "passive adult / birthday" refrain. It let the voting-habit argument fade, and it never answered the comparative-risk version of the persuadability point. CON's reply that adults possess these legal capacities even when they never use them was somewhat formal. Still, it was more fully developed than PRO's charge that adulthood is "just a label."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over CON's twelve-year-old challenge and PRO's answer in its second rebuttal. CON argued that if stake decides, younger children have more of it, so PRO must quietly rely on a maturity line. PRO replied that capacity sets the line. CON then answered that capacity which is "largely mature" at sixteen is "partly mature" at fourteen. This left PRO's developmental line looking as conventional as the eighteen line it attacked. That undercut PRO's central claim that only CON's line was arbitrary.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): A won because its reciprocal-sovereignty framework survived the main clash. A effectively converted PRO's strongest analogies—taxes, labor law, and adult criminal responsibility—into passive subjection rather than forward-looking authority, and it exposed PRO's stake-plus-capacity test as vulnerable to the twelve-year-old problem. B landed sharp attacks on the legal-adulthood line, but A answered the key pressure directly: adults need not exercise every duty because they possess the legal capacities of adulthood, while sixteen-year-olds lack them.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was A's turn on "being bound by law": obedience is passive subjection, not sovereignty, and adult criminal responsibility is backward-looking punishment rather than authority over others. This undercut PRO's core claim that taxes, work, and criminal liability already justify a vote.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a competitive, well-argued debate by building a more resilient normative framework and systematically dismantling Side B's core analogies. Side A convincingly framed voting as forward-looking sovereign authority over others that requires the reciprocal legal capacities of adulthood (contractual liability, jury eligibility, and legal independence), cleanly distinguishing de jure legal agency from whether an adult happens to pay net taxes or serve on a jury. Side A also turned Side B's own cold-versus-hot cognition distinction by showing how emotional heat and peer norms operate in political campaigns and compulsory schools, while Side B dropped its promising habit-formation argument in the second half and could not show why 16 is less of an arbitrary continuum cutoff than the existing legal boundary of adulthood at 18.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A2.2-A2.3] and [A4.3-A4.4], Side A decisively neutralized Side B's claim ([B2.1], [B3.2]) that taxes, driving, labor laws, and adult criminal trials prove 16-year-olds already bear adult legal burdens: Side A distinguished active sovereign rule from passive subjection to law (which also binds non-citizens and younger children), backward-looking criminal punishment, and labor rules designed to protect dependent minors.
