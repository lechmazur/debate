### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side B built the cleaner path to victory by centering the debate on who bears the costs of local vetoes. B's opening established a strong regional-benefits vs. concentrated-holdout frame, and B's rebuttals effectively turned A's justice argument: the veto does not protect vulnerable communities but lets organized, often wealthier localities shift harms onto less powerful neighbors. A's strongest point was that removing the veto may move delay into litigation, but B answered this directly by contrasting project-killing pre-approval vetoes with bounded post-approval review. A's closing crystallized its case well, but it leaned on the same litigation and sacrifice-zone concerns that B had already weighed against the status quo. B also handled pressure questions more decisively, specifying severe unmitigable harms that would still stop projects and explaining why post-approval litigation is less fatal than a permanent veto. A's case was competent and rhetorically sharp, but it never fully neutralized B's core weighing that the status quo imposes widespread harms on millions for the benefit of a narrow few.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal that the local veto redirects harm rather than preventing it was the most decisive exchange. It reframed A's equity argument by showing that killing regional lines does not protect vulnerable communities but instead leaves them with higher bills, fossil pollution, and blackout risk, while organized localities escape the costs.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing the structural hold-up problem of linear infrastructure and dominating the comparative weighing of harms. Side A showed that if every jurisdiction along a 500-mile corridor retains a final veto, Side B's counterproposal of deadlines and mediation still lets a single holdout kill projects outright, and persuasively distinguished bounded judicial review of clear statutory rules from an open-ended political kill switch. Crucially, Side A turned the environmental justice issue by showing that blocked lines lock in fossil-plant pollution in overburdened neighborhoods, higher bills, and blackout risks—severe harms Side B left unanswered by dismissing them as "aggregate convenience" or "developer urgency." Side B raised sharp points on regulator discretion over non-fungible harms and the Texas freeze's multiple causes, but undercut its case by blaming "fragmented, multi-jurisdictional decision-making" while defending local vetoes and incurred a minor penalty for referencing a nonexistent ID (C3.1).
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's paired rebuttals in [A2.1], [A2.3], and [A4.2]: exposing that front-end deadlines cannot speed a multi-county corridor if any single county retains an end-stage veto, distinguishing bounded post-approval court review from outright political vetoes, and turning Side B's "sacrifice zone" framing by showing that killed transmission lines perpetuate fossil-fuel air pollution in vulnerable communities.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): A wins narrowly on comparative weighing. It connected local vetoes to widely distributed costs and, under pressure, supplied a threshold: severe unmitigable harms still stop projects, while compensation covers only residual burdens. ### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): B (PRO) built the cleaner path: transmission is a regional network good, local veto creates costly delay and hidden taxes on ratepayers, and reform can preserve mitigation and judicial review while removing kill-switch power. A (CON) raised serious equity and efficacy concerns, but its key position remained slippery under pressure. B's Q2 asked whether CON would keep a categorical local veto, and A never supplied a workable limiting principle, instead disclaiming only "unqualified serial vetoes" while still defending rejection power. That let B crystallize the contradiction in closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure question on whether CON would retain categorical local veto and why it would not cause the same delay was the most decisive. A's A4.3 answer distinguished arbitrary serial vetoes without explaining how its preferred local rejection power would avoid the delay problem, which B then exploited to frame CON as unable to justify retaining destruction power.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A won solidly by pairing a clear structural case on multi-jurisdictional network holdouts with sharper institutional progression in the second half. Although Side B raised a credible political-economy critique around regulatory capture and host-community bargaining leverage, Side A neutralized both routes to victory: it showed that fragmented vetoes disproportionately empower wealthy, lawyer-heavy suburbs to push routes onto others while taxing low-income ratepayers through congestion and fossil backup, explained why eminent-domain valuation disputes are narrower than serial corridor-killing vetoes, and used Q2 to force Side B into disavowing unqualified serial vetoes while Side B largely repeated its litigation-delay assertion without adapting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 [A4.1-A4.3] combined with its Q2 fork [A3.2]: Side A dismantled Side B's core delay argument by distinguishing narrow eminent-domain valuation disputes from existential vetoes over whether a multi-state line exists at all, flipped the wealthy-suburb lobbying point against fragmented local vetoes, and forced Side B in [B4.3] to concede against unqualified serial vetoes.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): A built the clearer comparative case: fragmented vetoes impose diffuse delay costs while often empowering the best-resourced jurisdictions, whereas regional criteria, compensation, and review can protect host communities without an existential kill-switch. B raised legitimate capture and litigation risks but largely reiterated them rather than showing why local vetoes reliably protect disempowered communities or produce faster projects. A’s pressure questions exposed B’s underdefined alternative; B then disclaimed unqualified serial vetoes and introduced a late distributed-energy diversion. A’s closing somewhat overstated B as defending every categorical veto, but still offered the cleaner resolution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 directly answered both pressure questions, explaining how transparent regional review constrains biased routing and how a defined process narrows litigation from whether a line exists to valuation and mitigation. Combined with A’s earlier questions, this left B’s conditional-veto alternative without a convincing speed or fairness advantage.
