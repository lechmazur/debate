### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by separating human involvement from the protections that actually prevent harm. It showed that binding stays, reasons, audits, and human appeals can be legally enforceable without requiring a human to make every initial covered decision. Side B never established that human review is necessary or sufficient, narrowed the motion to adverse or contested decisions only after being pressured, and falsely characterized Side A's proposed legal duties as voluntary hope. Both openings were clipped, but the later clash clearly favored A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange was its answer that categorical stays can prevent irreversible harm without discretionary human approval, while systemic auditing can detect patterns that file-by-file review may miss. This directly defeated Side B's claim that every meaningful safeguard necessarily concedes the proposed right.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): B wins on the decisive comparison: direct protection vs symbolic gatekeeper. PRO's Dutch/US examples show harm from irreversible action without review, but CON shows stays, reasons, audits and contestable appeals target that harm directly, while a human signature is neither necessary nor sufficient and risks rubber-stamping and queues. B answered A’s pressure well — categorical stays need no discretionary human decision, and audit duties can be legally enforceable. A’s answers retreat to “adverse only” which concedes the motion’s overbreadth as written and still doesn’t solve rubber-stamping.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 and follow-on B4.3-B4.4: stay can be categorical, validation/audit duties replace witness-to-error, and PRO’s narrowing of “significantly affect” to “adverse or contested” plus reliance on reasoned-record review collapses the right into what CON already supports or invites intrusive litigation over deliberation.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): PRO successfully framed the debate around the timeline of harm, arguing that CON's alternative safeguards (appeals, stays) still rely on human decision-makers but only after the initial automated harm has occurred. PRO also effectively contrasted the scale of algorithmic errors (thousands of simultaneous cases) with human errors (case-by-case), and reasonably narrowed the proposition to adverse decisions to defeat CON's overbreadth claims. CON's arguments about rubber-stamping and delay were strong, but PRO's closing crystallized the clash effectively by showing that CON's architecture implicitly conceded the necessity of human judgment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 response to the rubber-stamping argument, contrasting the visible, case-by-case nature of human error with the simultaneous, opaque scale of algorithmic error, which materially damaged CON's claim that humans are just as unreliable as algorithms.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B won the central timing comparison: under Side A, meaningful review still depends on an affected person recognizing and successfully contesting an error, while Side B makes individualized consideration the state's duty before finality. Side A raised a serious backlog and scarce-attention objection, but never fully answered cases where errors look routine and vulnerable people cannot activate an appeal. Side B also supplied a plausible judicial standard for genuine consideration. Its repeated clipping narrows the margin, while Side A's closing incorrectly attributed its own answer of “nothing” to Side B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest response was that identical judicial enforcement tools do not create identical protection: an appeal duty helps only when someone activates it, whereas mandatory human review applies even when silence, detention, disability, or confusion prevents a challenge.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): PRO kept a live route: CON's human-on-trigger requires the affected person to recognize error and activate review, which fails for detained, disabled, or overwhelmed people. CON conceded in R2 A1 "Nothing does" ensures a human reviews before consequences, then claimed the problem is orthogonal, but PRO's front-end duty is automatic—state must justify with authority to depart and case-specific reasons [A4.1]. CON's strongest—identical judicial enforcement and backlog concentration—didn't neutralize that activation gap, and its variance/systemic-fix argument lost to PRO's point that consistent automation can be consistently wrong before detection. Pressure answers favored PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's handling of B's enforcement equivalence—A4.1 defines objective enforceable standard (authority to depart, consider material submissions, give reasons) and A5/R2 reframes as state obligation before finality vs citizen rescue mission, exposing B's R2 concession that missed appeals get 'nothing' under either model as inaccurate for PRO.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won the decisive clash regarding vulnerable claimants. While Side B argued that vulnerable people would struggle to engage with a front-end human just as much as an appeal process, Side A sharply clarified in the final rebuttals and closing that a mandatory front-end review forces the state to justify the decision based on the existing file, preventing silence or incapacity from resulting in automatic finality. Side B's reliance on "identical enforcement mechanisms" ignored that Side B's mechanism is conditional on the claimant successfully triggering an appeal. Side A's framing of state obligation versus citizen rescue mission was highly effective and resolved the core comparative burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing and second rebuttal distinction that front-end review evaluates the government's existing file without requiring affirmative action from the claimant, decisively neutralizing Side B's argument that vulnerable people would fail to engage with both models equally.
