  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's turn on A's concession that a contractor may use lethal force in immediate self-defence: B argued this shows contractor lethal discretion can be democratically legitimate when governed by law, so planned combat under public command and disclosure is not less accountable. That directly undercut A's core principle.

B's structural case rested on "public ownership by default" versus "diffusion by default," and A's rebuttal that defaults are created by law — a legislature can mandate a named commander, jurisdiction, disclosure, and termination for a contractor, but cannot make a classified uniformed unit visible — squarely neutralized that framing. A also answered B's "executive will audit itself" pressure with a strong symmetry argument: executive bad faith that defeats regulation equally defeats a ban, since the same executive defines "security," approves exceptions, and classifies substitutes. B's self-defence concession (guards may return fire) gave A a foothold, and B's rejoinder that planning is "the moment combat becomes a role" was neat but more assertion than argument. B's best surviving point — that a ban makes evasion itself a visible breach while regulation makes quiet combat compliance — was real but underdeveloped against A's memorable closing that a ban "preserves the force while deleting the paper trail." This was razor-thin, but A made the debate easier to decide by consistently converting B's structural claims into symmetrical burdens B could not break.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over "defaults" was decisive: B4.1 argued uniformed force carries public ownership by default while contract combat carries diffusion by default, and A4.3 countered that defaults are created by law — statutes can attach every accountability handle to a contractor, but cannot make a classified military unit visible — which B's closing ("handles law must bolt onto the chain") failed to break, since A showed every military handle is equally bolted on by law.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B wins by turning PRO's own sharp-line and enforcement arguments. If combat roles can be defined clearly enough to ban, the same definition can carry authorization, caps, public command, casualty counts, and jurisdiction. PRO's residual corporate-layer objection was reframed as a demand for state-owned labor rather than democratic accountability, and B showed that airtight controls make the trigger publicly owned. B also made the escape comparison stick: a ban drives demand toward proxies and euphemisms with fewer fingerprints, while regulated contracts leave a budget line, roster, commander, minister, and offense. A's ban-as-default point was answered by B's simpler default: no public authorization, no lawful armed contractor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's turn that a line sharp enough to ban combat is sharp enough to force visibility—and that PRO's "soldiers in corporate dress" point concedes public ownership of the trigger—was the decisive rebuttal exchange.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a close, high-quality debate on the strength of a persistent dilemma: PRO needs a legal line sharp enough to define "banned combat roles," but that same line can carry the caps, votes, tallies, and command rules CON proposes. CON's "trace comparison" — a regulated contract leaves a budget line, roster, commander, and offense, while a ban drives force toward proxies with no fingerprints — survived PRO's best counter (the ban flips the default so violations are visible without executive goodwill), which CON absorbed with the parallel default that no public authorization means no lawful armed contractor. PRO's strongest live route was the political-economy claim that governments hire contractors precisely to soften scrutiny and that executives administer regulation daily (B2.1, B4.5); CON answered honestly with non-waivable statutory conditions and by noting that the proposition's causal claim fails once the gap is removable, but the "real middle" point was never fully neutralized. CON also landed the sharpest crystallization by turning PRO's own closing phrase against it. Both sides followed format cleanly (proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2), used no fake sourcing, and used Nisour Square responsibly as background. PRO's rhetoric was punchier, but CON made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's "porous or airtight" trap (B4.5) versus CON's answer (A4.5–A4.6): CON argued that airtight controls defeat the inherent-undermining claim while porous labels under a ban are worse because they abolish the auditable channel — and then sealed it in the closing by noting PRO's concession that law sharp enough to ban combat is sharp enough to force visibility.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B better neutralized the motion’s causal claim: accountability loss is not inherent if the same combat line used for a ban can carry caps, command, counts, and non-waivable payment conditions. Side A pressed real incentives and the corporate intermediary well, but did not fully answer the substitution comparison or the symmetry of definition and regulation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal and closing: a line sharp enough to spot a “trainer” holding ground and firing is sharp enough to force authorization and visibility, while a ban pushes unmet demand toward proxies with fewer fingerprints.
