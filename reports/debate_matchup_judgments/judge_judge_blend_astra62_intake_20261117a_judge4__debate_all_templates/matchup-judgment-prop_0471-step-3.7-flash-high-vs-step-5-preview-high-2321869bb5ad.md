### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins on clearer clash management. Its opening argued that a swarm has no single point to stop. That gave it a mechanism-based framework, and it extended this framework consistently across rounds: partitions, Byzantine faults, latency, and secondary hazards. PRO's best moves were reframing the mandate as implementation-neutral and giving concrete answers to A3.1 and A3.2. Those answers handed CON two strong turns. First, the partition fallback is automatic, which undercuts the "human" element. Second, the "generic safe states" either need precomputed geospatial knowledge, which PRO's own premise in B1.1 says is impossible, or they collapse into a crude halt. PRO never answered either turn. Instead, it repeated "straw man," "trivial cost," and "zero routine cost" without engaging CON's point about continuous-readiness overhead. PRO had a real, symmetric counter available: CON's own bounded-harm envelopes also rely on anticipation. It also could have argued that humans add value for slower-developing harms such as the wildfire case. PRO only gestured at the first point in its closing and never developed it. PRO's legal-precedent claim in B1.3 was stated with more certainty than it could support. CON loses a little for citing the nonexistent IDs [P2.1/P2.2]. Its A2 answer about human slowness was also somewhat overstated, but it remained largely unrebutted. Overall, CON narrowed the debate more effectively and left PRO's mandate looking either ceremonial or harmful.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.4–A4.5: CON turned PRO's A1/A2 answers against it. The partition fallback means the law effectively mandates automatic behavior rather than human control. The "generic safe states" either rely on the pre-mapping PRO conceded cannot cover unforeseen cases, or they reduce to the stranded-on-live-tracks hazard CON identified at the outset. PRO never answered this point.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by forcing the mandated “hard human interrupt” to collapse on its own terms. Once Side B specified cryptographic commands plus automatic safe-state fallbacks, the control was either a harmful crude halt, a precomputed plan incompatible with unforeseen failure, or automatic behavior that is not human accountability. Side A answered the residual-risk pressure directly—humans are too slow and data-poor for the catastrophic window—and then weighed appearance of control against demonstrated harm bounds. Side B kept a vivid safety frame but never repaired that contradiction in the second rebuttal or closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it showed B’s partition fallback turns the human interrupt into pre-authorized automatic behavior, while “full stop” and “nearest open area” either recreate secondary hazards or require the foresight B said general-purpose swarms cannot have.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): B won by making the decisive clash about what a “hard human interrupt” could actually mean for decentralized swarms. A kept a strong normative safety case, but B forced clarification and showed that A’s clarified model either relies on an automatic safe-state fallback, making the human role ceremonial, or collapses into unsafe generic halts. B also handled pressure more directly and offered a coherent alternative regulatory frame: demonstrated containment and bounded harm rather than a mandated button.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 and closing turned A’s own A1/A2 answers against it: if partitions trigger automatic safe states, the human interrupt is not doing the claimed accountability work, and the proposed safe states recreate the secondary hazards B had warned about.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins clearly. It built a compact, coherent case: hard stops are a final-circuit safeguard, and voluntary restraint loses in competitive markets. It then won the decisive weighing that a false shutdown is a bounded harm, while an uncorrected swarm acting on a corrupted update may cause unbounded harm. That comparison was never squarely answered by CON. PRO adapted well across rounds: - It clarified that the duty is method-neutral (watchdogs, pre-authorized aborts, local trusted shutdown), which defused the "rigid standard console" framing. - It tied the "general-purpose" scope to reusable and recomposable architecture, which largely excluded CON's fixed-role warehouse example. - It answered both pressure questions directly with A1/A2 labels. PRO did have execution flaws. B2.2 is effectively empty, and PRO never fully neutralized the monoculture point that a standardized shutdown channel creates a shared exploit target. CON (Side A) had real material in the attack-surface and remote-deployment concerns. However, it repeated the same lines nearly verbatim across speeches. It kept describing PRO's mandate as a uniform standardized interface even after PRO explicitly disclaimed that. It also never engaged the bounded-versus-unbounded harm comparison. Its second pressure question was compound and leaned toward a mini-speech, and its "zero public risk" claims for remote swarms went unrebutted against PRO's point that general-purpose swarms can be recomposed for new roles.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B2.5/B4.3 response to the remote-deployment objection was the most decisive exchange. By reframing "human interrupt" as a human-set watchdog or pre-authorized termination state rather than live teleoperation, PRO removed CON's claim that the mandate makes deep-space and undersea swarms infeasible. CON's reply, that watchdogs cannot anticipate emergent tasks, cuts against CON itself, since unanticipated emergent behavior is exactly why a stop capability is needed.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B won by defending a flexible, method-neutral stoppability floor rather than a uniform console or live teleoperation, and by weighing recoverable interruption against unbounded harm from an unstoppable swarm. Side A kept a live concern about overbreadth and attack surfaces, but treated the mandate as inherently standardized and did not fully answer whether low-risk labels can be guaranteed or why after-the-fact liability is enough.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttal that the attack-surface claim proves too much, that implementations need not be one exploitable protocol, and that a false shutdown is bounded while an uncorrected swarm may not be.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO built the cleaner winning path: it framed the mandate as a flexible final safety circuit, gave a plausible definition of general-purpose swarms, and weighed bounded false shutdowns against potentially unbounded runaway cascades. It also answered pressure directly with A1/A2 and blunted the attack-surface objection by noting command layers are themselves exploitable. CON raised strong overbreadth and remote-use concerns, but overread standardization and never fully answered the recomposition risk.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s response to the attack-surface clash: even if a shutdown channel can be attacked, an authenticated ring-fenced stop is safer than an unstoppable swarm, and a false stop is bounded while a corrupted swarm may not be.
