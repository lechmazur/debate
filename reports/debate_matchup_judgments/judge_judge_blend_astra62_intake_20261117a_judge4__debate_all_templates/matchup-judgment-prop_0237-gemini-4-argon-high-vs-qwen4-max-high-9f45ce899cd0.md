### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins a competitive debate. PRO (B) had a real and persuasive incentive argument. It claimed that recent pathways drew people out of smugglers' hands only because they were paired with border ineligibility, and it pressed this consistently. CON's A1 answer, that people waited for port slots anyway, was the weakest moment of the debate. PRO's closing correctly turned it. CON adapted better across rounds, though. Its first strong move was showing that PRO's exception protects against danger in the transit country, not the home country. Its second was showing that "apply from home" and parole programs favor people with documents and sponsors. PRO's statutory-trigger answer looked like an after-the-fact invention, and CON exposed that the trigger counts visas issued rather than protection delivered. CON's decisive move was a dilemma. If regional hubs truly provide protection, the eligibility bar does no work. If they do not, the bar falls only on the residual person with no slot, no passport and no sponsor. PRO's closing restated the incentive story and never squarely answered this dilemma. CON's closing conceded the incentive point while showing it does not rescue the motion. CON also offered alternative levers: faster adjudication, more port slots, and work authorization on a clock. PRO never explained why removing eligibility was necessary instead of shortening waits. That left PRO without a clean answer to the core tradeoff.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.4–A4.6: CON took PRO's Costa Rica/Colombia hub answer at face value. If protection truly exists at the first safe border, the ban does no work, so the bar binds only the person with no slot, and for her the motion simply means the port says no. CON paired this with the point that the statutory trigger counts visas, not protection delivered to her. This reframed PRO's benefits as flowing from the pathways CON keeps, and PRO never neutralized it.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): A won the incremental-policy comparison. B's statutory trigger answered the initial claim that pathways were merely a promise, and its backlog-release rebuttal strengthened the incentive argument. But A adapted: a processing floor does not establish accessible protection for applicants excluded by the bar. B's vulnerability-triage claim and assumption that crossing a border escapes persecution did not adequately answer that safety gap. A therefore supplied the clearer reason to reject the restriction, although its claim that pathways work independently remained contested.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest rebuttal distinguished crossing a border from obtaining protection within that country. This directly undermined B's answer about applicants endangered while waiting and sharpened A's subsequent distinction between counting pathway slots and actually protecting excluded claimants.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side A built the cleaner path to victory by separating the benefits supplied by legal pathways from the additional harm imposed by the asylum bar. B answered the sequencing challenge with a statutory trigger and made a serious incentive-based case for pairing the policies, but its later speeches largely repeated claims about queue-jumping and political sustainability without resolving whether capacity-based slots actually protect people excluded from them. A directly answered both pressure questions, exposed the limits of transit-country exceptions and regional hubs, and closed with a effective arguendo comparison: even if restrictions make waiting compulsory, they still abandon the person for whom no safe, available slot exists.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: if regional hubs genuinely provide accessible protection, they deliver the claimed safety benefits; the eligibility bar additionally binds the residual applicant without a slot, sponsor, documents, or durable refuge. The point that B’s statutory trigger counts visas and slots rather than protection delivered sharply exposed the gap in B’s proposed exchange.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): This was close, and both sides were strong. Side B (PRO) had the better political-economy frame. Its line that restriction arrives by decree anyway, so only the bargain extracts lawful doors in return, was memorable and well developed. Its pressure questions were also sharp, especially the uncounted transit-death ledger and the demand for a mechanism without the trade. Side A (CON) did more damage to the motion's actual mechanism, which is why it edges the decision: - **Mischaracterized mechanism:** A correctly pointed out that a safe third country rule bars people at the border and returns them to transit states. B had recast it as Bogotá-style regional screening, and A exposed that shift. - **Exploited concessions:** A used B4.1, where B conceded unsafe states cannot receive returns. It argued that if Mexico and Guatemala are unsafe, the rule is either a fiction or cannot legally operate. - **Capacity argument undercut:** A used B4.3, where B adopted a credible-fear-style peril screening. A argued this means individual screening continues anyway, so the capacity savings largely disappear. B never re-established that the core transit states are actually safe. Its closing answered A4.4 only partially and did not address A4.3. B's closing turn, that A's executive tools exist but went unused, was clever. However, it bordered on a new argument and did not repair the safe-country premise. A's closing was somewhat repetitive but kept the decisive question in focus: what happens to people above the cap who reach the border.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3–A4.4 was the decisive exchange. A took B's own clarifications (no returns to genuinely unsafe states, and credible-fear-style peril screening) and argued that they leave the bar either legally inoperable for Mexico and Guatemala or yielding no capacity savings. B's later "safety valve, not the whole machine" reply addressed only half of this.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): A wins by focusing on people excluded from capped pathways: where they can wait safely, and why restricting their border access would reduce rather than redirect smuggling. B offered a plausible coalition argument and improved its peril exception, but never adequately connected expanded processing to safe third-country protection for those left out. A directly answered the transit-deaths pressure and supplied mechanisms for expansion without the restriction. The win remains competitive because A overstated B's concession about unsafe countries, while B effectively challenged A's claim that retaining screening eliminates all capacity savings.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's distinction between opening processing offices abroad and requiring protection in transit countries exposed the central gap in B's case. Its later response that in-country processing leaves a hunted family inside the danger zone sharpened that objection. Funding and lawful slots could help some applicants without establishing safety for those the border rule excludes.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): B built the clearer comparative case: lawful screening before a dangerous journey, statutory rather than smuggler-enforced caps, and a political coalition for expansion beat A’s comparatively rosy account of the status quo. A landed strong attacks on unsafe transit states, capped pathways, and the proposed peril exception, but sometimes overstated their force—especially by treating B’s conditional safety concession as an admission that all transit states were unsafe and by claiming the exception would save no capacity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s charge that credible-peril screening simply recreated the existing asylum process was the strongest late attack, but B effectively answered that a residual check on a fraction of arrivals is not full adjudication for everyone. B then turned A’s proposed executive alternatives against it by asking why those already-available tools had not produced expansion at the necessary scale without a bargain.
