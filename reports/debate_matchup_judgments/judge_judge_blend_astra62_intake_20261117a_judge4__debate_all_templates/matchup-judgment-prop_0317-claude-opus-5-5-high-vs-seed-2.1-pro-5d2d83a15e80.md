### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by turning Side A's admission that face value is deliberately underpriced into the central question of who should capture the gap. B answered the cap-design pressure with a receipt-plus-small-percentage formula that neutralized the strongest CON example about ordinary fans losing fees. A's later claim that it defends a modest markup gave B a decisive concession: once resale is bounded, the motion's principle is effectively accepted. A's off-platform fraud and inventory-drying concerns were plausible but less compelling because they depended on official channels failing despite the proposed verified face-value path, and A did not fully answer why broker markups beyond cost recovery serve fans better.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was PRO's pressure question asking what markup CON defends beyond cost recovery, followed by PRO's fee-inclusive statutory formula and the closing turn that CON's own "modest markup" defense concedes a capped resale principle.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) ran the cleaner path to victory. Its opening built a coherent causal story (platforms profit from every flip; identity-linked tickets make major platforms a real chokepoint), and its later speeches narrowed the dispute rather than widening it. B's A1 answer in Rebuttal 2—the receipt-plus-percentage cap formula that scales across ticket prices and automatically covers bundled fees—directly defused A's most concrete burden (sellers unable to recover costs), and the A2 answer (the law can cap channel fees; the motion does not ban smaller resale sites) blunted the entrenchment attack by noting A's fear describes the status quo. B's pressure questions exposed a genuine tension in A's case, and while A's Rebuttal 2 answer (bots buy for off-platform sale; casual sellers simply don't list) was honest and partially resolving, B's closing crystallization was decisive: the broker inventory that "dries up" was never extra supply—it was taken from the same worker at face value and sold back at triple. B also landed the reframe that A's defended "modest markup" already concedes the cap principle. A's best live route—casual-seller listing friction above and beyond cost recovery, e.g., passing tickets to friends with zero friction—was raised late and never squarely answered by B, which keeps A's case alive, but A's supporting claims (UK fraud spike, inventory drying up "entirely") were asserted with more confidence than the mechanism could carry. Both sides followed format (proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 labels). B made the debate easier to decide and won the key comparative frames.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1–B4.7: B's receipt-plus-10% formula answered A's cost-recovery objection with a concrete mechanism, then B4.5–B4.7 showed the answer eliminated A's strongest example and surfaced the bots/no-incentive contradiction, forcing A into a defensive Rebuttal 2 that conceded the dispute had shifted to cap-sizing—exactly where B's closing (B5.2–B5.4) finished the job by arguing the only seller who walks away from cost-plus-ten-percent is the flipper whose seats were taken from the worker anyway.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): PRO won by answering the central fee-recovery and implementation objections with a receipt-plus-10% cap that scales across events, undercutting CON's ordinary-seller example. - **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): CON (Side A) built the cleaner path to victory by showing the scarcity gap and profit survive a markup cap, forcing resale into unregulated channels.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.4-A4.5 turned PRO's 'flawless enforcement not needed' concession: flat capped fees remove the platform's profit motive for policing off-platform transfers, leaving imperfect enforcement paired with surviving resale profit—exactly the conditions for a black market.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a clear but competitive debate. A's core causal story—that caps don't eliminate the face-value/demand gap, only the legal place to collect it—survived every exchange. A's sharpest weapon was the contradiction press: B claimed platforms could lock out off-platform transfers entirely while also claiming platforms won't enforce rules they profit from, and B's answer (mandates align incentives and add penalties) was directly rebutted by A's observation that flat capped fees leave the platform earning nothing from policing, making imperfect enforcement exactly where black markets live. A also consistently weighed the teacher example: the cap creates no new seats, so queue-lottery rationing persists. B's best material—the incentive-realignment mechanism, the point that scam-ridden resale already thrives in the status quo, and the artist-leverage argument—was solid and well-grounded, and B answered both pressure questions directly with proper A1/A2 format. But A's follow-ups in Rebuttal 2 did more comparative work, and A's closing ("A cap doesn't kill scalping. It kills the receipt") crystallized the clash memorably while B's closing mostly restated the incentive claim without resolving the enforcement tension. Both sides were disciplined, used no fake sourcing, and followed format. A made the debate easier to decide and left more live unanswered routes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The A3.1 pressure question exposing B2.1 vs B2.3's contradiction, followed by A4.4-A4.5's killer follow-up: PRO conceded "we do not need flawless enforcement" while designing flat fees that eliminate the platform's profit motive to police off-platform transfers—imperfect enforcement plus surviving scarcity gap equals the black market PRO promised to prevent.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): CON wins the central mechanism clash: scarcity and the face-to-market price gap survive a cap, so profit moves off-platform rather than disappearing.
