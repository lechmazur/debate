### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins a competitive debate. PRO built a vivid sequencing and leverage story. Its sharpest move was arguing that CON's own toolkit, corridor restrictions and evidence-based ceilings, amounts to geofencing and capping, and that the "targeted" rules do not yet exist. That pressure was real. CON neutralized it by drawing a clean distinction between restrictions justified by a demonstrated risk and restrictions held in place only because unrelated policymaking is unfinished. CON then kept pressing a hypothetical PRO never squarely answered: a fleet that meets safety and curb requirements stays capped while councils argue about fare floors, so what danger does that cap prevent? PRO's answers were weaker than its opening: - Its exit test, "municipal legislation," was fairly exposed as a political event rather than an operational threshold. - Its attempt to make labor rules safety rules, via remote-operator limits and fare floors, felt stretched. CON showed that remote staffing can be regulated as a safety requirement on its own. CON also answered the empty-cruising question concretely: dispatch-linked repositioning and approved off-street staging. It flipped the entrenchment argument as well, noting that scarce-permit holders become a lobby of their own. PRO repeatedly cited nonexistent reference IDs ([C1.1], [C2.6], [C4.5]) and sometimes cast CON as favoring "uncapped" deployment despite CON's explicit concessions. Both cost PRO on grounding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.2/A4.7/A5.3 line was decisive. It isolated the fleet that meets safety and curb conditions but stays capped pending fare-floor legislation, and it showed that "passing an ordinance" is an exit mechanism, not a guarantee of exit. That squarely hit the motion's bundling of labor rules with safety and curb rules, and PRO's interlocking-risks reply never identified the specific danger that this continued restriction prevents.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): A won by sharpening the decisive clash: whether unfinished labor and curb policymaking should justify a blanket cap and geofence on an otherwise rule-compliant fleet. B made a strong timing and congestion case, but A answered with permit-based staging and anti-cruising rules, and exposed that B’s “municipal legislation” exit test was a political event rather than an objective operational threshold. B never fully neutralized the example of a safe, curb-compliant fleet still frozen by fare-floor disagreement. B’s repeated nonexistent C-reference IDs also weakened grounding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 was most decisive: it turned B’s exit test into a political event rather than an objective threshold, while pairing that attack with a concrete dispatch-based staging permit alternative to empty cruising.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by forcing the motion's actual decision rule: both geofences and numerical caps must remain until curb, labor, and safety rules are all clearer. Side A made a strong sequencing and entrenchment case, but never showed why a fleet already meeting enforceable safety and curb conditions must stay capped over unfinished labor policy. Side B supplied a workable interim alternative—permit limits on speculative cruising, staging, and separable safety staffing—and exposed that municipal legislation is a political event, not an objective exit test.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: it answered the empty-cruising and post-displacement questions directly, separated remote staffing from fare floors, and showed Side A's legislation exit test can indefinitely block a compliant fleet over unrelated political disagreement.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): This was a close, well-matched debate. PRO was more disciplined and epistemically careful. Its sequencing frame, "control exposure while conduct rules are incomplete," was coherent, and its closing landed a sharp point: CON's bonds, clearance deadlines and surcharges are themselves the rules the motion asks cities to establish. CON nonetheless edges it on the decisive exchanges. CON's pressure question forced PRO to admit that caps stay locked even when a fleet meets curb and safety standards, if labor rules lag. CON then pressed that admission hard as an incumbent veto that turns "until" into indefinite delay. PRO also conceded that capped fares shift some trips to human drivers. Its geofence fix, refusing trips that cross the boundary, gave CON a fresh accessibility attack. CON answered both of PRO's pressure questions concretely: suspension plus responder overrides and towing billed against operator bonds, and operator-funded per-trip surcharges. PRO's rejoinders, such as the claim that a surcharge is "a revenue stream, not an assurance," were good but did not fully neutralize the hostage-to-politics framing. CON is docked somewhat for overconfident claims about how reliably robotaxis comply.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2 pressure question and Rebuttal 2 follow-up drew out the most decisive concession of the debate. PRO had to confirm that restrictions remain even for a fleet meeting curb and safety standards if city hall stalls on labor. CON then framed that as an incumbent veto over safer streets. PRO's closing counter, that CON's safeguards are themselves the governance the motion requires, was its best reply but came only partway to repairing the damage.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins a clear but competitive debate by attacking the specific instruments—caps and geofences—on three fronts that PRO could only partially answer. First, the asymmetry point: capping only robotaxis while human taxis, ride-hail, and private cars remain uncapped undermines PRO's claim that quotas limit aggregate curb pressure. PRO conceded some fare shift back to human drivers, weakening its own aggregate-pressure argument. Second, the geofence accessibility point: PRO's answer that operators should accept only journeys they can complete effectively conceded service denial, which CON turned into a point about pushing wheelchair users and night-shift workers back onto uncapped human traffic. Third, the labor-bundling paralysis point: PRO admitted in B4.3 that caps and geofences stay locked even when a fleet meets curb and safety standards if labor rules remain unclear, which CON framed as an indefinite incumbent veto. CON's conduct-based alternative—bonds, clearance deadlines, responder overrides, and per-trip surcharges as ongoing permit conditions—was coherent and directly responsive to PRO's sequencing concern. PRO's strongest meta-point, that CON's alternatives are themselves rules needing establishment, was blunted by CON's plausible claim that these can be imposed as permit conditions from mile one without waiting for city hall consensus.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turned PRO's B4.3 admission—that caps and geofences stay locked over labor politics even when a fleet meets every curb and safety standard—into a fatal vulnerability, showing the bundled gate becomes an indefinite incumbent veto over safer streets.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B won by turning Side A's concessions into a clearer comparative case: caps on robotaxis alone shift demand back to uncapped human drivers, geofence limits deny cross-zone trips, and waiting on labor rules can freeze even curb- and safety-compliant fleets. Side A's sequencing principle was disciplined and well crystallized, but it did not neutralize those instrument costs or the veto risk as effectively as Side B's performance-based alternatives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal used Side A's admissions that some fares shift to human drivers and that caps stay locked if labor rules lag even when curb and safety standards are met, reframing the proposal as a political veto rather than demonstrated readiness.
