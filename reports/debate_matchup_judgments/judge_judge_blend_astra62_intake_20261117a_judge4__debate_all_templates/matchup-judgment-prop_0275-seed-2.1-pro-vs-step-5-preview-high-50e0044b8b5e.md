### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won the key clashes. B's strongest routes were (1) the "core house rules" definitional vagueness, (2) progressive unbundling destroyed by mandated averaging, and (3) browse-page technical infeasibility. A answered all three directly: A4.1 supplied a workable reasonable-traveler standard (mandatory fees plus financially penalized or stay-barring rules), A4.2 noted users already enter dates before browsing so dynamic totals are feasible, and A4.3 explicitly clarified hosts may keep fees separate and proportionate—only the hiding is barred—gutting B's "price averaging" line. B nonetheless kept asserting in B4.3/B5.3 that A "conceded" rate-padding, a mischaracterization after A's clarification. B's answer to A's sharpest pressure question (why hosts hide fees if trust mechanisms work) drifted into "fraud persists in every regulated market" rather than explaining the incentive, effectively conceding the search-ranking distortion A emphasized. B's browse-vs-decision-surface distinction and two-tabs point were genuinely strong and kept this competitive, but A's rebuttals narrowed and resolved the dispute more cleanly while following the A1/A2 format precisely.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3's clarification that hosts can keep unbundled, stay-length-scaled cleaning fees—they simply cannot hide them until checkout—decisively defused B's repeated "progressive unbundling / short stays subsidize long stays" attack, which B continued to run in closing despite the clarification.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A built the much cleaner and more coherent path to victory by showing that hiding mandatory fees and dealbreaker rules until checkout both wastes travelers' search time and distorts platform competition against honest hosts who display real prices upfront. Side A thoroughly neutralized Side B's reliance on existing remedies (reviews only occur after booking and staying, while fraud claims have too much friction to police routine drip pricing) and cleanly refuted the bundling straw man by pointing out that upfront disclosure lets hosts keep cleaning fees unbundled—whereas Side B's own short-stay versus long-stay subsidy math was backwards. Side B stayed competitive with a crisp UX distinction between browsing and checkout surfaces and a sharp critique of Side A's subjective materiality test for core house rules, but could not overcome Side A's point that platforms already collect dates and fee data to show dynamic totals and linked rules before checkout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's attack in [A2.2] and [A3.1] on Side B's market-remedy defense—pointing out that unbooked walks-away leave no reviews, fraud law is too cumbersome for small surprise fees, and hosts would already disclose upfront if trust alone rewarded it—exposed a collective-action flaw Side B failed to answer in [B4.1].
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A built the cleaner live route: hidden mandatory fees and material rules distort comparison and waste traveler time, while the required disclosure is a modest shift of information already shown at checkout. Side B raised real implementation and line-drawing concerns, but never fully proved that pre-checkout disclosure is unworkable or that reviews and existing law adequately replace it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s pressure question asking why checkout information would be manageable one click later but not one click earlier forced Side B into its strongest browse-versus-checkout distinction, but Side A’s answer about date-based dynamic totals and limited core-rule display blunted that concern.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Both sides executed the format well, but PRO built the cleaner path to victory. PRO's fee-schedule answer (show scaling structure before commitment, calculate exact totals once dates are entered) materially blunted CON's two core harms—inflated worst-case fees and unavoidable clutter—without abandoning the motion. PRO also landed a real contradiction: CON's own alternative depends on identifying "high-stakes" rules as searchable filters, which undercuts CON's claim that a materiality hierarchy before checkout is unworkable. CON's best surviving ground was that the pre-checkout click is not a binding commitment and casual browsers lack trip parameters, but CON conceded in B4.1 that no unique information appears at checkout entry—only "intent"—which PRO exploited effectively in the closing as a timing concession that preserves bait-and-switch headline-rate competition. CON's answers were competent and directly labeled, but the inflation and drowning arguments were increasingly answered while CON's core distinction (click ≠ commitment) was more asserted than won.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1–A4.3: PRO directly answered both pressure questions by separating fee schedules (shown pre-click) from dynamic totals (calculated on date entry) and by proposing structured fit-critical fields plus expandable full rules, dismantling CON's worst-case-fee and clutter scenarios in one move.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A won solidly by proving that comparison happens before the checkout click and systematically neutralizing both of Side B's downside scenarios. Side B competed well early on—rightly noting that checkout entry precedes payment, pressing the case of casual dateless browsers, and catching Side A's first-rebuttal attempt to narrow the motion to "material" rules. However, Side A's Pressure Questions forced Side B to admit that no new information uniquely arrives at checkout entry, and Side A's second rebuttal cleanly resolved both practical objections: platforms can display fee schedules for dateless browsers and dynamically calculate exact totals once dates and guests are entered (eliminating any incentive for inflated worst-case flat fees), and they can highlight standardized fit-critical rules alongside an expandable full rule set ("hierarchy, not delay"). Side B never answered that UI hierarchy solution, leaving Side A's core case on drip pricing, distorted headline-rate competition, and wasted search effort standing cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In Turns 5 and 7 ([A3.1]-[A4.4]), Side A both forced Side B to concede that no hidden information arrives only at checkout entry ([B4.1]) and neutralized Side B's clutter and fee-inflation arguments by explaining that the mandate governs timing rather than design—allowing fee schedules before dates are entered and prominent standardized rules paired with an expandable complete rule set ("hierarchy, not delay"), which Side B failed to refute.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): PRO built the cleaner route to victory: it treated the motion as a timing rule and offered plausible implementations—fee schedules, unbundled mandatory/optional charges, standardized fit-critical fields, and expandable full rules—thereby blunting CON’s variability, clutter, and worst-case-fee objections. CON made a strong practical case that personalized totals are easier after users enter trip details, but its own alternative effectively conceded the need for disclosure while moving it after the click the proposition targets. CON’s price-inflation and overload claims also remained less fully proven after PRO’s design answers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s second rebuttal was decisive because it answered the worst-case-fee pressure with fee schedules rather than fixed fake totals, and answered the house-rule clutter pressure with hierarchy and expandable disclosure, while pressing that CON’s filter-based alternative proved earlier disclosure was administrable.
