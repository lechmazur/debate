### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won by building a clean asymmetry and holding it through every round. The benefit of a mandate is conditional: the offender stays on the mainstream app, the content is retained, and every other evidentiary route fails. The cost is unconditional and population-wide, because the mandate creates an access path that outsiders can attack. CON (Side A) had real points. Migration has friction, careless offenders still cause grave harm, and the per-case interface with distributed custody is more nuanced than a single master key. PRO conceded that last point honestly, then reframed it decisively: custodians and logs answer "who may ask," not "who can reach the mechanism." CON's main pressure question rested on a stacked hypothetical. PRO answered it well by noting that escrow cannot read deleted or ephemeral content, and that retained content usually sits on a handset or in a backup a warrant can already reach. CON's later speeches largely restated the victim-centred framing. CON also conceded "partial coverage," and PRO turned that concession into a clean closing frame. CON never squarely answered the interface-exposure point or the claim that compromise is irreversible, so PRO's route to victory stayed live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over safeguards. CON argued that layered bank-style safeguards and a per-case interface make abuse auditable [A2.5, A4.1]. PRO answered that split keys and logs only test whether officials misbehave, not whether outsiders can reach the mechanism [B2.4, B5.2]. It added that Greece's interception system was lawful and logged yet still compromised [B2.5], and that revocation cannot un-leak a credential or remove the path itself [B5.3].
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): A wins a competitive debate through better responses to the decisive pressure. It establishes that alternative evidence and device exploitation are not guaranteed, explains when provider-held content makes exceptional access useful, and directly addresses interface security with distributed, per-case controls. B offers strong systemic-risk examples and memorable scale arguments, but insufficiently answers those controls and repeatedly assumes accessible offenders leave other usable evidence. Its closing claim that lost cases can be rebuilt contradicts the hard cases A established. A's second pressure question was misdirected, but its substantive answers and concession of partial coverage were stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.2 directly undermines B4.1: inaccessible devices do not imply that decryptable provider-held content is absent, and seizing a handset is not equivalent to reading it. B subsequently concedes partial coverage but never repairs its claim that the investigative benefits are replaceable through other evidence.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A built the much cleaner comparative case on overall public safety. Side B competed well by arguing that network effects keep opportunistic offenders on mainstream apps and that escrow helps when handsets are locked or wiped while provider ciphertext remains. However, Side A successfully narrowed B's benefit to a conditional slice of cases while showing that the security exposure is immediate, population-wide, and grounded in real lawful-interception compromises in Greece and US telecoms. Side A also dismantled B's safeguard analogies by distinguishing governance rules ("who is permitted to ask") from architectural attack surface ("who is able to reach the mechanism"), whereas Side B hurt its own pressure round with a polarity slip in Q2 [B3.2] that inadvertently framed the mandate as an unconsented weakness.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's bank-vault and evidence-room analogies in [A4.3] and [A5.2]: showing that physical evidence rooms are local, bounded, and noticed when breached while mandated access paths are remote, silent, and scalable—and that banks guard their own vault rather than holding a master key to every customer's home.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins by changing the counterfactual. PRO framed the choice as pristine encryption versus a weakened system. CON argued instead that blocking mandates does not remove bypass capability. It shifts that capability to an unaudited exploit market whose holes stay secret and unpatched. CON then pressed that point in every speech. PRO's main line was that a mandate creates a "permanent, centralized failure point." That point stayed live, but PRO restated it each round rather than developing it. PRO's "episodic, patchable exploit" response was never defended against CON's question of who tells the vendor. PRO repeated the patchable claim in its closing without answering it. CON answered both pressure questions concretely. For Q1, it named a necessary and reachable case: a seized groomer's phone with no backup, where the messages identify a child. For Q2, it described split per-device custody and honestly conceded the design was not coercion-proof, while arguing it was more auditable than the broker alternative. CON also landed a meta-argument that PRO's proposed burden of proof was effectively a veto, because no institution, including the courts PRO relies on, could meet it. PRO did offer a reasonable evidentiary standard and kept the scale and permanence argument alive. That keeps the margin competitive. CON's own rebuttal phrasing ("exceptional access is indispensable") was also confused, though it recovered well.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over whether a purchased exploit is "patchable." PRO called a bought exploit episodic, tied to one device and fixed once patched. CON repeatedly asked who would ever tell the vendor, given that the buyer depends on secrecy and the broker resells the tool to others. That turned PRO's permanence argument against PRO: CON showed both worlds hold a permanent capability, and only the mandated one is logged and auditable. PRO never answered this.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): A wins by making the marginal safety comparison more concrete and answering the key pressure directly. Its default-app, seized-device example establishes an investigative benefit that B's surviving-tools argument does not eliminate. A also explains how divided custody and per-device keys constrain compromise, while B increasingly asserts universal, irreversible failure rather than answering those constraints. B retains a credible systemic-risk argument, but weakens it by treating purchased exploits as necessarily single-device and temporary. A's own claims that exploits remain permanently unpatched and that mandates replace the exploit market are overstated, keeping this competitive rather than decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's distinction between catching a suspect and recovering evidence that identifies a victim directly defeats B's claim that ordinary offenders are already reachable. Its accompanying point that technical sophistication does not determine dangerousness also undermines B's dismissal of the offenders mandates could actually reach. B ultimately concedes real investigative gains without adequately defending why they must be narrow.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side B won solidly by reframing the counterfactual from pristine encryption to an unaccountable zero-day exploit market, specifying a constrained per-device hardware custody model, and exposing Side A's burden of proof ("resists coercion over time") as an unfalsifiable veto that would equally condemn courts, telecom carriers, and certificate authorities. Although Side B stumbled in Turn 4 by briefly misstating who needed to prove exceptional access is indispensable, it recovered decisively in Turn 8 by showing how unlocking a seized, unbacked-up phone is necessary to identify unlocated child victims even after metadata catches the suspect—a gain Side A conceded in closing. Meanwhile, Side A never explained how secret broker exploits get patched when buyers never notify vendors, mistakenly equated technical unsophistication with low danger, and misread Side B's "coercion-invisible" point in closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Turn 8 sequence ([B4.2]-[B4.4]), which candidly conceded that split-custody hardware access is not coercion-proof, contrasted it with Side A's "coercion-invisible" world of unlogged broker exploits, and showed that Side A's test requiring a custodian to "resist coercion over time" would disqualify the very courts and carriers Side A's own investigative alternatives rely on.
