### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B gave the stronger path to victory by turning Side A's own "surge capacity" and "quieter consent" language into evidence of political insulation, and by showing that the proposed regulation mostly delivers after-the-fact punishment rather than real-time command and consent. Side A's counter was sharp, especially the claim that compulsion is not the same as democratic legitimacy, but it rested on post-hoc accountability and publication that B plausibly framed as belated cleanup rather than control before and during the fight.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's use of A's admission that contractors are bought when citizen armies cannot expand overnight turned surge capacity into a democratic-consent bypass, and its pressure-question follow-up pinned A into admitting commanders cannot court-martial contractors for quitting mid-combat; A's reframe was memorable but did not fully restore real-time command or felt public cost.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Both sides were disciplined and well-grounded, but Side A (CON) did the more decisive reframing work on the live clashes. On the under-fire compulsion exchange, A honestly conceded commanders cannot imprison quitting contractors, then landed the sharp reductio that "a force that cannot quit is not thereby accountable," exposing that B's battlefield metric conflated command retention with democratic accountability. On the home-front clash, A's armor/all-volunteer-force analogy showed B's casualty-friction metric proves too much, and A paired it with publication of contractor deaths and costs, blunting the "quieter consent" attack that B had pressed hard. B's best surviving material was the enforceability comparison — a pre-deployment bright-line ban is easier to police than mid-ambush regulation — and the strong rhetorical frame of renting the trigger while calling the invoice accountability. But B's two core routes (severed chain of command, numbed electorate) were each partially neutralized by A's reframes, while A's central route — accountability flowing through authorization, appropriations, jurisdiction, and elections — was never squarely broken. Slight edge to A on argument and rebuttal quality; near-even on rhetoric and grounding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The B3.1 pressure question about court-martialing contractors who quit under fire was the pivotal exchange. A's A4.1/A4.2 answer — that legitimacy tracks who authorizes violence, not who can be forced to die — plus the "armor and all-volunteer force" reductio in A4.4 turned B's strongest concession-fishing into a reframing win that B's closing only partially answered.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by turning Side A's admissions into the decisive clash: "quieter consent" and the inability to compel contractors under fire show force escaping consent before and command during combat. Side B also answered the convoy objection with a foreseeable-combat distinction and embraced scaling back missions leaders cannot staff publicly. Side A's regulation, publication, and armor arguments were credible, but ultimately reframed rather than eliminated those accountability gaps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it distinguished emergency self-defense from foreseeable armed deployment, identified quitting under fire and off-roll casualties as failures regulation does not cure, and reframed mission shrinkage as democratic accountability working rather than a defect.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a tight but clear debate by turning Side B's core accountability standard against it. B's case increasingly rested on the claim that contractors cannot make the dead "ours," but A showed B still endorses foreign allies and local proxies, whose dead also do not vote locally. A also effectively exploited B's support-contract exception: even with B's later purpose-based distinction, the combat/support line can be relabeled, whereas A's alternative of subjecting all armed contracts to parliamentary votes, casualty rolls, courts-martial, and criminal liability more fully closes the loophole. B's stake argument was rhetorically crisp, but A gave the stronger comparative reasons that a ban would push force into less accountable covert channels and harm transparent self-defense.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal combining the foreign-allies inconsistency with the relabeling loophole was decisive: it forced B to concede that procedural accountability could exist on paper, then undercut the remaining "dead ours" test and showed B's combat/support distinction still permitted disguised lethal force.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by systematically dismantling Side B's accountability framework. B's opening rested on three pillars—authorization, visibility, responsibility—but was forced in B4.3 to concede that regulation can replicate authorization and responsibility "on paper," reducing the entire PRO case to the residual "the dead must be ours" stake claim. A then showed that this purity test proves too much: foreign allies' dead also "do not vote locally" (A4.4, A5.2), yet B endorses alliances as public political choices—while a parliament-voted, court-martialed contract would satisfy the very same standard. B's replies here ("a public vote on the invoice does not return the stake") were assertion rather than argument. A also landed a structural contradiction: B dismisses regulation because leaders evade law, yet B's own combat/support carve-out (B4.1) depends on labels executives can rename—A drove this symmetry home in A2.1, A4.1, and A5.3, and B never defused it. B's best material—the "respectable middle door" reframe, the memorable "accountability is a stake, not a file" crystallization, and disciplined A1/A2 answers—kept the debate close, and B's rhetorical craft was superior. But on the live clash that decided the motion as written, A neutralized more of B's case than B neutralized of A's. Both sides followed format cleanly and grounded claims in plausible mechanisms without fake sourcing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal (A4.1) was decisive: it answered B's pressure question by explaining the loopholes as fixable statutory gaps, then turned B's own combat/support distinction (B4.1) into a reclassification loophole that B3.1 feared and B could never close—combined with the allies/foreign-hires parallel showing B's "dead ours" test indicts cases B accepts.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by showing regulation can supply votes, casualty reporting, and criminal liability, while a ban would shift force toward less auditable proxies and block openly authorized defense by an invaded democracy. Side B's remaining 'dead must be ours' stake is powerful but is undercut by its acceptance of foreign allies and by conceding authorization and responsibility can be replicated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A Rebuttal 2 and closing on B's allies inconsistency: if a public vote makes allied force accountable despite foreign deaths, a publicly voted, court-martialed contract can likewise be a public political choice, while B's support/combat line invites the relabeling B condemns.
