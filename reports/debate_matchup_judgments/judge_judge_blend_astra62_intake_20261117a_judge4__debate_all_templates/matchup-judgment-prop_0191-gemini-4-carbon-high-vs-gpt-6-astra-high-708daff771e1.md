### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): CON won by drawing a precise distinction early and holding it to the end. Adequate subgroup evidence is one thing; a population-matching ratio used as an approval veto is another. PRO's strongest live route was post-market leverage: off-label prescribing, stalled follow-up studies, and regulators' reluctance to withdraw drugs. PRO pressed this well, and it forced CON to concede that revocation is unlikely. CON blunted it with escalating penalties, withholding broader indications, and a sharp point: if pulling a drug from current patients is unacceptable, withholding the same benefit from waiting patients is harder to justify. CON's pressure questions then did the decisive work. PRO honestly but damagingly answered "Yes, approval still waits," even when reliable evidence exists for every intended group. PRO also offered no real stopping rule. Its claim that the wait is "bounded" because patients are not scarce conflated disease prevalence with trial availability, and CON exposed this. PRO's two claimed safeguards were also undercut. Proportions do not guarantee frail seniors are enrolled, and rare-toxicity detection depends on sample size rather than population share. PRO's case was well grounded, citing the sleep-aid dosing example and the frailty problem in cancer trials. But PRO never established that the ratio itself adds protection beyond a sufficiency standard.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 [B4.5] was the most decisive exchange. It seized on PRO's A2 concession that approval would be blocked even with reliable evidence for every intended group. It pointed out that PRO had shifted from "reliable" evidence to evidence that merely "looks promising." It then showed that sample size, not population share, drives rare-toxicity detection, and that age quotas can still be filled with healthy seniors. Together, these points stripped away PRO's claimed clinical justification for an unwaivable ratio.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B made the decisive distinction between adequate subgroup evidence and a demographic-ratio veto. A offered a substantial case about unnoticed harms, off-label prescribing, and preapproval leverage, but answered the crucial hypothetical by conceding that approval should wait even with reliable evidence for every intended group, then substituted “looks promising” for “reliable.” A also failed to establish a genuine time limit or that proportional recruitment would necessarily include clinically representative seniors. B directly answered both pressure questions and consistently weighed certain treatment delay against unproven additional protection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it exposed A’s reliable-evidence evasion, explained why rarity detection and frailty are not guaranteed by population shares, and showed that “until proportions match” is a completion condition rather than a bounded delay.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by separating proportionate ratios from sufficient subgroup evidence and forcing A to concede approval would still be blocked even with reliable evidence for every group. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Both sides were competent and engaged directly. PRO built a careful epistemic case: representative enrollment shapes the pooled benefit-harm estimate, approval rules must be set before outcomes are known, and registries cannot recreate randomization. That case was well grounded and handled pressure honestly, including the frank A2 concession that there would be no exceptions even for urgent therapies. CON, however, turned PRO's own concessions into a sharper comparative frame. PRO admitted that subgroup results are often inconclusive, that minority samples rarely establish separate dosing, and that pooled-estimate shifts matter mainly when outcomes are "closely balanced." CON paired these with the no-exception admission to argue that the rule guarantees non-treatment for dying patients in exchange for marginal informational gains. CON also offered a credible middle path: bar arbitrary exclusions, run targeted pharmacology studies, fine sponsors who miss recruitment commitments, and require structured post-approval study. PRO's best counter was that statistical power within an unrepresentative trial does not license extrapolation. That point was strong but did not fully outweigh CON's asymmetry framing. CON lost some credit for assertive claims about how registries match patients, and some of its quota language overstated the motion. Overall, CON made the decisive tradeoff easier to grasp, so it wins narrowly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and closing combined two PRO admissions: that pooled-estimate shifts matter mainly when "closely balanced," and that no exception exists for urgent life-extending drugs. Together these framed PRO as accepting certain deaths for marginal informational gains. PRO's closing response, that a powered but unrepresentative trial still relies on extrapolation, was strong but did not fully neutralize this frame.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate. A’s strongest argument—that representative randomized outcomes can improve the overall benefit–harm estimate and prescribing decisions without powering separate subgroup analyses—remained substantive and was presented with admirable caution. But B better attacked the motion’s rigid, simultaneous proportionality requirement: small cohorts may yield only marginal pooled changes, while targeted pharmacology and sensible protocol design address many underlying risks without imposing the same bottleneck. A’s explicit refusal of exceptions for urgent, life-extending therapies gave B a powerful weighing advantage. B’s alternatives were not perfect substitutes for randomization, but its flexible approach ultimately addressed the tradeoff more convincingly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second pressure question forced A to defend approval delays without exceptions. B’s second rebuttal then converted that answer into the decisive comparison: certain loss of access during delay versus evidence gains A had narrowed mainly to closely balanced cases. A’s prospective-uncertainty answer kept the exchange competitive but did not defuse the rigidity objection.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won because PRO's pooled-estimate defense narrowed the benefit to inconclusive subgroup signals and marginal shifts in closely balanced cases, while PRO conceded no exceptions even for life-extending therapies. B paired that concession with plausible alternatives—targeted pharmacology, exclusion bans, labels, fines, and post-approval studies—making guaranteed delay look disproportionate to the quota's delivered certainty.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal was decisive: it turned PRO's 'closely balanced' admission and refusal of exceptions into a clear weighing loss, arguing a small underpowered cohort neither overturns clear breakthroughs nor reliably guides subgroup prescribing.
