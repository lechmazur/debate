### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) carried the motion's burden more cleanly. B's opening fixed the core economic paradox (stewardship undermines commercial prospects) and its rebuttals did real damage: the "pays twice" accounting rebuttal forced a direct concession from A, the rolling-stock answer blunted the expiry attack, and the option-structure comparison (contingent set-aside spend vs a subscription fee paid in full) was the debate's sharpest economic point. B's strongest unanswerable route was that prohibition does not deliver the preferred alternative: a buyer can require a bid form but cannot make an affordable delinked bid materialize, and a veto on high-price AMCs may simply mean no drug is developed. A's counter — that permission crowds out delinked bids — was asserted rather than grounded and was squarely met in B's closing. A's best attack (the surge year punishes the buyer while the firm keeps the upside) was answered by B's set-aside mechanism and B's concession that buyers should reject bids with intolerable surge risk, converting A's point into a reason for careful contracting rather than prohibition. A also incurred a real execution cost when Rebuttal 1 was clipped for exceeding the word limit. Both sides showed excellent epistemic discipline and memorable, epigrammatic prose, but B made the debate easier to decide and left fewer burdens unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange ran from B2.1 through B4.2-B4.3 to B5.1: B dismantled A's "public pays twice" claim with simple arithmetic, then answered the surge-invoice attack by showing the guarantee's contingent structure — money set aside for courses eleven through thirty is spent only if patients arrive, whereas a subscription fee is paid in full — and closed by noting that never-promised money is not proof of thrift but proof of an option correctly priced. A's reply that the set-aside "was the delinked sum all along" was clever but conceded the surge protection to the subscription in bad years without showing prohibition would actually produce that bid.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): PRO won the permission framing: permit does not require choosing an AMC, so countries can take a cheaper subscription when offered while retaining the AMC when no affordable delinked bid exists or surge provision makes it cheaper overall. CON's surge-price and crowding-out arguments were serious, but PRO answered them with a priced set-aside comparison and the closing point that forbidding the commitment does not create a drug or a subscription to buy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: the difference between a subscription fee and the guarantee could fund twenty extra high-price courses centrally, making the choice a priced surge-risk comparison rather than a ban, and exposing CON's assumption that a preferred bid will materialize.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): A wins because it treated the motion as permission rather than mandate and built a cleaner decision rule: compare high-unit-price AMCs against subscriptions, cap exposure, budget for surge, and reject bad bids. Its answer that a country may be able to afford a guarantee plus surge funding but not an unaffordable subscription remained live after pressure. B offered strong surge-pricing and stewardship critiques, but did not prove that forbidding such AMCs reliably produces better delinked alternatives, and its clipped first rebuttal was an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 answers to B’s subscription pressure: A conceded that high unit prices do not themselves protect surge patients, then compared a guarantee plus centrally reserved surge funds with a subscription, preserving the core optionality case.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won the decisive clashes. A's opening offered a coherent alternative (fixed availability payment plus affordable course pricing) and A's pressure forced two real concessions from B: B abandoned its opening price-based stewardship claim (shifting to clinical rules and automatic coverage, exactly A's framework), and B's clawback answer admitted the "agreed net" need not fall to supply cost, leaving A's dilemma intact — either the refund makes the effective price affordable (satisfying A's condition) or the developer's return still grows with each outbreak case, contradicting B's claim to have flattened the volume incentive. B's best material — the tender-leakage asymmetry and the empty-shelf/signability frame — was real but only defended the invoice label, not the net price, and A4.4/A4.5 persuasively reframed "the only form a developer will sign" as an endogenous bargaining posture. B's closing move ("a high invoice is a high unit price, that concession decides the motion") was sharp, but A5.1's paperwork-equivalence rebuttal was better reasoned about which reading of "unit price" the motion actually turns on. B was more quotable; A made the debate easier to decide and left more of its case standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A4.3 "follow the money on the first course above the floor": B's A1 answer conceded the clawback need not strip price to supply cost, and A immediately converted that into a dilemma — either the net is affordable (B converges to A's condition) or the discovery reward still grows with the outbreak — which B never cleanly resolved.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): CON showed the same discovery reward could be paid through a binding guarantee without a per-patient premium, and trapped PRO's clawback: if it removes the premium the effective price is not high, if it does not the reward grows with outbreaks. CON also answered the empty-shelf pressure with ex ante bargaining, while PRO's closing relied on treating a high invoice alone as conceding the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's coverage dilemma in Rebuttal 1 and Closing: a price the hospital never feels cannot steward, a price it does feel deters justified care, and a later clawback does not change the effective unit cost unless it eliminates the disputed premium.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by resolving the central price definition: if refunds or clawbacks make courses affordable, the effective unit price is not high, so A’s high-invoice concession becomes paperwork; if they do not, A leaves a per-patient outbreak premium and access or budget harms. B’s fixed-payment, affordable-supply model answered the incentive problem and neutralized A’s reference-pricing worry by allowing a high published figure while keeping the net price affordable. A was polished and pressed the empty-shelf cost, but never fully answered the outbreak-premium dilemma or proved that high net prices were necessary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure question and Rebuttal 2 on the clawback: A admitted the clawback stops above an agreed net, and B turned that into the decisive dilemma—either the effective price is affordable, undercutting the motion, or the firm keeps a premium per patient, rewarding outbreaks.
