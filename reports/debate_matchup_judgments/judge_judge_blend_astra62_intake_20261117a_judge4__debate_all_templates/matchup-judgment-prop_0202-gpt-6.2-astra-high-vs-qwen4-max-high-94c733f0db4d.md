### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): This was a close, high-quality debate, and PRO edges it. CON's strengths: - It built the more memorable structure. The "dial" framing held that safety-oriented opt-in restores the two-person sale, while loose defaults expose receivers to risk they never chose. - It added a sharp receiver-side phishing point: once a service is whitelisted, a compromised account there arrives as a trusted contact. - It pressed hard on suspension risk to seed-stage entrants. Why PRO still wins: - **The A1 answer held.** PRO showed that the dial is not symmetric: two people must agree to talk, but only one must become a customer. Accepting messages without installing an app or opening another inbox is categorically cheaper than migrating. - **CON conceded the key gain.** CON's "cheaper than migration, yes" admitted a real contestability improvement, which PRO converted cleanly in its closing. - **The moat argument was answered.** PRO's reply on daily use (the incumbent must earn it rather than receive it as a dividend on loyalty) blunted the claim that the moat never crosses the bridge. Weighing and execution: - PRO's security concessions were honest and bounded. It admitted added risk for those who opt in, while declining keeps that service's content out entirely. - CON's irreversibility weighing was strong but never quantified the residual exposure. It also did not explain why that exposure differs from compromised accounts within any single network. - PRO was more epistemically disciplined and adapted better across rounds. - CON's rhetoric and pressure questions were sharper, which keeps the margin thin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 ("CON counts the people involved and ignores what each is being asked to do") reframed the opt-in-as-two-person-sale attack. It forced CON to concede "cheaper than migration," which PRO then used as the deciding admission on contestability.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B wins because it exposed the central trade-off A needed to dissolve. A's safe version requires opt-ins and certified encryption, but B showed that opt-in reprices rather than removes the two-person barrier and leaves incumbent-mediated friction, while certification/suspension creates real risk for entrants. A's cheaper-than-migration point was genuine, but B made the comparative case more persuasive by emphasizing irreversible security loss and degraded features against conditional, contact-by-contact contestability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R2 answer turned A's own concession into a dilemma: if the recipient opts in, security exposure returns; if she does not, the contestability gain shrinks. That same-dial argument undercut A's attempt to claim both real entry and meaningful safety.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): A wins narrowly because it establishes a workable middle setting: an opt-in to a named service is far cheaper than migration, published encryption requirements differ from an incumbent success-based veto, and losses are bounded to accepted cross-service conversations. B's security dial and receiver-risk arguments remain strong, but B concedes entry becomes cheaper while not showing why that daily-use contestability is outweighed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 distinction that two people must agree to talk but only one must become a customer, paired with the comparison between a degraded cross-service chat and an otherwise empty inbox.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins narrowly because its structural case survives the exchange. Network effects block entry, and interoperability lowers that barrier. CON's own concessions confirm that the barrier really does fall. Under pressure, CON admitted three things: a bridge is better than severance for a sister who leaves; individual features like search and accessibility can win users and discipline the incumbent; and no feature is intrinsically unencodable. That leaves a real contestability gain on the table. CON's counterweight is a precise, well-built caregiver scenario. It shows recurring coordination costs: membership confirmation, patch timing, and suspensions that affect people who never switched. It also argues the gain is mostly limited to individual client choice rather than shared group innovation. This is sharp and disciplined analysis. However, it rests on one illustrative edge case whose magnitude is never established against PRO's market-wide entry argument. PRO's weighing is cleaner and more memorable for a reader. It sets permanent, unmeasured foreclosure against owned, fixable, bounded failures. PRO does overclaim in closing by implying a mandate obliges the incumbent to implement rival features, and CON correctly exposes that. Still, PRO's core comparison remains the easier path to a decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.1–A2.2 is the sharpest single exchange. It answers PRO's claim that the gatekeeper holds the pen by noting that "writing the rule is not shipping the fix," and argues that federated feasibility does not mean equal recovery time. This kept CON's reliability harm alive. PRO's strongest counter-pressure was its Q1, which contrasted a bridge that suspends with a world of permanent severance. That forced CON to concede the bridge is a gain for users who leave.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): A wins narrowly because it produced the cleaner comparative ledger: a permanent structural opening to contestability against visible, owner-assigned, recoverable bridge failures. B's care-coordination harms were vivid and real, but A successfully turned the deepest exchange—B's concession that no feature is intrinsically unencodable, only "who must act"—into the very point about mandating compliance over incumbent veto. A also answered B's strongest pressure better by exposing "keep both apps" as the moat politely described, making the counterfactual permanent severance rather than mild inconvenience. B retained a live route on recurring cross-provider reliability and limited shared innovation, but it did not outweigh A's systemic compounding benefit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was A's Rebuttal 2 answer to B's pressure questions: reframing "keep both apps" as the incumbent moat and shifting differentiation to the client via the email pattern. That neutralized B's baseline challenge and let A turn B's later handoff concession into a contestability argument.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B's shallow-versus-deep dilemma survived the exchange. Side A showed client-level choice and multi-homing, but B showed shared group innovation still requires incumbent implementation or coordinated migration, while deep interoperability creates recurring membership, patching, and reliability costs even for non-switchers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: an interface can carry a caregiver-acceptance event, but cannot make an incumbent client lacking that feature display or act on it, separating individual client gains from the group contestability at the heart of Side A's case.
