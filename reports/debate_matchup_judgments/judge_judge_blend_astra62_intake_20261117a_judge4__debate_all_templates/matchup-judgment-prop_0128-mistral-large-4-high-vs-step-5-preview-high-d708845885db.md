### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly with strong format compliance and no fake sourcing. CON's best live route was the "relocated gatekeeping" argument (dropping degrees shifts screening to polish, networks, portfolio time) plus the epistemic humility point that degree value is preventive and hard to prove ex ante. PRO blunted the gatekeeping worry by showing the degree already embeds tuition access and legacy networks, and the Q2 slippery-slope pressure forced CON into a bounded-line defense (A4.3) that was adequate but thin. PRO answered both pressure questions directly and crisply in B4, framed the decisive weighing question ("who must justify the gate") more memorably, and kept the burden comparison sharp in the closing. CON's A4.1 clarification of the alleged contradiction was sharp, but PRO's overall comparative work across rebuttals and closing gave a narrow but real edge. Razor-thin but real: PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B3.2/B4.3: PRO's slippery-slope pressure that "invisible preventive value" would justify degrees for every job, answered by CON's A4.3 bounding claim (degrees earn cost only in uncertain, tacit, failure-expensive roles). PRO then pressed in B4.4 that CON defends a screen it concedes cannot be validated ex ante—the sharpest and most consequential exchange of the debate.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): A narrowly won the distinction between a degree being indispensable and being useful for screening. B established substantial exclusion costs and offered concrete alternatives, but repeatedly equated “not necessary” with “adds no value,” leaving A’s longer-term prediction argument insufficiently answered. A also adapted more effectively under pressure. Neither side established its comparative predictions especially firmly, and B’s sweeping claims about employers’ reported outcomes weakened its grounding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A directly answered the “degrees for every job” challenge by distinguishing narrow, observable tasks from uncertain, coordination-heavy work. That bounded its signaling argument rather than conceding unlimited requirements. B’s closing repeated the universal-overreach attack without addressing this limitation, forfeiting an important opportunity to challenge whether those benefits actually justify exclusion.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B wins a competitive, high-level debate by out-executing Side A in the middle and late rounds on how hiring screens actually function under uncertainty. While Side A built a compelling case around the exclusionary cost of degree gates for two-thirds of adults and rightly corrected Side B's early "blanket ban" framing, Side B dismantled Side A's operational standard in three ways: exposing the circularity of Side A's "inspectable necessity" line on gray-zone roles like care coordination and logistics, showing that short-horizon work samples cannot capture multi-year preventive benefits like lower turnover and supervision costs, and arguing that stripping a portable credential shifts ordinary-pool screening toward opaque proxies like coaching, polish, and unpaid portfolio time. Crucially, Side B directly answered Side A's toughest pressure question in [B4.3] by bounding degree screens to uncertain, coordination-heavy, or failure-expensive work, whereas Side A largely ignored both that boundary and the ordinary-pool portability critique in Closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A2.2], Side A tried to neutralize Side B's care-coordination example by sweeping it into the motion's carve-out alongside licensed clinical practice while keeping logistics and IT support in play; Side B's reply in [B2.1]-[B2.2] (and follow-through in [B4.6]) sharply exposed that care coordination requires no clinical license yet turns routine checklists into high-stakes exceptions, proving Side A was redrawing its line after the fact and relying on a circular test of necessity.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Both sides ran clean, well-grounded cases with full format compliance, but PRO (B) controlled the decisive clash. PRO's burden frame — a gate must justify itself against direct evidence, not the excluded candidate — survived CON's "prove a negative" reversal because B4.1 correctly noted employers already validate licenses and safety credentials this way. PRO's dilemma in B4.6 (if direct evidence works the diploma is redundant; if it cannot, the gate rests on speculation) was never broken: CON's answer rested on hypothetical "validation showing incremental value" it could only assert, and stretched "performance" to promotion readiness. CON's best live route — probation is a post-hire bet and entry-level high-volume roles lack work samples or experience — was real but underdeveloped relative to PRO's concrete cheap alternatives (structured interviews, work samples, references, probation) and the vacancy-cost comparison. CON's repeated "blanket stop" framing also sat awkwardly against a conditional motion. PRO's market-externality explanation for persistent degree inflation was the debate's most original causal move and CON's rebuttal was largely assertion. CON's opacity-of-replacements fairness point was PRO's weakest answered area, but B5 mostly crystallized rather than resolved it. Rhetorically, PRO's lines ("attendance and debt," "proposition in a lab coat," "the gate is the harm") landed harder than CON's precise but drier prose.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.6's dilemma — "If direct evidence works, the diploma adds nothing; if direct evidence cannot work, the gate rests on speculation" — directly answered CON's core "distribution of future behavior" defense by showing the diploma fails that same standard, and CON's closing never restored an affirmative incremental link, only a conditional one.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): B better justified requiring employers to defend a categorical gate that excludes otherwise capable applicants. A offered serious objections about longitudinal reliability, assessment costs, and probation being post-hire, but potential predictive usefulness did not adequately establish why degree completion should remain mandatory. Its repeated characterization of the qualified motion as a blanket ban weakened its comparison. B supplied concrete alternatives and clearer exclusion harms, although its assertions that those alternatives are cheaper and more predictive were under-supported. Those remaining objections kept the result competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B directly answered A’s demand to rule out every hypothetical future failure: the relevant burden is affirmative justification for the requirement, not proof that removing it creates zero risk. This exposed an excessive burden in A’s pressure question and sharpened the distinction between defending a useful signal and defending a mandatory gate.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A wins a clear but competitive debate by defending the proposition's conditional rule cleanly and dismantling Side B's defenses of degree proxies. Side B smartly framed hiring as predicting a multi-year distribution of behavior rather than one-off task certification and landed a real hit in [B4.1] that probation is a costly post-hire bet rather than a pre-hire screen for entry-level applicants. However, Side B repeatedly mischaracterized a conditional motion as a "blanket stop," tripped over its own "prove a negative" framing in [B4.2], and created an internal tension between claiming formal validation is too expensive and market discipline already works, yet retreating in [B4.3] to keeping degrees only where company validation proves incremental value once skills and experience are controlled. Side A decisively won the degree-inflation clash, offered scalable pre-hire tools cheaper than prolonged vacancies, and showed Side B never justified hard degree gates that reject demonstrably capable workers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A2.5], Side A refuted Side B's [B1.5] market-discipline defense by showing that persistent degree inflation—despite longer vacancies and wage premiums—is the empirical counterexample, explaining that rivalry failed to self-correct because excluded workers bear the cost outside the hiring funnel. Side B defended existing gates as rational in [B2.4] before retreating in [B4.3] to dropping unvalidated requirements.
