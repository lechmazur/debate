### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO wins on a clearer causal story for "representative outcomes." It argued that voluntary turnout is patterned by social circumstance, and that compulsion changes campaign incentives because parties must now court groups they could previously ignore. The key move came in Rebuttal 2. Under a secret ballot, parties cannot tell who will cast a blank or protest vote. That directly neutralized CON's "coerced ballots carry no preference" objection. CON's closing simply reasserted that parties "can count her while ignoring her" and never engaged the secret-ballot point. PRO also answered the enforcement pressure concretely: automatic excuses, cure periods, no fine owed if access fails, and no criminal sanction. That blunted CON's regressivity argument, leaving only the thinner claim that even mild procedures will snag vulnerable people. CON had some good material: - Its access dilemma ("if access works, compulsion is unnecessary; if it fails, fining is perverse") was its strongest line. - Its freedom-versus-expertise answer on low-information voting was decent. But CON's own A1 effectively conceded that residual patterned absence would remain after access reforms. Its remedy there, "persuasion and respect for refusal," left PRO's core inequality claim standing. CON's consent framing was rhetorically clean but did not dislodge PRO's mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive exchange. CON's pressure question asked why parties would respond to coerced blank or random ballots. PRO answered that incentives operate before votes are known: secret ballots prevent parties from identifying protest voters, so once-unreliable groups must be courted as a whole. CON never squarely rebutted this and fell back on restating that coerced marks carry no preference.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B better defended the motion's criterion: representative outcomes require an electorate not pre-sorted by time, health, and alienation. Blank ballots and secret voting preserved refusal, while the incentive claim survived Side A's "no preference" objection because parties cannot identify who will blank and must still court previously ignored groups. Side A's access-or-respect dichotomy never supplied a remedy for residual patterned absence, and its enforcement critique was substantially blunted by automatic excuses and no fine when the state fails to provide access. Side A was clearer and more memorable on consent, but that reframe did not outweigh B's cleaner comparative path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2: secret ballots force parties to compete for irregular voters they cannot sort into blank versus preference ballots, and broad automatic excuses plus no fine if access fails closed the regressivity trap without conceding the representation gap.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): PRO built the cleaner route on the motion's own criterion: optional turnout predictably filters the electorate by time, health, income and alienation, so policy follows the reliably available. CON's presence-is-not-representation and liberty claims were forceful, but PRO answered that blank/secret ballots preserve refusal while universal participation changes parties' ex ante incentives to court previously ignorable groups. CON's remedy for residual patterned absence remained more access and persuasion, leaving PRO's central inequality unneutralized, while PRO's cure periods, broad excuses, hardship relief and no fine when the state fails access substantially blunted the regressive-penalty attack.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 A1 was decisive: secret ballots mean parties cannot identify blank voters in advance, so knowing irregular voters will participate forces campaigns to compete for them rather than price their absence into policy.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): PRO had the more intuitive core mechanism: near-universal turnout should push parties to court the whole public rather than only likely voters. But PRO defended it mainly through blank and spoiled ballots, saying a ballot that registers "I am here" makes citizens countable. That left the mechanism exposed. PRO never pressed the stronger point that most compelled voters actually choose a party. CON exploited the gap repeatedly, arguing that a blank or resentful ballot gives no governing judgment and remains discountable. Its tenant example made the point concrete: a spoiled ballot gives no more say over housing policy than staying home. On penalties, PRO's A2 answer offered a low cap, self-certified exemptions, and no collection escalation. CON turned this into a clean dilemma: a fine painless enough to protect the vulnerable cannot reshape turnout, while a credible fine brings back verification and bills. PRO's closing only restated the design and never resolved the dilemma. CON's "no mechanism ensures every party courts every adult" was a somewhat weak concession. Even so, CON reframed it competently as open access plus party competition. CON's progression across rounds was tighter, and its closing crystallized the "compliance, not representation" clash more effectively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over PRO's A2 penalty design. CON's B4.3 and B5.4 showed that a low-cap, self-certified, no-escalation fine is either avoidable, leaving the class gradient intact, or enforced, making vulnerability the enforcement target. PRO never answered this tension directly.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A won by keeping the motion’s promise—representative outcomes, not a higher attendance ledger—under sustained pressure. A showed that blank, spoiled, or resentful ballots remain discountable and do not force parties to earn trust or register governing judgments, while a penalty mild and exemptive enough to spare vulnerable citizens is not credible enough to produce the near-universal electorate B needs. B’s inclusion and incentive story stayed coherent but never squarely closed that tradeoff or the attendance-versus-consent gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal and closing, which turned B’s low-cap, self-certified, no-escalation design into a live dilemma: painless enough to protect the vulnerable, it cannot reshape party strategy; credible enough to do so, it bills vulnerability. B’s closing restated the specs without resolving it.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON exposed PRO's central tension: if mandated attendance is only appearance with blank or spoiled ballots allowed, it proves compliance rather than supplying a representative judgment parties must answer. CON also sustained the penalty dilemma—self-certified, non-escalating fines are either too weak to create near-universal turnout or require verification and collection burdening vulnerable citizens. PRO repeatedly asserted presence changes campaign incentives but never fully explained why discountable compelled ballots create that mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 and 2 dilemma was decisive: mere attendance cannot do representative work, while attendance claimed to make citizens count becomes coerced political conduct; PRO's answers restated countability without resolving it.
