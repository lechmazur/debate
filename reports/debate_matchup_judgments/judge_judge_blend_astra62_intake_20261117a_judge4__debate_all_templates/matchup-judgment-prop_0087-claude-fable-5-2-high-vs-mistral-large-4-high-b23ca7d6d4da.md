But A did not establish that caps eliminate rapid play's additional risks, and repeatedly demanded a separate injury rather than a distinct harm profile. Its displacement argument also lacked a convincing comparative advantage over B's combined approach. Faulty references on both sides, unsupported generalizations, and B's repeated clipping temper the victory.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins a competitive debate on the decisive comparison. PRO offered a clear speed-mechanism story, but CON landed the sharper rebuttals: a pre-set affordability cap defeats the repeated claim that losses can happen before any check triggers, PRO's substitution answer allowed CON to show the policy could treat hidden slow-product losses as success, and the monitoring asymmetry remained live. PRO's closing repeated already refuted points. Side A's clipped speeches are an execution cost, but the unclipped substance carried the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's answer to PRO's velocity-of-loss claim: PRO kept asserting a monthly loss can vanish in a burst before a check, but CON showed a genuine affordability limit binds in advance, then turned PRO's slow-product substitution into a concession that the policy was measuring tempo rather than harm.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): A won the comparative-policy clash. B established a plausible speed-driven chasing mechanism, but repeatedly mischaracterized advance affordability limits as interventions triggered after losses. A directly corrected that premise and challenged what additional protection speed restrictions provide, while exposing B's weak treatment of cash-betting visibility and substitution. B's psychological-friction argument remained relevant, but its later speeches largely repeated rather than resolved these challenges. A's overly narrow insistence that distinctness requires different injuries, categorical claims about gamblers, and repeatedly clipped turns keep this from being a larger win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest rebuttal explained that an affordability cap binds before unaffordable money can be lost: allowing rent money to disappear would mean the proposed safeguard was not properly affordability-based. B nevertheless repeated the same rent-loss scenario in closing, leaving its central comparison inadequately defended.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by attacking both the distinct-harm claim and the proposed regulatory comparison. Its pre-set affordability cap answered B’s repeated ten-minute-rent scenario, while its monitoring and substitution arguments showed how speed-tiered rules could merely move losses into less visible environments. B retained a plausible case that collapsed cooling-off intervals create a distinctive loss-chasing mechanism, but largely repeated that claim without proving what delays add once effective spending limits exist. A’s repeated clipping was an execution cost, but its substantive clashes remained clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s exposure of B’s contradictory treatment of cash affordability checks, followed by its use of B’s concession that migration to slower, higher-stakes gambling was “precisely what the rule aims to achieve,” decisively weakened B’s claim that the proposal measures harm rather than tempo.
