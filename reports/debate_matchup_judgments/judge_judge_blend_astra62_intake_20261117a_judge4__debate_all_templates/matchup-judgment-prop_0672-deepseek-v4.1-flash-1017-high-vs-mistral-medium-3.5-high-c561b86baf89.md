### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but CON better controlled the decision rule. CON reframed the comparison as expected deployment under diversified vs. concentrated supply, forcing PRO to defend a framework that treats tariff costs as certain while treating supply-chain fragility as zero. PRO's case leaned heavily on the 2018 Section 201 solar episode, repeating the same 20%/11% statistics across almost every speech without adapting to CON's repeated point that a narrow, carve-out-laden safeguard cannot license a general claim about broad, staged, credit-paired tariffs. In the pressure round, CON's answers (A1/A2) directly engaged PRO's certainty framing and distinguished timing-shift from net deployment loss, while PRO's answer to CON's second question mostly re-asserted the same 2018 figure rather than rebutting the "shift vs. cut" distinction. CON's closing crystallized this asymmetry memorably ("a missing column," "a premium is not automatically worse than an outage"), while PRO's closing largely recapped earlier points. CON's case does lean on optimistic assumptions about policy design that stretch the literal motion, and lacks comparably concrete evidence for outage risk, which keeps this close—but CON did more to reshape and win the central comparative question.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 response distinguishing the 2018 narrow safeguard from a staged, credit-paired broad tariff, combined with the "premium vs. outage" framing, did the most to blunt PRO's core empirical anchor and shift the burden onto expected-value reasoning that PRO never fully answered.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): CON successfully undermined PRO's reliance on the 2018 solar safeguard by showing it was narrow, temporary, and lacked offsets, so it could not bear the weight of a general proposition about broad strategic tariffs. CON's "expected deployment" framework exposed PRO's persistent failure to weigh the probability and cost of supply concentration, treating tariff premiums as certain while ignoring outage risk. PRO never bridged from one poorly designed case to the broad policy in question or recovered from the asymmetry charge. CON's rebuttals were sharper, its pressure questions more damaging, and its closing crystallized the decisive clash more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's dismantling of the 2018 solar evidence as an inapt template and its exposure of PRO's zero-weighting of supply fragility in the expected-deployment comparison.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively challenging Side B's counterfactual. Side B assumed that without tariffs, clean-tech deployment would continue on a smooth, frictionless trajectory, ignoring the severe choke-point risks inherent in concentrated supply chains (especially for semiconductors and critical minerals). Side A's "insurance premium vs. outage" framework provided a more sophisticated way to weigh the trade-offs, successfully arguing that a temporary transition cost is justified to secure expected long-term deployment against supply shocks. Side B's dismissal of supply chain fragility as merely "hypothetical" was a significant strategic misstep given the specific strategic imports listed in the motion, and their over-reliance on the narrow 2018 solar safeguard left them vulnerable to Side A's distinctions about broad, phased industrial policy.

Side A's "missing column" critique in the closing effectively crystallized Side B's failure to price in supply chain risk.
