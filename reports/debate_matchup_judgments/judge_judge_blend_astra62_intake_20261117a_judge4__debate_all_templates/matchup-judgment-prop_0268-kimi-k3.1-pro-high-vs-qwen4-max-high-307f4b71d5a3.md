### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): PRO (Side A) won the decisive clash with a clear causal story. Prices change loudly while quantities change silently. Unit pricing is a snapshot that compares rival products today, not a signal that a product has changed. PRO also defined a narrow trigger: same listing, quantity down, price not down. That trigger answered CON's baseline and concentrate objections well, and the "was £2, now £1.50" analogy did a lot of work on feasibility, identity, and the "verdict" charge. CON (Side B) built a competent proportionality case: product identity is hard to pin down, redesign exemptions could swallow the rule, and platforms must monitor even stable products. Its "easily escaped when narrow, costly when broad" dilemma was its best route to victory. However, CON's alternative relied partly on voluntary alerts. Its answer to PRO's incentive question was weak and conceded the alerts would be "incomplete." PRO's closing effectively turned CON's own "only the narrowest case" line: the narrow case is exactly the harm the motion targets. PRO's claim that quantity and price histories are already stored blunted the cost argument, though not completely. CON's best rejoinder was that confirming stability still requires monitoring. On balance, PRO made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came in Rebuttal 2. PRO's A1/A2 argued that regulators already judge product identity for "was" price claims and that net quantity is already a stored field for unit pricing. This neutralized most of CON's identity and cost machinery. CON's reply distinguished voluntary reference-price claims from mandated catalogue monitoring, which was sharp but less complete. CON's weak incentive answer on voluntary alerts then let PRO close by calling "incomplete" "a polite word for absent."
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side B won a high-level debate by proving a clear market failure—habitual shoppers do not re-check gram weights across a weekly shop while retailers highlight flattering discounts and keep size cuts silent—and systematically dismantling all three of Side A's status-quo substitutes (static unit pricing, deception law, and voluntary alerts), plus establishing an unanswered competitive harm to honest price-raisers. Side A kept the race competitive by exposing a real tension between narrow SKU triggers and anti-evasion adjudication and sharply distinguishing voluntary "was" price claims from continuous duties, but Side B persuasively blunted those costs by showing net quantities are already stored for unit pricing, redesigns carry real commercial friction, and catching plain shrinkflation beats guaranteed silence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's reliance on static unit pricing and voluntary alerts ([B2.5-2.6], [B3.1], [B4.5], [B5.3]) was decisive: showing that unit prices compare rivals today rather than flagging an 11% hike in a habitual default, and forcing Side A ([A4.1]) to concede that retailers lack consistent incentives to voluntarily announce hidden unit-price increases.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side A sustained the central asymmetry: current unit prices show today's offer but never signal that a habitual product quietly became dearer. Its narrow same-listing trigger, existing was-price machinery, and point that quantities are already stored fields blunted CON's baseline and cost objections, while CON conceded the core jar case and offered voluntary alerts whose incentive A exposed as absent. CON raised real identity and monitoring burdens, but never showed they outweigh catching the plain case the motion targets.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A's Rebuttal 2: trivial redesign is costly friction, product identity is already judged for was-price claims, and imperfect enforcement catching the identical lighter jar beats perfect silence; B's reply reasserted adjudication costs but did not restore a workable alternative.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined and well grounded, and the round stayed close. CON edged it because it repeatedly turned PRO's own framing against it. It used PRO's cereal arithmetic to show that unit prices already capture the change. It built the A/B shelf comparison: the cheaper shrunken box gets flagged while the dearer price-hiked box gets nothing. It also used PRO's temporary-notice design to argue the benefit is rationed by timing while compliance costs persist. PRO's best line held up reasonably well: habitual buyers anchor on the headline price, so a steady headline masks a change in value. Its A1/A2 answers were direct and honest. However, PRO never fully neutralized three things: - the bias concern that the flag targets how a rise is delivered; - the "diagnosis without treatment" point, since only the current unit price tells shoppers which way to switch; - the administrative burden of deciding product identity, which PRO kept deferring to unspecified "careful rules." PRO could also have exploited CON's alternative ("make unit prices bigger and uniform"), which arguably concedes part of the motion, but it largely let this pass. CON's weighing was crisper and more memorable, giving the reader a cleaner reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.3-B4.4 answer to A2 was the most decisive exchange. PRO argued that B's price rise is visible in the headline. CON replied that a shopper who cannot recall last month's unit price cannot recall last month's headline price either: "PRO's memory premise destroys PRO's visibility premise." That reframed the flag as tracking the mechanism of a price rise rather than any real information gap. PRO's closing reply, that perfect memory is unnecessary for noticing a headline jump, was plausible but came late and only partly repaired the damage.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): In a sharp, high-level exchange, Side A (CON) built the stronger comparative path to victory. Side B (PRO) established a credible behavioral point: static unit pricing answers cross-product value today rather than alerting habitual buyers that a steadily priced $5 item quietly worsened in value. Even granting that transition alert (though Side A overstated in [A4.4] that shoppers forget headline prices as easily as unit prices), Side A prevailed on two decisive fronts. First, Side A showed that flagging size cuts while leaving straight price hikes unflagged distorts shelf comparisons—putting a warning on a shrunken $5/450g box while an unflagged $5.60/500g rival is actually worse value per kilo—and pushes firms toward headline hikes or SKU churn. Second, Side A exposed an unanswered implementation trap: once Side B insisted a new barcode cannot escape disclosure, retailers and apps can no longer rely on automated SKU records and must instead adjudicate product identity across manufacturer reformulations and rebrands for a merely temporary notice. Side B's appeals to "standard definitions" left that permanent compliance and gaming burden largely intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A2.2]-[A2.3], Side A turned Side B's cereal example and "contestable through switching" defense ([B2.5]) against the motion: if Cereal A shrinks from 500g to 450g at $5 (~$11.11/kg) while Cereal B stays 500g and rises to $5.60 ($11.20/kg), only the cheaper per-kilo option carries a warning badge, penalizing steady headline pricing and risking misdirected switching toward the dearer unflagged box.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): PRO's central distinction survived: today's unit price enables comparison across products but cannot alert a habitual buyer that their usual product became dearer over time, a point CON effectively conceded in Rebuttal 2. PRO also answered the bias and temporary-notice objections by treating the flag as an alert prompting reconsideration, while the unchanged headline price plausibly signals false continuity in a way a visible headline rise does not. CON's baseline, timing, and cheaper-flagged-product objections were strong but showed implementation costs rather than eliminating the information gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 reframing comparison across products versus comparison across time was decisive; CON later conceded shoppers cannot discover the historical increase without a flag and was left arguing only that the fact was not actionable.
