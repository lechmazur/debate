### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B wins a competitive debate by holding PRO more tightly to the motion's specific burden: matching conventional subsidy scale before yield data is conclusive. B built two durable routes. The first is land sparing: inelastic demand means any lasting yield gap pushes farming onto wild land. The second is food buffers: fewer bushels mean higher staple prices, whatever farmers are paid. B also pressed the "same scale" dilemma: either millions of acres hit the dip at once, or conventional-scale billions go to a small minority of farms. Side A never cleanly resolved that dilemma. A's best counters were the feedlot-grain point and the cost-share/clawback contract model, and both had real merit. B blunted each of them. On feed, B argued that forage yields less digestible energy per acre and drains the surpluses A relied on. On contracts, B argued that clawbacks either bankrupt farmers after failed harvests or get politically waived, and that rigid input rules lock in practices before local data exists. A's central asymmetric-risk claim, that conventional breadbaskets face imminent catastrophic failure, stayed largely asserted rather than demonstrated. A's closing mostly restated that claim. B's closing crystallized the yield-gap-to-deforestation chain and offered a credible alternative: pilots, R&D and erosion controls now, with scaling later.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The strongest exchange came in B2.2 and B4.2. B argued that manure creates no new phosphorus or potassium, and that A's fallback to mineral supplements concedes the system relies on mined conventional inputs. B then turned A's feedlot-replacement answer by noting that forage yields less energy per acre than maize or soy, so the switch would expand land use rather than free up staple supply.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins because it kept the decision tied to the motion's demand for matching conventional-scale subsidies before conclusive yields, and it left live, mechanistically grounded risks that Side B never fully closed: calorie shortfalls, inefficient forage substitutions that can expand land use, drained grain buffers, and rent-seeking under hard-to-audit or rigid rules. Side B offered a vivid urgency case, a useful feed-grain distinction, and contract/clawback ideas, but those did not show why full-scale practice subsidies must precede data rather than targeted trials and R&D.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal most decisively undercut Side B's calorie and buffer defense by showing that lower-energy forage and mandated chemical cuts can still raise land demand and shrink shock absorbers, while breeding and regional trials can be funded without gambling breadbasket-scale subsidies.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won because its calorie, land-use, and nutrient arguments survived A's feed-surplus answer, while its pilots-plus-targeted-R&amp;D alternative directly undercut the need for equal-scale subsidies before proof. A framed the risks of soil loss and the Catch-22 powerfully, but never cleanly showed why full parity, rather than expanded trials and transition support, was necessary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B's response that subsidies do not create physical grain and that forage rotations produce fewer calories per acre, draining reserves and pushing farming into forests; A invoked feed-grain slack, but B effectively recast that slack as the food system's essential shock absorber.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides were competent, and the margin is thin. PRO built a strong opening around three ideas: market lock-in, the transition valley, and the asymmetry of risk from continued soil degradation. Its pressure questions also exposed a real tension, since CON endorsed government erosion controls while also claiming regulators would be "flying blind." CON, however, produced the cleaner decision tool: the scale dilemma. - If parity money converts vast acreage, transition yield dips hit food supply. - If it funds small rolling cohorts, it fails to create the market-wide demand PRO said pilots cannot create. PRO's answer ("rolling cohorts" plus a guaranteed market) partly concedes CON's second horn, and CON exploited that directly. PRO never fully resolved this. CON also answered PRO's regulator contradiction credibly. It distinguished edge-of-field erosion controls from calibrating whole-farm biological transitions. On herbicide subsidies, it argued that the fix is reforming existing subsidies rather than doubling spending. The land-sparing argument stayed live. PRO's best counter was that grazing on winter cover crops does not reduce cereal acreage, and CON met it with plausible compaction and capital-cost objections. PRO's reversibility frame ("rewrite subsidies in one budget cycle") was effective, but CON's closing flipped it with the irreversibility of cleared forests. Because the motion explicitly asks to act before conclusive data, CON's "prove first" framing aligned more tightly with the core uncertainty. Both sides misused reference IDs, so neither gains an edge there.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.3 was the most decisive exchange. It turned PRO's A1 answer ("rolling cohorts" funding front-loaded equipment) into a concession: converted acreage stays small, so the commercial-scale argument for parity over pilots collapses. If acreage is instead large, aggregate yield dips return. PRO's closing restated the claim rather than escaping the dilemma.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side A won by keeping the motion’s same-scale-before-proof burden central. Side B never fully closed the fork between adoption large enough to break lock-in and adoption small enough to avoid aggregate yield, price, and land-conversion risk. Its risk-asymmetry and reversibility case stayed live, but it kept framing the choice as parity versus doing nothing, even after Side A offered reform, targeted erosion controls, and R&D instead of doubling spending.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s scale contradiction remained decisive: equal billions either pull prime acreage into the admitted transition dips or fund cohorts too small to create the commercial demand Side B said pilots cannot supply, and the rolling-cohort reply did not escape that fork.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B sustained the decisive burden: PRO must justify equal-scale spending before conclusive yields. B's scale-versus-gradual-adoption dilemma, land-sparing/deforestation risk, and cheaper targeted R&amp;D alternative remained live despite A's strong soil-irreversibility and lock-in case. A's rolling-cohort answer narrowed but did not resolve why billions would both transform markets and avoid aggregate yield losses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 contradiction: parity either converts vast acreage with not-yet-bred seeds and causes supply shocks, or funds only small cohorts and cannot create the commercial demand A claims is necessary.
