### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): This was a close, well-matched debate. PRO edges it because its core causal argument survived the full exchange: majorities borrow against future, unrepresented taxpayers, and statutory rules cannot bind the majority that wrote them. CON never squarely answered that. "Correctable at the next election" was met directly by the point that future taxpayers cannot vote. "Yields move at the margin" sits awkwardly with CON's own UK example, where targets were rewritten and debt still built up. PRO also turned CON's concession that the rule "binds for ordinary governance" into a reason to vote PRO, since routine peacetime borrowing is where debt accumulates. CON landed the sharper implementation attacks: - Contested output-gap estimates would turn into constitutional litigation. - Ministries seeking credit may underbuild automatic stabilizers. - "War taxes" are a poor answer to an invasion. Some of PRO's replies were weak, especially the war taxes, central bank liquidity and industry-funded bank pools. On grounding, CON hurt itself by listing reunification among the times Germany suspended its debt brake, when the brake was adopted much later. The weighing favors PRO only narrowly. CON's best material shows that entrenchment is imperfect and messy. It does not establish that discretion does better on the chronic-deficit harm PRO kept centered. CON's rhetoric ("delayed detonator," "constitutionalize the fight") was more memorable, but it did not fully overturn PRO's failure-mode comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over the German example. CON argued the court ruling "detonated" the climate-fund gimmick late and froze budgets mid-year, and that the brake "binds for ordinary governance" while bending for large shocks. PRO seized on that concession in Rebuttal 2: ordinary governance is exactly where chronic debt builds up, so a rule that binds there is doing the job it was meant for. PRO also framed the court ruling as lasting deterrence, not failure. CON's best counter was the output-gap litigation point, which damaged the inflation-index analogy. It still left PRO's core point about future taxpayers who cannot vote largely standing.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): CON won because it turned PRO's flagship German example into evidence of asymmetric failure: the brake was gamed off-budget, bent for major shocks, and then detonated late through court rulings. PRO's best answer was that ordinary peacetime deficits matter most, but CON showed that even ordinary peacetime binding produced gimmicks and mid-year freezes rather than clean discipline. PRO also relied heavily on an incentive claim about future strong stabilizers that CON exposed as a wish about politicians rather than a requirement of the rule as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B's rebuttal of A's Germany point: B argued the constitutional court did not prevent the climate-fund gimmick but struck it down years later, causing a mid-year budget freeze, while Germany suspended the brake under heavy political pressure. That reframed A's own evidence into proof of delayed detonation and selective enforcement.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): B built the clearer comparative path: constitutional entrenchment relocates and delays fiscal conflict rather than reliably removing it, while A never established that the rule as written would produce strong stabilizers or prevent emergency gaming. A’s intergenerational argument and ordinary-governance weighing remained forceful, and B’s German historical claims were sometimes overbroad, but B answered A’s central institutional-incentive case more directly and framed the failure-mode tradeoff more convincingly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal dissolved A’s alleged toothless-versus-straitjacket contradiction through asymmetric enforcement: suspension under systemic pressure but costly binding in ordinary governance. B’s second rebuttal then explained why politicians seeking visible credit may underfund automatic stabilizers, directly weakening A’s main answer to the rule-as-written objection.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): CON wins a clear but competitive debate. PRO opened with a clean incentive story: legislatures are biased toward deficits, and the Odysseus precommitment framing illustrated it well. PRO's closing asymmetry was its strongest weighing: design flaws can be fixed, while incentive flaws are permanent. CON did more damage across the exchange. CON's best move was to hold PRO to the motion as written, which allows only automatic stabilizer exceptions. Under pressure, PRO added supermajority escape clauses, golden-rule capital exemptions and fiscal councils. CON showed that this concedes stabilizers alone cannot handle wars, panics or pandemics. CON then argued that each patch creates its own problem: - minority hostage power under a supermajority clause; - judges or technocrats ruling on what counts as investment; - debt-ratio correction triggers that are pro-cyclical. CON also turned PRO's eurozone example: those countries had rigid supranational rules and no sovereign central bank, which indicts straitjackets rather than discretion. On the pressure-question exchange, PRO declared "silence" on its Q1 before CON had any chance to answer. That mischaracterizes the exchange. CON then answered directly with Sweden's statutory expenditure ceilings. PRO's closing conceded Sweden's ceilings endured, which undercuts its claim that discretion fails "forever." PRO's rebuttal that U.S. pay-as-you-go lapsed was fair but only partial. CON's crisis examples were ordinary and accurate background: the House initially rejected the first bank-rescue bill, and the stimulus passed well short of two-thirds. They answered PRO's assumption that crises produce supermajority consensus. Net: PRO's incentive argument stays live, but it is weakened by Sweden and by PRO's reliance on additions beyond the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1/B2.2 was decisive. It exposed that PRO had to add emergency supermajority clauses, golden-rule exemptions and fiscal councils that the motion does not include. It then showed that the supermajority patch creates minority hostage power, a point later backed by the 2008 House vote. PRO's objective-trigger reply in A4.1 was answered by CON's point that recession data lag the crisis.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side A built a coherent precommitment case, but under pressure it repeatedly added features beyond the automatic stabilizer exceptions—supermajority emergency clauses, golden-rule capital exemptions, and technocratic fiscal councils. Side B exposed that retreat and turned each patch into a liability: minority hostage-taking in real crises, judicially managed investment definitions, and undemocratic enforcement. B also answered A's strongest pressure with PAYGO and Sweden examples; A's PAYGO-lapse reply was sharp but did not fully undercut Sweden or democratic revisability. B's closing on constitutional lock-in and the failure of 'draft it better' outweighed A's fixable-flaws asymmetry.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal that A had to rewrite the proposition was decisive: after A added emergency supermajorities, golden-rule exemptions, and fiscal councils, B showed even those patches created new failures—polarized minorities could block crisis response, investment labels invite gaming, and unelected enforcers seize fiscal policy.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B built the cleaner motion-specific path to victory. A’s precommitment case was forceful, but its defense repeatedly depended on emergency supermajorities, capital exemptions, fiscal councils, and recession triggers beyond the stated stabilizer exceptions. B answered both pressure lines, supplied statutory-discipline examples, and showed why constitutional lock-in makes drafting failures durable. A’s fixable-versus-structural framing was memorable but did not neutralize that objection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal exposed A’s added safeguards as a retreat from the motion; B’s second rebuttal then used the 2008–09 US votes to rebut assumed crisis consensus and explained why recession triggers would lag wars, bank runs, and pandemics.
