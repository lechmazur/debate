Both sides showed good epistemic discipline, with no fake statistics.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): The motion demands strict prohibition, so CON needed only one plausible, harmless, beneficial use to defeat it, and supplied one. CON separated using population data to decide what a plan covers from using it to decide whom the plan prices. The familial hypercholesterolemia example made this concrete: low claims counts understate undiagnosed need, and a national prevalence figure shows why. CON also proposed a checkable boundary: broad published statistics only, no workforce cuts, and no genetic justification for exclusions or incentives. PRO's re-identification argument fits genome-level or workforce-level files, but it was squarely blunted: a national prevalence figure has no individual rows to link. PRO's fallback, that a forecast is effectively subgroup pricing, was answered: multiplying a rate by headcount does not price any shift differently. CON's sharpest move was showing that a ban does not make silent misuse any more detectable than regulation would. PRO's claim that prohibition "removes the input" was never repaired against the point that the figure stays public. PRO's answer that screening "conscripts" families into costs was strained. Its concession that committees may follow a guideline but not the prevalence figure the guideline cites let CON build a clean two-committee comparison with no identifiable victim. PRO's closing restated distrust of employers rather than resolving that comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange began with CON's A2.3/A4.2 point that a strict ban, like a permission regime, produces no evidence of a silently omitted drug. PRO's main harm, selective omission, therefore does not distinguish the two rules. CON then turned PRO's A2 concession (guideline yes, cited prevalence figure no) into the two-committee comparison in A4.4-A4.5, where only the better-informed decision is forbidden and no family is harmed. PRO never answered it directly.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued at a high level and stayed on the live clash. Side B (PRO) ran a principled case: any employer permission to use genetic prevalence figures is a forecast of inherited cost that cannot be audited, and B's rebuttal-2 answers (the "bill that cheap identification creates and then abandons," and the guideline-vs-prevalence-input line) were sharp. But Side A (CON) built the narrower, more concrete path: the what-to-cover vs. whom-to-cover distinction, a specific FH screening example, an enforceable permission with reviewer checks, and then a decisive escalation in rebuttal 2 making affordable treatment coverage a condition of permission. A's strongest surviving thread was the enforcement asymmetry: a strict ban stops only the openly documented, beneficial use of a public figure while doing nothing to expose the silent omission (the drug never listed), which B's "prohibition removes the input" answer only partially parried, since proving silent mental use still requires evidence. B's re-identification opening was neutralized by A's row-less-figure distinction and largely abandoned. B's fallback — "the permission is the forecast itself" — was rhetorically strong but rested on asserting non-generous endings rather than showing harm under A's stated conditions, while A's two-committees comparison showed no incremental harm and asked why only that decision is forbidden. A made the debate easier to decide and answered more of the pressure squarely.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's enforcement-asymmetry argument ([A2.3]/[A4.5]/[A5.3]) against B's omission scenario: a ban cannot detect a committee that silently used a genetic figure to withhold a drug, but it does forbid the committee that openly relies on the figure to fund screening and treatment. B's "prohibition removes the input" ([B4.6]) and closing reframing ("the permission is the forecast itself") kept the point alive but never showed concrete harm under A's pathway-condition permission, leaving A's comparison standing.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by clearly separating population-level benefit design from worker risk sorting and supplying a concrete beneficial use that A's categorical ban would block. A was forceful on selective coverage, familial exposure, and employer incentives, but too often treated forecasting as equivalent to pricing and never showed how prohibition would detect silent omissions better than B's review regime. B's controlled guideline comparison left A defending a formal distinction without demonstrating a difference in worker harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's enforcement exchange was decisive: concealed formulary omissions are difficult to detect under either rule, while a blanket ban still blocks openly documented beneficial additions. B's comparison of otherwise identical guideline-based plans isolated the prevalence figure itself and showed no added harm; A's claim that prohibition simply "removes the input" did not close that gap.
