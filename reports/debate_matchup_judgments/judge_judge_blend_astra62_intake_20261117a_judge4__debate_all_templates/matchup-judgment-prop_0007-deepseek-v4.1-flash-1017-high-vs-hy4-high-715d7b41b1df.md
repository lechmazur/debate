### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Both sides were disciplined and avoided fake precision. CON (A) had the stronger opening structure: it separated routine growth promotion from targeted prevention, argued that a ban could shift use into broader therapeutic courses, and offered regulated stewardship as an alternative. PRO (B) won the decisive clash on administrability and weighing. When PRO's Q1 asked where stewardship draws its line, PRO itself answered the matching question with candor in A1: the ban prohibits both cases, and the legal line is diagnosed illness. It then supplied a falsifiable test, total treatment days checked against resistance prevalence. CON gave a concrete prescription standard in reply. However, its A2 conceded that it does not assume therapeutic use always creates more exposure. That reduced the substitution argument to two-sided uncertainty. PRO had already weighed that uncertainty using irreversibility and the incentive to reform husbandry: price and yield costs can be cushioned, while lost antibiotic effectiveness accumulates and is hard to reverse. PRO's point that "defined exposure events" recur at every weaning, arrival and feed change also made stewardship's limit look porous. CON's closing restated substitution without fully answering this. The margin is narrow because CON's net-exposure argument remained plausible and was never fully neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over the line-drawing questions. PRO answered openly that the ban covers short vet-directed prophylaxis, offered a clean test with a falsifier, and argued that CON's "short, specific risk" criteria would let routine prophylaxis back in through the side door. CON's best counter was that a ban controls timing rather than total exposure. That point was sharp, but its own A2 admission, that therapeutic treatment does not necessarily create more exposure, left the uncertainty to be weighed under PRO's irreversibility frame.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a high-level debate by dismantling Side A's "regulated stewardship" countermodel and answering the therapeutic-substitution risk with superior incentive analysis. Side A mounted the strongest possible CON case, arguing that banning vet-directed prevention for high-risk transitions (like weaning or cattle arrival) forces farmers to wait for outbreaks that require heavier therapeutic courses. However, Side B proved that because weaning and transport recur across every cohort, allowing prescriptions for "defined exposure events" keeps routine healthy-herd dosing alive across the production calendar. Side B also showed that removing cheap antibiotic insurance forces producers to reform stocking density, weaning, and sanitation rather than passively waiting for illness, while cleanly weighing bounded, cushionable food-price increases against cumulative, irreversible antibiotic resistance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side B's [B3.1] forced Side A to define its stewardship limit in [A4.1]—where Side A allowed "a single arrival or weaning course" with a stop date and vet authorization—Side B decisively exposed in [B4.4] and [B5.1] that weaning, arrival, and feed changes recur across every production cohort, making Side A's rule "prophylaxis with better stationery" rather than a real cap on healthy-animal exposure.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side B won a close debate by turning Side A's own success test against the ban. A made a strong irreversibility case and honestly admitted the ban covers targeted prevention of healthy at-risk animals, but never showed that a diagnosis-only rule reduces net exposure rather than shifting it into later treatment. B answered the line question with auditable limits and left the cleaner reason to reject a blunt ban: it controls timing, not the total pressure A accepted as decisive, while stewardship can still prohibit routine mass dosing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal and closing used A's total-treatment-days test to show the ban does not control dose, duration, numbers treated, or repeats, and may move use onto sicker animals, while stewardship regulates those drivers directly.
