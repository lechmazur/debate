### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): A won because it kept the clash fixed on what the proposition uniquely requires: a compulsory system-level classifier for ads, trackers, exceptions, and appeals. B made a strong privacy and defaults case, but A showed that B's most powerful mechanisms—default privacy signals and endpoint hardening—were compatible with a narrower conduct-based regime rather than proving this mandate. A's handling of the lock metaphor was decisive: B's own answer was architecture plus illegality, not a legal duty for every OS to adjudicate ads. B's silent-sale failure mode was vivid, but A plausibly answered with structural overblocking and incumbent-friendly exception queues. B's repeated clipped turns also hurt execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response to B's "blocker is the lock" was the decisive exchange: it used B's own endpoint-hardening examples to show they stop data before harm without requiring the motion's ad/tracker classification system, undercutting the core visible-hiccup versus silent-sale weighing.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B wins by keeping the combined ad-and-tracker mandate central. A persuasively defended default endpoint privacy protection, especially against irreversible collection, but repeatedly narrowed its defense to tracking while allowing contextual ads through. B exposed that mismatch and distinguished privacy obligations and architectural hardening from compulsory ad filtering. Its objections about funding, classification errors, and gatekeeper discretion remained live: A’s “one tap” answer addressed visible breakage, not structural costs. A’s clipped turns and B’s nonexistent reference IDs were execution weaknesses, but the substantive scope mismatch decides the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest rebuttal challenged A’s claim that agreement on defaults largely settled the motion. Default refusal signals and fingerprinting-resistant architecture do not themselves justify mandatory ad blocking. This directly weakened A’s closing “lock versus note” framing while preserving B’s narrower alternative.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a high-level debate by holding Side B (PRO) to the full scope of the motion—legally mandatory, system-level opt-out ad and tracker blocking—while cleanly navigating PRO's best pressure. PRO never defended blocking ads at the OS/browser level, repeatedly assuming contextual ads would pass untouched and retreating to browser-level cross-site tracker blocking and API hardening. Side A exposed that mandating "robust" ad and tracker blocking forces risk-averse vendors to overblock and creates allow-list exception queues favoring incumbents while silently draining publisher revenue (such as blocked ad-viewability checks that users never see to fix with "one tap"). Side A also handled PRO's pressure questions smartly by endorsing a default refusal signal plus ex ante collector and buyer bans, protecting passive users without breaking pages. PRO kept the round competitive through strong pressure on Chrome's conflicted incentives and offshore scofflaws, and each side incurred minor execution costs (three clipped speeches for B; four mistyped P/C reference IDs for A).
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A3.2], [A4.3]-[A4.4], and [A5.1], Side A asked how a system blocker catches the "undetectable" offshore fingerprinting PRO warned about, then turned PRO's answer ([B4.5]: storage partitioning and limiting high-entropy APIs) by showing those are non-opt-out browser architecture choices rather than the motion's compulsory ad and tracker classifier—and that anything truly invisible to regulation is equally invisible to a blocklist.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): CON won by successfully narrowing the motion to its overbroad ad-blocking half. PRO offered a coherent autonomy/consent principle, but CON exposed that blocking untargeted ads adds a major funding and platform-consolidation cost without targeting the accepted surveillance harm. PRO’s answer that Google’s own ads would also be blocked addressed formal nondiscrimination, but CON’s follow-up on formal equality versus practical platform leverage remained largely unanswered. The clipped second rebuttal was a modest execution cost, but CON’s core comparative case and direct-regulation alternative were clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s pressure question on whether Google’s own search and YouTube ads would be blocked forced PRO into a damaging concede-and-distinguish: PRO said yes, then could not solve CON’s follow-up that equal permission prompts still favor platforms over local publishers.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): B more convincingly challenged the necessity of legally default-blocking nontracking ads. A established meaningful privacy and device-control interests, but mostly asserted that attention-based consent overrides the funding tradeoff rather than weighing reduced free access. A answered the formal self-preferencing objection well; B adapted with a plausible distinction between equal permission rules and unequal ability to secure permission. B overstated revenue collapse and the completeness of targeted bans, keeping this competitive. A’s nonexistent references and B’s clipped rebuttal were additional, minor execution costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s closing turned A’s “chosen channel” distinction back on it: deliberately visiting a newspaper also selects an ad-supported page. This directly challenged A’s justification for treating even data-free banners as unauthorized device use, sharpening the unresolved distinction between preventing surveillance and mandating ad refusal.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by surgically separating covert tracking from untargeted advertising, showing that direct anti-surveillance laws address Side B's core privacy harms while mandating default ad blocking defunds data-free ads that support free journalism. Side A also cleanly answered Side B's definitional challenge by distinguishing ex-post regulatory enforcement from live vendor-coded script classification, and flipped Side B's late device-property argument by noting that readers voluntarily request a publisher's ad-supported webpage. Even after Side B boldly conceded Google and YouTube ads would also be blocked by default, Side A showed that logged-in platforms have far greater leverage to win opt-ins than independent outlets—an asymmetry Side B never answered. Despite a minor tail-end clip on Turn 8, Side A outperformed Side B, which also cited nonexistent reference IDs ([C1.2, C1.6], P3.2) and prematurely faulted Side A for not answering Q2 before Side A's rebuttal turn.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side A's Q1 [A3.1] forced Side B to state that both YouTube and local newspapers must ask permission to show ads [B4.1], Side A turned that concession in [A4.6]-[A4.7] ("Formal equality, practical monopoly"), showing that YouTube can gate videos for a billion logged-in users while an independent paper loses passing search visitors—a decisive point Side B dropped in closing.
