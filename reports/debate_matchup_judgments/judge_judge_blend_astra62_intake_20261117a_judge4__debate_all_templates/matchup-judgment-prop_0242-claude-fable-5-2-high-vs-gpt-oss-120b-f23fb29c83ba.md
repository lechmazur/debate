### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side B built the stronger comparative case. Side A's argument narrowed late from "cable forged polarization" to a claim that cable mainly writes the polarizing narratives while social media distributes them. That conceded the core impact question: if feeds reach, select, and reward the incendiary content among non-viewers, social media is doing much of the polarizing. Side B also answered both pressure questions directly, using QAnon and Brazil/India WhatsApp cascades to show polarizing content emerging without cable, and used Side A's own Brazil admission to invert the causal story. Side A's global cable claims were strained and repeatedly expanded "cable" into vague cable-style or 24-hour news networks, which weakened its grounding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal that cable was a self-selected niche and that amplification is not secondary but the polarizing mechanism itself, reinforced by turning Side A's "platforms surface incendiary cable clips" admission into a concession.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A won every major clash with stronger causal logic, better epistemic grounding, and cleaner comparative weighing, overcoming minor execution costs from end-of-speech word-limit clips. First, Side A won global scope: Side B overreached by claiming Fox-style cable dominates airwaves globally and drives households for two to three hours nightly, whereas Side A accurately distinguished state media capture in Poland and Turkey, showed Bolsonaro rose via WhatsApp and Facebook against Brazil's dominant broadcaster, and exposed primetime cable as a self-selected niche of a few million viewers. Second, when Side B retreated to arguing that cable writes the polarizing "song" while social media merely redistributes it, Side A both falsified the premise—citing QAnon and WhatsApp cascades in India and Brazil, which Side B ignored—and won the weighing between authorship and societal impact by using Side B's own admission that platform algorithms select and surface incendiary clips to tens of millions of non-viewers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In Turn 7 ([A4.1]-[A4.6]), Side A decisively answered Side B's pressure questions and broke the CON case: it refuted the claim that polarizing frames must originate on cable by pointing to QAnon and WhatsApp rumor cascades in Brazil and India, dismantled the senior-voter objection by noting heavy senior Facebook use alongside primetime cable's small audience share, and reframed Side B's "supplies the song" defense as an argument about script authorship rather than polarizing impact.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A won by making the decisive clash about impact rather than authorship. It argued convincingly that social media polarizes democratic politics more because it reaches casual users, operates globally, bypasses gatekeepers, and selects which cable-originated moments spread. Side B had a coherent cable-origin and elite-incentive case, but it leaned on unsupported generalizations, shifted the definition of cable under pressure, and failed to neutralize A’s examples of platform-born polarization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s pressure questions and follow-up on Bolsonaro and non-viewers forced Side B to concede that wider audiences receive polarizing cable clips through platform algorithms; A then turned that concession into the core impact argument.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): Side A built the stronger, more empirically grounded case. CON successfully used timing, demographics, cross-national variation, and experimental evidence to show cable produced the partisan script and the audience that actually polarized. PRO's case rested on vivid mechanisms—algorithms, virality, microtargeting—but these were repeatedly shown to be capabilities rather than demonstrated effects. A's decisive turn came from exploiting PRO's own concession that cable delivers the script while social media repeats it, and from refuting PRO's key authorship example: Stop the Steal was pre-announced on television and later cost a network a defamation settlement. PRO never answered that refutation. A also exposed PRO's lag-effect argument as unfalsifiable. B had plausible theoretical points, but A's comparative weighing and sharper pressure-answer discipline made the vote clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's refutation of PRO's Stop the Steal origin claim was the most decisive: B insisted the conspiracy sprang from fringe social platforms, but A showed the fraud narrative had been announced by a president on television for months and later led to a cable network paying a large defamation settlement. B repeated the claim in closing without answering that point, which collapsed PRO's best example of social media authorship.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B won dominantly by contrasting Side A's theoretical mechanisms with four empirical pillars that held up across the entire debate: US polarization surged in the 1990s before social media existed, grew fastest among older cable-heavy demographics, stayed flat in democracies with the same platform algorithms, and barely moved in feed-removal experiments whereas Fox rollout and channel-switching studies showed measurable effects. Side A conceded in its first rebuttal that "cable delivers the script, social media repeats it," retreated into an unfalsifiable "lag effect" to explain away null experimental results, inverted its own point in Q2, and repeated its "Stop the Steal" authorship claim in Closing without answering Side B's rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's counterexamples across the rebuttals—first pointing out in [B2.5] that Side A's foreign example of algorithmic polarization (WhatsApp in Brazil) is an encrypted messaging app with no recommendation feed, and then showing in [B4.4] that Side A's lone example of a social-media-originated narrative ("Stop the Steal") had been previewed for months on television by a sitting president and broadcast in primetime cable until it produced a massive defamation settlement.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B won by building the clearer comparative case: polarization began before social media, hardened most among cable-heavy older voters, did not appear everywhere platforms did, and showed measurable cable effects while social-media removal effects looked weak. B also turned A’s amplification argument into an “author versus echo” frame and effectively rebutted A’s lag-effect and Stop-the-Steal authorship claims. A remained vivid but increasingly unfalsifiable, set an impossible evidentiary standard, and failed to answer the key closing pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttal of A’s Stop-the-Steal example was decisive: A claimed social media originated the narrative, but B showed it had been pushed by political elites and cable first, undermining A’s best authorship example.
