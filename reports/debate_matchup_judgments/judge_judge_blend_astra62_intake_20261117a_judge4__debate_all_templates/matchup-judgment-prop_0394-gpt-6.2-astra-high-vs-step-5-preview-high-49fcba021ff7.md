### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO wins because it separated the lecture explanation from the performance exercises CON relied on. That move neutralized CON's chilling-effect examples: oral arguments, bedside practice and language drills. Each still falls within the motion's pedagogical exception, while the lecture explanation stays accessible. PRO's core asymmetry was never squarely answered. A teacher can identify which exercise needs protection, but cannot tell from a quiet room who missed the explanation. Lost recordings also cannot be recreated on request. PRO answered both pressure questions directly. It defined a "strong" reason and proposed a concrete budget priority that keeps specialist staff funded separately. CON's strongest live route was finite resources. It pressed this well with the point that a separate budget line does not repeal scarcity. However, it never showed that targeted requests reach students who never disclose. CON's "productive struggle" claim was turned by PRO: if reconstruction is the intended challenge, give it to everyone rather than only to those who missed the sentence. The "compliance tripwire" framing stayed largely speculative. CON's closing also misattributed a concession to PRO by citing its own [B4.1]. PRO turned CON's best line, "a recording is content," into support for its own case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2.1–A2.3 decided the debate. It pointed out that CON's examples remove the lecture entirely, and that teachers know in advance when an exercise begins. That turned CON's chilling-effect argument into a narrow, plannable exception rather than one that swallows the rule. PRO's closing then reinforced this by reusing CON's own "recording is content" line.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won the decisive clashes. The explanation-vs-performance distinction (record the model dialogue, protect the oral argument) blunted CON's chilling-effect case, and the retroactivity point — no adviser can hand a student Monday's recording if nobody made it — was never neutralized by CON's "proactive outreach" alternative. A answered both pressure questions directly and with a workable standard for "strong reason," while B's core reply (the presumption still invites defensive justification) rested on stretching a planning-level default into a real-time compliance tripwire. B's desirable-difficulty argument was cleanly reversed by A: if reconstruction is the intended challenge, assign it to everyone; otherwise it adds a hearing test to a physics test. B's resources point survived but shrank to "scarcity exists," which A met with a budget-priority answer. B's rhetoric was memorable ("a recording is not diagnosis; it is content") but A turned that exact line against B in the closing. B never established that the presumption as written forces the defensive-documentation regime it depicted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.4–A5.1: A's quiet-lecture framing plus the Tuesday-outreach/Monday-recording rejoinder destroyed CON's proactive-outreach alternative and made the default-vs-request comparison decisive.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by keeping the motion’s actual decision rule in view: default access to explanations and materials, with teacher judgment reserved for cases where a format would materially impair a specific learning objective. A cleanly separated recordable lectures from unrecorded student performances, answered the documentation and staffing questions directly, and left unrebutted the point that no later adviser can supply a recording that was never made. Side B landed a memorable compliance-and-scarcity frame, but overstated the motion as forcing cameras on performances and never neutralized the silent-student and irreversibility problems.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first rebuttal, distinguishing planned recording of an explanation from leaving oral practice, bedside work, or language exercises unrecorded, most damaged B’s chilling-effect case and kept the presumption from collapsing into surveillance.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): CON wins by aiming at the motion's actual decision rule: a presumption that can be overridden only by a strong pedagogical reason. CON accepted standing access and offered a concrete model: published access plans before enrollment, accessible core materials without request, recording where replay works, and an access office that verifies delivery. It then showed that legitimate exceptions exist that are not pedagogical. The two examples were a speaker's refusal to be recorded and real triage between scarce specialist tasks. PRO's A1 conceded that a guest's refusal can block recording even when replay would help. That is exactly a non-pedagogical exception. PRO's A2 conceded that tomorrow's readable equations take priority over captioning yesterday's replay. PRO then reasserted that only pedagogical reasons justify leaving a file unproduced, which sits uneasily with its own answer. PRO's strongest line was that CON's model shifts the burden onto students through a referral queue. CON squarely answered this in B4.2: planning is set in motion by the course team in advance, and student reports are a safeguard, not the trigger. PRO's closing nonetheless repeated the original characterization, which misdescribes CON's clarified position. PRO's general universal-design benefits were never contested, so they did not decide the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1–B2.2 answering A2.2: "capture the lecture and omit the guest" fails when the guest is the lecture. This forced PRO to concede in A4.1 that consent alone can justify withholding a recording even when replay would help, which exposed the motion's "strong pedagogical reason" exception as too narrow.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A ran a clean, well-structured PRO case: burden-shifting to the institution, universal design benefits, and a sharp attack that CON's office-based model is a disguised referral queue. But Side B executed the more targeted and original strategy. B's core move—separating captions (standing obligation) from recordings (consent-governed)—directly attacked the motion's own exception test, which licenses only "strong pedagogical reasons." B's pressure question (B3.1) forced A to concede in A4.1 that a speaker's refusal can defeat a recording "even when replay would help," i.e., a non-pedagogical exception. B then crystallized this in the closing: rarity "answers how often the question arises, not what makes the exception legitimate," and the patient "owes nobody a pedagogical defense of her refusal." That concession left A defending an amended motion rather than the one on the table. B also defused A's reactive-queue attack with the pre-enrollment published-plan model (reports as safeguard, not trigger), and won the specialist-prioritization exchange by showing workload triage is not a pedagogical reason. A's answers were honest and direct (proper A1/A2 format, good completion-date framing), and A's systemic burden-shifting case for captions/materials was never really overturned—but B had already conceded and matched that ground, narrowing the fight to the clashes B won. B made the debate easier to decide and controlled the decisive exchanges.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B's pressure question B3.1 (may the school honor a recording refusal on consent grounds alone?) against A's A4.1 concession ("Yes... even when replay would help"). B converted that concession into the closing argument that the motion's pedagogical-only exception clause cannot justify consent-based withholding, leaving PRO defending a modified proposition.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B wins by showing the motion wrongly treats recordings like other access tools and allows only pedagogical exceptions. It forced PRO to concede that consent can block a recording even when replay would help, and that scarce specialist time can favor a first usable lesson over a second route, without any pedagogical objection to replay. PRO never supplied the pedagogical justification the motion requires in those cases, and its closing still flattened B’s advance-planning model into a student-triggered queue after that distinction had been answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal and closing: once PRO accepted that an accessible account can meet the duty without a recording, the school needed no pedagogical reason to honor a refusal—the exact hole in the motion’s exception clause.
