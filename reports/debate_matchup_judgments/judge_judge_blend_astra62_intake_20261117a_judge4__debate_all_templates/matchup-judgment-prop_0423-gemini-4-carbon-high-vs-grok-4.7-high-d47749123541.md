### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): CON wins narrowly because it kept pressing a mechanism PRO never fully neutralized. Residency or membership labels do not track the people PRO says deserve access, such as working fans, disrupted neighbors, or loyal regulars. Affluent locals and dues-payers qualify just as easily. Meanwhile, shrinking the general pool raises the price for everyone outside the tranche: late locals, lapsed members, commuters past the line, and away fans. CON also drew a clean distinction that seats cannot compensate for noise and gridlock. That turned PRO's reciprocity framing into a category error. PRO's best routes did some damage: - It framed pure surge pricing as a wealth queue. - It argued that face-value odds beat guaranteed exclusion. - In the closing, it accused CON of contradiction for mourning priced-out workers while calling face-value seats an underpriced windfall. CON answered that contradiction plausibly: money is an imperfect screen, and a map-based screen is worse. PRO's support also weakened later. Its "fifth to a quarter" figure and its claim that city permitters audit reserves were asserted rather than grounded. Its atmosphere argument was never developed after the opening. PRO's best answer to the "optional claimant" question was to say the reserve tracks the burden. CON reasonably showed that this still leaves the choice to the organizer's discretion. CON pays a real execution cost because its first rebuttal was clipped. Its later speeches still adapted to the clash and narrowed it more effectively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's A2 and [A4.6] against PRO's claims that "fair odds at face value" protect working fans and that three-quarters left open spares outsiders. CON argued that the labels screen for zip code and dues rather than need. It also argued that the smaller remaining pool raises prices on everyone excluded from the tranche. Together these undercut PRO's central access-equity claim. PRO's closing contradiction charge was its strongest counter, but it did not repair that mechanism.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): A wins by grounding priority in off-market contributions and defending a mixed allocation despite unavoidable scarcity. It directly answered both pressure questions, including splitting reserves where both claims apply. B landed substantial objections about higher prices for excluded ordinary fans and imperfect eligibility, but did not adequately defeat the reciprocity rationale. Its repeated claim that one group remained optional overlooked A's clarification. A's exaggerated claims of guaranteed exclusion under dynamic pricing keep the margin competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response that an oversubscribed lottery still creates meaningful affordable access undercut B's repeated fixed-capacity objection. B correctly identified the resulting cost to outsiders, but established a distributive tradeoff rather than showing why it outweighed locals' and members' prior contributions.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO built the cleaner choice: rationing is unavoidable, so face-value odds for those bearing disruption or sustaining institutions beat rationing every seat by surge wealth. CON showed the reserve transfers scarcity and raises remainder prices, but never escaped defending a system that also excludes working fans, while PRO directly answered the 'meaningful' and locals-or-members pressures and preserved most inventory for general sale. CON's clipped first rebuttal also weakened its execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was PRO's Rebuttal 2: defining a substantial reserve, matching it to the actual burden, and reframing CON's 'lottery' attack as fair face-value odds versus guaranteed exclusion in a surge auction.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): PRO opened with a clean stake-and-fairness frame and a well-designed safeguard ("released if unused"). Its rebuttals were sharp, especially the dilemma it built from CON's own claim that most residents do not want tickets. But CON steadily eroded the foundations of PRO's case. First, it showed that "released if unused" does nothing on the sellout nights that actually matter. Second, it held PRO to its admission that tickets are not compensation for disruption, which weakened the residents' claim to "stake." Third, it pressed PRO on member rosters until PRO dropped member gates for tours altogether. The decisive moment came in PRO's closing, which explicitly conceded CON's arithmetic: holds raise prices on the remaining pool. PRO then argued that this cost is the price of fairness. CON exploited the concession well. PRO's own complaint was that the market rewards cash, yet the top bidders still get in, and the fan priced out is the moderate-budget regional buyer. That turned PRO's anti-"speed and cash" framing against it. PRO's best counter was the season-package point, which was a nice turn but only partly answered CON's single-night distinction. PRO was more rhetorically polished. CON left the cleaner comparative route to victory, despite some loose assumptions, such as treating every reserved seat as discounted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Q2/A2 exchange was decisive. PRO asked why CON counted the seats removed by a hold but not the demand removed with them. CON answered that published-price holds remove mostly low-bidding local demand, leaving the high bidders to compete over fewer seats. PRO then conceded this arithmetic in its closing, letting CON argue that holds hurt moderate-budget outsiders rather than the wealthy surge buyers PRO had targeted.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won the comparative fairness clash: reserved access can displace moderate-budget regional and newer fans, rather than the wealthiest bidders. A conceded higher general-sale prices but insufficiently justified imposing that cost based on residency or prior enrollment, especially after accepting separate compensation for local disruption. A scored by identifying season packages as reservations themselves, but this did not establish the broader default. B more consistently answered pressure and sharpened the choice across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: When A argued that reservations remove demand alongside seats, B distinguished discounted recipients from buyers willing to pay the dynamic clearing price. A conceded the price effect in closing, while B explained that the resulting exclusion falls on marginal ordinary buyers—not simply wealthy buyers losing a “sweep.”
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): CON wins by showing the reserve harms moderate-budget outsiders rather than wealthy surge buyers, while dismantling both justifications: fees already address local disruption and only nearby fans benefit, and member eligibility collapses into paywalls, legacy lists, or hoardable unpaid rolls. PRO's normative claim that the dynamic sweep is not a right was memorable, but it ultimately conceded the price arithmetic without adequately justifying why zip code or tenure should impose that cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 answer on demand removal was decisive: because sellout queues vastly exceed capacity and discounted holders often would not have cleared dynamic prices, the hold removes supply without removing top demand. Side B later granted that arithmetic and could only frame the resulting higher prices as an acceptable cost.
