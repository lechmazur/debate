### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins because it adapted its case as the clash developed while CON largely stood still. PRO conceded real costs and limits: compulsion is a burden, safeguards cannot eliminate class advantage, and training costs money. It then showed why those concessions do not defeat the motion. Its answers to the pressure questions were direct. It proposed reserve status conditional on demonstrated competence, recurring exercises, deferral that postpones rather than erases the obligation, and audited allocation. It also turned the comparison back on CON: volunteer reserves need continuing training too, and better pay cannot guarantee that vacancies get filled. CON made credible points about skill atrophy, turnover, perceived legitimacy, and opportunity cost. However, its later speeches mostly restated those points without engaging PRO's specific design answers. CON's closing claim that PRO "never refutes" the training overhead misdescribes the exchange, since PRO addressed it through phasing and budgeted refreshers. CON's assertion that the problem is "retention, not raw headcount" was unsupported, and PRO's closing correctly flagged that it assumes the outcome in dispute. CON's best line was perceived legitimacy on cohesion, but PRO's comparative framing held up against it: imperfect sharing is still broader than letting the comfortable opt out entirely.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to the skill-atrophy question was the most decisive exchange. It made reserve status conditional on maintained competence and removed lapsed members from deployable lists. It then noted that volunteers also require recurring training, so the real question is extra trained people at an affordable cost, not training versus no training. This neutralized CON's main readiness attack, and CON never squarely responded to it.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): B won the comparative policy exchange. PRO made a serious case for predictable conscript pipelines and shared obligation, but B's recurring-turnover and training-overhead objection attacked the readiness mechanism directly, and PRO's safeguards (phasing, conditional reserve status, recurring exercises) softened rather than resolved it. B also turned the cohesion claim against PRO through visible state-enforced inequality, and answered A's fallback question with a plausible retention/headcount account rather than merely assuming volunteer success. The closing crystallized the same trade-off: scarce money and instructor time spent on rotating reluctant conscripts rather than practiced reserves. PRO's moral fairness point remained live but not enough to overcome the readiness and cost comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive exchange was its response to the fallback question: treating the gap as retention, not raw headcount, and turning mandatory churn into a permanent training and cost drag on the very reserves the program was meant to create.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): B won a clear but competitive debate by directly addressing skill atrophy through competence-based reserve status and recurring training, while showing that compulsion and continuing local service are compatible. A’s opportunity-cost, motivation, and legitimacy objections remained substantial, but A often asserted that incentives and retention would suffice without establishing that they could fill the relevant gaps. B’s closing exposed that assumption and framed service as complementary to professional forces rather than a replacement. A’s claim that B never answered the recurring-training objection was also inaccurate, weakening an otherwise forceful closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it answered both pressure questions directly, reframed the readiness comparison as continuing training for either model rather than training versus no training, and gave concrete answers on lapsed qualifications, relocation, deferrals, and placement audits.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins clearly by setting the decisive test early and holding it throughout: what does compulsion itself add, beyond what better-funded training and voluntary institutions could deliver with the same scarce instructors and money? PRO never squarely met that test. Its main support was a sweeping, unverified assertion that voluntary models have failed across Europe for decades. That claim was repeated rather than substantiated. When pressed, PRO narrowed its defense benefit to dispersed basic emergency skills. CON exploited this well, arguing that PRO cannot cost the scheme as light local first-aid training while crediting it with deployable military readiness. On cohesion, PRO named the equal-status contact conditions but did not show that compulsion reliably produces lasting trust. CON's lines "equal attendance is not equal burden" and "attendance is not capacity" kept that distinction live. PRO's closing point, that CON's fixes are features of a good mandatory system, was its sharpest moment, but it actually concedes that those benefits do not depend on the mandate. CON answered the pressure questions directly and honestly, conceding it cannot guarantee zero vacancies while reframing the comparison as the next best use of resources.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.5 response to PRO's A1 was decisive. It turned PRO's narrowed claim ("light, local refreshers" for dispersed basic skills) into a dilemma. Either the scheme is cheap civilian emergency training, which locally recruited responders could provide, or it is real military readiness, which requires costly equipment, leadership and unit preparation. Either way, PRO's cost and benefit claims could not both stand.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B wins by keeping the debate focused on what compulsion itself adds over well-funded voluntary alternatives. A's strongest claim is that voluntary recruitment has failed, but B repeatedly shows that past failure does not establish that mandatory service is the best use of scarce instructors, money, and time. B's most damaging move forces A into a dilemma: if national service is cheap, light, dispersed emergency training, local voluntary responders can do it; if it is genuine territorial defense, basic refreshers are insufficient. A never resolves that tension and its closing "unfixable gaps" claim remains asserted rather than demonstrated. The cohesion argument similarly fails because equal attendance is not equal sacrifice or actual trust. B answered pressure directly and used sharp comparative weighing rather than new evidence dumps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal that PRO cannot price the scheme as elementary emergency training while crediting it with military readiness was decisive. It exposed the internal tension between A's low-cost, light-refresher framing and its defense-readiness promise, while offering the relevant alternative of locally recruited civilian responders.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A consistently focused on the motion’s decisive issue: what compulsion adds beyond training, local recruitment, retention, and participant support available under either system. It answered both pressure questions directly and exposed Side B’s unsupported claims that voluntary mechanisms had exhausted their potential and could never fill distributed gaps. Side B offered a memorable free-riding and shared-sacrifice case, but repeatedly asserted rather than demonstrated Europe-wide voluntary failure, superior cost-effectiveness, and lasting cohesion. Its narrowing toward basic emergency skills also weakened its claimed military-readiness benefits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal decisively separated light local emergency training from deployable territorial defense, then showed that stipends, local recruitment, and carer support do not require compulsion. Side B never squarely closed that gap, and Side A’s closing crystallized it as “attendance is not capacity.”
