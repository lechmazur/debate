### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by turning CON's strongest point, that bus, bike, and loading space only works when it is continuous, connected, and enforced, into a burden CON could not carry. PRO's network-gap argument showed that those conditions cannot be met while most curb stays protected for parking. PRO also argued that the status quo is itself a blanket default. CON's answer to the second pressure question then conceded that conversions should come from parking: all-day storage on bus corridors, spaces near frequent transit, and bike-route gaps. That concession largely affirms the motion's direction and weakens the "blanket reversal" charge. PRO answered A1 and A2 directly. It defined the line through published block criteria and set a whole-day value metric rather than peak throughput, which neutralized CON's idle-lane and midnight-bus-bay points. CON's nurse/plumber/parent framing was vivid and stayed somewhat live. However, CON's closing misattributed its own concession, saying "PRO concedes the land must come from parking [A4.2]," which is CON's own reference ID. CON also never squarely showed why "more" curb for these uses amounts to an unmanaged category reversal once PRO had specified its exceptions. Both sides grounded claims in plausible mechanisms without fake statistics.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive sequence was B3.2 → A4.2 → B4.5/B5.1. PRO asked which non-parking curb CON would convert to build the continuous lanes CON demanded. CON answered with parking conversions. PRO then framed CON's position as "require a finished network, then deny the space to finish it," exposing a circularity CON never escaped.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): A won by exposing the status quo asymmetry in B's standard: B demanded continuous bus lanes, connected bike routes, and enforced loading zones before giving up parking, while treating the existing default protection of most parking as already earned. B's later concession that the needed conversions would have to come from parking strengthened A's core point. A also answered B's pressure questions more cleanly with block-level criteria and time-management/enforcement rather than a fixed percentage. B offered real whole-trip concerns, but those concerns became exceptions A could accommodate, not a reason to preserve most parking by default.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's most decisive exchange was turning B's continuity requirement back on itself: continuous bus and bike networks cannot be built while most gaps remain protected parking, and B ultimately conceded the conversion land must come from parking.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side A offered a serious whole-trip access case and exposed real risks from idle, disconnected, or unenforced facilities. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO opened strongly with a clear throughput metric and a vivid cascade story. CON reframed the clash more effectively around predictable flows versus dispersed, address-specific needs, and that framing held through the whole debate. PRO's best line was the concession charge: CON accepts pricing and selective conversion, so it supposedly shares PRO's principle. CON answered this cleanly. A priced, time-limited general curb is not "storage," and managing every curb is not the same as reserving most of it. PRO kept attacking "indefinite free storage" even though CON had expressly given up that position, which reads as a strawman. CON's exceptions dilemma did real damage: either guarantee every hard case and you have renamed parking, or ration bays and need gets fined away. PRO's A2 answer of "None," plus its admission that the hard cases need legal bays, effectively conceded the dilemma's first horn. CON also answered the falsifiability question with a concrete, testable condition. PRO's closing was crisp but did not escape that dilemma. PRO also cited a nonexistent reference ID (C1.2), a small execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2 answer and the dilemma in A4.3 were decisive. CON gave a concrete falsifiability condition, then showed that PRO's plan of permit bays for every necessary stop either recreates general parking or rations access. That turned PRO's "lottery" charge into a "smaller permit lottery with paperwork."
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B wins a clear but competitive debate. A built a strong efficiency and equity case against private car storage, but B successfully shifted the decisive standard to unpredictable near-door access. B answered pressure directly with a falsifiable threshold and exposed a dilemma in A's plan: either brief-access exceptions expand until they simply recreate permitted on-street parking, or scattered necessary trips must be predicted and rationed. The distinction between predictable flow and unpredictable need, and between doors passed and doors reached, gave B the cleaner comparative reason to protect a managed general-use curb while converting only where reserves are proven.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive rebuttal was turning A's brief-access solution into a dilemma: if every necessary address-specific stop gets a bay, PRO has merely renamed parking; if exceptions are rationed, the plumber, disabled passenger, or late-shift parent must prove a need the city predicted. This materially damaged A's strongest live route.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): B won narrowly by preserving the motion’s central comparison: allocate more curb by access and productivity rather than retain most of it as private-car storage. Its answers distinguished short, necessary stops from indefinite parking and exposed the first-arrival weakness in CON’s flexibility claim. A offered the sharper critique of dispersed, unpredictable access and effectively rejected B’s alleged concession, but too often attacked a stronger version of PRO’s case—reserving nearly every block or abolishing address-specific stops—and did not establish why pricing alone justifies protecting most parking.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions, explaining that necessary trips receive timed legal access while discretionary long-duration storage loses its default subsidy; this substantially blunted A’s strongest plumber, caregiver, and disabled-passenger objection.
