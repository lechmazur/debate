### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Both sides were sharp, but CON did more to shape and narrow the decisive clash. CON's opening landed early by attacking PRO's HIV analogy. Gene therapies depend on manufacturing know-how and capacity, not just patents, so a bare compulsory license does not obviously produce doses. PRO adapted sensibly into a supply-and-transfer duty, and that is a fair reading of "compel access." CON then made PRO pay for the shift. It noted that the new remedy no longer adds producers the way the HIV precedent did. It showed that adding "the cost of further capacity" to the remunerated price pushes the figure back toward the launch price. It also argued that the state could buy that bargain by contract today. CON's weighing was clean: a certain, ongoing investment chill over small programs versus a hypothetical seller who refuses all payment. It pressed that PRO never showed a real case of such refusal. CON's closing dilemma was memorable: either the safe-harbor price equals the launch price, so the statute changes nothing, or a buyer-set tribunal names the margin after the bet is sunk. PRO's best asset was CON's honest admission that no contract overrides refusal, and PRO pressed it well. But PRO leaned heavily on the repeated "veto" framing and never fully escaped the circularity charge or showed that refusal, rather than capacity and budgets, is the real-world bottleneck.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.1/B5.1 answer to PRO's Q1 was decisive. A price covering delivery, capacity expansion, and a portfolio return is roughly the launch price. So PRO's safe harbor either changes nothing or lets the buyer set the margin after the capital is sunk. That turned PRO's narrow-trigger defense into a dilemma PRO's closing only relabeled as "renaming the veto."
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): B made the stronger case for an enforceable backstop beyond voluntary bargaining and directly addressed manufacturing through compensated supply and know-how transfer. A raised substantial capacity and investment-risk objections, but its decisive comparison depended on unsupported assumptions that launch prices approximate necessary returns and tribunals necessarily ignore failed programmes. B did not fully resolve regulatory uncertainty or affordability, keeping the win competitive rather than decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response that licensing could bind the existing producer, rather than merely hand patents to an unqualified entrant, materially weakened A’s opening objection. A then honestly conceded that voluntary contracts cannot override refusal, leaving B with a distinct policy benefit that A’s alternatives did not supply.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by grounding the entire clash in the technical and economic realities specific to gene-editing therapies: viral vector bottlenecks, trade-secret manufacturing processes, specialist delivery networks, and tiny rare-disease patient cohorts. Side B pivoted capably from bare patent licensing to compensated originator supply-and-transfer orders, but Side A trapped that pivot in a decisive dilemma by Turn 8 and Turn 10. Either the tribunal's remunerated price covers delivery, new vector capacity, and portfolio-wide R&amp;D risk (which replicates high launch costs and could be contracted voluntarily without compulsion), or it forces prices down ex post on the single succeeding therapy while ignoring failed trials—chilling private capital precisely where ultra-rare genetic pipelines are frailest. Side B never squarely resolved that portfolio-risk asymmetry.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In Turn 8 ([A4.1]-[A4.6]), Side A turned Side B's own capacity fix ([B4.1]) against the motion by showing that adding new vector-plant costs to delivery and R&amp;D returns destroys the promise of an "affordable" price unless tribunals slash margins ex post while ignoring failed trials, then candidly conceded that voluntary contracts cannot override refusal before showing why real-world access fails from scarce capacity and budgets rather than irrational seller holdouts.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): PRO wins a competitive debate by making the decisive clash clearer. CON's opening landed a real hit: autologous gene therapies cannot be reverse-engineered the way small-molecule HIV and hepatitis C drugs can, so a compulsory licence does not deliver the capacity to make and give them. PRO adapted well. It pointed out the tension between "inert paper right" and "devastating seizure," showed that delivery capacity is a burden both sides carry, and answered the liability question cleanly. PRO's strongest move was attacking CON's alternative, the advance commitment, on leverage grounds: a sole seller with a surviving patent loses little by walking away, so a tender only has teeth if the state has a fallback. That leaves CON's alternative partly dependent on the lever it opposes. CON's best live route was reference pricing and revenue erosion in rich countries, sharpened by tying [A1.1] to the motion's universal scope. PRO answered with "affordable relative to the issuing country" but never fully explained why a rich-country pathway for its own poor patients would not erode development revenue. That kept CON competitive. Still, CON's closing largely restated the "empty book" claim without rebutting the leverage critique. CON's insurability argument was plausible but speculative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The advance-commitment exchange was decisive. PRO's Q1 asked what makes a monopolist accept an affordable price when refusing costs nothing. CON answered that volume and certainty beat an empty book. PRO's closing then argued that an empty book threatens only a seller who can be replaced, so CON's mechanism borrows its teeth from compulsory licensing. CON's closing reasserted its original claim rather than defusing this point.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): A narrowly wins through sharper comparative rebuttal. Guaranteed volume and earlier payment provide a concrete bargaining mechanism that B never successfully defeats. A also exposes why country-relative affordability does not establish B's claim that rich-market revenues remain intact under domestic compulsory licensing. B convincingly makes hospital capacity a shared burden and answers the liability question directly. A's unsupported certainty about investor flight and insurance limits the margin, but B leaves the central revenue tradeoff insufficiently answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing directly defeats B's claim that procurement has leverage only through compulsory licensing: a surviving patent does not restore revenue lost by refusing guaranteed sales. This materially weakens B's characterization of A's alternative as merely asking the monopolist nicely.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): In a high-level, fiercely contested debate, Side A (CON) won by grounding every argument in the specific operational and economic realities of autologous gene-editing platforms while dismantling Side B's R&amp;D insulation claim. Side B scored real points by framing hospital transplant capacity as a shared burden and pressing the bargaining weakness of voluntary tenders against an unthreatened monopolist. However, Side A proved that compelling trade secrets and cell-processing know-how creates dual failures: it chills ex-ante platform investment—especially since Side B never answered A's point that "severe, curable disease" covers virtually every successful gene-editing therapy—while still failing to supply validated manufacturing or insurability for a second producer. Crucially, Side A trapped Side B on scope, showing that combining a universal mandate ("Countries") with Side B's inclusion of poor patients in rich countries forces domestic price compulsion inside the very high-income markets that fund future cures.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Q2 [A3.2] and follow-up in [A4.4]-[A4.6] caught Side B in a fatal textual bind between including "poor patients in rich countries" [B1.1] and claiming the motion "never touches German prices" [B3.2]; when Side B refused to withdraw either claim and defined "affordable" relative to each issuing country [B4.3], Side A showed that rich countries would compel down their own domestic benchmarks, gutting Side B's core defense that licensing only affects zero-revenue markets.
