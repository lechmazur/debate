### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): CON built the cleaner path to victory by turning PRO's own enforcement and weak-state arguments. The satellite-monitoring point was decisive: if satellite pings can police reserves, they can also police industrial-exclusion zones without banning local canoes. CON also exposed a live contradiction in PRO's compensation promise, showing that poor states cannot both lack treasuries and fund years of lost catch. PRO's insurance and collapse-risk framing was strong, but CON's shore-enforcement and paper-park rebuttals left the 30% no-take mandate looking less enforceable and more immediately harmful in the very dependent communities the motion highlights.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's satellite-transponder refutation was the most decisive exchange: it took PRO's core claim that reserves are simple for weak states and showed the same tool could ban industrial trawlers selectively, undercutting the need for a blanket no-take zone while sparing local fishers.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side A built a powerful insurance framing ("planned partial closure vs. unplanned total collapse") and skillfully forced B to defend selective tools as "no-take zones with expiry dates," but B consistently dismantled A's routes on the ground A chose. B's beach/market enforcement answer (gear and size rules are checked on land, needing no sea patrols) directly neutralized A's "watch only 30%" pressure question, and B's satellite inversion (transponders track industrial ships, not wooden canoes) exposed a real flaw in A's weak-state enforcement story. B also caught a live internal contradiction: A funds compensation by redirecting foreign-fleet access fees while simultaneously expelling those fleets. A's "imperfect cushion" fallback preserved the weighing but conceded the funding damage. Meanwhile A never resolved B's counter that upper-size limits and trawl bans protect old females everywhere, nor B's point that displaced fleets crowd the open 70% and boundary-fish mobile stocks — using A's own edge-fishing evidence. A's closing was sharp ("a fish measured on the sand is already dead"), but B answered it (nets seized on shore never enter the water) and landed the memorable pantry/looters reframe. Both sides argued the motion as written with plausible, well-grounded examples and no fake sourcing; B simply won more of the decisive exchanges.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1–B4.3): the answer that gear rules enforce on shore at beaches and markets demolished A's "how do you police 100% of the sea" pressure question, and the follow-up catch — that A's compensation plan relies on foreign-fleet fees A's own plan eliminates — was the debate's most damaging single exchange.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B better resolved the motion’s hardest case: poor, fishing-dependent communities with weak states. B undercut A’s compensation story by showing foreign-access fees cannot fund a policy that expels those fleets, and answered enforcement pressure with a sharper shore-versus-sea distinction. A’s insurance and spillover case stayed coherent but never fully neutralized placement, poaching incentives, or the certainty of immediate hardship.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal, which showed that satellite exclusion of industrial fleets does not require banning local fishers, that beach and market rules avoid sea patrols, and that A’s fee-funded transition contradicts expelling the fleets that pay those fees.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): A (CON) won by converting PRO’s concessions into an asymmetry: coastal villages still give up grounds, so the day-one loss is certain, while the promised benefits depend on stipends, subsidy cuts, patrols, and currents. A also made the effort-reduction point stick—cutting subsidies and better management, not the 30% floor, do the heavy lifting—while PRO’s political-enforceability appeal never fully overcame the paper-park/conditional-compensation problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A’s exploitation of B’s concession that coastal villages do give up part of their local grounds [B4.1] and its reframe of B2.4/B4.1: if reserves are sited offshore to spare villages, spillover cannot reliably reach dependent reefs; if nearshore, the cost is immediate and guaranteed. This forced PRO to defend the 30% quota as more than a symbolic add-on.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. B's core strategy—showing that every mechanism A invoked (subsidy reform, enforcement, community-chosen reserves) was B's own toolkit, leaving the 30% figure as pure added risk—survived the whole exchange. B exposed A's placement dilemma (offshore closures don't restock village reefs; nearshore closures take canoe fishers' grounds), and A's hybrid answer in Rebuttal 2 conceded the certain, immediate cost while the benefits stayed conditional on stipends and currents. A's best pressure—the political-will contradiction—was answered well by B's "graceful degradation" comparison and the point that subsidy cuts are A's plan too. A landed sharp moves (boundary nets as spillover, visible mapped boundaries resisting erosion), but never neutralized the asymmetry that B crystallized in closing: unconditional loss for the poorest fisher, conditional benefits from a captured government. B's framing was also cleaner and more memorable without fake precision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1–B2.2, where B caught A's retreat that 30% "never requires walling off a canoe fisher's home bay" and showed it gutted A's own spillover story; this forced A's Rebuttal 2 concession that villages "do give up part of their local grounds," which B then weaponized into the decisive cost-benefit asymmetry in B4.5 and B5.2.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the motion's real burden in view: a rigid at-least-30% no-take rule even where communities depend on fishing. B dismantled A's false choice early, then used A's own concessions—that closures can be routed partly offshore, that villages still lose some grounds, and that weak governments bend under pressure—to show certain near-term losses against conditional stipends, currents, and enforcement. A had a credible biological and capacity case, but never cleanly showed what the 30% floor uniquely adds once subsidy cuts, spawning closures, and effort rules are granted. B's failure-mode comparison (partial compliance still helps; an unenforced 30% mainly harms the poorest) was the cleaner vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal, which answered the capacity and breeder questions and then locked onto A's admission that villages give up local grounds, contrasting certain costs with hoped-for larvae, stipends, and political will.
