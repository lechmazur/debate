### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by showing that a limit on message timing targets the wrong variable. Its core argument held throughout: what matters is whether the employer commands the worker's time. The "4pm assignment with a morning deadline" example shows the rule can be complied with while still taking the evening. The "clerk who opts into shift offers" example shows it can forbid harmless or wanted contact. PRO never squarely answered either case. PRO's opening and rebuttals were polished but leaned on general claims about collective action and coercion. PRO also overcommitted in its answers to the pressure questions. Saying that repeated after-hours outreach is itself a violation walked straight into CON's opt-in counterexample. Saying a manager cannot cite off-duty work in a promotion invited CON's point that "an inbox records when someone wrote, not why someone advanced." PRO's best line was that a duty-based standard forces workers into retrospective litigation. CON blunted it by requiring employers to set out staffing responsibility in advance and by treating the shop's own plans and follow-ups as evidence. PRO's closing largely restated its opening themes and did not engage CON's concrete cases. CON's closing sharpened the comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.4–A4.5 exchange was decisive. It used PRO's own A2 answer, that repeated outreach alone is a violation, to show the rule both punishes wanted shift offers and is evaded by moving the demand to 4pm. In contrast, a duty-based standard forces the employer to change the deadline, the staffing, or the pay. PRO never answered this.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A built a clearer route to victory. Its core argument was that after-hours messaging creates unpaid standby and coercive ambient pressure that individual workers cannot safely resist, and its rebuttals met Side B's strongest challenge directly. Side B's work-duty framing was sharp and original in the opening, but Side A exposed a costly tension: B conceded the phone buzz at dinner is standby, yet its remedy still asks vulnerable employees to prove after the fact that a duty was imposed. A's pressure questions then forced B into a difficult tradeoff. B answered competently, but A's answers were cleaner and more aligned with the proposition as written: repeated outreach itself triggers protection because compliance cannot rest on proving the employer's subjective intent. B's late timestamp critique was the best CON point, but A could reasonably absorb it by noting the rule also bars rewarding off-duty availability, shifting the issue to staffing. Overall, A was more consistent and decisive after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's answer to B's promotion question was the most decisive exchange: A flatly said a manager cannot cite off-duty work as a deciding promotion factor without violating the right, which neutralized B's strongest practical objection by converting informal evening availability into a formal, compensable staffing problem rather than an unreviewable promotion gray zone.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by making the decisive clash about enforcement and targeting rather than good intentions. A gave a clear, rhetorically strong case that ambient after-hours messages create coercive standby pressure, but B repeatedly showed that a communications ban may miss workload coercion, be evaded by earlier instructions, and suppress voluntary opportunities. A’s answers were crisp but overbroad, leaving B’s 4 p.m. assignment and requested-shift-offers points live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal best resolved the debate by comparing the two enforcement regimes: PRO’s timestamp rule can be avoided by moving the demand earlier, while CON’s duty-based rule forces employers to staff, pay, or move deadlines.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO wins clearly because it built a precise, consistent framework and kept it intact under pressure. The core distinction is between a favor, which anyone may decline, and a duty, which must be assigned and paid for. That distinction answered each CON line in turn. Asynchronous posting stays legal, so the parent who works after bedtime is unaffected. Tokyo questions can wait, or else someone must be formally assigned to cover them. Enforcement targets concrete employer-imposed duties, not hidden motives. CON's main route was that liability-averse firms will impose blunt digital lockouts, and PRO squarely answered it. A rule that expressly permits evening posts to mutable channels makes shutoffs unnecessary. Shutoffs also fail to stop the manager who simply calls instead. CON's R2 dilemma was its strongest moment: either the law creates liability that drives shutoffs, or it leaves the dinner-table problem unsolved. PRO had partly pre-empted it, however. Its fix of keeping assignments open through the next shift solves the allocation problem without shutting any server. CON also weakened itself in several ways: - It conceded no one had violated the right in the Q1 scenario, after earlier claiming workers would be "legally barred from responding." - It repeatedly mischaracterized PRO as wanting to "outlaw responsiveness" or ban interactions entirely. - Its closing largely recapped the lockout and outsourcing assertions without new engagement. PRO's closing, by contrast, crystallized a memorable decision rule: employers should not get "the reliability of a duty at the price of a favor."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 and Q1 exchange was decisive. PRO showed that a parent posting at nine, one colleague replying, and another waiting until morning violates nothing. That forced CON to admit "legally, no one has violated the statute." It also exposed CON's lockout mechanism as speculative overcompliance rather than a consequence of the policy as defined.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A wins because its duty-vs-favor framing survived the exchange: asynchronous sending can remain allowed while employees may mute, and only genuinely synchronous coverage requires a paid, assigned duty. B's strongest harm—that legal liability will push employers to impose blunt digital curfews and offshoring—was plausible but speculative and repeatedly ignored A's design that sending after-hours to a mutable channel is not a violation. B also could not fully answer A's Tokyo pressure: if the question can wait, no rota is needed; if it cannot wait, coverage is a real duty, not an attack on flexibility.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's async-versus-synchronous rebuttal and Tokyo pressure were decisive. It forced B to either admit the message could wait or concede someone was expected to be on call, which undercut B's claim that the right to disconnect necessarily requires rigid rotas or destroys international collaboration.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by building a clearer, more workable account of the right to disconnect: it protects workers from being penalized for not answering after-hours messages while still allowing asynchronous posting, voluntary response, and genuine coverage arrangements. Side B’s flexibility and offshore concerns were forceful, but they relied too heavily on an overstated compliance cascade in which any after-hours communication triggers blunt digital lockouts. A directly answered the key pressures, especially on promotion penalties and international handoffs, and closed with the cleaner decision rule: employers may ask for a favor and accept no answer, or assign duty and pay for coverage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal and closing together defused Side B’s dilemma by separating voluntary responsiveness from imposed availability: if nobody volunteers and no duty was assigned, no worker has failed; if coverage is truly needed, the employer must identify and compensate it.
