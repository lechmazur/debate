### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a competitive debate. Its strongest route is lead time: deflection works only if capability exists before a threat is confirmed, and spacecraft cannot be crash-developed on a deadline. PRO also reframed "permanent" as a refreshed, exercised program rather than a frozen warehouse. That move blunted CON's rigidity attack and stayed within the motion. CON's best material was real. It argued that the correct tool depends on the object, that impacting a rubble pile could shatter it, and that a full portfolio either guesses wrong or costs far more than a "rounding error." Its late answer that the existing space industrial base keeps pads and crews warm was also a solid reply to PRO's mechanism question. But CON never fully neutralized PRO's point that this base keeps launch capacity warm without keeping asteroid-specific competence warm. CON's own wording, that asteroid-specific capability "decays without exercise," let PRO turn this point in the closing. CON's first rebuttal was thinner and ended with an all-caps slogan. PRO's "eyes and no hands" framing and its before-versus-after-the-event distinction made the clash clearer. PRO had some execution costs. It overstated CON's concession on surveys, used a loosely sourced budget comparison, and claimed CON had never answered its mechanism question before CON's second rebuttal had been given. These trim PRO's margin but do not reverse the result.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B2.5–B2.7 was the most decisive exchange. It turned CON's own premises, that gravity tractors need decades and that rubble piles need characterization, into reasons for standing capability, arguing that reconnaissance and tested hardware must already exist when a threat appears. CON's reply in A4.1, renting the existing industrial base, was its best counter. PRO then exploited it in B5.3: that base keeps the pads warm but leaves the asteroid-specific mission cold.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): B wins by better distinguishing sustained operational readiness from a frozen fleet. A's existing-space-industry alternative credibly answers who maintains launch capacity, but less convincingly addresses tested deflection systems and exercised response procedures. A repeatedly returns to obsolete inventory despite B's explicit refresh model. B's inadequately supported cheapness claims, overstatements of A's position, and premature accusation of unanswered questions keep this competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response that permanent infrastructure can refresh hardware and maintain a portfolio materially weakens A's central rigidity objection. A's subsequent appeal to shared launch infrastructure is useful, but does not establish that adaptable engineering and permanent deflection readiness are incompatible.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): A built the stronger comparative case by defining permanence as refreshed hardware, characterization, launch relationships, and rehearsed governance rather than a frozen warehouse. B applied real pressure on cost, uncertain threat characteristics, and short-notice scenarios, and directly answered the industrial-base question. However, B never fully explained why research plus generic launch capacity would preserve asteroid-specific readiness, and its closing returned to the “frozen fleet” framing A had already answered. A’s cost case remained thin and its claim that B never answered was overstated, but its warning-time and capability comparison survived more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first rebuttal turned B’s warning-time and reconnaissance objections into reasons for continuously maintained capability. B’s later industrial-base answer addressed generic launch capacity, but A’s response about mission-specific exercises and decision protocols left the more damaging unresolved gap.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON wins on a clear but competitive margin. Its strongest move was the decades-or-months dilemma. Long warning lets a tailored mission be built fresh. Very short warning means civil defense, not interceptors. Either way, a targetless standing fleet adds little. CON also exposed a real drift in PRO's case: it moved from "maintained vehicles" to surveys and protocols to "pre-qualified modular stacks." CON argued that the survey part PRO leaned on is really detection and monitoring, which CON fully endorses funding. The motion's distinctive element is deflection infrastructure, and that element was left thinly defended. PRO's best ground was the intermediate case of years rather than decades of warning. Its A1/A2 answers were direct and plausible: retained teams, qualified components, and a pre-authorized command chain. CON countered with three points: the existing aerospace and defense base, DART having been built without a standing fleet, and the need to redesign for each specific target. PRO's closing never answered the redesign point. PRO also kept relying on the detection-bottleneck "contradiction," which CON had defused by distinguishing well-funded monitoring from neglect. CON's clipped second rebuttal is a real execution cost, but its core points were already delivered. PRO's repeated tagline was memorable but became less persuasive once its scope narrowed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.1–A2.3 had the most impact. It showed that PRO's rebuttal had swapped "maintained vehicles" for surveys and protocols. It also turned the detection-bottleneck point against deployable hardware: deflection hardware adds no telescope coverage, and short-warning cases call for civil defense rather than a fleet. This set up the drift narrative that PRO never fully escaped.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): B wins on the intermediate-warning case: maintained components, launch arrangements, and practiced authorization plausibly preserve response time that surge funding cannot simply purchase. A correctly separates detection from deflection and raises meaningful opportunity costs, but does not substantiate its decisive claims that deflectable threats provide decades of warning or that readiness saves only months. A also unjustifiably narrows permanent infrastructure to a targetless parked fleet. B’s repeated equation of research with neglect and incomplete cost comparison keep this competitive; A’s clipped rebuttal adds a modest execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure question about years rather than decades exposed A’s false binary. B then directly identified capabilities that reduce preparation delays. A’s existing-aerospace-base response was relevant, but did not establish that general industrial capacity eliminates the value of dedicated qualification, launch planning, and authorization readiness.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by keeping the motion’s distinguishing burden—permanent deflection deployment rather than sustained detection and research—at the center. A’s strongest argument was the intermediate-warning case, and A answered both pressure questions with concrete latency-reducing capabilities. However, B directly answered that existing aerospace capacity plus target-specific construction could supply much of that capability, while offering clearer opportunity-cost and institutional-atrophy weighing. B sometimes overstated A’s supposed retreat and assumed decades of warning too confidently, and its clipped rebuttal costs execution credit, but its core comparison survived more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal most decisively separated telescope coverage from deflection hardware and answered A’s years-not-decades scenario by invoking the existing aerospace base, forcing A to prove the marginal value of a dedicated targetless program rather than merely the value of preparedness.
