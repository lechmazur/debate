### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A built a coherent ability-to-pay case for taxing standing wealth and answered pressure questions with a creative true-up mechanism and capped deferral. But Side B consistently won the decisive clashes: the private-manufacturer valuation asymmetry (equally wealthy owners facing different bills based on priceability), the worker analogy showing capacity does not justify repeatedly taxing the same principal, and the devastating true-up paradox in B4.5/B5.2 (a sale price cannot distinguish past undervaluation from a recent gain, since "if the jump reflects a real recent gain" states exactly what the collector must prove). B's convergence argument — that both sides end up deferring collection at sale with interest for illiquid stakes, so the levy only adds a hard-to-price bill on the principal — went essentially unanswered in A's closing, which instead leaned on the "concession decides the debate" line that B5.1 directly turned back. A's principled core (standing wealth confers power) survived but was never given a normative foundation beyond assertion, while B's alternative (mark-to-market on listed gains, deferral with interest on private gains, taxing transfers) was specified and defended across every round. B made the debate easier to decide and answered more of the live pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2: the true-up paradox ("Same sale price, same prior assessment, entirely different back taxes") plus the convergence point that PRO now accepts collection at sale with interest for illiquid owners, so the systems differ only in what the collector must prove — a gain between two endpoints versus a whole history of hypothetical prices.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won solidly by neutralizing Side A's core grievances—untaxed appreciation, buy-borrow-die, and dynastic compounding—via annual taxation of listed gains, realization-plus-interest on private gains, and transfer taxes, while proving that an annual net worth tax creates unworkable valuation asymmetries. Side A argued coherently that standing wealth confers ongoing power, but its administrative patches collapsed under Side B's questioning: conceding interest-bearing deferral for illiquid stakes surrendered earlier cash collection, and its proposed sale-time true-up could not separate a late value jump from years of undervaluation. Side B also overcame Side A's closing weighing by showing that valuation ranges do not merely cause minor horizontal error, but systematically penalize transparency and encourage shifting wealth into opaque assets.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B3.1], Side B asked how Side A could tell, if a private firm sells for twice its safe-harbor valuation, whether it recently jumped in value or enjoyed years of tax discounts. When Side A proposed a true-up in [A4.1] charging back taxes for undervalued years unless "the jump reflects a real recent gain," Side B dismantled the circularity in [B4.5]-[B4.6], showing that an identical sale price follows both a last-minute contract win and years of underassessment—meaning a sale reveals endpoint gain for an income tax but still leaves a wealth-tax collector unable to establish the intervening annual values.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B neutralized the core borrow-spend-bequeath loophole by taxing listed gains annually and private gains at transfer with interest, without taxing principal repeatedly. B also showed annual valuation systematically rewards opacity, while Side A's safe-harbor true-up and illiquidity deferral conceded much of the timing advantage and left the valuation problem unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 decisively dismantled the true-up: a final sale price cannot distinguish a recent gain from years of prior undervaluation, so the collector would still need the missing historical prices that make annual private-asset valuation unfair.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A (PRO) built the stronger path to victory. The core clash became whether holding wealth itself constitutes taxable capacity, and A's optionality case (a portfolio household can borrow, hold, or pass on assets while a salary household cannot) was never squarely neutralized. B's best response — that drawing on a portfolio is already reachable by realization or consumption taxes — failed to cover borrowing against shares, and A explicitly flagged the hold-vs-sell asymmetry [A4.5] without a real answer. B's practical objections (compounding liens, contested appraisals) landed, but A's symmetry arguments survived: inheritance tax forces the same liquidity crunch at transfer, and A correctly exposed that B's measurement defense evaporates for publicly traded portfolios, reducing B's case to the asserted "stock is not flow" category error, which is largely the conclusion restated. A's pressure questions were sharper, both sides answered pressure honestly with correct format, and A's closing crystallized the means-vs-measurement distinction memorably. B was a strong, disciplined opponent with effective rhetoric, but A made the debate easier to decide and left the more decisive live route standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 [A4.1-A4.5]: after conceding honestly that heirs may eventually surrender equity, A pivoted to the symmetric point that an inheritance tax also forces a sale without creating cash, then delivered the sharpest blow of the debate — for publicly traded shares the valuation defense collapses entirely, forcing B to defend the pure normative claim that unrealized holding confers no taxable capacity. B's [B4.3] response (volatility, no consumed resource) restated the contested premise rather than answering it.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A built the cleaner comparative path to victory on the proposition as written. Side A showed that relying on income and consumption taxes treats households with identical paychecks and spending as equally able to contribute even when one holds vast net worth, and that realization and inheritance taxes ignore decades of economic capacity while penalizing rebalancing over holding. Side B kept the round competitive by exposing the real risk that deferred wealth-tax liens and interest persist even if asset values later fall, but Side B incurred a minor opening clipping cost, leaned heavily on inheritance taxes outside income and consumption taxation, and could not overcome Side A's point that conservative valuations above a high threshold beat assigning large fortunes zero annual weight.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B2.7], Side B claimed that inheritance and gift taxes avoid wealth-tax problems by pricing wealth when a transfer makes it liquid. Side A's [A3.1] pressure question asked how leaving a private factory to a child without selling shares creates either a market price or cash, forcing Side B in [B4.1] to concede it supplies neither and must also rely on appraisals and liens.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): PRO maintained a cleaner comparative case: equal income and spending can conceal vastly different economic capacity, liquidity should affect timing rather than erase liability, and an imperfect appraisal is better than assigning large wealth zero annual weight. CON's stock-versus-flow and valuation objections remained forceful, but PRO showed that inheritance taxation faces similar valuation and cash problems while ignoring how long wealth was held.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it conceded eventual equity payment under deferral, then turned the liquidity and appraisal objections against CON's inheritance alternative and argued that one transfer valuation cannot distinguish holding a fortune for one year from holding it for forty.
