### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): This was a close, high-quality exchange. CON opened strongly on overbreadth and delay, and its "amendment, not an answer" attack on PRO's framework-agreement tiers was sharp. Over the debate, though, that route weakened. PRO's Q2 forced CON to admit that its own pre-deployment duties need the same lines between routine and material systems. CON then said outright that "line-drawing is inevitable," which blunted the dragnet and chokepoint harms. PRO also plausibly argued that negotiating a tiered protocol is still negotiating on deployment, so it is within the motion rather than a rewrite of it. The decisive remaining clash was direct non-waivable rights versus bargaining above statutory floors. PRO framed this cleanly as "floors plus bargaining versus floors plus executive autocracy." CON answered with reasonableness standards and binding consultation. PRO effectively pointed out that these still leave management drafting the system and workers relying on uncertain enforcement after the fact. CON's "traded-away safeguards" point was partly met by PRO's claim that statutory remedies stay independently enforceable. CON's case stayed coherent and well-weighed, but its model drifted toward something resembling negotiation. PRO held the slightly cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 and the A4.4 follow-up turned the line-drawing problem back on CON's direct-rules model, showing it needs the same triggers. This led to CON's concession that "line-drawing is inevitable," which defused much of CON's opening overbreadth and delay case.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Both sides debated the motion as written, answered pressure questions directly with A1/A2 structure, and avoided fake sourcing. Side A built a coherent case: statutory floors plus elected bargaining beats executive unilateralism in the "harsh but lawful" zone, and the closing image of a "receipt for complaints" was memorable. But Side B controlled the decisive framing: the motion's blanket duty on all workplace AI deployments, and A's repeated reliance on framework agreements, tiered clearances, and low-risk carve-outs functionally conceded overbreadth — B's "amendment, not answer" thread ran from B2.1 through B5.1 and was never fully dissolved. Critically, B4.1 and B5.2 blunted A's strongest closer ("executives holding the pen") by specifying non-waivable pre-deployment justification duties where ignoring a nurse's warning is a violation, not a courtesy — matching A's benefits without a bargaining gate. A's labor-contract analogy for framework agreements was clever but strained against the motion's plain text. Narrow but real edge to B on framing, weighing, and answering the most important pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1: rejecting A's "national pick-rate formula" strawman by noting law uses reasonableness, justification, disclosure, audit, and remedies rather than one number — combined with making ignored worker warnings auditable violations — directly dismantled A's core "executive autocracy above the floor" route.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): A wins narrowly because its framework-agreement model plausibly answers the dragnet objection while preserving negotiation over all AI, and its above-the-floor argument survives: statutory minimums and reasonableness duties still leave daily pacing, appeals, and retraining to whoever holds pre-deployment power. B's direct-rights alternative is strong, but A effectively shows that recorded, answerable consultation still lets management deploy first and litigate later.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it recast tiers as one negotiated deployment protocol covering every system, and compared bargaining not to perfect statutory protection but to unilateral executive choice above non-waivable floors.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): CON built a dilemma early and carried it through the whole debate. Either the duty to bargain gives councils hold-up leverage, or it collapses into the non-binding consultation that PRO's own opening called structurally weak. PRO defended competently. It limited the duty to material changes, proposed a fast-track for updates, and tied bad-faith review to process rather than outcomes. In Q1, PRO also landed a fair consistency challenge: if audits and disclosures are administrable, why is good-faith negotiation not? But PRO's direct A1 answer conceded the cost of its own defense. Management may reject every proposal and deploy the AI unaltered. That let CON reframe the mandate as a heavy institutional burden for record-building that direct statutory disclosure, audits and appeals could also deliver. Those burdens include council elections, union and non-union fragmentation, and disputes over what counts as "AI." PRO's best surviving route was that councils act as local fact-finders before lock-in. CON answered it adequately: real bugs surface in live workflows, and statutory appeals reach applicants and smaller-firm workers whom councils exclude. CON also scored on two further points: - PRO's shift to a "material workplace effect" trigger strains against a motion that is specifically about AI systems. - PRO's fix of keeping an inseparable update running while not using its outputs is operationally awkward. PRO's closing honestly embraced "answerability, not winning," which is coherent. Still, it left CON's comparison standing: direct, uniform standards can ban abusive metrics outright, whereas councils can only flag them. Overall it was a clear but competitive CON win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B3.1 into A4.1, then B4.3 and B5.1. CON's pressure question forced PRO to admit that an employer may reject every council proposal and deploy the unaltered AI. CON then turned that admission into the dilemma's "toothless" horn: heavy institutional friction for no guaranteed protection, which its direct-regulation alternative could match or exceed.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Both sides ran disciplined, well-grounded cases built on plausible mechanisms rather than fake citations. The decisive clash was B's dilemma: the mandate is either toothless consultation or hold-up leverage. A answered B's sharp pressure questions directly and concretely (A1: employer may deploy the unaltered AI after an honest process; A2: fast-track, firewall, or standstill on changed outputs), and importantly turned B's own alternative into support — if B's ex ante audits, disclosures, and appeals are administrable, so are notice and reasoned meetings, and councils supply the local facts (bathroom breaks, complex calls, unstable shifts) that any disclosure regime needs. B's closing counter — that "the value is not that councils win" shrinks the mandate into record-building with heavy institutional costs (elections, fractured partially unionized workplaces, "AI vs. spreadsheet" line-drawing, vendor update paralysis) — was B's best material and landed hard, but A had already argued that forced reasoned engagement before lock-in plus a factual trail for appeals is real, non-zero protection, and B never fully neutralized the "workers as local sensor" turn. B's operational objections (multi-tenant vendors, live scheduler paradox) were clever but somewhat speculative against A's offered workarounds. A made the debate easier to decide by resolving the central dilemma rather than merely restating it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The B3 pressure questions meeting A4: B forced A to choose between veto and toothlessness ("may the employer deploy the unaltered AI anyway?"), and A's direct A1/A2 concession-plus-explanation (deployment allowed after honest process; process review checks procedure, not design) defused the dilemma while B4/B5's "zero guaranteed protection" counter kept the exchange genuinely live to the end.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B sustained the central dilemma: if employers may reject every proposal and deploy after impasse, mandatory bargaining collapses toward the consultation Side A condemned; if courts scrutinize genuine consideration, councils gain costly hold-up leverage. Side B also answered the administrability challenge by distinguishing objective statutory audits and appeals from bilateral good-faith litigation, and showed direct standards could gather local facts and ban abusive outcomes without elections, AI-definition disputes, or update standstills. Side A's bounded-process model was clear and honest, but its closing emphasis on record-building made Side B's less burdensome alternative comparison decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal most decisively turned Side A's answers against the mandate, especially the concession that management could deploy unaltered AI and the contradictory instruction to run an inseparable live scheduler while not using its scheduling outputs during a standstill.
