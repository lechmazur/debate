### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built the cleaner path to victory. The core clash was whether a 90-day technology-access mandate adds value given slow qualification and capacity constraints. B's circularity argument (a hub cannot reserve a line without guaranteed access, yet a developer can cite the idle line as proof no partner is ready) was original and never squarely answered. A's strongest pressure — the capacity and safety arguments — was effectively neutralized: B's Q2 forced A to concede in A4.3 that the deadline only requires technology sharing, not production completion, which undercut A's entire corner-cutting line. A's answer to B's Q1 ("the hub should never be able to insist on access") honestly but damagingly conceded that a fully prepared hub could be left idle indefinitely, which B exploited in closing ("End the license holder's power to say 'never'"). B also handled the innovation argument by distinguishing paid nonexclusive licensing from expropriation and noting compensation can be priced into advance contracts; A mostly repeated the claim. A's late pivot to "developers might delay authorization" appeared first in Rebuttal 2, a mild format cost. B's progression across rounds — narrowing to who bears the uncertainty, with concrete concessions (regulator retains veto, royalties, donations continue) — made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question Q2 (how would skipping a safety test help meet a technology-sharing deadline?) and A's A4.3 concession that the 90 days only require sharing, not production, decisively deflated CON's safety corner-cutting argument and clarified the mandate's actual scope in PRO's favor.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won dominantly by breaking the classic stalemate between intellectual property and physical capacity while exposing fatal contradictions in Side B's case. Side A cleanly distinguished a 90-day deadline to share documentation and trainers from regulatory approval to release batches, accounted for R&amp;D incentives via royalties and advance procurement pricing, and showed that developing nations cannot rationally fund standby capacity if developers retain an indefinite veto. Side B repeated boilerplate talking points, misattributed its own AstraZeneca example to Side A, and contradicted its own Rebuttal 2 concessions by continuing to claim the 90-day sharing window would force rushed safety testing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's capture of Side B's regional hub proposal across Rebuttal 1, Pressure Questions, and Closing: by asking what happens when a fully staffed, equipped hub is refused a paid license and how skipping batch safety tests could help meet a documentation-sharing deadline, Side A forced Side B to concede in B4.3 that skipping tests would not help meet the deadline (collapsing B's safety impact) and in B4.1 that a ready hub lacking only the developer's process knowledge should "never" be able to insist on access (undermining B's own preparedness alternative).
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B defended the decisive mechanism: legal access need not create factories or waive regulation to let capable producers begin qualification rather than wait indefinitely for permission. Its responses on compensation, voluntary partnerships, and safety kept those policies complementary. Side A repeated “capacity, not consent” even under the ready-hub hypothetical, conceded that the deadline concerned sharing rather than production, yet continued linking it to unsafe output. Its “never” answer left capable but refused producers without recourse, which B exploited effectively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure questions forced A to endorse indefinite refusal and admit that skipping safety tests was irrelevant to the technology-access deadline. B’s A1/A2 answers then cleanly separated earlier qualification from instant production while preserving regulators’ power to reject failed batches.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won by converting PRO's own concessions into decisive clash. The "outbid" reframe (A2.1) showed that relocating factories does not by itself defeat wealthy buyers, and PRO's answer — local governments can prioritize domestic output — never addressed the poorer neighbor without a factory or the financing gap (A4.5). CON's pressure question about postponing transfer to repair a line serving low-income clinics forced PRO into a flat "No" (B4.1), which CON exploited memorably (A4.4, A5.3): the deadline removes discretion even when discretion saves lives. On the obstruction-trigger alternative, CON refined the mechanism across rounds (assess in advance, order on refusal, compensation, no need to prove bad faith), blunting PRO's core speed argument, and crystallized it with "why require governments to disregard what they learn?" PRO's rebuttals grew repetitive (COVAX outbid, Omicron, "only mechanism," "tragic cycle") and never resolved whether a universal deadline beats case-by-case orders when both require technical evaluation. Both sides followed format cleanly and used plausible background knowledge without fake precision, but PRO's later speeches recapitulated rather than adapted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.4 turn on PRO's pressure-question answer: when PRO said governments may not postpone transfer to keep specialists repairing a line serving low-income clinics, CON seized the concession — "Calling that a planning failure does nothing for the clinic awaiting tomorrow's shipment" — and carried it through the closing ("Do not make delaying a repair the price of enforcing that right"), making PRO's rigidity the decisive image of the debate.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won solidly by isolating the operational harm of a rigid post-authorization calendar deadline while neutralizing Side A's case against voluntarism. By backing statutory power to compel licensing and technical assistance whenever a viable producer is refused—paired with pre-crisis funding and dose reservations—B left A repeating an outdated critique about "corporate goodwill" and "corporate consent." Meanwhile, B proved that real transfer requires scarce human specialists, forced A to bite the bullet that its deadline would halt repairs on active lines serving low-income clinics, and showed that domestic prioritization by producer states neither finances initial runs nor helps poorer neighbors without factories.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: When Side B asked in Q1 whether a government could postpone the deadline so scarce specialists could repair a stalled line actively supplying low-income clinics, Side A answered "No," calling it a "planning failure" and claiming early sharing puts multiple producers online. Side B dismantled both defenses, noting in Rebuttal 2 that "nobody can schedule a breakdown" and in Closing that during the qualification interval when the second plant still needs those specialists, clinics cannot draw doses from a future production run.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by attacking the proposition’s fixed deadline rather than opposing technology transfer itself. Its specialist-allocation example showed how the mandate could delay doses to low-income clinics, while its “outbid” argument exposed why local manufacturing does not itself guarantee access. A also answered both pressure questions directly with enforceable licensing, technical assistance, compensation, and advance assessment. B offered a clear equity case but repeatedly asserted that only a universal deadline could work; its answers that deliveries could not be postponed and that local production inherently prevents wealthy buyers from cornering output left its central vulnerabilities unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s pressure-answer exchange was decisive: B refused to allow even a delivery-protecting postponement and could not explain how the mandate secures a new factory’s first production run for poor patients. A then connected both failures to its narrower case for compulsory transfer where it unlocks supply, without subordinating patients to a calendar.
