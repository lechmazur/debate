### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): CON (B) wins clearly. PRO's case rested on brittle precision that it could not ground. Its opening calls Santiago, Chile a "coastal city" with a 500-million-litre-per-day plant, which is a dubious example. Later speeches add more unsupported figures: a 30% ceiling on leakage cuts, 8–10% annual demand growth, $0.55–0.70 versus more than $1.00 per cubic metre, and a 0.5‰ salinity limit. The cost numbers also arrived late, in the second rebuttal and the closing. PRO's Q1 misdescribed CON's position. It claimed CON "admits" the 30% and growth figures, when CON had challenged them. PRO's Q2 effectively conceded CON's environmental points. PRO's closing then claimed desalination's marginal cost is "fixed" with no recurring outlays. That contradicts CON's point about energy, membrane, and maintenance costs, which PRO never answered. Overall, the closing mostly restated the unsupported cost figure. CON stayed disciplined throughout. It argued that "primarily" carries a general burden. It explained that one-time fixes like repairing mains are not perpetual costs. It also noted that plant cost per cubic metre is not the same as the delivered, all-in cost. Its strongest point was an asymmetry argument: conservation is flexible and can be tested first, while desalination is a large, irreversible commitment sized to uncertain growth. That gave CON a cleaner decision rule that PRO never squarely answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 A1 and its closing took apart PRO's cost comparison. CON showed that the $0.55–0.70 figure ignores utilization, financing, intake, transmission, and brine costs, and that it confuses plant production cost with the right test for allocating a budget. CON paired this with the sequencing argument: fund testable demand-side savings first, then size desalination to the remaining gap. This neutralized PRO's only claimed decisive advantage.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won by holding the correct burden: "primarily" requires PRO to show desalination generally outperforms conservation, reuse, and leakage control, and PRO never met that bar. Side A's case rested on increasingly brittle precision (the $0.55–$0.70/m³ figure, "below 3 kWh/m³," "exceeds $1.00" for demand-side), unsupported universal growth claims ("8–10% annually"), a dubious example (Santiago described as a coastal desal city), and a pressure question that misattributed its own "30% leakage" figure to CON. Side B systematically dismantled each: production cost is not delivered cost, desalination also carries recurring costs, brine dilution is not elimination, and the sequencing/flexibility asymmetry (testable savings first, then size desalination to the residual gap) gave a clean reason to vote CON. B's answers to pressure questions were direct and format-compliant; A's answers complied in form but substituted invented numbers for the missing project-level comparison. B's closing crystallized the decisive asymmetry while A's closing mostly restated its disputed cost figures.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1–B4.3: Side B's second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions, rejecting PRO's per-unit cost figure as not a delivered-cost or allocation-test comparison, and dismantling the "universal mitigation package" by noting diffusers shift rather than remove harm and that monitoring detects but does not prevent damage — leaving PRO's economic and environmental routes both effectively dead.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won by making the decisive burden clear: “primarily” requires showing desalination generally beats conservation, reuse, and leakage control on cost, reliability, and sequence. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): A wins clearly. PRO built one coherent route to victory: conservation produces savings, not new capacity, so it has a ceiling, while desalination can keep supply going in a dry year. PRO held this line consistently and used Pressure Questions to force the ceiling issue into the open. CON's answer then conceded it. In C4.1, CON admitted that savings top out around 35% and that further cuts harm cooking, hygiene, staple crops and health services, so rationing returns. That is essentially PRO's case. CON's later claim that conservation delivers "the same volume of water" contradicts that concession. CON's closing also introduces an unexplained "targeted low-impact supply" fallback without engaging PRO's ceiling argument. CON's Pressure Questions largely echoed PRO's own questions back, and the first one confusedly attributed CON's 25–35% figure to PRO, so they applied little real pressure. CON also leaned on brittle precision presented as authority: the Israel 30% figure, the Saudi mangrove link, California's 15% yield figure and 0.5% desalination share, and unnamed "field studies" showing a 10% threshold. CON's strongest point was cost per cubic metre, and it pressed it consistently. PRO neutralized it by reframing the comparison as unit cost versus the losses from repeated shortfalls and rationing. PRO's caution about mangrove causation and its argument that modular staging reduces stranding risk were also measured and credible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2 argued that the 25–35% savings come from eliminating existing waste and have no sustainable ceiling beyond that. This drew CON into conceding in C4.1 that further cuts hit essential needs and that rationing would inevitably return. PRO's closing then cited that concession directly as "rationing by another name," turning CON's own answer into the deciding point.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A won the decisive clash. A's core route—conservation saves water but cannot create supply, so demand management inevitably ends in rationing once the ceiling is hit—survived the whole exchange and was actually confirmed by B's own A1 answer ("the empirical ceiling lies around thirty-five percent"). A exploited that concession cleanly in closing. A's pressure questions were pointed and effective, and A's rebuttals answered the cost and brittleness attacks by reframing the comparison around avoided drought losses and modular, staged deployment. B kept a live cost argument but leaned on brittle precision (Israel 30%, Saudi mangroves, California 0.5%, 3-4 kWh/m³, membrane lifespans, "field studies" on 10% thresholds) without grounding, misattributed A's claims in the Pressure Questions round by echoing A's own questions back as accusations, and introduced new late-stage evidence rather than resolving the ceiling clash. B's closing was recap-like and never answered whether conservation could meet essential demand in a dry year without restrictions. A's more disciplined grounding and sharper comparative weighing carried the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 / Closing sequence: after B conceded a roughly 35% ceiling on demand-side savings, A crystallized that further conservation means cutting cooking, hygiene, and staple crops—"rationing by another name, not a supply strategy"—which neutralized B's cost advantage by showing the cheap water runs out before the demand does.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A won by making the central clash about structural supply deficits: conservation can reduce waste but eventually hits a ceiling and becomes rationing. Side B gave stronger cost and environmental pressure, but never convincingly answered how demand-side cuts close an unavoidable supply gap, and its late claim that the residual deficit is small was unsupported. B also used some brittle, overconfident specifics and had a slightly confused pressure question.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s pressure question forced B to concede that demand-side savings have a rough ceiling, after which A crystallized that further cuts would mean rationing essential needs; B’s cost response did not resolve the supply-gap issue.
