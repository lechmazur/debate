### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins. CON opened with a substantive case: money buys claims rather than consultations, the fiscal guarantee is open-ended, and the line between "elective" and "supplementary" care is unstable. Its strongest point came in Rebuttal 1: the private tier competes for the same clinicians and so erodes the public floor. PRO turned CON's own endorsement of "universal access to a defined, evidence-based core" [A1.5] into a near-concession of the motion. It then reframed workforce and package objections as design requirements rather than defeaters. CON's execution damaged it badly in four places: - **Malformed questions.** Its Pressure Questions were confused, apparently written from PRO's perspective and asking about CON's own "alternative." PRO exploited this cleanly in A1. - **Bad references.** It cited nonexistent IDs [P1.2, P2.3]. - **Clipped turns.** Both Rebuttal 1 and the closing were cut off. - **Concessions in Rebuttal 2.** CON admitted that neither model conjures clinicians and that universal funding may finance expansion. PRO's Rebuttal 2 answered the workforce question with a plausible mechanism: stable funding supports training pipelines and salaried posts. It also showed the constraint applies equally to CON. PRO's closing then crystallized the remaining clash: a universal floor versus assistance that can be "capped, narrowed, or withdrawn," using CON's own words. CON's coverage-versus-access distinction and its clinician-competition argument stayed partly live, which keeps the margin from being dominant. Overall, PRO made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was PRO's B4.3–B4.4. It answered the workforce challenge by arguing the guarantee itself funds the pipeline, then showed that CON's subsidized core recruits from the same constrained pool. That forced CON in A4.3 to concede that neither model conjures clinicians, which neutralized CON's strongest live route.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by forcing the choice onto a universal front-door floor versus assistance Side B itself said could be capped, narrowed, or withdrawn. Side B's supply critique and same-clinician competition point stayed partly live, but Side A answered that package rules, staffing, and workforce limits burden both models, while stable universal funding is what finances expansion and private-tier regulation remains compatible with the motion. Side B's malformed pressure questions, self-citations, and fake reference IDs made that comparison easier to decide for the guarantee.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal: specification and clinician shortages are design problems for both policies, not a defeater unique to a guarantee, and only a stable universal payer finances the training pipeline that Side B never supplied.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): PRO kept a live, simple route: primary care prevents costlier catastrophes, while CON's own defined-core alternative looked like a weaker, means-tested version of the guarantee. CON's clinician-drain argument pressured PRO, but PRO answered that stable universal funding can finance the pipeline and that workforce scarcity afflicts targeted subsidies equally. CON never neutralized the working-poor eligibility gap or the force of its own admission that targeted help can be capped or withdrawn.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's first rebuttal and closing turned CON's "defined, evidence-based core" into a near-concession, narrowing the debate to universal versus withdrawable targeted coverage, where PRO's downstream-cost argument was strongest.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) was the more vivid speaker. "A promise with a receipt" and "fails smaller" framed the stakes memorably, and the conditionality attack (the guarantee works only if efficiently designed and funded) was a real line of pressure. But its central mechanisms lean on assumptions PRO repeatedly contested: - **Single capped purchaser.** CON's monopsony and rationing story assumes universality means one public buyer setting fees. PRO denied this from B2.1 and B2.5 onward, pointing to mixed provision, contracting and adjustable fees. - **Funding risk is not unique.** PRO correctly noted in B2.2 and B4.3 that scarcity and funding risk apply to targeted schemes too. CON's own A1 conceded vouchers face the same fiscal constraint. - **"Fails smaller" ignores non-qualifiers.** CON never answered who covers people outside targeted eligibility. PRO's closing point that targeted systems "improve the budget at the expense of the floor" went unrefuted. - **Brittle precision.** CON's repeated "nine-month wait" was asserted as if typical, with no grounding. PRO's case was flatter rhetorically, and its clipped second rebuttal is a real execution cost. Even so, PRO answered both pressure questions directly. It also kept the comparison on its strongest ground: who bears the gaps, plus continuity of care for chronic patients and people between jobs. Its questions exposed that CON's rationing critique was not unique to universality. On balance, PRO has the slightly more defensible path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Q2 and CON's A2. PRO demanded a harm that universality uniquely worsens. CON answered with monopsony fee-capping, its strongest point: "no penalty manufactures a doctor." PRO's closing, building on B2.5, replied that a guarantee does not require a single capped purchaser and that fees and workforce policy are adjustable implementation choices. That left CON's unique harm resting on an assumption about delivery design rather than on the entitlement itself.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by turning Side A's own defenses—that the guarantee depends on efficient design, funding, and later penalties—into a concession that the entitlement is conditional, then comparing underfunding more cleanly: targeted programs fail smaller while a universal floor queues everyone and can distort fees and workforce. Side A made a coherent equity and visibility case and rightly rejected a pure monopoly, but did not fully neutralize those delivery and incentive problems; the clipped second rebuttal also left the case less complete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal, which answered both pressure questions and showed that access penalties cannot create clinicians discouraged by capped public fees, while narrowing the clash to which system degrades more gracefully when money runs short.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B sustained the decisive distinction between guaranteeing entitlement and monopolizing delivery, showing contracting and regulated private provision could preserve the floor. It also turned scarcity back on Side A: targeted aid faces the same budgets while leaving non-qualifiers with no enforceable claim. Side A's queue, capped-fee, and graceful-failure arguments were sharp but depended heavily on an assumed single capped purchaser that B repeatedly and plausibly denied.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was B's Rebuttal 2 and closing response that underfunding makes universal delays visible breaches of duty, whereas targeted markets hide exclusion as private misfortune; this blunted A's 'fails smaller' comparison.
