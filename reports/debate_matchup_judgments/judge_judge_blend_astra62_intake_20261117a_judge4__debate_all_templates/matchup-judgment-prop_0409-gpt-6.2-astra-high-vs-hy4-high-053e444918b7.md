### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): A won because it kept the core clash on access and affordability rather than letting maintenance justify exclusion. Its best move separated funding from exclusive use: a city can pay for lifeguards, paths, and cleaning without selling permission to occupy the water. B's later safeguards—peak-hour free capacity, no-net-loss wading frontage, resident allocations—effectively conceded that free access at the usable times and places is the decisive value. B never showed that a regulated paid zone would outperform a properly funded public beach with concession revenue, and its strongest rhetorical turn, that A accepted some paid experiences, still required maintaining broad free water. That made B's case a heavily conditioned management compromise rather than a defense of the proposition's reverse.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's capacity-versus-payment answer was the most decisive exchange: turning a child away because a cove is full is not the same as turning her away when there is room but her family cannot pay. That defused B's claim that free timed slots exclude just like prices do and forced B into protecting free peak-hour wading as a condition of any legitimate lease.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a high-level debate by cleanly unbundling maintenance and capacity caps from gate exclusion, accounting for hidden welfare and shoreline opportunity costs, and trapping Side A (CON) on the wording of the motion. Side A built a strong fiscal case that bounded leases tie dedicated revenue to upkeep and can expand supervised capacity, but Side B showed cities can mandate pre-season service floors funded by non-exclusive concessions and public budgets without paying operators in scarce shoreline rights. Under pressure from B3.1-B3.2 and B4.1, Side A conceded caps on gated frontage and a no-net-loss floor for peak-hour free sheltered wading (A4.1-A4.2)—conditions that define a waterfront remaining "broadly free." Side A's closing attempt (A5.1) to treat the proposition as a categorical ban on every single paid gate misread "broadly free ... rather than be increasingly enclosed," leaving Side B with the cleaner path to the ballot.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's B4.1 answer to A3.1—distinguishing turning a child away when a cove hits a safe limit (where a free draw and day-of holds still give her a chance each Saturday) from pricing her out when room exists (giving her zero chance any Saturday)—combined with B3.1-B3.2 to force Side A into conceding peak-hour free-capacity floors and gated-frontage caps in A4.1-A4.2, which B5.1-B5.5 then crystallized as conceding PRO's core principle.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) better defended the motion as written. CON (A) pressed real risks of underfunding and crowding and proposed safeguards, but those safeguards (no-net-loss peak free wading, reserved free capacity) largely preserved the broad free access PRO demanded. PRO separated funding from selling admission, showed non-exclusive rents and an enforceable service floor can maintain the shore, and weighed the public rights surrendered against the operator's check. CON never cleanly showed why exclusive enclosure is required once that floor is funded, and its claim that any legitimate paid zone collapses the proposition over-read "broadly free rather than increasingly enclosed."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R2 distinction that a full cove still leaves a free chance while a price the family cannot meet leaves none, plus the specified service-plan funding floor, kept equity and public ownership decisive against CON's claim that bounded paid access necessarily does more for the child.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won because B built the more concrete and adaptable comparative case. A’s publicness argument was principled and rhetorically sharp, but B repeatedly exposed its fiscal weakness: saying a council could publicly fund the same services did not answer who owed the bill when funds were scarce. A’s answer that the council should forgo weekday lifeguards rather than sacrifice toilets or the threshold let B turn the debate’s key example against A: a free but unguarded Tuesday is not a usable safe swim for the family with no other day. B also handled A’s best pressure reasonably by requiring audits, free walk-up places, sunset dates, and fresh votes, and by pointing out that a shrinking free shoreline makes each new gate harder to justify. A’s closing warning about a shrinking baseline was strong, but B had already partially answered it with sunset and renewal safeguards, and A never fully resolved the tough triage problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive exchange was the rebuttal that separating service obligations from funding exposed A’s public-contract alternative: a council can write “daily lifeguard” into a contract, but under a public model the council still bears the staffing risk, while under a lease the operator does. This pressure later forced A into the uncomfortable position of choosing unstaffed Tuesdays over gated supervised swimming, which B turned into the central access tradeoff.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B made a compelling civic case that gating ordinary shoreline use turns unconditional belonging into conditional sorting and normalizes solving public-service gaps with turnstiles. However, Side A won a clear, competitive victory through superior fiscal mechanics, honest symmetric weighing, and sharper tactical execution. Side A showed that nominal free entry leaves spaces unusable for weekday families when municipal budgets cannot fund supervision, proved that private leases transfer revenue risk and cross-subsidize free walk-up access without raiding funds for poorer parks elsewhere, pinned Side B into accepting unstaffed weekday beaches as the real-world alternative, and cleanly inverted Side B's closing attack on its marginal-shoreline safeguard.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After showing in [A2.1]-[A2.2] that regulatory power to specify daily lifeguards is not the budget to pay for them, Side A used [A3.1] to force Side B to choose unstaffed weekday beaches over cutting toilets across town ([B4.1]), then turned that concession in [A4.3]-[A4.4] into the concrete baseline—a warning sign and a lost summer—that a price-capped lease with free walk-up slots easily beat.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins on the motion as written. Side A built a strong, concrete case that a capped, sunset lease can staff a cove the city will not fund, and it rightly forced B to admit an unstaffed Tuesday. But B kept the decisive comparison: a repairable service shortage versus a changed default in which ordinary access is sorted, booked, or exempted. A's visit-count metric and remaining-shoreline test never neutralized the operator incentive or the precedent of solving care with a gate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's concert distinction was decisive: identical hours do not make the gates the same, because a concert sells an added event and then public use returns, while a swimming gate sells the beach's ordinary purpose and makes care itself the reason for the till.
