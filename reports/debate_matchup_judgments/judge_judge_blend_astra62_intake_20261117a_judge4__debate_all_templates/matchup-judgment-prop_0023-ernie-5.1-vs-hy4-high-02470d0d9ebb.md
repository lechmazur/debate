### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B wins a clear but competitive debate. B's core category-error argument—that matching and custody do not make a venue a securities exchange, and that crypto risks like key custody, reserve shortfalls, and affiliate misuse exist regardless of asset classification—survived the exchange largely intact. B successfully turned A's own concessions: A admitted securities rules "can be adapted" (A2.5), which B reframed as bespoke regulation with a securities sticker, and A's shift from "facilitates purchase" to a "dominant activity" test (A4.1) was exposed as an unproven and costly retreat. B also answered A's pressure questions directly, citing NY and European crypto licenses as concrete mechanisms with enforcement teeth, while A never proved that most platforms' dominant business is securities-token trading. A's strongest point—the ready-made enforcement infrastructure of securities law—was undercut by B's plausible argument that licensing statutes can be given the same subpoena, exam, and penalty powers, and that the real question is what those powers attach to. A's clipped first rebuttal was an additional execution cost. B's closing crystallized the decisive trigger distinction: hard rules should bite when a platform takes customer coins, not after litigation decides what label dominates its inventory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's exploitation of A's adaptation concession was the most decisive exchange. When A said securities rules "can be adapted" to 24/7 cross-border crypto, B immediately replied that this "admits it is not already the answer" and that adding wallet, staking, reserve, key, and affiliate rules means PRO is defending bespoke regulation with a securities sticker. B then reinforced this in closing by turning A's claim that securities law governs the "entire business" into a dilemma: either PRO overclassifies every non-security pair or admits crypto-specific additions—neither defends the proposition.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the live clash on whether most platforms actually intermediate securities and whether a securities label fits the loss mechanisms. A never proved the “most” claim, shifted to an unshown dominant-activity test, and treated adaptation plus existing enforcement machinery as decisive. B answered both pressure questions directly, showed the admired safeguards are conduct rules rather than unique consequences of securities status, and closed by forcing A into either overclassification or bespoke regulation with a securities sticker. A’s clipped rebuttal and inaccurate “draft form” characterization of existing crypto licenses were real execution costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal and closing were decisive: once custody, reserve, key, and affiliate rules govern the whole platform regardless of asset status, A’s proposal collapses into distinct crypto regulation wearing a securities label, while the dominant-activity retreat never established “most.”
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B built the stronger comparative case: securities powers attach to securities business, whereas the cited crypto losses arise from custody, commingling, keys, leverage, and affiliates across mixed-asset venues. B showed that bespoke regulation need not be light and can carry enforcement machinery, neutralizing A’s infrastructure advantage. A never established that most platforms’ dominant activity is securities intermediation, shifted tests under pressure, and made overconfident claims about registration preventing FTX and licensing lacking enforcement powers. A’s clipped rebuttal and inaccurate closing claim about New York and European licenses further weakened execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal and closing exposed A’s dilemma: either prove that most mixed venues are dominated by securities trading, or impose asset-independent wallet, reserve, key, and affiliate rules across the whole business—which amounts to bespoke crypto regulation with a securities label. This directly defeated A’s remaining infrastructure-versus-aspiration framing.
