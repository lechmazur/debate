### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Both sides were sharp, but PRO kept a cleaner, more realistic route to victory. PRO's core chain held up after the exchange: labor demand is structural, much Gulf irregularity is manufactured by sponsorship rules, and mass expulsion creates a labor vacuum that refills through smugglers. CON's answer to that vacuum was that "citizens, legal migrants and contracted workers" would staff the sites while "projects may slow." That answer is thin for Gulf construction and North African agriculture, and PRO exploited it in closing. CON's best line was the symmetry argument: if a state cannot remove violators, it cannot audit corridors either. CON also argued forcefully that "yes" without a credible "no" invites fraud. However, CON steadily slid from defending mass deportations to defending a generic "enforcement boundary." PRO's A2 answered that move directly: prioritizing corridors demotes mass expulsion, not targeted enforcement. CON's reply that targeting "ends in removal" conflates targeted removal with mass deportation, which is what the motion actually names. PRO's voluntary-compliance point, that workers stop hiding when the offer is legality, also partly answered the capacity symmetry. PRO slipped at times, notably the "overnight" smuggling claim, and CON's North Africa transit point was decent. On balance, PRO made the decision easier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over B3.2 and A4.2 decided the debate. CON asked whether "prioritize" demotes mass deportation or all enforcement. PRO answered cleanly that it demotes only mass expulsion while freeing resources for targeted enforcement. That answer exposed CON's reliance on equating enforcement with mass deportation, a gap CON never fully closed in its second rebuttal or closing.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): A won by turning B’s administrative-capacity objection against mass deportation and clearly contrasting voluntary compliance with the coercion required for location, detention, documentation, hearings, and repatriation. A also showed how removals-first could recreate irregular hiring through an immediate labor vacuum. B offered strong fraud and enforcement-credibility objections, but too often equated prioritizing corridors over mass deportations with abandoning enforcement altogether, despite A’s explicit distinction between mass expulsions and targeted regulation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first rebuttal and Pressure Q1 exposed the asymmetry between B’s frictionless permit caricature and simplified removal trigger. B’s later answer explained the legal trigger for removal but never squarely addressed its operational burden at scale.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side A won the central capacity and sequencing clashes. Its voluntary-compliance account explained why willing workers and employers registering for portable permits is easier than locating, detaining, adjudicating, and repatriating unwilling migrants at scale, and why deporting first recreates the labor vacuum smugglers refill. Side B's credible “no before yes” framing and warnings about fraud, regularization, and transit migration were strong, but it mainly asserted that removal is a simple trigger without adequately answering the detection, documentation, and staffing costs pressed throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 capacity contradiction, developed in Rebuttal 2 as voluntary compliance versus hostile coercion, most damaged Side B's claim that due-process mass deportations are administratively simpler than legal corridors.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): PRO (A) built a coherent causal story: corridors make migration visible, and visibility enables targeted enforcement. PRO then adapted this cleanly across rounds. Its corridors-plus-residual-coercion model blunted CON's charge of "no consequences." It also answered the amnesty and capacity pressure directly with conditional, one-off windows and the "if you can't triage files you can't triage buses" point. CON (B) had the sharpest offensive route. North African transit states lack the vacancies needed to make corridors meaningful, so most migrants would face departure orders and avoid registering. CON pressed this well, and it exposed PRO's narrowing of the policy. However, CON never showed how mass deportation lawfully resolves the same caseload it says paralyzes individual processing. Its closing claim that verifying status for a bus is "straightforward" was asserted, not defended. CON also leaned heavily on deterrence assumptions. Finally, CON cited nonexistent reference IDs (P2.3, P4.2, P5.1), which is a minor execution cost. On balance, PRO left a cleaner, more internally consistent path to victory under the motion's "prioritize" framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The capacity exchange was decisive. CON's A1 argued that individual due-process removals at scale paralyze the state. PRO's closing turned this back: lawful mass removal also requires names, status and a basis, so a state that cannot triage files can only sweep classes. Sweeps destroy the records needed for cutoffs and employer sanctions. CON's only reply was an unsupported claim that status verification for buses is simple.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): A offered the more elegant enforcement theory and answered both pressure questions directly, but B kept the decisive scope problem alive: A conceded North African vacancies were narrow, then relied on registration, individualized returns, and conditional regularization without showing how labor corridors address large Europe-bound or jobless populations at scale. B also exposed the residual-enforcement and cutoff problems. A’s closing point that mass removals also require files was strong, and B sometimes asserted rather than proved deportation’s deterrent effect, so the win remains competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 turned A’s “narrow vacancies” concession into a registration trap: migrants without realistic job matches would avoid registration, while individualized returns would recreate the scale problem. A’s capacity riposte was effective but never supplied the missing corridor-scale solution.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side A won the prioritize-versus-abandon framing: corridors plus residual, individual removals and employer liability remained a coherent alternative to mass deportation as the default. Side B landed strong North Africa job-scarcity and registration-trap pressure, but never proved mass removals durably break employer demand, broker incentives, and re-entry.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 most decisively answered both pressure questions by distinguishing conditional, cutoff-based status resolution and due-process return from class-wide mass deportation, recasting coercion as a backstop rather than the governing engine.
