### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO won by holding the debate to the motion's comparative, joint test. CON raised real objections: zoning as the binding constraint, contested land assessments, and base volatility. PRO's central reply was that every constraint CON named applies equally to transaction taxes and rent caps, which neither build housing nor fund services. CON never squarely neutralized that reply. CON's best line was that LVT "punishes patience" on stalled permitted sites, and it was reasonably developed. But CON's eventual defense split the motion into separate jobs: rent caps prevent displacement, and a narrow turnover levy raises modest revenue. That effectively conceded that neither alternative improves supply and finance together. PRO's downturn argument also stood: transaction revenue loses both value and volume when sales freeze, while LVT remains a recurring base. CON's claim that rent caps "preserve effective supply" was thin and contradicted PRO's uncontested mobility point. PRO's opening was clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost, but its core case had already landed and later speeches were tight and adaptive. CON's closing was rhetorically sharp ("costs certain, benefits not") but mostly restated earlier points.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2.1 and A4.2 turned "supply follows permission" into a non-comparative point: no tax legalizes apartments, and taxing rapid resales removes none of the cleanup, financing, or assembly obstacles. Together with PRO's Q2 on the joint test, this forced CON to defend a divided-tools approach that conceded the motion's combined standard.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A controlled the comparative frame throughout. Against B's strongest opening argument (zoning is the binding constraint), A correctly noted that no tax legalizes apartments, so the comparison must be within a given planning regime — neutralizing B's supposed trump card. A's response to the assessment objection (construction does not enter the tax base, so valuation error cannot recreate the old property tax's marginal penalty on building) was analytically decisive and never truly undone; B's counter retreated to general bureaucratic drag. A also won the fiscal clash: transaction revenue requires both value and actual sales, so it collapses doubly in downturns, while LVT stays recurrent. B's endgame — praising narrow tools that each do a smaller job — was effectively turned by A's closing line that this concedes the joint test: caps fund nothing, and narrowing the transaction tax shrinks its base. A answered B's pressure questions directly and honestly with the required A1/A2 format; B's answers were competent but its A1 largely reasserted prior framing. A's clipped opening is a small execution cost, but the substantive case landed fully. Neither side used fake citations or brittle precision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.4): answering B's threshold and downturn questions, A dismantled the unequal-burden frame — LVT must solve zoning, contamination, finance, and valuation while a "small honest base" is praised without showing it can fund services — and showed transaction revenue uniquely depends on turnover freezing in downturns, while LVT remains recurrent.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the debate fixed on the motion’s joint test: LVT can press permitted land into use without taxing improvements and supplies a recurrent fiscal base, while transaction taxes depend on turnover and rent caps produce no revenue. B made real zoning, assessment, and volatility objections, but never showed that transaction taxes or rent caps improve supply and public finance more. B’s combined-tools answer also split the motion rather than meeting it directly. A’s replies were cleaner, though the clipped opening is a small execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s answer to B’s zoning/threshold pressure was decisive: even if LVT’s supply gain is constrained by planning permission, transaction taxes and rent caps still offer no comparable supply or revenue advantage, exposing the unequal standard in B’s case.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): PRO began with a clean, standard LVT story: tax location rather than buildings, make idle holding costly, and avoid the mobility and volatility problems of transaction taxes and the supply damage of rent caps. That is a plausible prima facie case. However, PRO largely repeated the incentive mechanism rather than developing it under pressure. CON did more effective comparative damage across the rebuttals: - **Pressure versus homes.** CON pressed hard on the gap between "pressure to use land" and "additional homes": zoning-constrained sales, nonresidential uses, and turnover are not new housing. - **Capitalization.** CON argued that a lower purchase price only reflects the future tax bill, so the builder's total cost does not simply fall. PRO answered with vague language about "return on marginal capital" and assumed the feasible active use that was in dispute. - **Self-adjusting base.** CON also exploited PRO's claim that LVT "self-adjusts" when land value collapses. That point undercut PRO's claim to a dependable revenue base. CON's rent-cap concessions were honest. CON's infrastructure-funding point is somewhat weak, since LVT revenue could also fund infrastructure, but PRO never made that response. PRO's best point was that CON offered no positive supply mechanism. CON reasonably answered that the burden of proving comparative superiority sits with PRO. CON's opening was clipped for exceeding the word limit, which is a real execution cost. Even so, CON made the decisive clash clearer and answered pressure more directly, so CON edges the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1–B2.2 was the most decisive exchange. It turned PRO's own "sells or builds himself" and "tax self-adjusts" lines into a dilemma: under binding zoning, LVT either reshuffles ownership without adding homes or loses its revenue base. It then added the capitalization point that a lower land price is not free finance. PRO's A4.3 answer never squarely neutralized either half.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides were technically strong and avoided fake sourcing. B's sharpest weapons were the capitalization argument (a lower purchase price merely bakes in the future tax bill) and the correct burden framing that PRO must affirmatively establish comparative superiority. But B's case had structural costs: the opening was clipped mid-sentence, and B effectively conceded rent caps on both prongs (no revenue, no supply mechanism), which matters under a comparative motion. On the zoning-bind scenario, B's own logic neutralized its attack: where building is prohibited, neither alternative produces homes, and PRO correctly noted the transaction tax's base vanishes there too while LVT still attaches cost to immobile land. PRO's answer to the capitalization pressure, while not crisply articulated, contained the right move: capitalization shifts who feels the tax (idle incumbents) without removing the holding penalty. B's retreat to "feasibility is in dispute" left the feasible-market comparison to PRO. A's pressure question exposing that B offered no supply mechanism at all for the alternatives was decisive; B's burden-reversal answer was legitimate but left B defending pure skepticism. Edge to A on argument and comparative weighing; edge to B on rebuttal precision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The A3.2 pressure question ("what mechanism does CON offer that actually increases housing supply?") versus B4.3/B5.3's burden-reversal answer. B's move was technically correct—CON need not present a complete housing program—but it left B with no affirmative supply story, while PRO retained the "expire the option to bank scarcity" mechanism. Combined with B's de facto concession of rent caps on both supply and revenue, this exchange decided the comparative motion for A.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B won by turning the debate on the proposition’s key burdens: PRO had to show not just pressure on landowners, but comparatively more housing and more reliable public finance. B’s “changing owners is not adding homes” and capitalization points materially damaged PRO’s supply story, and its liquidity/assessment/reserves arguments weakened PRO’s fiscal stability claim. PRO remained clear and rhetorically sharp, but never fully answered how pressure becomes additional homes under zoning, finance, and infrastructure constraints. B’s clipped opening is an execution cost, but not enough to overturn its substantive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s rebuttal sequence converting PRO’s zoning answer into the concession that forced sales do not create homes, then pressing that lower land prices merely capitalize the future LVT liability rather than proving cheaper feasible projects.
