### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides were strong, but PRO built the cleaner path to victory by trapping CON on a live contradiction: CON's own alternative (banning deceptive health claims, requiring substantiation) requires someone to classify claims as medical, cosmetic, or wellness, and CON admitted in [B2.1] that "someone must decide." PRO leveraged this across rebuttals and the closing, arguing the only remaining dispute was whether the classification stays in a compliance file or reaches the buyer, and that CON "never gave an honest reason for hiding it." CON's best counter—that classification is an internal enforcement step, not a consumer remedy, and that a visible label risks legitimizing vague wellness claims—was genuinely sharp, and the flip of PRO's sponsorship analogy in [B4.4] landed real damage. But CON's legitimacy argument stayed speculative while PRO offered a concrete asymmetry: teens who scroll past the label are left no worse than under CON's regime, while teens who read it gain a pre-sale truth plus an enforcement hook. CON's vagueness/chilling point was blunted by PRO noting the motion binds brands that script talking points, and that border phrases are equally unenforceable under CON's own deception standard. PRO's clipped openings/rebuttals are a real execution cost, keeping the margin competitive rather than solid.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's [A2.2]-[A2.3] turn showing CON's proposed "prohibit deceptive health claims / require substantiation" alternative presupposes the very medical-cosmetic-wellness distinction CON rejects, converting CON's framework into an argument for the motion; CON's strongest reply was [B2.1]'s distinction between classification as internal enforcement step versus consumer-facing label, later sharpened by flipping PRO's sponsorship-tag analogy in [B4.4].
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side A wins a high-level debate by exposing the false dichotomy in Side B's counterplan and winning the comparative risk calculus, despite incurring an execution cost from having three speeches clipped at the word limit. Side B made strong, realistic points that visual framing and parasocial cues drive teen purchases more than verbal taxonomy, and smartly argued in [B4.2] that deception law polices implied meaning in context. However, because clear category distinctions do not preclude substantive bans on false claims, Side B needed to prove that visible distinctions cause net harm. Side A dismantled Side B's chilling-speech and perverse-legitimacy claims—showing that clarifying a pitch is "not a treatment" demotes rather than certifies it—while proving that upfront public classification catches strategic ambiguity before the sale rather than waiting months for case-by-case enforcement, and at worst leaves scroll-past viewers no worse off than Side B's status quo.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side B argued in [B4.2] and [B4.4] that contextual deception rules catch implied claims without labels and that sponsorship tags prove disclosures fail to stop parasocial persuasion, Side A closed the loop in [A5.2]–[A5.4] (building on [A4.1] and [A4.6]): ex-post contextual enforcement happens months too late after teens have bought the product and skipped a doctor, "not a treatment" cannot plausibly be read as an official endorsement, and teens scrolling past a label at worst end up exactly where Side B leaves everyone.
