### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): CON wins because it built a consistent case and its key points held through the whole exchange. **CON's three live routes:** - **Distributive vs. constitutive.** Using risk scores to allocate finance is not the same as using them to allocate voting power. - **Perverse incentive.** Under risk-weighting, a state that adapts successfully would lose voting weight. - **Defection.** High emitters would walk away from pathways shaped by risk-weighted vetoes. PRO never squarely answered the defection point. **PRO's case was weakened by several problems:** - **Inaccurate status quo.** PRO described current talks as GDP- or population-weighted. Climate talks actually run on one-state-one-vote and consensus. - **Unsupported history.** PRO asserted that the Paris language was "watered down" because vulnerable states lacked leverage, without support. - **Shifting definitions.** PRO first expanded "existential risk" to cover Bangladesh and Nigeria, then narrowed it to loss of territory in A2. This made the boundary problem worse rather than resolving it. - **Misattributed paradox.** PRO's Q1 and closing pinned CON's adaptation paradox on the status quo. In fact it is a flaw of PRO's own proposal, so the closing misdescribed the opponent's claim. **PRO's strongest point** was the charge that emissions-based differentiation has produced three decades of failure. CON answered it adequately: an enforcement gap is not proof that a less acceptable system would work better.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1–B2.2 did the most damage. It drew the distributive-vs-constitutive distinction against PRO's claim that "metrics already allocate finance." It then added the perverse-incentive point that successful adaptation would demote a state's voice. PRO never answered this coherently and later misattributed the paradox to the status quo.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A wins on the cleaner comparative case. CON successfully turned the debate from moral appeal to institutional viability by distinguishing distributive finance from constitutive voting power, exposing the adaptation paradox, and warning that risk-weighted vetoes invite major-emitter defection. PRO's strongest attack on the status quo was powerful, but it did not neutralize these live objections; its answers on thresholds and boundaries were only partial, and its adaptation pressure question ended up strengthening CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's constitutive-versus-distributive distinction was decisive: risk metrics that allocate finance cannot automatically justify permanent voting power, directly undercutting PRO's main feasibility argument.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B won solidly by neutralizing Side A's operational and historical claims while establishing unanswered practical costs. Side B cleanly distinguished distributive climate finance from constitutive voting power, showed that risk-weighted vetoes would alienate major emitters and invite defection rather than compliance, and caught Side A expanding a SIDS-specific motion into a boundless vulnerability franchise covering Bangladesh and Nigeria. Side A never explained why sovereign high emitters would accept risk-weighted super-votes, prematurely accused Side B in Turn 7 of dodging a Turn 5 question before Side B's turn to answer, and badly misread Side B's adaptation paradox in Q1 and Closing—attacking Side B and the status quo for a political demotion flaw created exclusively by Side A's own proposal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B2.1]-[B2.2] and [B4.4], Side B dismantled Side A's central analogy to loss-and-damage finance by distinguishing adjustable financial distribution from constitutive voting power, showing that rolling risk recalculations would both trigger recurring battles over voting shares and punish states that successfully adapt with reduced political voice—a turn Side A misread and inadvertently validated.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): PRO built a coherent and memorable principle, that influence should track what a decision can destroy. It also handled two of CON's attacks well. On measurement, PRO proposed a published formula built on observable physical indicators and noted that population and GDP figures are also modeled. On moral hazard, PRO pointed out that vote weight cannot build a sea wall. PRO's opening, however, contains an internal tension: it first says voting tracks population or GDP, then complains about formal equality. The decisive clash was over consequences, and CON won it. CON argued from the opening onward that weighting would push major emitters to withdraw or dilute their commitments. CON's Q2 sharpened this into a direct challenge: louder island voices, fewer actual cuts. PRO answered by recasting the threat as proof that today's consensus treats survival as negotiable. That is rhetorically strong but does not show that weighted votes would produce more mitigation. PRO also conceded that "no voting rule guarantees cuts." CON's closing turned that concession into a clean decision rule: Tuvalu needs emissions reductions, not a weighted gavel. CON's execution had real costs: - It overstated how binding Paris obligations and compliance mechanisms are. - It claimed GDP figures are "immune" to manipulation. - Its answer on a confidence threshold partly sidestepped the question. These costs narrow the margin. They do not neutralize CON's central route, which PRO never squarely broke.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The best exchange was CON's Q2 and PRO's A2 answer. CON asked whether islands would end up with louder votes but fewer emissions cuts. PRO reframed this as an indictment of consensus rather than refuting the causal claim, and conceded that no voting rule guarantees cuts. CON's closing exploited that concession effectively.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO built the stronger path by making the clash about whether equal formal votes are neutral when one state faces territorial extinction and another faces slower growth. CON’s practical objections—withdrawal risk, measurement uncertainty, moral hazard—were plausible, but PRO answered the measurement and moral-hazard pressure and reframed the cooperation objection as an admission that current consensus depends on keeping survival negotiable. CON’s fallback that AOSIS voice, loss-and-damage finance, and Paris compliance already suffice was decisively weakened: PRO showed voice and ex post compensation are not binding power to prevent noncompensable loss. That left CON defending stability without fully restoring its effectiveness claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttal that being heard is not power, and that a loss-and-damage fund compensates after loss rather than preventing it, most damaged CON’s existing-mechanisms route to victory.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side B won the decisive clash over incentives and treaty participation. While Side A dismantled B's weak moral hazard claim, sharply noted that post-hoc funds compensate only after loss, and proposed plausible physical-exposure proxies in [A4.1], Side A never answered how weighted voting prevents major emitters from exiting or diluting commitments. Pressed in [B3.2], Side A conceded that risk-weighting "does not guarantee cuts" and retreated to recording asymmetry symbolically in the rulebook, allowing Side B to show that sacrificing emitter cooperation leaves island states more physically exposed. Side A also hurt its execution by contradicting itself on status-quo voting rules and citing nonexistent C1.x IDs in Turn 3.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pressure question [B3.2] and closing rebuttal [B5.1]-[B5.4] formed the decisive sequence: after Side A claimed equal votes "authorize disappearance by majority" while conceding risk-weighting "does not guarantee cuts" ([A4.3], [A5.3]), Side B countered that "disappearance is authored by emissions, not by headcount," proving that trading polluter participation for a symbolic "weighted gavel" is self-defeating.
