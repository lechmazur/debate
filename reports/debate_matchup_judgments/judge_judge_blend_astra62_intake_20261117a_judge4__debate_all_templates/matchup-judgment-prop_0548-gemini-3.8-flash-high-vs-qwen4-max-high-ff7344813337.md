### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): PRO opened with a plausible case built on certainty over severity, the concentration of violence in small places, and the age-crime curve. After that, PRO mostly restated the same themes. CON adapted better across the rounds. CON turned the age-crime argument into a case for incapacitation and corrected PRO's "back end of the sentence" framing. CON showed that the added years would cover the late twenties, which are still prime offending years. CON also pressed a tension in PRO's case: PRO says shooters are instantly replaced, yet relies on each individual being deterred. PRO's A1 answer was reasonable: deterrence attaches to the place, so it works on whoever stands there. But CON kept extending the attack. It pointed out that "unpredictable patrols" undercuts the earlier promise of "certain detection." It noted that hot spots are themselves mapped from past shootings. It added the strong framing that repeat offenders are, by definition, people on whom deterrence has already failed. PRO's closing largely repeated the "autopsy before activation" line and never engaged these points. CON therefore finished with clearer comparative weighing and fewer unanswered burdens.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.3/B4.3 exchange on timing decided the most. CON showed that enhancements extend custody into the late twenties, which are still offending years, rather than into "aging" middle age. CON then tied this to PRO's replacement claim: if corners instantly recruit, the released man recruits himself. That neutralized PRO's diminishing-returns route. B4.6 added the point that repeat offenders are the already-undeterred population, which PRO never answered.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side B won by more sharply damaging the live mechanism and leaving the comparison easier to decide. PRO offered a coherent place-based prevention story, but CON showed that added sentence years cover still-active offending ages, that hot-spot effects are temporary and shift-bound, and that PRO’s own answers undercut “certain” detection. The replacement-versus-deterrence clash stayed unresolved in PRO’s closing, while CON crystallized why longer custody better targets repeat offenders who already ignored prior risk.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal and closing, which used PRO’s own “unpredictable patrol” concession and replacement claim to argue that either shooters ignore nudges or a patrolled corner is only a temporary queue.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A made the comparison concrete at the margin: additional custody years for an already-convicted repeat offender versus rotating, probabilistic patrol. B’s place-based prevention case remained plausible, but A supplied the sharper clash, adaptation, and comparative weighing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO wins by turning the precondition logic into the decisive clash. Longer sentences can only act on people who are already caught and convicted, and hot-spot presence is what produces that capture. Presence also reaches unidentified shooters whom no sentence enhancement can touch. CON's strongest point was incapacitation, along with its reply to aging-out: repeat urban shooters are often young and at peak offending, so added years are not wasted. But CON never answered the coverage gap convincingly. Its claim that evasive shooters are "inevitably" caught on other charges was asserted rather than supported, and PRO exploited it in closing by asking who does the catching. PRO also exposed a real inconsistency: CON said crews adapt instantly to patrol but cannot replace a jailed instigator. CON's "lethal talent is scarce" reply was only partial. CON had execution problems as well. It cited a nonexistent reference ID ([P2.3-P2.4]), overstated PRO's answer as a "concession" to the revolving door, and gave a closing that mostly restated its opening imagery without sharpening the comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1/A2 answers and closing were the most decisive exchange. PRO reframed CON's revolving-door question to show that the arrest is the scarce step and that PRO's policy supplies it. It then answered the adaptability point with "A cruiser can move two blocks. A cell cannot." Finally, it seized on CON's "inevitably caught" to show that CON's durability argument depends on PRO's own enforcement mechanism.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by keeping the comparison upstream: sentence length is a multiplier on convictions, while accountable hot-spot presence can deter unidentified shooters and generate the arrests any enhancement requires. Side B landed real damage with the revolving door, peak-year timeline, and scarce-talent frame, but its “inevitably caught” answer conceded victims first and borrowed the capture mechanism from policing. A’s cruiser-can-move asymmetry and closing made the cleaner vote on the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s pressure on uncleared shootings, then the closing use of B’s “inevitably caught” concession to show that durability still depends on an officer making the arrest.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): B built the clearer comparative path: longer sentences magnify only cases reaching conviction, while accountable hot-spot deployment can intervene earlier, cover unidentified offenders, and adapt across time and place. A’s incapacitation and durability argument remained substantial, but its answers increasingly relied on absolute claims—that elusive shooters are inevitably caught, imprisoned instigators cannot be replaced, and patrol merely postpones violence. B answered both pressure questions directly and exposed these asymmetries without late evidence dumps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 and closing were decisive: “a cruiser can move two blocks; a cell cannot” exposed A’s adaptability double standard, while the multiplier-on-convictions argument turned A’s “inevitably caught” answer into an assumption of the very detection capability at issue.
