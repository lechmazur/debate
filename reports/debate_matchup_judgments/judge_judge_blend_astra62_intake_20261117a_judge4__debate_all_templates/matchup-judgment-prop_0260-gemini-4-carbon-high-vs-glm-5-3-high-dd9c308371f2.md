### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): This was a close, well-argued debate. CON edges it because it made the deciding clash easier to see and kept more of its structural objections standing. PRO's strengths: - It anchored the debate in concrete harms: silent app telemetry and malvertising aimed at non-technical users. - It turned CON's own default-inertia concession against CON's optional market remedies. - It gave solid answers on gatekeeper power: open, auditable lists, and rival browsers still free to block more. PRO's weakness: - Its "contradiction" attack in Rebuttal 1 was cleanly dismantled by CON's explanation of how defaults work. CON's decisive pressure: - It took PRO's reliance on defaults and turned it into a dilemma. If defaults are sticky, publishers lose revenue because users never toggle. If users do toggle, PRO has rebuilt the per-site consent fatigue it called a farce. - PRO's distinction between invisible settings and active value trades was plausible, but only partial. Most sites rely on third-party ads, so the "ask once" toggle would recur widely. Governance and breakage: - CON's point about who maintains the list, plus lobbying for carve-outs, was answered aspirationally rather than mechanistically. - PRO's claim that breakage will force cleaner engineering stayed a prediction. CON's execution costs: - Its second rebuttal was clipped, though the loss came at the very end. - Its targeted-remedy pivot (exchange liability, data-broker rules) came late. PRO exploited that fairly in its closing. On net, CON's crystallized closing gives a narrower and cleaner reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q2 and the follow-up in its second rebuttal and closing turned PRO's default-inertia logic against PRO's own plan for publisher survival. PRO's answer distinguishing invisible settings from active value trades only partly neutralized it, because the one-tap toggle still implies widespread per-site prompts.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing a live contradiction in Side A's central mechanism: PRO leaned on default inertia to prove optional tools fail, but then needed users to actively toggle off blocking for valued sites. B argued these cannot both be true without either collapsing free-content revenue or recreating consent fatigue. A offered a plausible distinction between invisible settings and active value trades, but B's governance critique also remained live: someone still has to maintain the mandated blocklist, and legal compliance could entrench gatekeepers rather than check them. A made a strong consumer-harm case, but B gave the cleaner structural reason to reject the mandate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pressure and rebuttal on the default-effect contradiction was decisive: if defaults determine behavior, the one-click publisher pause won't happen; if users do toggle, the mandate rebuilds the per-site consent burden PRO had condemned.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by turning CON's concession that defaults determine behavior against CON's reliance on optional market tools, while offering a plausible distinction between invisible background tracking and a motivated one-tap exception for valued sites. CON's revenue, gatekeeper, and breakage objections remain serious, but B never squarely answers system-level app telemetry or why ad-funded gatekeepers would voluntarily set protective defaults.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: its invisible-settings versus active-value-trade distinction blunted B's alleged toggle contradiction and reframed the default concession as fatal to B's market-remedy case.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): PRO argued well on a few points. "Whose default" is a strong reframe that turns CON's own claim about the power of defaults back on CON. The line-drawing symmetry argument also put real pressure on CON. However, PRO's opening was clipped for length, which is an execution cost. More importantly, PRO never neutralized CON's central structural argument. That argument was the first-party asymmetry: system-level blocking at the DNS and network layer spares walled gardens' native ads while defunding independent publishers' separate ad domains. When pressed, PRO's A1 conceded that an OS cannot reach inside encrypted first-party traffic. That undercut PRO's earlier claim in A2.8 that "robust" closes first-party loopholes, and CON exploited the contradiction cleanly. PRO also never directly answered whether zero-tracking contextual ads ship blocked. Instead, A2 retreated to the vague phrase "control over ads," leaving CON's dilemma intact: either ethical ads get blocked, or acceptable-ads tollbooths appear. CON answered the symmetry pressure with a plausible distinction: public conduct rules enforced through courts versus conflicted private gatekeepers policing their rivals. CON also corrected PRO's conflation of a direct ban with GDPR-style disclosure rules. PRO's closing restated its consent framing but dropped the ad and publisher harms. CON's closing pointed out that omission and tied PRO's own "referee paid by one team" line to the store-rules enforcement mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's Q1 on how an OS could block first-party ads inside encrypted native-app traffic. PRO's A1 conceded that an OS cannot reach inside encrypted first-party traffic and retreated to DNS filtering and store rules. CON then used that concession to show the mandate spares giants' native ads, defunds independent publishers, and empowers conflicted platform gatekeepers.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): CON built the cleaner path to victory. PRO's strongest move was reframing defaults around the device owner, but it never answered the decisive technical and distributional problem. After claiming robust blocking would close first-party loopholes, PRO conceded OS-level blocking cannot reach inside encrypted first-party native traffic and retreated to DNS/per-app/store rules. CON showed this either leaves walled-garden first-party ads untouched while defunding independent publishers or forces a hedge through acceptable-ads gatekeepers. PRO's symmetry line-drawing answer also failed because CON distinguished objective statutory conduct rules from platform-run tollbooths. The closing's omission of the word "ad" and evasive answer on contextual ads further confirmed the pressure landed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's exploitation of A4.1 was decisive: it turned PRO's "robust closes first-party loopholes" claim into a fatal concession by showing OS-level blocking cannot stop native first-party ads without breaking HTTPS or reverting to crude DNS blocking that spares walled gardens and harms independent publishers.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B won the decisive technical and definitional clash. PRO's "whose default?" frame was strong, but PRO retreated from robust system-level blocking to only what an OS can do via DNS, conceding B's first-party/encryption dilemma, and never clearly answered whether zero-tracking contextual ads are blocked or spared through an acceptable-ads tollbooth. B also distinguished its direct ban on covert brokerage as a public conduct rule from deputizing conflicted platform gatekeepers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttals exposing the contradiction between browser/DNS blocking and closing first-party loopholes, then tracing how that concession spares walled gardens while defunding independent publishers.
