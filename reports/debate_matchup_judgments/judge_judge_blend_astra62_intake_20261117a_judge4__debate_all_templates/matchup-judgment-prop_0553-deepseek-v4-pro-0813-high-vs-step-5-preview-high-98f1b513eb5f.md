### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins on the clearest live route: CON concedes that people should be able to repair their devices but never supplies a mechanism that reliably delivers that outcome. CON's A1 answer lists the Competition Act, consumer and warranty law, and recall powers. PRO's closing shows these work only in narrow cases: abuse of dominance needs proven market power and a costly inquiry, and recall powers need a defect or hazard. That leaves the ordinary refusal to supply a part or manual unremedied. CON then concedes in A2 that manuals and genuine parts are not a security bypass, which shrinks its security objection to diagnostics and calibration tools. PRO answered that remaining area with authenticated, revocable access tied to registered shops. That answer is somewhat detailed for a later round, but it responds directly to a pressure question. CON's main fallback, that PRO must defend the bare text of the motion without any implementing rules, reads as an overly literal framing. A national law naturally includes delegated rulemaking, and PRO's 'the motion says law, not bare sentence' reply largely defuses the objection. CON's cost and counterfeit-battery points were reasonable and stayed partly live, but they were never weighed decisively against PRO's recurring-repair-tax comparison. CON was competent and disciplined, but PRO made the decision easier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive sequence came after PRO's Q1, which demanded a mechanism for CON's conceded outcome. CON's A1 listed narrow existing laws and its A2 conceded that manuals and parts are not a bypass. PRO's closing then showed that those laws reach only dominance or defect cases, leaving routine refusals unremedied. This collapsed CON's "targeted remedies" alternative and confined its security objection to the calibration tools that PRO had already addressed.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): B wins because a national access requirement can accommodate category-specific safeguards; A repeatedly treats the proposition as complete statutory text rather than defeating that implementation. B also explains why dominance, warranty, and recall remedies leave routine repair-access refusals uncovered. A’s strongest surviving argument concerns compliance costs imposed on all buyers, while B overstates what authenticated tools can prevent. These weaknesses keep the debate competitive, but A does not establish that the risks outweigh the access benefits B identifies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s “law, not bare sentence” response directly defeats A’s claim that adding certification and security rules changes the motion. A subsequently identifies infrastructure costs, but its closing returns to the weaker objection that safeguards are not explicitly written into the proposition.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A built the more complete and persuasive path to victory. Once Side B conceded that consumers should be able to repair devices and that genuine parts and manuals are not security bypasses, Side A proved that Side B's status-quo alternatives (Competition Act dominance inquiries, warranties, and recalls) offer no enforceable remedy for routine out-of-warranty repair refusals. Side A also cleanly neutralized the security and scope objections by explaining how statutes operate via delegated technical rulemaking and how authenticated, revocable tokens allow calibration without exposing raw pairing keys. Side B raised credible concerns regarding upfront compliance costs on budget phones and oversight limits in India's informal repair sector, keeping the round competitive, but over-relied on an unconvincing hyper-literal claim that defending regulatory safeguards meant abandoning the written motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's sequence from [A3.1] to [A5.1]-[A5.2] exposing the gap in [B4.1]—showing that abuse-of-dominance cases, warranties, and recalls never give an ordinary independent shop access to withheld parts or manuals—paired with [A4.1]-[A4.4] directly answering [B3.1]-[B3.2] via delegated rulemaking and revocable hardware-validated tokens.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON ran the sharper single line of attack: the motion mandates only access, not certification, liability rules, or price floors, so PRO was "defending a different bill." Its security point, that screens carry biometric sensors and batteries pair through secure hardware, was concrete and well developed. However, the strategy rests on reading "requiring access" as "requiring only access," which the motion does not say. PRO answered that reading credibly as a category error: access is the precondition, and existing frameworks plus later calibration can operate on top of it. PRO also kept CON from ever squarely neutralizing its comparative baseline. Today's grey-market repairs already happen without genuine parts, manuals, or traceability. CON's reply that access "changes the label, not the risk" was asserted rather than shown. CON then conceded that access is "necessary, not sufficient" and accepted a baseline anti-nullification floor. PRO used that concession effectively to recast CON's objections as drafting preferences within right to repair rather than reasons to reject it. CON's claim that access-only is worse than the status quo was never comparatively proven. Both sides had clipped turns, which offset each other. PRO's evidence was somewhat looser, with unsupported claims that e-waste is "dominated" by fixable devices.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came in Rebuttal 2. CON answered A2 by accepting that a supply mandate should bar commercially unusable terms while still attacking access as insufficient. PRO had framed that move in advance: in B4.3, PRO argued that if CON objects to monopoly parts pricing, its objection is to access itself, and that one cannot discipline a supply that is legally hidden. That framing let PRO's closing turn CON's concession into proof that CON's complaints are implementation details within right to repair rather than a reason to reject the motion.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): B makes the stronger comparative case: access improves the existing informal repair market rather than introducing third-party repair into an otherwise safe system. A effectively challenges guaranteed affordability and automatic liability resolution, but its central objection treats the proposition as an exhaustive bill that excludes compatible safeguards. B's calibrated-law defense remains within the motion, particularly once A accepts a supply mandate conditioned on usable terms. B's overclaims about transparency and existing regulatory frameworks limit the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B answers the unsafe-repair argument by comparing genuine parts and documented procedures with repairs already occurring without them. A's response that genuine batteries can still be installed badly establishes residual risk, not increased risk. A's upstream-monopoly rebuttal is its strongest answer, limiting B's savings claims without establishing that access should be rejected.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side B won a high-level debate by systematicallysevering the link between bare supply access and Side A's promised safety, security, and affordability gains. Side A built a strong comparative baseline around India's existing undocumented grey-market repair sector and argued persuasively that supply transparency is a precondition for regulation. However, Side A initially leaned on non-textual certification regimes and security carve-outs before conceding in Turn 7 that affordability is absent from the text and falling back on general laws. Side B capitalized cleanly: it showed that OEMs can shift monopoly pricing upstream into proprietary parts and tool licensing without violating general competition law, proved that modern screen and battery repairs cannot be neatly separated from biometric and secure-pairing hardware, and left Side A with no clear answer to the consumer liability triangle when unlicensed shops fail. Side B's closing synthesis—that access is necessary, not sufficient, and creates uncompensated security and safety risk without those missing guardrails—gave the cleaner decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B2.6]-[B2.7], Side B dismantled Side A's competitive-pricing claim by showing that mandating access merely pushes the manufacturer monopoly upstream into proprietary parts pricing and diagnostic tool licensing; when Side A conceded in [A4.3] that affordability is not in the text and relied on India's Competition Act, Side B sealed the exchange in [B4.3] by noting that if general competition law could break OEM aftermarket bottlenecks, captive repair would not be the status quo.
