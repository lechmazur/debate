### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined and well grounded, but PRO is the clearer winner. CON's main weapon was the "redundant or rigid" dilemma: either the floor merely renames ordinary negotiated terms, or it is an irrational mandatory instrument. That dilemma was sharp, and "clause worship" framed it memorably. Its weakness is that redundancy does not defeat the motion. If good deals usually satisfy the floor, the rule is still correct as written. PRO exploited this effectively. When CON answered PRO's Q2 about remedies for residents, it listed traffic, noise, and sanitation covenants, community-use dates, and withheld installments dedicated to mitigation. PRO's closing then pointed out that these are themselves enforceable neighborhood-benefit requirements, so CON's best package complies with the motion. That left CON defending only the narrow case: a subsidy with neither revenue sharing nor any neighborhood obligation. CON never built a compelling example of that case, because its own "better package" kept including mitigation terms. PRO's "at no price" answer was a costly bullet to bite, and CON pressed it well. PRO softened the damage by distinguishing the risks each term addresses: retention and overrun clauses versus distribution of gains and local disruption. PRO also stressed that rejecting the subsidy, not waiving protection, is the right response when protection is too expensive. The debate stayed competitive, but PRO made the decision easier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Q2 about enforceable remedies for residents. CON's A2 answer listed mitigation covenants and community-use obligations. PRO's closing turned that answer: CON's strongest alternative itself satisfies the neighborhood-benefit route, so CON's dilemma collapsed into the claim that the rule is merely redundant, which does not defeat the motion. CON's counter that "at no price" creates lexical priority was its best reply, but it lacked a concrete deal that contained no neighborhood obligations and was still clearly better.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a high-level, closely contested clash by dismantling Side A's central "redundant or rigid" dilemma and exposing the distributive gap in deals that omit both safeguards. Side A (CON) mounted a sharp economic critique showing that mandatory clauses carry bargaining prices and accounting friction that could displace lower subsidies or larger private contributions, especially after forcing Side B to concede in [B4.2] that whole-deal review never overrides the floor. However, Side B persuasively showed that flexible compliance options do not make a floor redundant when cities routinely face pressure to subsidize below-cost leases offering only team retention and construction-overrun caps—terms that bound losses or secure presence without sharing commercial upside or protecting disrupted neighbors. When Side B's Q2 pressed on neighborhood disruption, Side A's best alternative relied on enforceable transit, access, and mitigation covenants that actually satisfy the motion, leaving Side A unable to justify net taxpayer subsidies lacking both forms of reciprocity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's sequence from [B3.2] and [B4.3] to [B5.1] was decisive: after refuting Side A's "redundant or rigid" framing by showing a rule can allow multiple ways to comply while still barring deals that offer none, Side B pressed Side A on what enforceable remedy disrupted neighbors receive when district forecasts fail—forcing Side A in [A4.2] to propose enforceable street, transit, community-access, and mitigation covenants that Side B cleanly showed satisfy the motion's neighborhood-benefit requirement.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side A survived B's redundant-or-rigid dilemma by defining a substantive but flexible floor: community access and mitigation can qualify, while rent, retention, and overrun protection alone do not.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A's Rebuttal 2 and closing turn: B's traffic, noise, sanitation, and community-use covenants were shown to be neighborhood-benefit requirements, collapsing B's claim that a better enforceable package would necessarily be blocked.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined and avoided fake precision. PRO built a vivid reciprocity case around upside capture, lost leverage, and an "unpriced call option," and its framing was the more memorable. CON, however, targeted the motion's categorical structure more effectively. It showed that a substantial fixed-rent deal is itself an enforceable public claim that can price expected upside in advance. It also exposed a tension in PRO's either/or rule: neighborhood harm cannot explain why a revenue share with no protections qualifies while fixed rent does not. PRO's answer, that fixed rent omits both protections while a revenue share omits only one, was reasonable. But CON turned PRO's own "proceeds can fund mitigation" point by noting that fixed rent can fund mitigation equally well. CON also contested PRO's claim that added clauses merely reveal a hidden cost, arguing they buy a different bargain that can leave taxpayers worse off. PRO's closing largely reasserted that claim rather than defeating the counterexample. Because the motion requires a necessity rule, CON's showing that a better enforceable bargain could be prohibited left it the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was the [B2.6]/[B3.2] inconsistency pressure followed by [B4.2]. PRO defended revenue-share-only deals on the ground that proceeds "can fund mitigation." CON replied that fixed rent can fund mitigation too, so neighborhood protection cannot separate the permitted mechanism from the prohibited one. This blunted PRO's "can is not must" closing line.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that cities can secure binding, enforceable value—such as substantial fixed rent paired with public ownership, overrun guarantees, and maintenance/repayment duties—without using either of the proposition's two prescribed contract mechanisms. Side A cleanly neutralized Side B's "forecast versus leverage" framing by showing fixed leases also bind teams before construction while revenue percentages still rely on forecasting, exposed that pricing expected upside into higher guaranteed rent is not leaving upside "unpriced," and proved that Side B's own either/or rule permits revenue sharing with zero neighborhood protections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's takedown of Side B's neighborhood-mitigation distinction across [A2.6], [A4.2], and [A5.2]: after Side B defended revenue-sharing-only deals because proceeds "can fund mitigation" [B4.2] yet attacked fixed rent in closing because "'can' is not 'must'" [B5.1], Side A noted that fixed rent can equally fund mitigation while Side B's permitted revenue share likewise imposes no mandatory mitigation.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B showed enforceable fixed rent can price uncertain upside and deliver greater guaranteed public value, while A's either/or rule cannot distinguish its permitted options: revenue sharing need not fund neighborhood protection and fixed rent can. B also answered the price objection directly—visibility does not justify prohibiting a better bargain or assuming extra obligations come without tradeoffs. A's upside and leverage framing remained forceful but was substantially neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 and closing were decisive: candidly admitting fixed rent gives no automatic revenue percentage or displacement clause, then showing revenue sharing has the same neighborhood-protection gap and that converting rent into a revenue-sharing floor changes, rather than preserves, the bargain.
