### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): CON (A) won clearly. Its core argument was that PRO's instruments suit temporary friction but fail if jobs actually disappear. CON pressed this point consistently and comparatively in every speech. It also exposed a contradiction in PRO's case: if retrained workers land jobs paying more than before, there is no wage gap for wage insurance to cover ("the boom and the bridge"). CON's questions forced two damaging admissions. PRO conceded that no observable signal could tell it to wait, and its plan to fund support from the productivity that supposedly erodes the middle-class tax base was circular. PRO's grounding was poor. It misdated and misdescribed the UK Job Retention Scheme as a 2008 reskilling programme and backed it with an unverifiable 78% figure. Its second rebuttal added fabricated-sounding figures: a 12% fall in vacancies and a 45% rise in AI-related postings. Its first rebuttal was also clipped for length. PRO never squarely answered CON's alternative of claims-triggered, scalable benefits. That alternative arguably counts as "preparing now," and CON half-admitted as much, but PRO did not exploit the opening. PRO's closing mostly restated its asymmetry and moral-duty claims and added a new "constitutional" appeal rather than engaging the instrument-mismatch critique.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1–A2.3 decided the debate. CON showed that the Job Retention Scheme was the 2020 furlough programme, which kept workers attached to jobs that still existed, so it supports CON's "friction, not disappearance" thesis. CON also showed that PRO's claim of higher salaries after retraining makes wage insurance unnecessary. PRO never recovered from this exchange.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): Side A (CON) won dominantly by dismantling all three proposed policy instruments on their own mechanics while maintaining far superior epistemic discipline. Side A proved that retraining, wage insurance, and permanent income support are calibrated to temporary friction rather than the structural middle-tier disappearance Side B forecasts, whereas scalable, claims-triggered unemployment systems pay laid-off workers immediately on separation without needing to diagnose AI causation or lock in permanent fiscal commitments on an unfalsifiable forecast. Side B relied on brittle, inaccurate claims—misidentifying Britain's 2020 pandemic furlough as a 2008 reskilling program and introducing unverified exact job-posting percentages in Rebuttal 2—dropped Side A's sharpest critiques, and had Rebuttal 1 clipped for exceeding the word limit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A2.1]-[A2.3], Side A pointed out that Side B's flagship example—the UK Job Retention Scheme—was actually Britain's 2020 COVID furlough keeping workers attached to existing jobs rather than a 2008 reskilling bridge for vanished occupations, turning the example to prove that wage subsidies bridge temporary friction rather than disappearance, while also catching Side B's contradiction in claiming reskilled workers earn higher salaries yet still require wage insurance.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B won by showing PRO's tools fit friction, not disappearance: wage insurance needs a new paycheck, retraining targets jobs AI hits first, and income support relies on the tax base displacement would erode. B also exposed PRO's unfalsifiable forecast and offered scalable, claims-triggered preparation that survives being wrong about timing, while A relied on unsupported statistics and repeated risk asymmetry without answering the mismatch.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's correction of the UK furlough example and the boom-versus-bridge contradiction: if reskilling yields higher-paid ML jobs there is no wage gap to insure, and the cited scheme worked only because jobs returned.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won clearly. It framed the motion as a question of sequencing under uncertainty and built a clean asymmetry: CON's errors (idle classrooms, wound-down pilots) can be refunded, while PRO's errors (permanent earnings scarring, a lost cohort) cannot. It also turned CON's own alternative, scalable portable benefits, into a concession that preparation before proof is acceptable. PRO further showed that CON's "modest productivity gains, no headcount cuts" evidence and its "hidden exits" admission confirm that the harm would never show up in CON's trigger. In A1, PRO gave a concrete scale-up trigger, separating cheap design work now from contingent payouts later. CON (A) leaned heavily on unverifiable historical anecdotes: - dot-com "knowledge-economy" retraining - 2015 European digital curricula - OECD wage-subsidy offshoring - a fabricated-sounding WIOA figure of "0.3% of GDP / $90 billion" These are brittle fake precision and earn little credit. CON's moral-hazard mechanism was never substantiated against PRO's point that wage insurance pays workers, not firms. Two of CON's speeches were clipped for exceeding the word limit. In closing, CON misattributed the multi-dimensional trigger to PRO when it was CON's own. CON's own trigger (a two-year rise in unemployment duration plus a 15% earnings gap) handed PRO its best closing line: such a trigger only confirms damage already done.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing seized on CON's A1 trigger, which waits for a two-year rise in unemployment duration and a 15% earnings collapse. PRO called it "a receipt for damage already booked," not a threshold for action. Paired with CON's "hidden exits" concession, this showed that waiting for proof guarantees help arrives too late, which neutralized CON's core route to victory.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side B built a dominant case around institutional lead times, white-collar displacement hiding in junior hiring freezes, and the asymmetry between refundable administrative preparation and irreversible wage scarring. Side B cleanly separated modest upfront blueprint and pilot costs from contingent payouts, dismantled the moral-hazard claim, and proved that portable health coverage and pensions do not replace lost earnings. Side A repeatedly undercut itself: it conceded that hidden exits cause long-term earnings losses invisible to layoff counts, proposed waiting until average unemployment duration rises by two years before starting to build programs, relied on fabricated WIOA administrative-cost figures in Rebuttal 2, had two speeches clipped, and confused its own claims with Side B's in both Pressure Questions and Closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side A conceded in [A2.4] that hidden exits inflict long-term earnings losses without showing up in layoff statistics, Side B's [B3.1] forced Side A to define "definitive proof." When Side A answered in [A4.1] with a threshold requiring at least a two-year jump in average unemployment duration over three quarters, Side B's closing [B5.1-B5.2] devastatingly exposed that standard as "a receipt for damage already booked" before help is even designed.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A built the stronger comparative case: preparation takes years, displacement can arrive via hiring freezes and hidden exits invisible to layoff proof, and errors are asymmetric — idle pilots are refundable, earnings scarring is not. A turned B's safety-net alternative into a concession for preparing, rebutted moral hazard by noting firms automate when software beats salary, and used B's own extreme trigger to show help would arrive only after damage. B never defended its billions claim credibly and relied on brittle, unsupported cost precision.
