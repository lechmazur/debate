### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): A wins by distinguishing prioritizing corridors from abandoning enforcement and explaining how lawful recruitment, job portability, and wage protections address incentives producing irregularity. B raises a legitimate quota-and-deterrence challenge but repeatedly assumes that A eliminates credible removal, never establishing why mass deportations are necessary rather than targeted enforcement. A also turns B's acknowledgment of employer blackmail into a persuasive comparative argument. A's categorical claims about screening and origin-state cooperation are overstated, limiting the margin, but B's repeated prerequisite claim remains substantially less defended.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A directly answers B's central reward-effect argument: recruitment occurs at origin, irregular crossing forfeits quota access, and targeted removals remain available. B continues asserting an eventual-status incentive without explaining why those safeguards fail. This leaves B arguing against an amnesty-and-nonenforcement model rather than A's actual proposal.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that mass deportation cannot be a workable prerequisite: existing sweeps already fail to empty labor demand, sponsor abuse and deportation threats manufacture irregularity and wage blackmail, and scaled screening plus origin-document cooperation without mobility deals is implausible. Side A kept a live deterrence intuition, but its triage-desk and “remove the pool first” answers did not neutralize those structural contradictions or the continued role of targeted enforcement under corridors.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 most damaged the case by answering the open-door and wage questions directly, then exposing the contradiction between mass removals, individual abuse screening, and origin cooperation.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): B built the stronger causal case that legal corridors, portability, and employer enforcement reduce irregularity while retaining targeted removals. A offered a coherent deterrence argument but repeatedly assumed that prioritizing corridors meant weak enforcement and that mass, abuse-screened returns were administratively feasible. B answered pressure more directly, exposed those assumptions, and gave clearer mechanisms linking smuggling, wage undercutting, and sponsor abuse.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both questions directly and turned A’s admission that employers weaponize deportation threats into an argument that further mass removals deepen wage undercutting. A’s streamlined-triage response asserted screening capacity without adequately resolving that contradiction.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B more convincingly challenged A's central diagnosis: existing Gulf legal recruitment does not eliminate incentives for irregular hiring, and North African transit migration cannot simply be treated as unmet local labor demand. Its pressure questions exposed unresolved quota, overstay, and onward-migration problems. A answered with overstated assurances about eliminating backlogs and tracking migrants, then largely repeated its opening in closing. A's precise, unsupported deportation-history claims also weakened its grounding. B's victory is limited by its insufficiently established leap from necessary enforcement to prioritizing mass deportations, alongside overconfident cost and deterrence claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange concerned North African transit: local work rights do not remove Europe's wage attraction, and legal earnings can finance onward travel. A's response about registration, coercion, and rushed crossings never established why those benefits would outweigh this competing mechanism.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by showing that prioritizing corridors cannot govern the problem the motion covers. Gulf irregularity already coexists with large legal systems and excess supply, while North African flows are transit-driven, so local visas can fund rather than replace smuggling. Side B’s revolving-door and job-matching story stayed coherent but never neutralized scale, overstay, youth-displacement, or migrant-agency pressure, and the closing mostly restated the unmet-demand thesis.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s rebuttal that North African informal work is a stopgap to fund Europe, so seasonal visas become smuggler savings accounts while removal before the fare is paid starves the route, most damaged the claim that corridors can replace deportation capacity.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A built the stronger comparative case by distinguishing Gulf labor-surplus and employer-evasion problems from North African transit migration, then showing why capped corridors cannot govern people outside their quotas or eliminate Europe’s wage pull. Side B offered a coherent revolving-door critique of deportations and a useful kafala distinction, but its answers relied too heavily on assumptions that job-matched quotas would collapse irregular incentives and that origin-state cooperation would follow. Its closing repeated the labor-demand story without resolving those scale and transit objections. Side A answered both pressure questions directly and sharpened, rather than merely recapped, the central clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered the revolving-door and smuggling questions directly, then exposed Side B’s concession that seasonal visa holders could still pursue Europe, turning legal local earnings into potential smuggler fares while leaving outside-cap arrivals and overstays unaddressed.
