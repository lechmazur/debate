### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases and answered pressure questions directly. B edges it on the decisive clash: A's core suppression argument (immigration props up low-wage models) was progressively converted by B into support for B's own model — A's own account blamed cheap, bonded labor, and B's humane, well-paid, mobile immigration is precisely the cure A keeps describing, culminating in B's "one drives the other" synthesis. A's dilemma response (tied or lost to Tokyo) was answered concretely with regional pathways. A's strongest late turn — that wage pressure requires scarcity, which mass immigration erases — landed only in the closing and went unanswered, but it contests rather than breaks B's synthesis. A's scale infeasibility route survives but was blunted by B's Canada-rate comparison and broader source-pool answer, and B's "slow, not stop" concession was reframed effectively since the motion is about prioritization, not halting decline. B answered A's pressure questions with more concrete substance (directional numbers, source countries, regional visas), while A's answers were honest but more concessive. Razor-thin but real edge to B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 catch that Side A had complained about both bonded workers [A2.1] and free-to-move workers [A3.2], exposing an apparent inconsistency, combined with B's reframe that worker mobility is exactly the wage pressure A's productivity mechanism needs — though A's price-and-scale answer [A4.1] partially recovered.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): B narrowly wins by better defending the actual proposition: materially easing workforce decline rather than eliminating it. Its recruitment and regional-retention answers preserved a plausible route to adding caregivers and taxpayers alongside automation. A effectively defended augmentation and distinguished targeted migration from cheap labor. However, it repeatedly demanded complete demographic or rural-staffing solutions from B, while accepting continued adaptation itself. Its final investment argument also assumed that significantly more immigration would erase scarcity, despite B proposing only to slow workforce decline. B's opening overclaims keep this competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response to the demographic treadmill was decisive: significantly more immigration need not stabilize the population to provide substantial working-age contributions. A subsequently conceded that neither plan stops contraction, weakening its repeated use of “slow, not stop” as an objection to prioritizing immigration.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): B won by turning A's strongest points into CON mechanisms: cheap, bonded labor pools explained why scarcity never forced modernization, and significantly expanding immigration risks recreating that reservoir. B also forced A to concede immigration only slows decline while adaptation remains necessary under both plans, then offered the cleaner comparison of prioritizing controllable, compounding productivity and higher wages over an uncertain permanent recruitment race.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first rebuttal was decisive: it reframed the surviving fax machine not as proof scarcity fails, but as proof firms repeatedly avoided scarcity through cheap labor, directly damaging A's claim that more labor supply would drive upgrading.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Both sides were strong, but PRO (B) held the stronger overall path. PRO stayed on the motion as written, committed to a concrete number when pressed (roughly doubling net inflows), and gave a real mechanism that blunted CON's treadmill dilemma: immigrants bridge the postwar cohort bulge, pay in for decades, and raise working children, so the ratio returns "gentler" rather than simply repeating. CON's dilemma (either large enough to treadmill or small enough to be meaningless) was sharp, but PRO's bulge-bridge answer plus the "converged fertility still produces children; yours produces no one" rebuttal left it only partially standing. CON's alternative case (raise care pay, deepen hours, consolidate firms, shrink the footprint) was plausible and well-turned, but PRO's weighing that every CON remedy only reshuffles a shrinking pool while only immigration enlarges it went largely unanswered in kind. Both sides answered pressure questions directly with A1/A2 structure and used background knowledge responsibly. CON's closing was rhetorically the best speech of the debate, but it rested on the converging-fertility assumption PRO had already contested. A close, competitive win for the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's A4.4-A4.5 turn claiming PRO's own half-million-a-year figure was the disowned "Toronto a year" and that 20 million arrivals cannot be scaffolding. PRO had already planted the counter: the boomer bulge passes within decades, newcomers pay in for forty years, and their Japanese-raised children work — so the ratio returns with a bigger base, not the same problem repeated. CON never fully neutralized that mechanism, while PRO's "only immigration makes the pool bigger" weighing survived CON's pay-and-consolidation alternative.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): B narrowly wins by connecting immigration priority to hard-to-automate care needs and the release of family caregivers into employment. A offered credible domestic-hours and consolidation alternatives, but never established that immigrants' eventual aging negates decades of benefits or requires ever-larger inflows. Managing decline need not mean permanently reversing it. B's awkward shift from “modest” inflows to half a million annually, incomplete engagement with productivity gains, and premature accusation of unanswered questions keep the margin narrow.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's caregiver example directly challenged A's fiscal accounting: an immigrant aide can also preserve a family member's employment and taxes. A's reimbursement-and-pay response identified an alternative staffing mechanism, but did not eliminate that indirect benefit.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): CON won the central scale dilemma: PRO first called large inflows a straw target, then under pressure committed to half a million net arrivals yearly for decades. CON showed that either immigration is modest and cannot fix the worker-retiree ratio, or it is large enough to matter and recreates the aging and integration burden later. CON also answered the care-work pressure by explaining expanded hours, higher pay, consolidation, and a shrinking footprint, while PRO's bridge argument never fully escaped the treadmill.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it converted PRO's own half-million-a-year answer into the previously mocked Toronto-scale inflow, totaling twenty million over forty years, and argued that this cohort would age into the same ratio problem rather than serve as a temporary bridge.
