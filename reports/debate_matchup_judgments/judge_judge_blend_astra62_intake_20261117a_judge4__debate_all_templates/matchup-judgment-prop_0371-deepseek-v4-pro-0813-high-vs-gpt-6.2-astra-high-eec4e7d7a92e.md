### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won every major clash by showing that requiring upfront dual-price disclosure solves drip pricing without the distortions of a bundled carry-on mandate. Using a concrete comparison ($99 personal item / $119 roller vs. $109 bundled), Side A proved that light packers and roller-bag travelers have opposite cheapest options, so forcing the bundled price to lead and relegating no-bag fares to secondary discounts inflicts search friction on backpack travelers and steers them toward overpaying. Side A also won the operational clash—showing that visible no-bag savings incentivize flexible travelers to leave overhead space for others rather than rationing scarce bins by boarding order and gate-checking, a point Side B dropped after Turn 3—and dismantled Side B's enforcement advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side B argued that a headline mandate avoids the burden of policing dual totals across ads and emails [B3.2, B4.3], Side A exposed in [A3.2] and [A4.5-A4.6] that because Side B permits "secondary" no-bag discounts, regulators must still inspect those exact same ads and emails to judge prominence while also running booking checks—collapsing Side B's claim of enforcement at the source.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that honest, equally visible dual prices already stop drip pricing, while a mandatory carry-on headline steers light travelers away from their cheapest flight and, once no-bag discounts are allowed, recreates the same ad-policing problem. Side B’s anchoring and bright-line enforcement arguments were strong but depended on Side A’s display rule failing and never resolved the ranking conflict or scarce-bin incentive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal and close: equal side-by-side totals fix the omission without choosing whose trip counts, and policing whether a no-bag price is “secondary” collapses Side B’s claimed enforcement advantage.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B made a coherent case for an inclusive headline fare, but Side A more effectively showed that mandatory dual-price disclosure addresses hidden charges without imposing the carry-on traveler’s price on backpack-only passengers. A directly answered both pressure questions, exposed the remaining enforcement burden in B’s “secondary discount” rule, and turned B’s anchoring argument against the mandate: sorting by the traveler’s actual trip is arithmetic, not deception. B’s responses were polished but relied increasingly on an idealized “ordinary” traveler and did not neutralize that comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal and closing were decisive: A showed that B’s mandate still requires inspecting advertisements, while a single headline cannot serve travelers whose cheapest fares differ. The closing’s $99/$109/$119 comparison made the consumer cost of B’s tidier default especially clear.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins a clear, high-level debate by dismantling Side B's (PRO's) core justification on its own terms while establishing two durable harms from mandatory bundling. Side B smartly conceded that upfront fee disclosure can solve basic fare-comparison math for travelers who know what they are packing, pivoting to protect uncertain early bookers from post-booking lock-in fees. However, Side A showed that mandatory inclusion does not spare uncertain travelers from paying for unused capacity—it simply makes prepaying for bag insurance compulsory for everyone, forcing personal-item-only flyers to cross-subsidize roller-bag users (a cost-shift Side B repeatedly conceded). Side A also cleanly separated legitimate marginal scarcity/handling costs (where a $0 marginal bag price worsens roller demand and bin congestion) from abusive post-booking lock-in rents, showing the latter calls for booking-time disclosure and lock-in policing rather than outlawing no-bag fares.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side B pivoted in [B2.1] and [B5.1] to argue that uncertain early bookers face a bad choice between wasting money on prepurchased bag allowances or paying monopoly lock-in fees later, Side A turned the point decisively in [A2.1]-[A2.2] and [A5.1]-[A5.2]: a mandatory baseline forces every passenger—both uncertain packers who end up traveling light and certain light packers—to prepay for an unused allowance anyway, while removing the opt-out altogether.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by keeping a less restrictive answer to Side B’s best points: booking-time disclosure plus optional prepurchase can police lock-in and comparison tricks without abolishing the no-bag fare. Side B conceded that certain light packers get a worse deal and never showed why that cost shift, or the remaining bin-demand problem, was required to stop post-booking rent. The closing clash therefore favors leaving the opt-out in place.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal and closing: a later bag fee above marginal cost is lock-in rent, and the fix is a competitive booking-time option the light packer can decline, not a universal bundle.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): B made the stronger comparative case by shifting the question from whether bags impose costs—which B acknowledged—to whether airlines compete on the full price of an ordinary trip while passengers can still switch. B answered both pressure questions directly, supplied a workable regulator-set baseline, and exposed the limits of disclosure for travelers whose packing needs are uncertain. A retained a substantial objection: certain light packers lose a genuine opt-out, and bag fees can reduce bin demand. But A relied increasingly on repeating that conceded cost-shift without showing that disclosure and prepurchase eliminate post-booking lock-in rather than merely reveal or insure against it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to “disclosure plus prepurchase” was decisive: prepurchase can recreate the same unused-allowance risk A attributes to bundling, while waiting leaves the passenger facing one already-chosen carrier. A correctly replied that optionality still protects certain light packers, but did not fully repair the uncertain-packer case.
