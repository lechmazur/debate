### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): This was a close, high-quality exchange. CON built a disciplined case that adult agreement is only a decision procedure, not evidence of benefit, and its dilemma on "appropriate" was sharp: either it assumes the disputed evidence, or it reduces to variable individual judgment. PRO's efficacy claims also leaned on assertion, such as calling incongruent puberty "the primary trigger" and saying "zero evidence" favors counseling. PRO nonetheless won the decisive clash on symmetry. CON conceded early that waiting is non-neutral and that the same expected-outcome question governs both paths. In A4.2 it admitted counseling alone is not proven superior. In A4.1 it still answered "No," refusing access even when strict safeguards are met. That left CON demanding comparative proof from treatment while defaulting to non-treatment without equivalent proof. CON never gave a convincing reason why the burden should fall only on intervention when puberty is also irreversible. PRO exploited this cleanly in its pressure questions, its second rebuttal and its closing. CON's narrow reading of the motion as "diagnosis plus agreement alone" was also somewhat contestable, since PRO reasonably read "appropriate" as importing clinical standards.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 and its follow-up were decisive. Asking what evidence shows psychological care alone yields better outcomes forced CON to concede that counseling is not proven superior (A4.2). PRO then turned that concession, together with CON's flat "No" on the safeguarded case (A4.1), into the charge that CON applies its evidentiary demand to only one path. CON's best counter was the "appropriate" dilemma in A4.4, but it never fully explained why the default should be non-intervention.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): B wins a competitive debate by distinguishing reasons to consider treatment from justification for the proposed access rule. A convincingly established that waiting carries irreversible costs and offered a plausible mechanism for relief. However, A overstated predictable psychological benefits and what monitoring could establish, while repeatedly portraying B’s expected-benefit requirement as a demand for certainty. B answered the pressure questions directly and kept the selection problem live: clinical agreement does not itself resolve comparative benefit and irreversible-risk concerns. B’s reading of the motion was somewhat rigid, but its central rebuttal remained stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s statement that waiting’s non-neutrality “poses the comparison; it does not settle it” accepted A’s strongest premise without conceding the conclusion. Coupled with its distinction between present improvement and overall expected benefit, this materially challenged A’s argument. A’s closing instead returned to an impossible-guarantee standard that B had expressly rejected.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side A won by keeping the vote tied to the motion as written. Side B persuasively showed that waiting is not neutral and that distress can be severe, but never closed the gap between "someone must decide under uncertainty" and "this gate yields better expected outcomes." A repeatedly showed that "appropriate" either assumes the disputed benefit or is only a permission procedure, that B's clearest-case vignette imported safeguards the motion does not require, and that preventing physical change is not the same as proving net adult benefit. B's pressure on A's refusal even under stricter safeguards stayed live, but A's direct answers and tighter burden work made the cleaner reason to reject the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal was decisive: meeting age, persistence, and counseling standards still would not justify this rule, and "appropriate" either begs the efficacy question or collapses into the adult agreement B had to defend.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Both sides were disciplined and responsive, and the result is close. PRO set a smart burden from the start: the motion only requires access in safeguarded cases, so CON must defend categorical denial. PRO pressed that frame consistently, and its Q1 exposed what looked like a contradiction: CON says clinicians cannot predict outcomes, yet seems confident that waiting is better for everyone. CON neutralized that pressure more effectively than PRO neutralized CON's core route. CON recast the age limit as an ethical rule under uncertainty rather than a universal prediction. It then built a clear error-asymmetry argument: - If dysphoria persists, adult transition remains available. - If a minor is wrongly medicalized, lost fertility, impaired sexual function and permanent hormone changes cannot be undone. CON added the proxy-consent limit, illustrated by the point that parents and clinicians could not elect sterilization for a sixteen-year-old. That reinforced why the motion's "parents and clinicians agree" condition is not enough. PRO's best counter was that natal puberty also causes enduring changes and that years of suffering are not refunded at eighteen. CON partly conceded this but explained why the two errors are unequal. PRO restated symmetry rather than defeating the asymmetry argument. On the pressure questions: - PRO's answer to the request for a concrete standard was mostly process language. CON exploited that sharply. - PRO's psychiatric-medication precedent was plausibly distinguished by CON: those drugs have side effects clinicians try to reverse, whereas this pathway works by suppressing healthy development as its core mechanism. PRO was slightly more epistemically careful. CON leaned on confident claims, such as that puberty "often" resolves distress, without much hedging. Overall, CON left the clearer reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came in A4.1 and A4.3. CON answered PRO's "you can't predict either" question by reframing the age boundary as an ethical rule about unequal errors rather than a prediction. CON then showed that PRO's A1 offered only process verbs, not a criterion that separates persistent dysphoria from transient or comorbid distress. That turned PRO's individualized-judgment framing into "irreversible guesswork."
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): A better justified retaining a safeguarded option by explaining why both treatment and mandatory waiting can produce lasting harm, then specifying staged assessment and grounds for declining treatment. B correctly challenged A’s excessive claim that an age restriction requires proving nobody could benefit, and its future-autonomy argument kept the contest competitive. However, B repeatedly moved from imperfect prediction to wholly ineffective screening without establishing that inference. Its comparison also insufficiently justified why support and eventual adult transition outweigh the costs of delay. A’s qualified, individualized approach therefore had the stronger grounding and comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest rebuttal was that natal puberty is not a neutral baseline and psychosocial support can accompany rather than necessarily replace treatment. B eventually acknowledged waiting’s harms, but did not adequately establish its decisive claim that the unequal risks categorically favor waiting over screened access.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A won by locking the debate onto unequal errors and answering the hardest pressure more cleanly. Side B strongly framed the motion as safeguarded access, not treatment on demand, but never gave a concrete way to separate lifelong dysphoria from clinically identical distress that might resolve; its psychiatric-drug precedent was also distinguished as off-target side effects rather than intended disruption of healthy development. Side A conceded that waiting is painful, then weighed the live choice: adult transition remains open after natal puberty, while wrongful medicalization can permanently cost fertility and sexual function. That ethical-rule response neutralized the charge of a universal prediction, while Side B’s individualized standard stayed procedural.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal, which recast the age boundary as an unequal-error rule rather than a prediction that every minor benefits from waiting, and rejected the psychiatric-drug analogy because infertility and sexual impairment are the pathway’s mechanism, not reversible side effects.
