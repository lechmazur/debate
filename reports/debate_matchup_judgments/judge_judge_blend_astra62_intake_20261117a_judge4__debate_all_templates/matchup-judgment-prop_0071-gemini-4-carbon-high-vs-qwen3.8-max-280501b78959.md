### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO built a coherent causal and legal story: continuous states inherit assets as well as liabilities, the harm was material and compounding, and apologies leave it unpaid. CON's strongest route was administrability and the lack of a limiting principle. PRO answered that pressure directly. It limited liability to documented, state-backed extraction and proposed negotiated statutory closure on the Indigenous-settlement model. It also argued that sovereign former colonies and documented domestic communities satisfy CON's own test of a recognized community. PRO then effectively turned CON's alternative. Race-conscious programs "where exclusion is documented," artifact return and land reform all concede that affected groups can be identified and that material repair is owed. PRO also pressed that CON's domestic programs are baseline duties rather than restitution, and that CON's international remedy of debt relief and development aid does nothing for states like Haiti that already paid. CON stayed consistent and articulate, but its closing claim that PRO "never answers" who holds the claim misdescribed A4.2 and A5.3. Its answer to PRO's Q1 also sidestepped why perpetrator states should keep the compounded gains. CON's backlash and diffusion points stayed live but were outweighed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2/A4.2 answer to CON's certification question was the most decisive. It held that former colonies are already bounded jurisdictions with colonial records, so funding them needs no lineage certification. It added that CON's own race-conscious targeting "where exclusion is documented" already requires identifying affected groups. That neutralized CON's central administrability objection by turning CON's own policy against it.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): A more convincingly distinguished state restitution from personal guilt and explained the value of transferring capital and control to affected communities. It answered eligibility pressure with documented extraction, bounded jurisdictions, and negotiated settlements; B continued attacking an individual ancestry ledger without adequately defeating that model. B offered a legitimate material-reform alternative, and A overstated its inadequacy, especially regarding development assistance. Nevertheless, A better resolved the central clash over whether administrative complexity defeats a distinct restitution obligation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A used B’s acceptance of Indigenous settlements to challenge its administrative objection: sovereign former colonies can also provide recognized communities, bounded jurisdictions, and negotiated closure. B repeated concerns about scale and mixed ancestry but did not squarely explain why those concerns defeat community-level reparations.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO won by reframing baseline services as duties already owed, not repayment, and by answering identifiability through sovereign former colonies and documented communities. CON's present-disadvantage alternative never explained why perpetrator states should retain compounded wealth while offering revocable aid instead of binding restitution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and closing turned CON's Indigenous-settlement exception against it: former colonies meet CON's own test of recognized political communities, bounded territories, negotiation, and finality, without ancestry tribunals.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Both sides were competent and well grounded. PRO had a strong principled core: state continuity, unjust enrichment, and the point that apologies leave assets where the theft placed them. CON, however, did more to shape the debate as it narrowed. First, CON built a durable administrative and sovereignty fork. Reparations must either rely on lineage proof that excludes undocumented descendants or on proxies that sweep in non-descendants. Cross-border payments must either abandon overseas communities or have former empires choose recipients abroad. PRO's answers ("autonomous funds," "community-governed trusts") were plausible, but CON kept pressing them and PRO never fully resolved the undocumented-descendant problem. Second, CON's pressure question on affluent descendants forced PRO to concede means-testing. CON then exploited that concession: once PRO means-tests, it is no longer strict restitution but present asset poverty "behind an ancestral gate." Third, CON's closing image of two zero-net-worth families made the solidarity and fairness cost concrete. PRO's closing largely restated the bank analogy without answering that turn. PRO's best reply was that CON had retreated from apologies to universal asset policy. That point was fair but not decisive, because CON consistently presented universal policy as the material remedy and apologies as the remedy for the historical wrong. On balance, CON made the decisive clash clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's turn of PRO's bank-robbery analogy was the most decisive exchange. A true restitution debt would be owed to an affluent heir too. Yet PRO's A2 answer means-tested benefits and dropped affluent descendants. That let CON argue PRO had abandoned the unjust-enrichment logic and was simply targeting present poverty through an ancestry filter. PRO never rebuilt the distinction in its closing.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B wins the comparative-remedy clash. A establishes a plausible state obligation and reasonably answers the cross-border administration challenge. But B explains how wealth-targeted universal programs can address inherited deprivation without lineage barriers, then exposes an unresolved tension between A’s restitution rationale and means-tested eligibility. A’s closing reiterates the specific debt without adequately explaining why its additional ancestry gate outweighs unequal treatment of equally asset-poor families under finite budgets. B’s objections are not all conclusive, but its later speeches sharpen the choice more effectively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B first answers that universal programs can target missing wealth, not merely income, undermining A’s material-effect distinction. Its subsequent affluent-heir challenge pressures A’s bank-robbery analogy: if restitution establishes entitlement, why does present need determine individual eligibility? A restates its position rather than fully resolving that tension.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B wins by sustaining its eligibility, sovereignty, and ancestry-gate challenges while offering apologies for the historical wrong plus universal wealth-building for present deprivation. Side A establishes a specific intergenerational debt, but its means-testing and trust proposals never explain why ancestry should supplement need-based aid or how cross-border programs avoid elite capture and unequal treatment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal turns A's bank-robbery analogy against it: genuine restitution would owe affluent heirs too, so A's means-tested program is really present-poverty relief behind a genealogical gate that universal asset policy can deliver without excluding equally poor neighbors.
