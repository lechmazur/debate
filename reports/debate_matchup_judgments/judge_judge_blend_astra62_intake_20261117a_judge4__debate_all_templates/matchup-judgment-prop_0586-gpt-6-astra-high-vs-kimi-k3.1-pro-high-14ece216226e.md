- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): B won a competitive debate by keeping the motion’s dual burden live: substantial, broadly shared pay gains and fewer employment losses. B’s later rebuttal and close crystallized that flexibility is real while dependable dual superiority remained assumed. A’s clipping-free structure and careful answers helped, but B’s comparative pressure decided it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s R2/closing trap: if care gets smaller raises, broadly shared gains fail; if it gets large raises, the employment edge disappears—so sectoral flexibility is not yet dual superiority.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO maintained a live comparative advantage: equal legal thresholds do not equalize economic pressure, so sectoral boards can set larger increases where monopsony exists and staged meaningful increases where funding-constrained, preserving broadly shared gains with less avoidable job loss. CON's valid points about within-industry heterogeneity and fallible learning limit but do not reverse that precision gain, and its demand for identical pay delivery was neutralized by B's Q2. Clipped openings also cost CON execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response that imperfect differentiation still outperforms no differentiation, and that an across-the-board policy that varies by industry to avoid losses has adopted sectoral logic — equal phase-ins keep PRO's where/how-tightly advantage.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully framing sectoral wage boards as a multi-sector optimization problem that avoids the deadweight loss of a blunt, across-the-board minimum wage. Side A effectively neutralized Side B's institutional objections by pivoting to statutory wage boards and handled the trade-off critique by pointing out that a uniform minimum wage forces a single compromise that either destroys jobs in vulnerable sectors or leaves wages unnecessarily low in profitable ones. Side B's strongest argument that calibrating down to save jobs means failing to raise pay substantially for the most vulnerable was adequately answered by Side A's point that the alternative for those workers under a high uniform floor is job loss. Furthermore, Side B suffered a significant execution cost by having both its Opening and First Rebuttal clipped for exceeding the word limit, leaving its early arguments incomplete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it directly answered the pressure questions and crystallized the multi-sector optimization advantage, effectively dismantling Side B's dilemma that boards must either suppress raises or recreate job losses.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): A won by making the decisive comparison clearer: once phase-ins/review are common to both policies, differentiation still changes where wage pressure lands, while a large uniform rate cannot. B scored heavily on bargaining bias, capture, and “permission ≠ sound targeting,” and rightly pressed that caution can just mean smaller raises—but never neutralized A’s where-versus-when point or the relative burden on the motion. A’s answers to B’s pressure questions were more complete, and A’s closing crystallized the live clash better than B’s continued demand for demonstrated perfection of boards.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s pressure/response sequence: a nationwide floor still cannot distinguish resilient from fragile employers (WHEN ≠ WHERE), so uniform overshoot retains the larger employment risk even after staging and review.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO kept the comparative test live: a uniform large hike concentrates risk where absorption is weakest, while sectoral boards can phase and vary pressure before the full shock and keep a statutory backstop. CON showed boards are imperfect and that smaller raises in fragile sectors is a trade-off, but conceded in A4.1 that a nationwide rate cannot distinguish cases and could cause greater losses, and did not neutralize PRO's answers on reallocation of pressure and public-member safeguards. That left PRO with the cleaner path to fewer employment losses for comparable pay gains.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's handling of Q1/Q2 across B2 and B4 – that nationwide phase-ins only change when, not where, burden lands, that CON's incumbent-protection and information critiques apply equally to a single rate, and that sectoral differentiation with public approval, published reasons, and classification rules governs the risk rather than eliminating targeting altogether.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully defending the core comparative advantage of sectoral bargaining: the ability to differentiate wage pressures across varied economic realities, which a uniform minimum wage cannot do even with phase-ins. While Side B raises strong critiques regarding incumbent capture and information problems, Side A effectively turns them by pointing out that a blunt national increase also harms fragile firms and small competitors, often without giving them a voice or sector-specific exemptions. Side A's framing of the trade-off and its direct responses to the pressure questions were slightly more decisive in resolving the central clash, despite minor execution costs from clipped turns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it answers the pressure questions by explaining how wage pressure is reallocated rather than just reduced, and turns the incumbent protection argument by noting that a high uniform floor also squeezes weak firms without giving them sector-specific variation or voice.
