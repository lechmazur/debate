- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): Side A (CON) ran a coherent, disciplined case: solidarity is better funded through open, explicit redistribution (taxes, subsidies, risk adjustment, guaranteed issue) than by banning actuarially fair pricing, and distinguishing cost information used for transfers from individual surcharges neutralized PRO's central "hidden tax" frame. A answered both pressure questions directly with A1/A2 structure and dismantled PRO's empirical examples as confounded. Side B's core solidarity/adverse-selection story was plausible, but its evidentiary discipline collapsed: the "30% Part D" claim and especially the "2014 silver-only community-rating experiment" with a 12% diabetic enrollment decline and 9% premium rise were brittle, ungrounded precision—and incoherent, since B cited evidence that community rating reduced coverage, which undermines B's own proposition. B also never resolved A's point that community rating without mandatory participation still invites adverse selection. A's pressure questions were sharper and B's A1 response dodged the causal question. A made the debate decisively easier to judge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.4): it directly answered B's pressure questions with the required format, then flipped B's own concession about imperfect risk adjustment against the prohibition case, and exposed that B's "silver-only" example actually described community rating harming enrollment—a self-defeating piece of evidence that broke PRO's empirical route to victory.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): Side A won by showing the proposition demands a legal ban on risk-based premiums that is unnecessary for solidarity. A kept a live alternative—guaranteed issue, compulsory participation, subsidies or reinsurance, and risk adjustment—so expected-cost data can fund open transfers without surcharging sick people. B never showed why that package fails, conflated actuarial information with individual surcharges, and inverted adverse selection by claiming community rating alone removes the incentive to exit. B’s precise Part D and “silver-only” figures were unsupported closed-book precision and did not isolate pricing rules from benefit design and subsidies. A answered both pressure questions directly; B restated its causal story instead of neutralizing the confound.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered imperfect adjustment with guaranteed access and public financing, separated claims data used for transfers from an individual surcharge, and showed B’s silver-plan numbers could not prove a ban caused loss of essential coverage.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): B won by distinguishing using risk data for public transfers and subsidies from surcharging sick individuals, showing solidarity can be financed openly without banning actuarial pricing. ### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won because CON (A) progressively conceded the motion. A's opening flatly opposed legal limits on actuarial pricing, but by Rebuttal 2 A was defending a "narrowly tailored ceiling" that caps differentiation for immutable high-cost risks and guarantees essential coverage—a legal limit on actuarial fairness, which is exactly what the proposition demands. B's pressure question Q1 forced this contradiction into the open, and A's answers (A1/A2) entrenched the concession rather than repairing it. B's closing then crystallized cleanly: the remaining dispute is calibration, not principle, and B had consistently defended regulated risk bands (not equal premiums), making A's "blanket cap" attack a strawman. B also dismantled the incentive argument by distinguishing immutable conditions from changeable behavior, and correctly flipped the Medicare Advantage example. A's rebuttals leaned on vague "empirical observations" and the implausible claim that charging sick people more encourages treatment adherence. A's speeches were competent in isolation but self-undermining across the exchange; B adapted, narrowed, and resolved the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure Q1 exposing that A had conceded a legal cap "protects" borderline cases, followed by B's closing [B5.1]-[B5.2] showing CON had adopted PRO's own regulated-band structure, leaving only scope—not principle—in dispute.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B consistently defended legally bounded actuarial pricing for essential coverage, distinguishing unchangeable risk from behavior and showing subsidies alone do not secure access. Side A opened against any curtailment but then endorsed a legal ceiling for immutable high-cost risks, mischaracterized B as demanding flat premiums, and asked pressure questions aimed at its own claims. B exposed that concession and left the live dispute as calibration, not the motion's principle.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure questions and closing, which pinned A's [A2.4] and [A4.3] endorsement of a legal cap and showed A had abandoned opposition to the motion.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A consistently defended legally bounded, actuarially informed pricing for essential coverage. Side B's opening opposed caps, but from B2.4 onward it conceded that legal caps protect borderline and chronically ill patients and closed by advocating a calibrated cap.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A's pressure questions and closing exploiting B2.4: B admitted a legal cap ensures universal access, then defended that cap in B4 and B5, collapsing the CON case into a dispute over calibration rather than principle.
