### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) ran the cleaner, more disciplined case. Its core route — that some socially valuable comparisons (e.g., showing an expensive upgrade offers no advantage) will find no corporate sponsor, and that a pooled reservation lets a provider sell machine time without controlling the science — was never truly neutralized. Side A's counterexamples (Switch Transformer, OPT) showed firms publish their own methods, not independent adversarial tests, and A's Switch Transformer description was inaccurate ("efficient attention" mischaracterizes sparse mixture-of-experts work). A's clipped opening cost it ground, its "single gatekeeper for all frontier compute" claim was exposed as inflated by B's Q2 and quietly narrowed in A4, and A introduced late new evidence (ITER/ISS) in Rebuttal 2, widening rather than resolving. B answered pressure questions directly in required A1/A2 format with the sharpest lines of the debate ("a credit is a budget, not a reservation"; "losing a corporate sponsor should mean losing that sponsor's money, not losing the chance to find out whether the sponsor is right"). A's speed/bureaucracy argument survived but B blunted it by noting a panel's refusal binds no firm while a firm's refusal currently binds researchers. B wins on argument, grounding, and clash resolution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure Q2 and follow-up in Rebuttal 2: by showing the panel only governs the public reservation while firms remain free to pursue what it rejects, B dissolved A's "single, easily captured gatekeeper" argument and forced A into a visibly weaker, narrowed version of its own claim.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by keeping the decisive comparison on independent access to experiments that might otherwise never run, rather than raw corporate cluster speed. Its operation/allocation distinction, credit-versus-reservation answer, and nonexclusive public panel survived B’s main pressure. B showed that firms sometimes publish useful research, but not that independent, commercially unrewarding comparisons are reliably funded; its closing repeated a false-dilemma framing A had already rejected. B’s clipped opening and repetitive later speeches further weakened execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s pressure questions exposed the weakness in B’s operational-bottleneck and single-gatekeeper claims: engineers could still manage reserved capacity, and a public panel’s refusal would not bind firms. B’s response narrowed the gatekeeper to the public block and shifted back to review speed without answering A’s central counterfactual of research that receives no corporate sponsorship at all.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A sustained its central distinction between selling compute and selecting research: firms may efficiently operate clusters while still underfunding independent, publishable comparisons whose results benefit rivals. Side B's speed and capture concerns remained relevant, but it mainly repeated them and never showed that firm-chosen publications substitute for researcher-initiated access. A's closing weighing of slow access versus no access was cleaner and better supported.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's pressure questions and Rebuttal 2 decisively separated machine operation from project allocation, showed why a provider might sell time without sponsoring the result, and exposed that neither a rejecting panel nor a declining firm would remain a sole gatekeeper.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by converting B's structural "existence problem" into a comparison B never resolved. A's core move — judge what the same public dollar buys, and note that a reserved-block contract gives a provider a business case to install specialized hardware even for unprofitable science — directly answered B's pressure questions and closing claim that "no grant can purchase a machine that does not exist." A's rejoinder that if the team is the only customer, the grant covers the installation "so would a public center" neutralized B's strongest route. A also exposed the gap between "enough worthy applicants who need compute of some kind" and demonstrated demand for a specific configuration, and B's answer (workloads "converge around predictable configurations") was an assertion, not evidence. B leaned heavily on EuroHPC/CERN repeats and restated the shareholder-values frame rather than engaging A's pilot-results-versus-prepurchase timing argument. Both sides were grounded without fake sourcing; A's economic weighing was more disciplined and adaptive across rounds, while B's later speeches became repetitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Turn 8: A's A1/A2 answers dismantled B's pressure questions — the grant subsidizes the researcher, not the rate, and a bid specifying memory/interconnect/service gives providers reason to build specialized systems; if only one team needs it, the grant covering the full installation costs "so would a public center." This exchange collapsed B's existence-problem argument into a funding comparison B never won back.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by directly answering A’s strongest supply argument: funded bids can motivate custom installations, while public ownership does not erase scarcity or single-user costs. B also exposed the unresolved distinction between general demand for compute and demand for one shared configuration. A presented a plausible access-and-independence case but increasingly relied on absolute market claims, repetition, and treating EuroHPC as broader proof than it established.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal decisively answered both pressure questions: grants subsidize researchers rather than requiring providers to cut prices, and contracts can specify custom hardware. B then showed that public provision bears the same installation cost and that open proposals do not prove researchers would choose the shared machine over alternatives.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by forcing a same-dollar comparison: grants can pay full commercial prices and contract for publication rights, while public ownership risks buying the wrong configuration and paying for idle core capacity. A's existence argument—that grants cannot buy unavailable specialized machines—was answered by paid bids to install them, with the cost of a single-customer machine falling on the public either way.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: providers need not discount or endorse research if a grant pays for a specified reserved block, and open proposals show what researchers would accept free, not what they would choose over alternatives once pilot results and real costs are known.
