### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Razor-thin but real edge to Side A (CON). Both sides executed the format cleanly, answered pressure questions with direct A1/A2 labels, and crystallized well. B's skirt and chapel questions were the best traps of the debate and forced real concessions, but A answered both with a principled distinction (exposure to teaching vs. compelled profession; "she may be made to listen, not testify") rather than evasion. A's decisive live route — that "genuinely voluntary" does no work after joining, since the dissenting sixteen-year-old's "no" is settled by transfer logistics, a "scheduling test" — was never fully neutralized; B's reply that all voluntary institutions have stable terms was plausible but did not explain why a signature at seven should bind a dissenting minor. A also correctly noted that all three of B's strike-downs involved monopoly or timing, not a failure of voluntariness itself. B's closing turn ("punishes an institution for saying why") was powerful but slightly misdescribed A's skirt answer by ignoring A's stated additional variables (gendered application, moral standing). A's line — "consent she cannot withdraw was never consent" and "a community that needs her held was never voluntary" — made the choice marginally easier for a third-party reader.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Pressure Questions exchange: B's Q1 (identical knee-length skirt) and Q2 (chapel attendance) cornered A into concessions, but A's Rebuttal 2 converted them into the "listening vs. testifying with her body" distinction, while B's Rebuttal 2 honestly conceded that withdrawal of consent does not void the rule — handing A the "scheduling test" attack that framed the rest of the debate.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): B better defended the motion's limited permission: shared dress conditions can survive where participation and exit are genuinely available. A sharply challenged parental consent and the conversion of religious disapproval into dismissal, but did not defeat the remaining consenting-adult cases. Permission to dress religiously individually does not preserve a collectively chosen environment. - **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing that PRO's "genuinely voluntary" does no independent work: PRO admitted a student's withdrawal of consent does not void the rule, leaving only transfer logistics, parental consent transferred from age seven to sixteen, and strike-downs based on monopoly or timing. ### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a close, high-quality debate by dismantling the motion's limiting clause from both directions. When B clarified that "alternatives" include any reachable public school, A pounced: the "enforcement mechanism" then does almost nothing in the school half of the motion, and where it does bite (island, valley, parish schools), it revokes the community's dress norm without delivering the stranded teenager her credits or her mother's job — "collective forfeiture for an individual injury." B's "price, not sanction" reframe was elegant, but A's rejoinder that the island school cannot build a ferry — remoteness is not a choice — left the price effectively unpayable for exactly the scattered minorities B invoked in its opening. B's best pressure (name the rule that protects the sixteen-year-old) was answered plausibly with accreditation and anti-retaliation law, and A flipped B's "legislative hope" charge by noting PRO's condition is equally unenacted, leaving only the question of which rule aims at the injury — which A won. B's ministerial-exception point in closing was its strongest late hit but arrived too late to be developed. Both sides followed format, answered pressure directly, and used plausible background knowledge without fake sourcing; A simply won the decisive weighing exchanges.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal (A4): answering B's Q1 pressure with general-law protections, then landing the remedy mismatch — "PRO's remedy lands on the hijab, not the harm" — and exposing that B's own chaplain scenario forces a judgment on inner conviction, which B's closing "we count his options" only partially repaired.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): B narrowly won by defending meaningful consent as the boundary on enforceable dress expectations. It corrected A’s same-faith-alternative premise and directly addressed the trapped chaplain. A’s direct-remedy alternative and rebuttal to the “legislative hope” comparison were strong, but credit portability and retaliation protection did not resolve geographic lack of alternatives or protect trapped religious-role employees. A demonstrated costs to B’s condition without sufficiently explaining why mission-linked expectations should remain enforceable when participation is not genuinely voluntary. Both sides overstated uncertain legal baselines.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to the chaplain dilemma rejected A’s proposed binary: permission could depend on the individual’s actual options rather than simply institutional location or job classification. Its explicit concession that a genuinely trapped chaplain also receives protection exposed a remaining gap in A’s role-based alternative.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by showing PRO's condition is either nearly empty when any public school counts, or crude and misdirected where it bites. Revoking a school's dress norm does not restore a student's credits or a parent's job, while role-based expectations plus direct credit-portability and anti-retaliation rules target the actual harm. B's price/incentive answer works for institution-controlled penalties, but A decisively notes an island or valley community cannot create transport, another school, or a thicker labour market, making the condition a burden on the isolated minorities B claimed to protect.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was most decisive: accreditation and anti-retaliation may be imperfect, but PRO's sanction still leaves the stranded teenager without credits or her mother without work, imposing collective forfeiture on freely choosing families instead of repairing exit directly.
