### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): This was a close debate. Side B (CON) wins narrowly on the central causal question: whether splitting firms actually produces field-level genetic diversity and error correction. B pressed consistently that ownership fragmentation leaves the real drivers of convergence intact: the same gene pool, agronomy, buyers and procurement systems. Fragments can license the same elite lines and so make "many identical mistakes." A's answer, "portfolio competition" forcing differentiation, is plausible. But it stayed a market forecast that B contested directly, and A never showed why differentiation would overcome shared agronomic pressures. A's strongest line was "fix the chokepoint once vs. supervise it forever." It is memorable, and it scored on the political-economy problem of relying on mandates. B blunted it with a symmetry point: if incumbents can defeat mandates, they can also litigate, delay and lobby a breakup, so the same enforcement capacity is needed either way. A's closing restated the frame without engaging that symmetry. A's closing also claimed B "never supplied a mechanism" for error correction. That misdescribes the exchange, because B's A1 named trials, farmer rejection, surveillance, liability and regulator withdrawal. A also carries an execution cost for the clipped opening. B's transition-risk point was sharpened by its description of partitioning living germplasm and registrations, and it held up against A's "proves too much" reply. A had better rhetoric and a sharper pressure-question round. B had the cleaner causal case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answer to A's Q2 was the most decisive exchange. A asked why entrenched firms would ever permit mandates. B turned the political-feasibility argument around: any power that can defeat mandates can also defeat or delay a breakup, so enforcement capacity is needed either way. This neutralized much of the force behind A's closing frame.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B won by severing the causal link between corporate headcount and field-level genetic diversity while establishing unrefuted transition and capacity costs. B showed that shared germplasm, farmer yield incentives, and uniform buyer specifications drive monoculture regardless of how many logos sit above breeding programs, and that breaking up integrated firms splinters transboundary pest surveillance, regulatory dossiers, and stewardship into licensing thickets during active climate and disease stress. Side A mounted a sharp political-economy push—framing breakup as a one-time fix versus perpetual regulation of powerful incumbents—but B persuasively countered that dominant firms can equally stall a breakup through litigation and lobbying while fragmented private survivors still require ongoing public breeding and open-access rules anyway. Minor execution slips by A, including a slightly clipped opening and claiming in closing that B offered no error-correction mechanism despite B4.1, reinforced B's clear edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B2.1], Side B dismantled Side A's attempt in [A2.1] to blame the 1970 corn blight on supplier concentration, explaining that Texas male-sterile cytoplasm spread across firms because of agronomic attractiveness, common extension advice, and uniform procurement channels—decisively showing why ownership fragmentation does not prevent common-mode biological convergence.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): A made the decisive comparison cleaner: dividing ownership does not necessarily divide germplasm, traits, commercial incentives, or common-mode failures, while it can fragment response capacity. B’s chokepoint-versus-permanent-regulation frame and portfolio-competition mechanism were forceful, but A directly answered both pressure questions, showing that political feasibility also burdens breakup and identifying error-correction channels independent of ownership. B’s closing claim that A never supplied such a mechanism was therefore inaccurate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1–A4.4 was decisive: A answered error correction and political feasibility directly, then showed that post-breakup breeders could still license identical elite lines and face identical pressures, substantially weakening B’s central differentiation mechanism.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): PRO built a coherent causal story: integrated ownership of genetics, chemistry, and distribution makes correlated failure rational, and its "tollbooth" image was memorable. CON won the remedy-fit clash, which became decisive. It conceded concentration but pressed consistently that forced breakup leaves patents, IP rules, and commodity-driven uniformity untouched. Its scale argument and its "who buys the divested lines" pressure pushed PRO in Rebuttal 2 into conceding that post-breakup firms would mostly do regional conventional breeding and license traits where scale matters. CON then turned that answer back on PRO as "the world we already have, minus a decade of litigation." CON also landed a symmetry point: PRO's plan already depends on regulators pre-qualifying buyers and policing re-concentration, so the same regulators can enforce licensing mandates. PRO's closing restated the tollbooth framing and its speed claim but never squarely answered that patents survive a breakup. PRO's reply on wheat and rice, that they still pass through concentrated multiplication and input bundles, was plausible but asserted rather than developed. CON's malformed reference "[ P2.5]" was a minor execution flaw. Overall, CON made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4.3–B4.5) seized PRO's admission that "cross-licensing among competing owners is access" and its retreat to regional breeding plus trait licensing. CON reframed both as proof that an access mandate delivers PRO's goal without breakup. It added that PRO's own buyer-vetting scheme requires the same regulatory capacity CON's conduct remedies need. PRO never effectively answered this.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling the causal link between corporate breakup and on-farm genetic diversity while winning the comparative remedy clash. Side A presented a coherent, vivid case that vertical seed-chemical integration incentivizes uniform crop-input packages and correlated failure, but Side B showed that patents and scale bottlenecks survive a breakup intact, won the empirical exchange on wheat and rice, and turned Side A's second-rebuttal concessions to show that direct licensing mandates and public breeding investment address the bottleneck faster without a decade of restructuring.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In Turn 8 ([B4.3]-[B4.5]), Side B turned Side A's answers in [A4.1] and [A4.3] against the case for breakup: showing that Side A's post-breakup vision of regional conventional breeders licensing costly biotech traits largely mirrors the status quo with the same patents intact, and that if regulators have the capacity to slice up global firms and police divestiture buyers indefinitely, they can enforce FRAND trait-licensing and germplasm-access mandates far more directly.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): A built the cleaner path to victory: corporate fragmentation would leave patents, commodity-driven uniformity, public underinvestment, and costly trait development intact. B gave a plausible integrated-chokepoint account and answered both pressure questions directly, but never fully established that breakup would produce greater farm-level diversity rather than recreate today’s regional-breeding and trait-licensing system. A’s remedy comparison and regulator-capacity reversal were especially damaging, though B remained competitive on distribution control and correlated systemic risk.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal turned B’s own answers against the motion: if post-breakup firms would breed regionally and cross-license traits, then mandated access could deliver the benefit without restructuring, while B’s proposed purchaser supervision undermined the claim that conduct regulation was uniquely demanding.
