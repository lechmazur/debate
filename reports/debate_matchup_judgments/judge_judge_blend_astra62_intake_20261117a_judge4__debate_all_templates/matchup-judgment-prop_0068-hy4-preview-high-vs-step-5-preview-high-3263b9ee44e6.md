### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a competitive debate. CON's strongest weapon was a consistent overbreadth dilemma: a message-level label becomes "wallpaper," while a campaign-level record becomes an "unread archive." CON pressed it well across rounds. PRO neutralized much of that fork in two ways. First, it drew an "object of optimization" line: model-chosen substantive wording and appeals are covered, while placement-only targeting is not. Second, PRO openly accepted the consequence that CON treated as fatal. When CON said dynamic creative optimization rewrites copy, PRO answered that such systems are exactly what the rule targets. PRO then argued that widespread labels would inform the public rather than become noise. CON never squarely rebutted that frequency argument. It kept asserting that common labels warn no one without explaining why the public learning how much persuasion is machine-shaped has no value. CON's "prior restraint" and "speech tax" framing was overstated. PRO's point that disclosure is not suppression largely stood. On the pressure questions: - **PRO's answers** directly addressed the line-drawing and agency questions. The surgery analogy for "agency without a full data dump" was a reasonable illustration. - **CON's A2** partly dodged. PRO had asked where the notice should appear; CON flipped the question back onto PRO instead of answering it. CON's consent rebuttal was a fair hit: the label does not reveal the optimization objective. However, PRO's interpretive-frame answer, that the label lets readers treat the message as a tuned stimulus, kept that route alive. Overall, PRO left the cleaner live path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B4.1–B4.2 delivered the decisive answer to CON's line-drawing challenge. It distinguished placement optimization from model-selected substantive wording and accepted that rewritten headlines fall under the rule. Combined with B2.5's reply that common labels are informative rather than meaningless, this blunted CON's overbreadth-or-noise fork. CON's later replies largely restated that fork without engaging the bullet PRO had bitten.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): B wins narrowly through its surviving transparency case: notice reveals model-selected rhetoric and can enable critical distance. A effectively showed that ordinary dynamic creative optimization fits B’s definition, weakening its narrow-scope assurances. But covering ordinary campaigns is not itself a defect under this motion. A insufficiently established that widespread labels necessarily become useless or that platform-provided factual disclosure constitutes prior restraint. B’s plausible informational benefit and administrative mechanism outweigh A’s overstated consequences.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B distinguished knowing one was demographically targeted from knowing the wording itself was optimized, directly answering A’s agency challenge. A subsequently demanded objectives, data, and withheld alternatives without establishing why the narrower disclosure lacked value.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): CON wins because its overbreadth and placement clash stayed live. PRO offered a useful “object of optimization” line, but CON credibly argued that ordinary dynamic creative and retargeting already optimize wording, images, and emotional framing for conversion, making the boundary hard to police. CON also preserved the dilemma that a user-visible label either spreads across routine optimized messages or becomes the kind of buried archive PRO criticizes. PRO’s consent and transparency case was clear and rhetorically strong, but it never fully solved the workability burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal turned PRO’s campaign-record answer into a concrete archive-versus-wallpaper dilemma and directly attacked the new object-of-optimization line by showing that routine ad tools already optimize message substance for behavior.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): This was a close debate, and CON wins by a narrow edge. CON opened with a sharp scope attack: under PRO's own trigger, the personalized feed is already AI-optimized persuasion. That forced PRO to narrow the motion to "campaigns" with an identified sender, a defined segment, and a persuasive objective. CON then exploited the narrowing. A label that covers a county health reminder and a scam alike is either inert, because sender identity already does the work, or stigmatizing. PRO's best pressure question, "meaningless or meaningful?", was clever. CON answered it adequately: the label is empty in content but loaded in connotation. PRO's closing still leaned on the contradiction as though it had been left unanswered. PRO's operational line was a reasonable answer to CON's Q1. However, its answer on record retention partly smuggled in a recordkeeping duty the motion does not state, and CON flagged this. PRO's "evidentiary floor" point remained live and was its strongest route. Its weakness was that CON's alternative of audit access plus limits on harmful targeting is not obviously dependent on user-facing badges. CON's own alternative was underdeveloped, and its vendor-evasion point was somewhat speculative. Even so, CON adapted more across rounds and kept PRO reacting to its framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's A2.3 against PRO's B2.3. PRO admitted that a tailored public-health message deserves the same badge as a manipulative one. CON turned that admission into the "inert or stigma" dilemma: the badge adds nothing beyond the sender's identity, and if it does change behavior, it does so by implying an accusation PRO disclaims. PRO never fully escaped that dilemma.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): B better defended the proposition by distinguishing coordinated persuasion campaigns from ordinary feed ranking and explaining why targeting information adds something beyond sender identity. A never established its central claim that disclosure must be either useless or stigmatizing; covering legitimate campaigns does not itself make a factual label misleading. A did substantially weaken B’s audit rationale: labeling does not automatically require retaining variant libraries or objective functions. That limits B’s victory but does not defeat its independent autonomy argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s distinction between disclosing a technique and condemning a message undercut A’s repeated comparison between health reminders and scams. A’s strongest counter was that a badge is not an audit trail, exposing B’s overstatement that public labels are necessary and sufficient foundations for scrutiny.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): A wins narrowly because it gave a clearer operational line between ambient feed ranking and coordinated persuasion campaigns, answered the audit pressure by tying labels to implied recordkeeping, and exposed the tension in B calling the badge both empty and stigmatizing. B’s overbreadth, evasion, and variant-library critiques were strong, but they did not fully neutralize PRO’s visibility and audit-floor case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 answer to B’s retention pressure: it treated the label as implying auditable records and made log deletion evidence of concealment, preserving PRO’s audit rationale.
