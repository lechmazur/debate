### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): A won because it built the cleaner path from B's own concession that beginning farmers cannot outbid funds bidding above agricultural returns. That admission strengthened A's core story: institutional capital locks inflated prices into farmland, and post-auction fixes arrive too late. B's alternatives—purchase assistance, lease rules, disclosure, and speculative taxes—were plausible but never fully answered the structural price-distortion point. B also faced pressure on its seeming contradiction between demanding speculative/working distinctions for tax policy while denying governments could define non-farming ownership. A exploited that tension effectively by arguing the same legal line would enable a cap. B's underinclusion argument about wealthy individuals and corporate farms mattered, but A plausibly framed the cap as complementing other regulations rather than replacing them. The result was a clear but competitive A win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 and closing crystallized the decisive exchange: once B admitted farmers cannot outbid institutional funds, A turned that into proof that credit subsidies or post-purchase taxes cannot undo an inflated acquisition price. A also exposed the tension between B's proposed speculative-tax regime and B's claim that governments cannot define non-farming ownership.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won by keeping the vote on the motion's ownership-form trigger and breaking Side A's central inference. A plausibly showed that institutional liquidity can bid farmland above farm returns and lock in rent pressure, and it landed real hits on tax pass-through and definitional tension. B answered that pressure directly, conceded the outbidding fact, and showed it does not entail this cap: other non-farming buyers can still take the parcel, conduct rules can target rent, conversion, and land banking, and a numeric institutional cap both over-includes useful capital and under-includes equivalent concentration. A's closing treated that concession as if every alternative had collapsed; a reader can see it had not.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's reply to the outbidding concession: barring a fund neither creates an affordable farm nor stops a wealthy individual or corporate farm from buying, so the admitted bidding problem is not itself a remedy.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side A won by turning Side B’s strongest point into a limited problem rather than a remedy. B made a vivid case that institutional capital can inflate farmland prices and extract rents, and its enforcement-contradiction pressure was sharp. But A conceded the outbidding point while arguing that a cap is a blunt ownership trigger that may simply substitute wealthy individuals or corporate buyers, block patient capital, and fail to create affordable access. A also gave a cleaner comparative framework: regulate conduct, speculation, lease terms, and conversion directly. B’s closing overclaimed from the concession and did not sufficiently answer substitution or over-inclusion, leaving A with the more decisive path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s response to B’s outbidding pressure: conceding that farmers may not outbid funds, but arguing that excluding one institutional buyer does not itself lower the price or create an affordable parcel, keeping the cap’s bluntness live.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won because the more damaging pressure landed and was not fully resolved. A’s position conceded that a cap would not broadly make farmland affordable, that leasing remains crucial for young farmers, and that vertically integrated operating agribusinesses fall outside the cap as a separate policy problem. B turned these into a coherent backfire: the cap removes patient leaseback landlords and depresses farm equity while leaving room for corporate mega-farms that more directly displace local operators. B also argued persuasively that direct tenancy, environmental, and anti-concentration rules could address the harms without the collateral damage to rural wealth. A’s closing “cap is not a ban” and “separate policy problem” answers softened but did not eliminate the contradiction. A retained a real path on ownership control, but B gave the cleaner comparative reason why the policy as written could worsen resilience and rural communities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive exchange was turning A’s concession that corporate farms require direct rules into the conclusion that the ownership cap becomes unnecessary, while pairing that with the land-equity/collateral pressure from A’s own admission that leasing remains crucial.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won by making the motion's marginal effect the decisive clash. Where a non-farming ownership cap actually binds, it blocks the buyers most likely to lease to local operators and leaves operating agribusinesses and consolidating farm buyers exempt, while also imposing the asset-price and collateral costs A ultimately conceded. A's control-and-resilience case stayed live, but A never showed why a share cap protects communities better than direct tenancy and practice rules, and its answers to price and exemption pressure were partial concessions rather than neutralization. B's substitution story therefore gave the cleaner reason to reject the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal was decisive: it used A's concessions that leasing remains crucial and that operating companies are a separate problem the cap does not stop, then showed the cap shrinks the passive-landlord pool and relies on the same direct rules that would address harms without the distortion.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): A wins narrowly by turning B’s concessions into a coherent backfire story: where the cap binds, it reduces passive leasing and farm equity while leaving vertically integrated operating firms exempt. B’s control-versus-regulation case was principled and epistemically careful, but it did not fully neutralize the binding-cap dilemma or the leasing and wealth costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal and closing used B’s admission that operating farm companies are a separate problem to argue that the cap substitutes passive landlords with more displacement, while also pressing the asset-price and leasing tradeoff.
