### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): B wins a close, substantive debate. A's hours-per-dollar turn and its point that cost savings alone do not automatically eliminate jobs were sharp and forced B's case onto harder comparative ground. But B's central distinction carried the net-loss clash: AI does not merely automate arithmetic as spreadsheets did; it automates the thinking-shaped work itself, so lower-cost demand often flows to the model rather than to new human labor. B also isolated large fixed-volume white-collar categories such as claims, compliance, payroll, and bookkeeping, where substitution produces contraction without comparable new demand. A's gains remained possible rather than probable, while B successfully treated the early-policy half as effectively conceded. The [C#] reference mislabels were minor execution costs, not enough to overturn the clearer causal story.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal of the spreadsheet analogy was the decisive exchange: spreadsheets automated arithmetic and left thinking to people, while AI automates drafting, summarizing, and coding, so A's demand-expansion path no longer reliably flows into human office jobs.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A wins a high-level, competitive debate by establishing a stronger structural case for likely net white-collar job loss while securing practical agreement on early policy preparation. Side B landed sharp microeconomic pushback—showing that partial engagement compression lowers break-even demand thresholds, organizational bargaining and live exception-handling resist automation, and expanding advisory or engineering work does not require bounded payroll volumes to grow—and showed slightly cleaner epistemic discipline (Side A also committed three minor citation-label slips using C instead of B). Still, Side A's macro-arithmetic carried the day: massive volume-bounded office categories (claims, tax, compliance, bookkeeping, support) and junior pipelines face direct consolidation, while the destination white-collar sectors Side B relied on for offsetting job growth are simultaneously compressed by the same AI tools and leak respent savings into compute, leaving induced demand struggling just to preserve existing advisory headcount rather than absorb large back-office losses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side B argued in [B2.1] and [B3.1-B3.2] that cutting one day of a five-day engagement lowers the break-even client threshold and that clerk payroll savings can fund new engineering hires, Side A countered in [A3.1] and [A4.1-A4.6] that AI shrinks the remaining memo and coding work inside destination firms too, major clerical categories are volume-bounded rather than price-elastic, and respending leaks into compute—pushing Side B to admit in [B4.1] that compressed engagements need two-thirds more volume merely to keep existing staff busy, without promising practices even clear that bar.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by repeatedly forcing a full net comparison. Its partial-task, vendor-pooling, repeat-engagement, and lower-price-unlocks-existing-budget arguments show that faster tasks do not automatically become fewer jobs. Side B powerfully establishes bounded demand and displacement, but never shows those losses outweigh hiring in expanding implementation and advisory work. A also preserves the policy distinction between insuring displaced workers and accepting likely net loss.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing symmetry rebuttal was decisive: B compared firms after a staffing cut with firms before a sale, whereas an hours saving is automatic on neither side of the jobs ledger.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built the stronger path to net white-collar loss. Its core asymmetry argument survived: existing back-office work can shed labor without new customer demand, while offsets require enough new clients to absorb the hours saved. Most decisively, B distinguished current AI from past automation waves: spreadsheets and ATMs removed a calculation/transaction bottleneck but left cognitive and communicative follow-up work to humans, whereas generative AI can take a first pass at that work too. CON's historical record and latent-demand case were strong, but B's tenant/lawyer dilemma directly blunted the claim that newly affordable services will restore professional hiring. CON pressed a real burden objection, but B sharpened the likelihood claim rather than merely asserting possibility. The early-tracking policy was also tied to the predicted quiet hiring decline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal of the spreadsheet and ATM analogies was the most decisive: earlier tools did not write the executive briefing or discuss a customer's borrowing options, but AI can take a first pass at both, undercutting CON's strongest historical route to demand-driven rehiring.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): In an exceptionally sharp debate from both sides, Side A earned a clear win by building a stronger structural case for net displacement and dominating the policy half of the motion. Side B mounted a formidable historical base-rate defense—arguing that spreadsheets, ATMs, and coding tools also cut task hours first before latent demand grew professional employment—and smartly pivoted to priced-out legal and tax markets where fees mostly reflect labor hours. However, Side A successfully explained why this wave breaks that pattern: across most industries, office work is overhead attached to products whose prices are dominated by non-administrative costs, so efficiency cuts headcount without inducing proportional product demand; meanwhile, AI compresses the very drafting, briefing, and advisory layer that absorbed displaced clerical workers in earlier waves, allowing even new firms to scale with fewer hires. Side A also exposed an internal tension between Side B's claim of heavy liability scrutiny and collapsing consumer fees, while forcing Side B to abandon its opening defense of ordinary unemployment insurance once Side A showed that junior hiring freezes close career entryways without triggering layoff claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: When Side B argued in [B1.2] that cheaper processing lowers insurance prices and expands underwriting jobs, Side A showed in [A2.1]-[A2.2] that loss payouts dominate premiums so halving paperwork cannot halve prices, forcing Side B to concede insurance [B2.3] and pivot to legal and tax services [B2.4]—where Side A's Q1 [A3.1] and follow-ups [A4.5, A5.1] pinned Side B between claiming AI requires costly liability review [B2.6] and claiming fees fall enough for extra lawyers—not just a fuller waiting room—to outweigh back-office cuts across the broader economy.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO built the cleaner likelihood mechanism: savings apply across fixed, derived-demand back-office work, while offsets require enough new customers each needing fewer hours, and one accountable reviewer can replace several producers. CON's historical analogies and unserved-market volume were strong, but PRO specifically differentiated AI as automating the complementary analysis and conversation work that previously absorbed labor. CON showed the human role survives, not that headcount does, and never fully answered that leverage point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: spreadsheets and ATMs removed bottlenecks without drafting briefings or customer advice, whereas AI does, so greater output need not restore prior hiring.
