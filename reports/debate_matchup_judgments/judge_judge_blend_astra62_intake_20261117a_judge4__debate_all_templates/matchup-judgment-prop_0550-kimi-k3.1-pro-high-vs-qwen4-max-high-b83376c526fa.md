### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): PRO wins narrowly. It built the clearer causal story early: expansion reaches the marginal enrollee, that student has the weakest odds of finishing, and the premiums CON cites (management, licensure, later mobility) mostly belong to people who complete. PRO kept that frame through every round. Its pressure questions were well chosen. Q1 exposed that CON accepts selection when targeting college subsidies but objects to employer selection in apprenticeships. Q2 forced CON to admit there is no general completion threshold. PRO also handled CON's symmetric-risk demand well in A4.4, describing how a failed apprentice and a failed college entrant actually differ. CON was disciplined and often sharp. It insisted that both sides' risks be weighted, attacked the scaling claims, and showed that "converting existing jobs" does not create additional youth served. Its closing point that training wages are not net gains, because the apprentice might otherwise have worked, was the best single analytical hit. However, CON mostly argued that PRO had not proven its case. It never gave a positive reason why subsidy-induced marginal enrollees would out-earn apprentices by age fifty, beyond asserting that degree compounding exists. PRO overstated B4.3 as a "concession," and some of its examples (career escalators, German and Swiss transferability) are optimistic. Still, PRO left the cleaner reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The pressure question on the completion threshold, followed by CON's answer in B4.3. PRO forced CON to admit that whether college wins depends on a probability-weighted threshold it could not locate for induced enrollees. CON's reply, that college should not expand below the line, was principled. But it let PRO argue that expansion by design pulls in students near or below that line. CON's best counter came only in the closing: the point about counterfactual wages, which landed but too late to fully reshape the weighing.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): This was a close, well-matched debate. Side A (CON) was analytically disciplined throughout: the scale/comparability critique was consistent, the "relabeling existing jobs is not additional youth served" counter [A4.5] was sharp, and the closing's opportunity-cost point [A5.1] — that training wages compensate labor and are not net earnings gains — was the single most technically damaging blow, puncturing B's "earned vs. wagered" metaphor. However, Side B (PRO) did more to advance and resolve the clash. B's completion asymmetry (marginal induced enrollees least likely to finish) was never affirmatively rebutted, only blunted by "targeting," which B effectively turned via the selectivity-symmetry question [B3.1]. B answered pressure directly and creatively (converting existing badly-supervised jobs into structured training; broadening beyond trades to medical-assistant and help-desk ladders), and the B4.4 comparison — the marginal graduate as a general-business major in a saturated field — went unanswered. B's pressure questions exposed A's refusal to weigh completion probability, and B leveraged that silence into the cleanest voting issue in the closing. A's strongest material was largely burden-shifting and meta-comparison rather than an affirmative earnings case, and its best point arrived in the final speech where B could not respond. B's superior clash progression, memorable weighing ("wages banked, not debt owed"; "downside protected, upside preserved"), and honest engagement with pressure give a razor-thin edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The B3.2/A4.3/B5.1 exchange: B demanded the completion threshold at which a marginal enrollee beats an apprentice; A offered only an abstract decision rule; B's closing converted that dodge into the decisive framing that expansion itself recruits students past the unspoken line, while A's late opportunity-cost riposte partially repaired the damage but arrived too late to be tested.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): A won the marginal, lifetime comparison. B established genuine cash-flow and downside advantages, but repeatedly assumed subsidy-induced students were poor completion bets while granting apprentices successful progression and portability. A directly challenged that asymmetry, explained how subsidies can improve persistence, and answered the completion-threshold question without inventing precision. B’s scaling response identified possible training opportunities but did not establish superior lifetime gains. A more convincingly distinguished a safer starting point from the proposition’s greater long-run earnings.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest rebuttal answered B’s claim that every paid training month increases lifetime income: apprentices might otherwise have worked, so wages received are not automatically earnings added by the policy. This crystallized A’s earlier demand to compare net, probability-weighted career gains rather than guaranteed paychecks against uncertain diplomas.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): This was a close, high-quality debate. PRO set the right decision rule: compare outcomes for the marginal, subsidy-induced young person. PRO pressed it consistently, asking whether induced enrollees complete often enough to capture the degree premium. PRO also answered CON's Q2 honestly, declining to invent a placement-quality share, and ran a credible double-standard critique. CON did more to shape the long-run comparison. It recast PRO's advantages as a one-time "level" and the degree's advantage as a recurring "slope." Its deadweight asymmetry point was effective: a transfer to an inframarginal student still yields a degree, while a subsidized slot buys either an existing hire or a weaker trainee. Its "design fixes apply to college too" reply neutralized PRO's conditionality toolkit. The pyramid argument was CON's strongest late move. PRO's own named progression roles are rationed by the org chart. PRO answered by listing more rungs and alternative growth sources, but never showed those rungs widen at scale. CON's own answer on marginal completion ("a slope to a minority can beat a level to everyone") was more asserted than demonstrated, which keeps the margin thin. CON's closing "scarcity thins with expansion" point edged toward a new angle, and PRO never turned that same logic against the degree premium. On balance, CON's rebuttals did more material damage to PRO's route to victory: the claim that apprenticeships deliver ongoing wage growth. PRO's route still relied partly on design aspirations that CON successfully made symmetric.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.3/B4.4 sequence was the most decisive exchange. It turned PRO's A1 list of progression roles (lead technician, supervisor, production manager) into evidence of a rationed pyramid rather than a broad slope. It also argued that PRO's screening, co-investment, and progression conditions could equally be attached to college aid, which erased PRO's design advantage. PRO's closing replies to both points were brief and did not fully restore apprenticeships' long-run growth claim.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a high-quality, closely fought exchange. Both sides framed the debate correctly around marginal returns. B's strongest live route was the missing link — that enrollment subsidies buy attempts, not completions — and its Q1 pressure landed well. But A's A4.1 answer was decisive: a recurring slope captured by only a fraction of completers can still beat a one-time level paid to everyone, and the subsidy itself relieves the money-and-hours causes of stop-out. B's response that "'could' is not a comparison" dodged that arithmetic rather than engaging it. A also won the pyramid exchange: B answered the rationed promotion ladder by naming more rungs, while A showed those rungs depend on scarcity that minting more apprentices thins. A's grant-and-cost move (conceding the credit-constrained apprentice, then showing it implies near-deadweight subsidy) and the symmetrizing point that B's design toolkit (advising, completion funding) works for college too further damaged B's case. B's double-standard charge had real force but was blunted by A applying the marginal test to both sides. Both sides used plausible background knowledge without fake precision; B's honest refusal to invent a placement share was admirable but left its burden unmet. A's closing crystallized the level-vs-slope and scaling arguments more cleanly than B's, which restated the missing link without resolving the fraction arithmetic.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's answer to B's Q1 in Rebuttal 2 ("a slope paid to a minority can still beat a level paid to everyone," plus subsidies relieving money/hours causes of stop-out) directly neutralized B's central completion-rate pressure, and A's follow-up grant of the credit-constrained apprentice — showing it implies near-deadweight firm subsidies — turned B's own concession into a cost.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): B built the stronger marginal comparison: paid learning addresses financial barriers while producing earnings, verified skills, and experience-based advancement that need not depend on supervisory vacancies. A's recurring degree-premium argument and recognition that subsidies improve completion kept this competitive. However, its categorical flat-apprenticeship trajectory and asymmetric treatment of deadweight weakened its case. B answered pressure more directly and acknowledged uncertainty rather than inventing placement outcomes. Neither side established the comparative magnitudes, limiting the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B effectively dismantled A's claim that subsidized apprentices must be weak trainees: upfront costs and poaching can prevent valuable training, while college subsidies also fund activity that would happen anyway. A conceded capable constrained apprentices but never repaired its inconsistent treatment of additionality.
