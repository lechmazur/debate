### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by showing that the proposition's ex-ante, system-wide mandate is overbroad, exposes imperfect metrics to strategic gaming, and creates a heavy continuous reporting regime untethered from injury. Side B mounted a compelling case—especially in Pressure Questions and Closing—that subtle, diffuse algorithmic downgrades in shifts or routes mimic ordinary management and are hard to challenge without prior knowledge that a score exists. However, Side A directly mitigated that concern with decision-specific reason-giving and record preservation while winning the scope, gaming, and regulatory-burden clashes cleanly after Side B committed to disclosing both design thresholds and ongoing disparate-impact and change logs across any tool or spreadsheet that scores workers, leaving Side A's critiques of gaming, small-sample noise, staleness, and privacy largely unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's two-step trap in [A3.1]-[A3.2] and [A4.3]-[A4.5], which pinned Side B into claiming coverage based on downstream use (including alerts and scoring spreadsheets) while requiring "both" design rules and ongoing disparate-impact and material-change logs [B4.1]-[B4.3], proving Side A's charge of an unstable, continuous pre-harm audit—closely followed by [A2.4] turning Side B's "output review" answer on mouse jigglers into support for decision review over system disclosure.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by keeping the motion as written in view and forcing a cleaner comparison: prior system-wide disclosure versus decision-specific reasons, records, and triggered investigation. Side B’s narrowing made the duty more attractive but never fully escaped overbreadth, gaming, and continuous-reporting costs, while Side A showed visible harms can still start accountability without publishing the control map in advance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 and closing distinction—that reason-giving must reveal whether a score caused this lost shift or pay cut, while the motion demands prior system disclosure—most damaged Side B’s claim that enforcement is impossible without the mandate.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): A won a clear but competitive debate by keeping the motion’s system-wide, prior-disclosure burden distinct from narrower decision-specific review. A directly answered both pressure questions with visible-harm triggers, mandatory reason-giving, record preservation, and targeted records demands, then effectively turned B’s answer that regulators need “both” design and outcome disclosures into a cumulative continuous-reporting burden. B’s hidden-harm argument remained forceful and its scope answers were competent, but it did not fully neutralize the gaming, scope instability, and regulator-burden objections, and sometimes overstated why after-the-fact enforcement must be impossible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered B’s enforcement dilemma with the trigger-versus-dossier distinction, challenged B’s “plain scope” defense as unstable, and used B’s “both” answer to sharpen the comparative cost of the mandate.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a high-level debate by prevailing on both the regulatory/worker trigger and the metric-gaming clash. From the opening, Side B smartly distinguished worker notice of monitored inputs and main factors from confidential regulator access to weights and validation, neutralizing trade-secret objections. Side B's Turn 5 pressure questions then forced Side A (CON) in Turn 8 to concede mandatory employer reporting of automated tools to regulators and mandatory disclosure of relied-upon record types whenever pay, shifts, or status change. That pivot adopted virtually the same coverage boundary Side A had attacked as too costly for small firms, while still leaving workers unable to preserve contemporaneous evidence before penalties land or know the standing rules governing their work. Finally, Side B convincingly answered Side A's central gaming dilemma, whereas Side A dropped Side B's Turn 7 response in Rebuttal 2 before inaccurately claiming in closing that its fork went unrebutted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Turn 7 response ([B4.1]-[B4.2]) to Side A's specificity fork ([A2.1], [A3.1]): Side B showed that disclosing evidentiary record categories (such as call transcripts, break timers, or route logs) without thresholds or weights lets workers contest bad inputs without handing over an optimization formula—and that a driver already knows speed matters, so secrecy only stops her from proving the route tool blamed her for a closed road.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins a close debate by defending a modest prior-disclosure duty—monitored categories, main factors, affected decisions, and confidential regulator documentation—without equating it to publishing weights. Side A’s decision-moment reasons, record retention, and on-demand audits were a serious alternative and answered the irreversibility objection well, but they still leave standing scores shaping conduct and evidence before any explanation. Side A’s gaming fork was narrowed by that distinction and then overclaimed as unrebutted, so the cleaner vote is for the mandate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s separation of contest-relevant notice (record categories, date ranges, main factors, contest routes) from weights and thresholds directly damaged Side A’s claim that any useful disclosure is also a gaming manual, and it exposed the closing overclaim that the fork was unrebutted.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): B built the stronger case for prior, functional notice: workers could learn record categories and uses without receiving weights or source code, while confidential regulator documentation supplied the audit trigger. B also answered the scope and gaming pressure directly. A’s contemporaneous reasons, record retention, and regulator-access alternative was a strong response and kept the debate competitive, but it addressed adverse-action correction better than ongoing secret monitoring and effectively conceded substantial disclosure while leaving its timing too late.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 answers were decisive: evidentiary notice was distinguished from an optimization recipe, and a downstream-use test answered the boundary objection. A’s contemporaneous-notice counter was effective for individual appeals but did not fully cover surveillance and behavior-shaping before any penalty.
