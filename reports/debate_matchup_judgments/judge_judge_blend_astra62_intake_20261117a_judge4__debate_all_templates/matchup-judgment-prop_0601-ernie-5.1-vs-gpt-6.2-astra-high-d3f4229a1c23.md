### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A built a punchy case on speed, cost, and Latin America's record of stalled rail projects, with plausible examples and no fake precision. But Side B consistently narrowed the dispute to the decisive comparison and won it: (1) the TransMilenio distinction that dedicated-lane benefits come from road space, not electric motors, so early fleet replacement should only be credited with diesel saved and additional riders; (2) the concession-forcing point that both strategies include obvious bus purchases at retirement, so the real question is the marginal dollar; and (3) the devastating turn that A's "diverted funding stalls projects" warning describes the consequence of choosing buses and cannot simultaneously serve as the forecast for choosing rail. B's closing crystallized this into a clean framework — certainty governs how heavily to discount a saving, not how large it is — while A leaned repeatedly on blanket delay pessimism that B had already converted into an argument for finishing fundable links. A answered pressure questions in format but answered B's Q1 (which trips does early replacement remove beyond the lane) only with a weak "quieter buses attract riders" claim B had pre-empted. B made the debate easier to decide and controlled the decisive clash from Rebuttal 1 onward.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3: B's turn on A's stall argument — the warning that a line stalls when its final tranche is diverted is the consequence of choosing buses, and cannot also be the forecast for choosing rail. This neutralized A's central delay-pessimism pillar and exposed A's double-counting of delivery risk in the closing.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by sharpening the live comparison: accelerating bus replacement mainly saves interim diesel until the scheduled date, while a fundable rail link can displace more private-vehicle travel inside the near-term window. Side A’s delay history was forceful but did not show why that interim saving generally exceeds forgone mode shift, nor why a nearly finished link should be treated as equally unlikely to open if funded. B answered the pressure more directly and left the cleaner decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal: diverting the appropriation is itself what creates the stall, so that risk justifies finishing the link when funding is the blocker, while electrification may claim only incremental riders and fuel saved—not the dedicated lane’s whole mode-shift gain.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won the comparative weighing: electrification only earns credit for diesel saved by early replacement and any extra riders, while finishing a viable rail link can displace larger car and motorcycle emissions. Side A relied on generalized rail delay and conflated dedicated bus lanes with electrification, without answering that incremental comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: a line stalling after its final funding is diverted is a consequence of choosing buses, not a forecast for funding rail, and delivery certainty affects how to discount savings rather than proving buses save more.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by keeping the comparison honest and answering pressure directly. A's core mechanism — that electrifying at fleet renewal costs only the electric premium while displacing diesel on trips already being made, independent of any behavior change — survived the whole exchange. A answered both pressure questions with proper A1/A2 labels, conceding honestly that mixed-traffic buses don't beat cars while showing the emissions math doesn't require it. A's sharpest weapon was applying symmetric discipline to rail: a rider switching from a still-running bus is "a fare for the railway, not a diesel saving," and B never established that enough verified car displacement exists for rail to clear the cumulative-emissions-per-dollar hurdle by decade's end, despite A pressing this twice. B's counterattacks had real force (recurring fleet turnover vs. compounding corridors; the "main strategy" capital-allocation framing) and B's closing was punchy. But B's "inconsistency" charge about clocks was misdirected — A's framing (start both clocks at commitment, count savings during rollout) was consistent — and B's best near-term example, electrifying existing Mexico City suburban rail, is not rail *expansion* as the proposition states, a point A flagged and B never resolved. B also leaned on confidently asserted but unquantified specifics (São Paulo mode shift, per-passenger magnitudes) that A successfully kept on the table as unverified.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3–A4.5): responding to B's bus-lane fragility question by conceding buses don't need to outperform cars, then flipping B's logic — grade separation explains why riders switch, not whether a seat was filled by a retired car or a still-running bus — which exposed that B's mode-shift advantage remained an unquantified assertion for the rest of the debate.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by holding the motion to cumulative near-term emissions per scarce dollar and forcing Side B to clear a construction lag with retired diesel service and verified car trips. A’s fuel-accounting distinction—that a rider leaving a bus that keeps running is not a climate saving—survived B’s attempt to treat it as a fatal concession, while B’s mode-shift, capacity, and compounding claims stayed largely asserted. A also answered the trickle-purchase and bus-lane pressure more directly than B met the catch-up burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s rebuttal that a bus-to-rail switch saves no diesel if the bus keeps running, so rail must prove enough cars stay home and enough service is retired soon enough to beat electrifying buses at renewal.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A built the cleaner near-term test: cumulative emissions saved per dollar by decade's end, starting both clocks at commitment. Its renewal-premium argument showed diverting funds to rail often means buying another diesel bus, while its capacity-versus-displaced-trips distinction forced rail to prove car trips removed and diesel runs retired. Side B asserted rail's structural mode-shift and capacity advantages but never answered that catch-up burden or substantiated the São Paulo example.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it honestly conceded buses need not beat cars, because electrifying existing well-used routes cuts diesel without mode shift, then turned the same discipline on rail—a rider drawn from a still-running bus is revenue, not a carbon saving.
