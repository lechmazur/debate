### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Both sides were competent, but B controlled the decisive clash. B's opening reframe — that the motion's "when" clause establishes a problem, not a solution — set the burden, and B's tools (fee for money, scheduling for peaks, clean-fuel rules for pollution) each targeted a specific harm the cap does not. A's best move, the "dictionary trick" that staggering and rerouting are themselves caps, was effectively blunted by B's both-ways trap: if caps are just timetables, "half the ships, half the fuel" disappears. The pressure exchange was decisive: B's Q1 forced A to concede capping before alternatives are tried ("Yes" [A4.1]), and B's follow-up — the ledger counts tools a city *has*, not tools *available* — exposed a real gap in A's rescue that A never answered. B also won the housing sub-clash by showing the longer-stay guests A courts need beds in the core, forcing A to adopt B's own let-regulation tool. A landed good material (reversible caps vs irreversible resident loss; the fee that "changes the arithmetic" without clearing the pavement), but B answered both: fees adjust with a signature while itineraries don't snap back, and the timetable clears the pavement next season. B made the debate easier to decide and closed with the sharper weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's B2.1-B2.2 "both-ways" trap against A's definitional move: if "cap" means any scheduling rule, then A's own "half the ships, half the fuel" claim evaporates because nobody was removed — a clean dilemma that neutralized A's strongest early rebuttal and forced A to argue sequence instead, where A's later "Yes" concession under B3.1 pressure let B take the first-resort/last-resort framing for the rest of the debate.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B made the stronger policy comparison: harms exceeding revenue establishes a need to intervene, not that caps outperform targeted controls. A repeatedly attacked fees and delayed shore power without resolving B's timetable and clean-fuel alternatives, which directly challenged its urgency argument. A also never convincingly connected reduced day-tripping to restored housing. B overstated caps' inability to reduce aggregate harm, but its comparative case survived more intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B distinguished tools already operating from tools still available: A's negative current ledger did not establish that targeted alternatives had failed. Coupled with the argument that timetables and fuel rules could operate as quickly as caps, this materially weakened A's claim that rejecting immediate caps meant making residents wait.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): A won a close, high-quality debate by keeping the central distinction intact: scheduling, cleaner fuel, fees, and housing regulation can address the named harms without reducing total arrivals, whereas the motion endorses caps before those alternatives are exhausted. B’s reversible-cap-versus-irreversible-resident-loss framing was forceful, but its claim that A had conceded the motion was overstated, and its closing never squarely answered A’s argument that a berth timetable could relieve crowding just as quickly. A also left more of B’s case answered, including the fee arithmetic, housing mechanism, and last-resort concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s rebuttal of B’s “dictionary” move was decisive: staggering arrivals is not necessarily cutting them, and accepting caps after other tools genuinely fail does not concede a motion that permits capping before they are tried. B’s later closing emphasized the fee while largely skipping that unresolved timetable point.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side A won a tight, high-quality exchange by cornering B on instrument design. A's trap — that finite landing slots and auctioned berths are themselves caps — was pressed through every round and never fully escaped: B's own closing concedes "an auctioned slot turns away the same marginal ship." A's cap-and-charge hybrid (A2.6, A4.7) absorbed B's revenue advantage, since charging every admitted ship funds shore power too, while only a ceiling guarantees what a medieval lane needs. A also won the crowding clash: B's levy that need not keep "a single family home" leaves the street as full, and B's fallback (price the peak) reintroduces the deterrence A showed is rationing by class, answered by A's memorable "cap rations by calendar." B's best route — the trigger ledger is uncomputable — was blunted by A's zoning/noise-ordinance analogy and the tu quoque that setting any price or slot count requires the same judgment. B was sharper on housing instrument-fit and more original in framing, and both closing statements were excellent, but B's throughput-vs-triggered-cap distinction ultimately could not explain what B does when a city wants fewer arrivals than physical capacity allows without replicating A's policy. A's three clipped turns versus B's one are a real execution cost and keep the margin competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange ran from A2.1 ("a slot system with a finite number of slots is a cap") through B4.4-B4.5 (auction the peak: the marginal ship turns itself away and the city is paid) to A5.1 quoting B's own admission that "an auctioned slot turns away the same marginal ship." B's gate-open/gate-closed distinction kept the clash alive, but A's cap-and-charge answer meant B was left defending a price that either leaves crowds untouched or becomes the class-based exclusion B denounced.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): A won the central instrument comparison: finite arrival ceilings address residual crowding, and caps can coexist with fees and auctions. B legitimately challenged the housing mechanism and whether net harm automatically justifies this particular remedy, but ultimately relied on scheduling and scarce berths while denying their limiting function. B also repeatedly claimed caps collect nothing despite A explicitly proposing cap-and-charge. A answered the measurement challenge with defensible multi-indicator judgment; B never established that the motion requires one monetary unit. A’s overbroad opening claims and repeated clipping temper an otherwise solid win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s closing exposed the decisive inconsistency: B’s auctioned slot “turns away the same marginal ship,” while A had already proposed charging admitted ships. This undermined B’s cap-versus-revenue dichotomy and left its answer to persistent overcrowding dependent on a limit it refused to acknowledge.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): B built the stronger path by showing that A’s alternatives do not eliminate quantity limits: scheduling works only while quieter periods remain, a non-deterrent levy leaves crowding intact, and finite auctioned slots still exclude the marginal arrival at saturation. A’s distinction between throughput scheduling and a harm-triggered quota was sharp, but the motion requires practical judgment rather than a single monetary unit and does not require reducing annual totals. B therefore kept a live case for daily ceilings, while A increasingly depended on relabeling them.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s sustained slot challenge culminated in quoting A4.5’s admission that an auctioned slot “turns away the same marginal ship.” A4.1–A4.3 offered the strongest counter by distinguishing rescheduling from refusal, but did not escape the full-calendar scenario.
