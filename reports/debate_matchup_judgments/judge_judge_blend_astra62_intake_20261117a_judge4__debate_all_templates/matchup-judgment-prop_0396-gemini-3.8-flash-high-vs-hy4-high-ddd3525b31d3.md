### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B wins by reframing the motion around what a credit report actually records: a verified unpaid claim on future income, not the illness itself. Its line "Exogeneity defeats blame, not capacity" splits PRO's voluntariness argument from the predictive question the motion turns on. B's two-applicant comparison is simple and stayed unanswered. Its false-negative point, that exclusion makes no liability and hidden liability look identical, was never neutralized. PRO relied heavily on repeated assertions that medical debt carries "zero signal." It never offered a mechanism and instead restated involuntariness. PRO also conflated credit reports with only voluntary contracts, which B contested directly. PRO's A2 answer distinguishing utility and telecom collections was its best moment, but it again rested on choice rather than prediction. PRO also cited nonexistent references [C1.2, C1.3, C2.2], a misreferencing cost. B answered both pressure questions squarely. PRO's A1 answer sidestepped the verification point by repeating the involuntariness theme. PRO's speeches largely recycled the opening framing rather than adapting. PRO's opening and Rebuttal 1 also asserted that medical collections are "overwhelmingly static, disputed" and produce "widespread false positives" without grounding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answer to Q2 decided the debate. "Static describes schedule, not economics" showed that a lump-sum or enforceable claim draws on reserves even without amortizing. Paired with "a layoff does not appear as layoff," this undercut PRO's core reason why medical debt lacks signal. PRO never answered it beyond re-asserting voluntariness.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): A built the stronger case by separating moral blame from repayment risk: even an involuntary, verified medical liability can encumber income or savings relevant to future repayment. B answered the pressure questions in form but mostly repeated that involuntariness and non-amortization imply “zero signal,” without overcoming A’s capacity argument or the false-negative cost of hiding valid liabilities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal decisively answered that static collections can still draw on reserves through settlement, payment plans, or enforcement, while verification screens billing disputes; blanket exclusion therefore makes hidden and absent liabilities indistinguishable.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B won by reframing the report from a test of blame or voluntary discipline to a probabilistic record of encumbered capacity. A repeatedly asserted zero signal from involuntary illness but never answered how a verified, long-unpaid claim fails to affect reserves and future repayment, while B's regulated-inclusion alternative neutralized the billing-error concern without blanket blindness.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: answering A1/A2 directly, it showed verification removes the dispute objection and that static debt still draws on savings through settlement, plans, or enforcement, turning A's 'zero signal' into an asserted false negative.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): This was a close debate. PRO built a coherent case that credit reports should capture chosen borrowing conduct, while medical collections mix illness with billing errors, pending insurance and stale entries. Its pressure questions were sharp. CON answered both questions adequately, though: - On the stopping point, it limited reporting to formal collections and enforceable liabilities. - On the fixes, it compared verification buffers to the grace period before an unpaid card balance is reported. That blunted PRO's claim that needing repairs proves the entry is noise. PRO then made a meaningful concession: a valid, final liability can reduce margin, and a lender "may respond to a verified obligation." That fits CON's middle path of reforming reporting rather than banning it, and it strains PRO's absolute claim that medical debt should never appear because illness carries no meaningful signal. PRO's split between "capacity" and "repayment behavior" is clever but narrower than what underwriting predicts. CON pressed that gap consistently through its closing. CON lost some credit on discipline: - It cited nonexistent reference IDs (P2.x). - It slightly mischaracterized PRO as evaluating "character," although PRO had explicitly disclaimed a moral framing. - Its argument that a ban would push hospitals toward lawsuits was asserted more than demonstrated, and PRO answered it reasonably. On balance, CON's capacity argument plus its reform-not-ban alternative left the cleaner surviving route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came from CON's Q1 and PRO's A1. PRO conceded that a valid medical liability reduces margin and tried to separate the raw report entry from the verified debt. CON's Rebuttal 2 turned that concession into the main clash. It argued that verification buffers fix accuracy without making the signal meaningless, using the card grace-period analogy, which left PRO defending an absolute ban against a narrower reform.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): A won a clear but competitive debate by keeping repayment capacity within the meaning of future default risk: an involuntary liability can still impair solvency and repayment. B offered the sharper critique of unreliable raw collections and exposed A’s occasional substitution of verified debts for disputed entries, but never established that administrative noise destroys all predictive value or justified an absolute ban rather than verification. B’s narrow concession that valid medical liabilities reduce financial margin left A’s central route intact. A’s claims about lawsuits and correlation were somewhat overconfident, but its core underwriting argument survived.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions: enforceable collections supplied a principled reporting boundary, while verification buffers addressed inaccuracies without conceding that the underlying liability was meaningless. It then used B’s capacity concession to reconnect medical debt to default probability.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B wins by reframing prediction as default probability, not moral character or willingness alone. Side A conceded a valid medical liability reduces financial margin, undermining its claim that medical debt is not a meaningful repayment indicator. B also answered the accuracy objection more proportionately: verification periods and removal after payment address disputed bills without imposing A's absolute reporting ban. A's raw-versus-verified distinction was useful, but never explained why verified, substantial medical debt that constrains cash flow should still be categorically excluded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it used A's admission that a final liability can reduce margin to collapse the involuntariness argument, and analogized reporting buffers to credit-card grace periods rather than treating them as a concession that medical debt is meaningless.
