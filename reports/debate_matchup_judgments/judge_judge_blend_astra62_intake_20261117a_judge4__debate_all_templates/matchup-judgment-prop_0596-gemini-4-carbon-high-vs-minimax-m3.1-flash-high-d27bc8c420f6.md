### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO wins because it built a coherent case and kept adapting it. Its causal story had two parts: leverage induces voluntary deals, and know-how plus data licensing makes compulsion credible for complex biologics. It defended that story through every round. CON's strongest point was that legal access does not equal therapeutic capacity, and it pressed this persistently. PRO neutralized it comparatively. Exclusivity and withheld know-how bar existing contract facilities, so licensing is a prerequisite rather than a guarantee. CON's own preferred alternatives (nonprofit manufacture, existing case-by-case compulsory licensing) suffer the same capacity limit, and are weaker still without know-how transfer. PRO exploited this tension sharply. CON calls ordinary licensing insufficient, yet offers it as the fallback. CON's closing claim that the two are "not logically incompatible" partially conceded the point. CON also admitted that "some exception may sometimes be useful." CON's concerns about vague triggers and politicized pricing were legitimate. However, PRO gave plausible answers: approved therapies only, health-technology assessment (HTA) criteria, risk-adjusted royalties, and regulator inspection. CON never fully rebutted these, mainly repeating that they are "contestable." Both of CON's rebuttals were clipped, which is a real execution cost. PRO overclaimed in places ("sacrifices zero safety," "guaranteeing zero supply"), but its comparative weighing was clearer and its answers to pressure were direct and properly formatted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 and its contradiction framing in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing were decisive. CON had argued capacity is the bottleneck and that ordinary licensing yields "a nominal right with no medicine." PRO turned this into proof that CON's own status-quo alternatives (nonprofit manufacture, existing compulsory licensing) cannot work without the know-how transfer the pathway provides. CON never cleanly escaped this, offering only the claim that the two approaches are not logically incompatible.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): A wins a competitive debate by making B's own admissions decisive: B conceded that ordinary licensing yields a nominal right with no medicine and that gene-editing therapies require know-how beyond patents, yet its alternatives relied on nonprofit manufacture and existing compulsory powers. A pivoted this into a coherent case that only a specialized pathway creates a credible fallback and real bargaining leverage. B's strongest objection—that legal access is not therapeutic delivery—was partially answered with regulator-certified facilities, technology transfer, and conditioned royalties, though some capacity uncertainty remained. B's clipped rebuttals and late retreat to vague case-by-case licensing cost it clarity. A made the cleaner comparative path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure question and rebuttal 2 exposed a live contradiction: B endorsed nonprofit/public-manufacturing alternatives while admitting ordinary licensing is insufficient, leaving no way for those producers to make gene therapies without the compelled technical know-how the pathway would provide. B never satisfactorily reconciled that tension.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by turning Side B’s alternatives against it. B endorsed nonprofit manufacture and case-by-case compulsory licensing while admitting ordinary patent licensing cannot yield medicine for complex gene therapies, yet never showed how those tools secure the know-how it refused to compel. A’s leverage account—a credible transfer threat induces voluntary affordable supply, with regulators guarding safety and royalties protecting returns—remained the cleaner reason to require a specialized pathway. B’s caution that a license cannot command tacit capacity stayed live, but it also undermines B’s own fallbacks, and two clipped rebuttals left that contradiction incompletely repaired.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first rebuttal and closing pressed the decisive clash: B cannot rely on existing compulsory licenses and nonprofit production after conceding that patent-only licensing gives a nominal right with no medicine for gene therapies.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): CON wins because it tied the motion to how gene editing actually works, while PRO argued about compulsory licensing in the abstract. CON's opening set up three points PRO never fully neutralized: - The process is the product, and a patent does not transfer the tacit know-how needed to make it. - Autologous doses are made from each patient's own cells, so there is no inventory to import. - One-shot cures for small, shrinking cohorts make capped returns especially damaging to investment. PRO's best line was the "lawful backstop when negotiation fails." It stayed somewhat live, but CON eroded it in two ways: - **Paper permission.** A license yields no usable therapy without the originator's cooperation, which domestic law cannot command abroad. PRO conceded this in A1. - **Plausible leverage.** A developer earns nothing from an unused rare-disease cure, so stalled talks reflect budget collisions rather than refusal to sell. On PRO's A2, the royalty arithmetic retreated to "public procurement can carry the cost." CON exploited that well as an admission that public financing, not compulsion, delivers affordability. PRO's answers were honest and direct, but they mostly reframed CON's objections as "design details" rather than refuting them. PRO's closing was also weakened: - It was clipped for exceeding the word limit. - It misattributed a concession to CON at [A4.4], which was PRO's own speech.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's B4.3–B4.4 response to PRO's A1/A2. CON argued that conditioning a foreign holder's domestic patent on technical transfer gives no leverage when the patent is devalued and no rival can copy the therapy without the originator's secrets. CON then showed that PRO's affordable-price-plus-full-recovery royalty scheme collapses arithmetically for tiny cohorts, forcing PRO to fall back on public funding that CON's own alternatives already provide.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won because it showed the proposed pathway cannot reliably compel cross-border tacit know-how or create supply for autologous therapies, and that compulsory royalties cannot both slash prices and recoup failed trials. A's response retreated to public procurement, which B turned into zero added affordability. A's clipped closing left the enforcement and arithmetic pressure largely unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: a domestic patent condition gives no leverage because an innovator can forfeit a devalued local patent rather than surrender global manufacturing secrets, leaving accredited networks with paper permission and no therapy; and capping prices on a tiny cohort mathematically caps royalties below multi-trial research costs.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B won by tying the motion to the distinctive biology and economics of gene-editing cures. Compulsory licensing could not reliably force usable know-how, vectors, or autologous manufacture across borders, and Side A’s fallback—that public budgets must carry unrecovered costs—left the pathway adding launch deterrence without a unique affordability fix. Side A’s consent/backstop point stayed live but was not enough once enforcement and incentive objections went largely unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: conditioning a devalued domestic patent cannot extract cross-border manufacturing secrets no rival can copy, and low patient prices cannot fund portfolio failures, so public funding—not compulsion—does the real work.
