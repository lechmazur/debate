### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B maintained a tighter, more consistent narrative across rounds: a default anti-retaliation/communication-force rule solves a collective action problem that individual enforcement cannot, with France/Ontario offered as feasibility evidence rather than overclaimed proof. B's pressure question ("may an employer punish silence?") forced A into an answer (A4.2: "No") that B then used decisively to argue the substantive dispute was settled and only delivery mechanism remained in question—a sharp, well-executed rebuttal exchange. A's distinction between "limiting communications" and "limiting the obligation to respond" was a legitimate and under-addressed point, and A's targeted-enforcement alternative (pay for required work, explicit on-call compensation, anti-retaliation) was a serious, well-reasoned counter-proposal. However, A's opening and first rebuttal were both clipped for exceeding word limits, a real execution cost that denied the judge full development of key arguments (especially on small-firm burdens and the enforcement-ambiguity point), and left A's case feeling less complete than B's. B's closing line reframing A's concessions ("they rebuilt my house and objected to the address") crystallized the clash more memorably than A's closing counter. On balance B's case was more unified, better executed procedurally, and landed the more damaging rebuttal sequence, giving it a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question 2 ("may an employer punish an employee for not answering a 10pm message?") combined with A's concessive answer in Rebuttal 2 (A4.2: "No"), which B then leveraged in Closing to argue the substantive right was conceded and only the delivery mechanism (default vs. litigated) remained contested.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): PRO won the definitional and pressure exchange. - **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by effectively trapping Side A (CON) on the core substance of the motion. When CON conceded in the pressure round that employers should not punish workers for ignoring after-hours messages, PRO successfully framed the remainder of the debate as a choice between a universal default right and a clunky, litigation-reliant enforcement of existing overtime and anti-retaliation rules. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): CON built a tighter, more exploitable throughline: the right protects "silence," and silence generates no record, so the worker who needs protection most (the junior employee fearing retaliation) can never safely invoke it, while the remedy PRO offered (employee reporting/compliance review) runs through exactly that unsafe channel — a tension CON flagged early and PRO never squarely dissolved. CON's France point, while anecdotal, was used cautiously ("chronic overwork persists") and forced PRO into a "no counterfactual" defense that, while logically valid, read as evasive rather than affirmatively demonstrating success. CON's flexibility/trapdoor argument (parent example, on-call reclassification) was pressed consistently across rounds and answered by PRO mostly with definitional assertions ("genuine on-call means defined periods") that CON correctly noted just relocates the fight to litigation over what counts as "genuine." PRO's best material — the burden-reversal framing and the distinction between overtime law (compensating performed work) and disconnection law (regulating the demand itself) — was substantive and the single real counter-weight, but CON's closing directly engaged it ("reversing a burden on paper is not reversing it in life") and reasserted the unanswered enforceability gap. PRO also suffered three clipped speeches (Opening, Rebuttal 1, Rebuttal 2), an execution cost under the judging frame, while CON's speeches were clean and fully delivered. Both sides followed the Q&A format correctly with proper A1/A2 labeling, so no separate penalty there, but the clipping pattern compounds PRO's comparative disadvantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's recurring "silence generates no record" point, especially combined with the pressure-question exchange (A3.2 on France outcomes, B3.2 on retaliation enforceability) and CON's closing rebuttal that PRO's own success metric (employee reporting) runs through the very unsafe channel CON identified — this was never fully neutralized by PRO.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO maintained a cleaner path: overtime compensates timestamped work but does not remove the standing duty to be attentive, so the anticipation harm [B1.3, B4.4] remains distinct. CON's strongest pressure – silence generates no record and retaliation is invisible [A4.1, A5.1] – was met by a plausible definition of genuine on-call and a measurable test of required responsiveness [B4.1, B4.3], while CON's flexibility claim that the parent at 8pm would lose accommodation collapses because that time is within agreed hours under the motion [B3.1, B4.5]. France persistence was answered as no counterfactual.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.4 / A4.3 clash on overtime vs availability: B sharpens that overtime covers work performed but not the power to demand attention, A counters that silence leaves no evidence while an answered message does – the debate's most decisive weighing point.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A won by establishing a decisive analytical distinction between regulating "silence" (which leaves no paper trail and invites invisible retaliation) and regulating "action" (timestamped overtime work, which creates enforceable records). Side A convincingly argued that the right to disconnect protects the very behavior that is hardest to prove, while simultaneously chilling the informal flexibility that workers rely on. Side B's defense that "imperfect protection is better than none" failed to overcome Side A's demonstration that the policy actively harms flexible workers without materially empowering vulnerable ones.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it answered the pressure questions by explaining exactly how liability chills informal flexibility and crystallized the "silence generates no record" contrast with overtime enforcement, effectively neutralizing Side B's core mechanism.
