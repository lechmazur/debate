### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): PRO built the cleaner decision rule. Its marginal comparison asks what the next purchase lets a country do that it cannot do today, and it credits the next patrol aircraft only with the extra sorties it adds. That framing landed. PRO paired it with a coherent market-failure mechanism: carriers cannot capture avoided social losses, so independent routes and reserved capacity are underbuilt across many regions. CON's best line was its burden framing. It argued that "much more" spending needs more than local gaps, and that flexible defense assets help in many crises while a cable route is static. That was well articulated, and the landing-site and power asymmetry was a sharp counter. However, CON repeatedly narrowed PRO's case to "duplicate construction," a distortion of the motion. The motion covers protection and redundancy broadly, including reserved capacity and repair retainers. In answering PRO's Q1, CON conceded that funding those remedies across failing regions could amount to substantially more cable spending. CON then called this a "capped remedy" rather than an answer. That concession largely grants the motion's direction. CON's "defense adequacy" threshold was cleverly tied to PRO's A2 answer. Still, PRO had already shown how to compare purchases at the margin rather than as wholesale cuts. Both sides kept good epistemic discipline and avoided fake precision. PRO wins a clear but competitive decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Q1/A1 exchange was decisive. PRO asked which gaps CON would leave open if the cheapest remedies (spare-capacity contracts and repair retainers) still required substantially more cable spending. CON answered that this would be a "capped remedy, not duplicate construction." That concedes that much more protection and redundancy investment may be warranted. PRO's closing then exploited the concession: a limit on what is protected is not a reason to freeze what is spent.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by keeping the burden on “much more” spending funded from defense. A gave the stronger concrete procurement example and answered both pressure questions directly, but narrowed the case to stress-tested local gaps, using commitments where sufficient and construction only where necessary. That establishes a persuasive case for targeted resilience without establishing a general, substantially larger transfer from defense. B’s repeated incentive-versus-demonstrated-gap distinction and its symmetric scrutiny of cable dependencies left A’s scale claim unproven.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both questions, showed that A’s aircraft example implicitly accepted a defense-adequacy threshold, and then used A’s own conditional remedy to separate a justified local exception from the motion’s broader budget doctrine.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B wins because it kept the debate fixed on the proposition's burden of much more and forced Side A's stress-test standard to collapse into targeted, capped fixes.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal was decisive: it accepted A's pressure-test framing, limited the remedy to essential load, and turned A's concession that the existing squadron could cover priority missions into proof that PRO had not met the much-more burden.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): This was a close, high-quality exchange. PRO had a strong frame: cables are a common substrate, so their failure can hit command, finance and logistics at once, while one more escort is only a marginal unit. PRO also landed real pressure by showing that CON's own remedies (failover tests, repair contracts, paying for resilience customers won't buy) are protection spending. CON, however, held the more disciplined line on the motion as written. "Much more" is a general rule that displaces procurement, and CON kept asking which concrete deadline the extra spending would save. CON exposed double-counting: once a first working fallback exists, the next layer only earns its keep against failures the current arrangements cannot handle. PRO never answered that point squarely. PRO's best answer, that structural public-goods incentives predict underinvestment, was fairly met by CON's reply that incentives explain why gaps exist but not how large the bill is. A tested right to use existing spare capacity is not the same cost as a new route. PRO's "the test is decorative because the escort stays first" attack was neutralized when CON said outright that the escort is deferred whenever the test shows greater harm. PRO's closing then narrowed "much more" to capacity bounded by testing, which drifts toward CON's case-by-case position. The edge goes to CON, narrowly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over sequencing. PRO argued in A4.4 that CON's test is decorative because procurement always stays first. CON answered in B4.4 and B5.1 that the test can move money either way and that the escort is delayed when a separate route averts more harm. CON paired this with the point that incentives cannot tell us which bill to expect, which turned PRO's strongest attack into support for case-by-case spending rather than a general "much more."
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B made the decisive comparison clearer: additional cable spending must prevent a concrete essential-service failure whose harm exceeds the defense capability deferred. A offered a compelling systems-leverage case and plausible reasons for commercial underinvestment, but never established that costly gaps are generally large enough to justify “much more” spending. A’s closing also inaccurately claimed B always keeps the escort first, despite B explicitly allowing procurement delays when cable remedies pass that test.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal and closing answered both pressure questions with operational deadlines, distinguished cheap enforceable access from expensive new capacity, and exposed A’s conflation of conditional cable investment with a general commitment to much higher spending.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won narrowly by keeping the burden on PRO to justify “much more” as a general rule rather than merely showing cables matter. PRO gave a vivid systemic-leverage case and smartly treated CON’s tests and contracts as resilience spending, but it never fully proved the scale of underinvestment or why the marginal cable package beats deferred visible procurement across cases. CON’s test-and-compare framework was more disciplined: it allowed shifting resources when a specific route or repair gap clearly outweighs an escort gap, while blocking PRO from counting every network-dependent mission as an incremental benefit. That made CON’s path to victory slightly cleaner after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s rebuttal to PRO’s leverage claim—“count the missions likely to fail without the proposed improvement, not every mission that uses the network”—was decisive because it forced PRO to defend marginal harm prevented rather than general dependence on cables.
