### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built a clear causal story: state collapse, the disbanded army, AQI growing into ISIS's cross-border territorial state, and Iranian corridor expansion. It also defended a coherent baseline: a contained but intact Iraq. CON (A) was undermined by its own internal contradictions. In its opening (A1.6), its first rebuttal (A2.6) and its second rebuttal (A4.6), CON asserted that a centralized Saddam-era Iraq would have offered "more regional order" and that "the plausible non-invasion baseline is more stable." Those claims affirm the motion. CON's closing then pivoted to the opposite claim, that removing Saddam had stabilizing effects, but this sat awkwardly with everything it had argued earlier. CON's "contribution is not counterfactual proof" framing had some merit early on. It collapsed once CON itself endorsed the counterfactual. PRO answered the pressure questions directly with A1:/A2: labels, offered a plausible containment mechanism for the baseline, and kept the debate narrow. CON's own Q2, which asked PRO to justify continued Saddam as a baseline, was effectively an attack on CON's own position.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal (B4.3 and B4.4) answered both of CON's questions and then pointed out CON's admission that a centralized Iraq "could have provided more regional order," calling it decisive. CON's attempt in A4.4 to recast the line as a mere "possibility" failed, because in A4.6 CON again stated that the non-invasion baseline was more stable.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by converting Side A's own statements into the counterfactual verdict. A conceded that a centralized Iraq "could have provided more regional order," that a reduced ISIS would lack "the same Iraqi territorial reach," and in Rebuttal 2 that the non-invasion baseline was "more stable" on territorial conflict, interstate crisis, and state continuity—while still denying the proposition, an internal tension A never resolved. B answered both pressure questions with concrete mechanisms (the Baathist state denying jihadists and Iran geographic scale; containment surviving a decade without repeat aggression), and B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly with the "closed channel vs. open channel" frame. A's best material—the contribution-vs-counterfactual distinction and ISIS's dependence on Syria's independent collapse—was solid but ultimately defensive; A repeatedly retreated to "possibility is not probability" and burden-shifting rather than offering a positive case that the region would have been equally or more unstable without the invasion. Both sides used appropriate, non-fabricated background knowledge and clean formatting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1-B4.6) was decisive: it answered both pressure questions with the "state denial of scale" mechanism—jihadists existed but could not hold cities, Iranian allies existed but lacked a land corridor—and then locked in A's own concession that centralized Iraq "could have provided more regional order," turning A's hedged possibility into the counterfactual margin that decided the motion.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): B established regional spillover mechanisms and defended containment as a comparatively more stable baseline. A's distinction between contribution and net instability was legitimate, but it never established offsetting benefits sufficient to challenge B's comparison. Indeed, A's second rebuttal explicitly described the non-invasion baseline as more stable on territorial conflict, interstate crisis, and state continuity. B occasionally overstated what a merely possible alternative proves, but A repeatedly supplied counterfactual support for B rather than neutralizing its case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B effectively answered the pre-existing jihadism and independent Syrian conflict objections: the relevant difference was Iraq-enabled territorial capacity and cross-border scale, not whether other causes existed. A conceded that reduced ISIS reach mattered, while B's containment argument weakened A's proposed offset from eliminating Saddam's interstate aggression.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins a competitive debate by building the more grounded causal chain. Its case ran from dismantling the Iraqi army and Ba'ath state, to sectarian civil war, to a jihadist base, and it added a second strand: removing Iraq as a counterweight to Iran. CON (A) never squarely answered the Iran-balance argument; it mentioned Iran only as a reason Saddam would re-arm. CON's strongest work was attacking PRO's counterfactual. It argued that uncertainty should not default to PRO and that Syria and Yemen had independent Arab Spring drivers. That causal pruning was effective and narrowed PRO's ledger. However, CON's alternative depended on stacking contingencies: re-arming, renewed aggression, WMD breakout, and a violent succession. PRO exploited this well with its observed-versus-contingent asymmetry and its likelihood/scale/duration weighing. PRO also turned CON's concession that ISIS exploited post-invasion Iraq into a mechanism. Both sides had grounding problems: - **CON** placed the invasions of Iran and Kuwait "in the decade before 2003" and implied continued WMD concealment. - **Both** accepted a shaky claim that inspectors were expelled in late 2002. PRO also paid real costs. Its first rebuttal was clipped, and it ended with a line conceding "real uncertainty" that undercut its own claim. It also initially overclaimed that Syria and Yemen were consequences of the invasion. On balance, PRO's mechanisms survived better than CON's speculative alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 answer in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive exchange. It weighed by likelihood, scale, and duration and argued that CON "treats an avoided risk as though it had arrived" while PRO's costs were demonstrated events. CON's reply that avoided risks still count in comparative judgment was fair. But it never showed that the re-armed-Saddam path was actually probable, so PRO's asymmetry largely held.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won the counterfactual clash that decided the motion. CON's case required stacking contingencies: containment collapse, Saddam re-arming, renewed aggression, and WMD breakout, while PRO consistently weighed demonstrated events (institutional dismantlement, sectarian civil war, ISIS, Iranian gains) against contingent dangers. B4.3 and B5 crystallized this asymmetry cleanly, and B honestly conceded pre-2003 unrest while still holding the comparative. CON's best material—pre-2003 instability, eroding containment, correlation-vs-causation on Syria and Yemen—was real pressure, and A2.4 was sharp, but PRO absorbed it in B5.2 by keeping the Iraqi-collapse mechanism alive. CON's grounding wobbled on WMD ("unresolved WMD threat," "WMD assets in play") when Iraq in 2003 had no WMD, and A never supplied a mechanism for its likelier violent-succession claim beyond assertion. B's minor word-limit clipping was a small execution cost but cost little substance. Both sides used pressure questions well and answered directly with A1/A2 labels.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3's weighing: CON's danger requires multiple contingencies (evading containment, choosing war, breaking out), while the invasion's destabilization was direct and already demonstrated—a contrast B5 sharpened into "observed consequences vs contingent possibilities," which CON's closing "avoided risks count" line answered rhetorically but never fully neutralized probabilistically.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): B wins through the stronger causal chain: invasion, institutional collapse, and regional jihadist spillover. A correctly challenged attribution of Syria and Yemen and emphasized that avoided risks count, but never established why renewed Iraqi aggression or WMD escalation was sufficiently probable to outweigh those destabilizing effects. B ultimately narrowed its case rather than depending on every subsequent regional conflict. Both sides relied on shaky containment claims; B’s initial causal overreach, occasional burden-shifting, and clipped rebuttal limit the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s distinction between strained containment and probable rearmament, WMD breakout, and renewed war weakened A’s central offset. Its closing then accepted excluding Syria and Yemen while preserving the Iraq-collapse and jihadist-spillover mechanism, answering A’s strongest causal objection.
