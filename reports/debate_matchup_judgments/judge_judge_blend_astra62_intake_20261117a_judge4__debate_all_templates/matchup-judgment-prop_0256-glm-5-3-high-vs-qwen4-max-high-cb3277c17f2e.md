### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by holding PRO to the motion's distinctive claim: new planned refuge cities instead of coastal rebuilding. A argued that the real competitor is not foolish repeat rebuilding but a toolkit of buyouts, elevation, and capacity in existing inland towns. PRO granted that buyouts are relocation and later endorsed phasing construction against observed in-migration. A turned both concessions into evidence that PRO had drifted toward A's demand-led approach, with an expensive speculative first phase added. A's pressure questions forced costly answers. On "rather than," PRO kept defended coastal workforce housing, which let A argue the new city is an additional bill rather than a replacement. On "recoverable capital," PRO retreated from recovery to the weaker "idle versus destroyed." PRO did push back with real force. Its point that coastal rebuilding has no stop condition was good, and its closing frame of "lead versus follow" was sharp. A effectively answered both, though. The buyout deed works as a one-way exit, and the closing frame swaps the motion, since state-led planning in existing towns also counts as leading. Both sides used plausible, non-brittle examples. A's China-during-mass-migration point was especially effective grounding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer to PRO's ghost-city distinction was the most decisive exchange. PRO said those cities failed for lack of demand. A replied that China's empty districts were built during the largest rural-to-urban migration in history, because "movers choose destinations; planners only choose construction sites." That reply undercut PRO's demand argument, inverted the reversibility claim, and set up the later phasing concession.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B won by pinning the debate to the motion’s distinctive claim: planned new refuge cities, not inland investment generally. A made a strong case against repeated rebuilding and for managed transition, but B repeatedly showed that existing-town capacity, permit reform, utility expansion, and buyouts can achieve that transition without requiring a speculative new city. B turned A’s own concessions—phased construction against observed migration and protected coastal workforce housing—into evidence that the new city becomes an additional bill rather than a replacement. A’s closing reframe to “lead versus follow” was not quite the proposition; B’s alternatives are also state-led. B also handled pressure questions more cleanly, while A’s “recoverable capital” answer never fully resolved the stranded-infrastructure problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it used A’s own phased, demand-following construction concession to argue the plan was B’s toolkit with a speculative first phase attached, and used the protected coastal workforce answer to show the new city still did not replace repeated coastal defense.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side B won solidly by holding Side A to the specific burden of building greenfield planned inland cities rather than letting the proposition blur into managed retreat or inland capacity planning generally. While Side A indicted repeat beachfront rebuilding persuasively, Side B showed that expanding existing inland metros ahead of arrivals—paired with permanent buyout deeds, honest insurance pricing, and defense of working coasts—beats new refuge cities on agglomeration, timing, and sunk-cost risk. Side A's retreats to demand-phased construction and protected coastal workforce housing conceded Side B's core mechanisms, leaving the first phase of a new city as an unrecoverable, additional bill rather than a replacement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B2.2], after Side A claimed historic ghost cities failed only for lack of demand whereas climate migration guarantees demand, Side B noted that China's empty districts sat vacant during the largest rural-to-urban migration in human history—proving that macro housing demand does not create demand for a planner's chosen site because movers follow existing jobs and networks.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a competitive debate by anchoring it to the marginal dollar: the fourth or fifth payout to the same most-exposed street. CON's strongest material was the record of failed planned cities, the density argument for seawalls, and the charge that PRO's metro-adjacent siting dissolves the motion into ordinary urban expansion. These hurt, but PRO answered each one: - **Planned-city failures:** PRO distinguished monument cities from places built to meet displacement demand that already exists. - **Density:** PRO confined seawall math to dense ports, where it holds, and away from sparse barrier islands. - **Dissolving the motion:** PRO defined its distinct content as sequence and risk-bearing: land assembly, water rights, and reserved lots before arrival. CON's own concessions narrowed the dispute in PRO's favor. CON accepted buyouts once expected claims exceed relocation cost, and it admitted a capacity lag. That reduced the clash to whether destination capacity should exist before or after people arrive. PRO's closing used that ground well. It pointed out that CON trusts forecasts to end coastal homes but not to build destinations, and that the lag is billed to the poorest arrivals. CON's reply was its best moment: buyout thresholds are correctable, while trunk lines are sunk, so timing errors heal and concrete errors compound. However, that reply leaned on speculative claims, including officials pressuring families into the destination, and on loose historical analogies such as Houston having no price spiral. It also never fully reconciled CON's defense of 'a fourth rebuild houses willing families' with its own buyout threshold. PRO's staged financing, tied to signed buyout commitments, partly blunted the stranded-debt risk. Overall, PRO's path to victory was cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing turn on CON's own buyout rule was the most decisive exchange. PRO argued that CON relies on a forecast to close a family's coastal door but refuses to forecast where pipe should go. PRO then priced CON's admitted 'season's lag' as a cost borne by displaced households. CON's reversible-versus-sunk reply was strong but came only in the final speech. It also could not undo CON's earlier concessions on buyouts and inland capacity.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): B wins a clear but competitive debate. PRO's opening and closing were rhetorically sharp, and PRO turned CON's migration admission into a case for pre-built capacity. But CON did more decisive work: it rebutted the failure-mode comparison by showing an underbuilt planned city can become stranded debt, offered a workable alternative of targeted buyouts and portable checks, and exposed the motion as weakening into ordinary urban expansion once PRO conceded building near existing metros and keeping ports. PRO's late point that CON's buyout threshold is also a forecast did not neutralize CON's strongest distinction: a property-level buyout decision is revisable, while sunk trunk lines and pre-built districts are not. That reversibility asymmetry carried the final weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal of "an underbuilt city leaves an asset" was the turning point: it reframed underbuilding as stranded debt supported by real planned-city failures, then turned PRO's staged precommitment into a manufactured-demand or coercion risk.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Both debaters performed at an exceptionally high level, but Side B built the more decisive comparative case. Side A persuasively indicted repeated low-density coastal rebuilds and highlighted the human cost of short-term inland housing lags, yet Side B showed that coastal displacement creates dispersed housing demand across dozens of existing metros along family and job networks rather than concentrated demand for a state-chartered city. Side B trapped Side A in a tight structural dilemma: financing trunk lines and schools ahead of arrival risks stranded municipal debt if migrants scatter elsewhere, while staging build-out near existing metros after signed buyout commitments either collapses planned cities into ordinary urban growth or pressures officials to steer vulnerable households into the state's project. Finally, Side B cleanly neutralized Side A's closing forecast critique by distinguishing reversible, parcel-by-parcel buyout thresholds from lumpy, irreversible sunk-concrete bets.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B5.1-B5.2], Side B directly answered Side A's sharp closing catch [A5.1-A5.2] that CON's expected-claims buyout rule is also a forecast, showing that parcel-level hazard thresholds are adjustable every cycle and end in a portable check, whereas pre-built municipal trunk lines sink irreversible public debt on a speculative spatial forecast of where communities will congregate.
