### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): PRO wins clearly. Its decisive move was separating precautionary uses, such as fatigue alerts, cockpit warnings and confidential burnout support, from evaluative uses in hiring and rating. That neutralized most of CON's safety case without PRO having to deny any of it. PRO also defeated CON's "human hunches" framing by offering a concrete replacement: structured work samples with criteria set in advance, recordings kept, and each deduction tied to an observed action. CON never engaged that replacement. It kept insisting PRO's alternative was undocumented supervisor judgment, which misdescribed PRO's position. PRO answered its pressure questions honestly. It admitted it had no head-to-head study, then explained why that gap does not matter to the motion. CON's support leaned on unverifiable claims: "numerous studies," "research repeatedly shows," and airline pilot-tests showing near-miss involvement "over the following twelve months." These are the kind of brittle fake authority that deserves discounting, especially since CON's own pressure question demanded peer-reviewed citations from PRO. CON's closing mostly repeated earlier points and ended confusingly with "only a ban defeats that imperative." PRO's closing turned CON's appeal answer against it and sharpened the evidentiary-burden comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's two-captains example (A4.4–A4.5) settled the decisive exchange. A replay can confirm that a pilot raised her voice, but nothing on the recording establishes that she was dangerously stressed. If sound handling of the emergency is enough to reverse the deduction on appeal, then performance should have been scored in the first place. CON's answer was that the pilot would need "an equally objective, timestamped record," which effectively conceded the dilemma. PRO's closing then pointed out that the pilot had already supplied exactly that record.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A won dominantly by dismantling every pillar of Side B's case with superior analytical precision and epistemic discipline. First, Side A cleanly separated real-time safety alerts and burnout support—which the motion permits and where false alarms cost little—from hiring and worker evaluation, where false alarms directly harm careers. Second, Side A neutralized Side B's transparency advantage by proposing standardized, timestamped recordings scored on observable job actions rather than inferred feelings. Meanwhile, Side B repeatedly attacked a strawman of undocumented human hunches, misread [A2.5] in [B2.3], leaned on a suspiciously tailored and unverified twelve-month airline pilot-testing claim in [B4.1] while Side A honestly declined closed-book citation bait in [A4.1], and walked into a fatal contradiction on how workers could appeal an emotion score.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Q2 [A3.2] and follow-up in [A4.4]-[A4.5] and [A5.1] decisively broke Side B's auditability defense: asked how a pilot who handled an emergency well could overturn a penalty for raising her voice, Side B [B4.2] demanded a timestamped record linking the behavioral cue to a performance outcome—allowing Side A to show that the simulation recording already supplied that exact evidence, meaning the emotion score is either redundant or impossible to refute.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A won by keeping the motion scoped to hiring and worker evaluation, cleanly separating cheap precautionary alerts from costly career judgments, and showing that observable work can be recorded, scored, and appealed without an emotion inference. Side B never neutralized that distinction and leaned on unverifiable predictive specifics that a closed-book debate cannot support.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's first rebuttal, later reinforced under pressure, that a pilot-break warning can be useful precisely because a false alarm is cheap, whereas the same inference is not a fair basis for promotion when equal performance can be audited directly.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by building a narrow, concrete counterexample: a voice audition where the emotion being judged is the performance itself, not the performer's inner state. B attached that example to a demanding conditional-permission regime. Under it, the employer carries the burden of proof, the tool is compared against human judgment alone, disparities are measured, tests run blind, a human-only alternative is offered, and deletion is auditable. That framework directly targeted the gap between "flawed tool" and "must be banned outright." A leaned on a repeated, unsupported ">20% error rate" figure, which is brittle precision. A also argued from a slippery slope and from appeals to dignity and categorical mental privacy. A never engaged the work-sample distinction. A then conceded in A4.3 that predicting perceived grief does not uncover a private mental state, which undercut its own privacy pillar. A's closing kept invoking micro-expressions and physiological cues even after B had limited the case to voice-only readings. A also never answered B's point that a ban leaves the equally fallible human director unchallenged. B adapted across rounds, answered both pressure questions concretely, and gave the cleaner decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive move came in B4.5 and B5.1–B5.2. B used A's own A2 answer, which admitted that predicting perceived grief is "not uncovering a private mental state," to collapse the privacy objection. B then reframed the recording as the work sample itself. This directly reversed A's closing claim that emotion data is unlike a work sample, and A never squarely answered it.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won decisively by testing the blanket prohibition with a sharp counterexample—voice-acting auditions where conveying perceived emotion is the work sample—paired with a strict burden-of-proof regulatory model that bars surveillance of private feelings while permitting opt-in tools proven in blind trials to rescue misunderstood performers and narrow disparities. Side B answered every operational question with concrete shadow-trial and cryptographic deletion mechanisms, whereas Side A repeatedly misdescribed blind rechecks as steered by visible labels, attributed facial micro-expressions and physiological sensors to audio-only recordings, cited a nonexistent reference ID (P1.1), and repeated standalone error rates without engaging the unassisted human baseline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B3.2], Side B asked what private feeling is uncovered when a delighted actor deliberately makes a line sound grief-stricken; after Side A conceded in [A4.3] that this uncovers no private mental state while implausibly claiming voice auditions still capture facial micro-expressions and lack a business need, Side B used [B4.5] and [B5.1]-[B5.2] to collapse Side A's core privacy claim by pointing out that an audio reading needs neither a camera nor a pulse sensor and literally is the work sample.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by reframing the ban as overbroad and forcing a cleaner clash: job-relevant analysis of a submitted performance versus surveillance of private inner states. B answered the error-rate and enforcement pressure with a comparative shadow-trial rule and cabined safeguards, then used A's concession that a correct grief label does not uncover delight. A kept treating any capture as categorical mind-reading and did not neutralize why a failed test would already deny permission, or why an opt-in, deleted, audio-only second hearing of a work sample is equivalent to wiretapping.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal seized A's admission that predicting heard grief is not uncovering a private feeling, then weighed a tested blind recheck against a ban that settles the comparison before anyone hears the takes.
