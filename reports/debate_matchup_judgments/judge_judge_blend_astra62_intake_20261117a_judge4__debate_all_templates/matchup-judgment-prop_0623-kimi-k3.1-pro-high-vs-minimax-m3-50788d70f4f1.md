### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): CON built a sharper, more durable structural distinction: statutory floors are public law, revisable by elected institutions, while sectoral bargains are private coordination among incumbents that binds non-consenting rivals and absent workers. This crystallized into a clean dilemma in Rebuttal 2 ("either bargaining retains controlling power, raising the private-delegation objection, or government approval makes it a backstop to direct rulemaking"), which PRO never fully escaped—PRO's answer ("shared authorship secures commitment and durability") asserted a value without explaining why consultative public rulemaking could not achieve the same durability once government retains final approval/rejection power. PRO's opening was strong on power imbalance and administrability, and its representation/election safeguards were a reasonable design response, but it was clipped for length (a real execution cost) and its later speeches largely repeated the leverage/exclusion framing without landing a knockout answer to CON's institutional trap. CON's closing also correctly pressed the motion's "across entire industries" language, arguing PRO showed sectoral bargaining can sometimes help but not that it is the necessary general default—an argument PRO's closing did not squarely rebut. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions format correctly with proper A1/A2 answers, but CON's answers were tighter and more directly responsive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 dilemma—"either bargaining retains controlling power... or approval makes it a backstop to direct rulemaking"—was the most decisive exchange; PRO's closing response (asserting "shared authorship" without resolving why unilateral rulemaking with consultation couldn't match it) failed to neutralize this trap.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that public consultation and statutory minima do not provide the countervailing power or industry-specific coordination that bargaining supplies. Side A raised serious capture, cost, and legitimacy risks, but its claim that government oversight makes bargaining either undemocratic or redundant was too rigid: B plausibly defended negotiation and public review as complementary. B also weighed capture as a contestable design risk against worker exclusion as a structural weakness of A’s alternative. The clipped opening was an execution cost but did not erase that comparative advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest response was that consultation merely informs government whereas bargaining gives workers standing to shape terms, with public review preserving accountability. This directly weakened Side A’s central redundancy dilemma and made the choice between the frameworks easier to decide.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO won the central leverage clash: A showed why statutory minima + voluntary bargaining leaves isolated/subcontracted workers without countervailing power, while consultation vs bargaining framing clarified stakes. CON's strong private-delegation/cartel objection was partially answered by public approval, representativeness criteria, and occupational/regional flexibility, and CON's answer to leverage pressure redefined floors rather than solving exclusion. A's concrete A2 on elected nonunion reps and separate approvals met B's selection-pressure better than B met A's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.2-A4.4 providing elected nonunion workers, guaranteed small-employer seats, and reframing capture as design failure vs exclusion as structural in CON's model, directly answering B3.2 and turning B's legitimacy critique.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): PRO built a coherent coordination-failure case and tried to neutralize CON's heterogeneity/capture objections with a stack of design safeguards (tiers, phase-ins, public parties, review). CON's decisive move was to show that each safeguard either reproduces CON's own baseline (statutory floors, consultation, public inspection) or sacrifices the uniformity PRO wants, and to isolate what is actually "distinctive" about sectoral bargaining: compulsory joint authority binding nonmembers, entrants, and freelancers who were never at the table. The home-care exchange was particularly telling: PRO conceded bargaining "cannot repeal the funding arithmetic" and pivoted to transparency, which CON correctly noted is available under direct statutory accountability too. CON's closing turned PRO's own words ("joint authority to settle binding terms") into an admission that the framework's real contribution is compulsion over outsiders rather than any benefit unavailable elsewhere, and PRO's closing did not squarely rebut this reframing, largely reasserting legitimacy through safeguards already shown to be concessions. CON's opening was clipped, a real execution cost, but the core argument survived intact and was developed cleanly across rebuttals, giving it the stronger, more decision-ready case overall.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4.3-B4.7), responding to PRO's home-care funding answer and inspector argument, which crystallized the "shared benefits vs. distinctive coercion" framework that PRO's closing never fully answered.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by showing that enforceability, consultation, inspection, revision, and worker input can exist under direct statutory regulation, while compulsory extension by industry representatives creates distinctive risks of mismatch, capture, and exclusion. Side B defended flexible design well, but too often answered those risks by adding safeguards rather than proving why binding joint authority was necessary or superior. Side A also crystallized the tradeoff more sharply, despite the clipped opening.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B candidly conceded the home-care funding trilemma and argued bargaining would make it explicit; Side A then showed that public fiscal review can expose the same tradeoff without empowering negotiators to bind nonmembers. This reinforced Side A's broader comparison of shared benefits against distinctive coercion.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B won the weighing. PRO offered real coordination and enforcement benefits, but CON's replies that safeguards (tiers, phase-ins, reviews) concede the mismatch risk and that benefits are shared with statutory minima + consultation + inspection left PRO without a distinctive advantage for compulsory extension. The home-care funding pressure and insider-capture pressure stuck, while PRO's accountability answer conceded the arithmetic. B crystallized the trade as nonexclusive benefits vs distinctive coercion and mismatch.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.6 answering A3 Qs and A4: reframing competition to minima as lawful boundary, showing funding trade-off unavoidable even with sectoral bargaining, and that participation and adaptation exist without giving selected representatives binding power over nonmembers.
