### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO wins because it kept the debate tied to the motion's actual subject: disclosure rules. It defined parity as equal substantive rigor in disclosure, meaning clear, prominent, timely and enforceable. Under that reading, the bedroom-creator burden largely disappears. CON built a clever dilemma. Either "as strict as" means only a label, which adds nothing, or it imports broadcast compliance machinery, which is unworkable. Both horns are weaker than they look. - **The "full regime" horn:** It relies on folding pre-clearance, claim substantiation and network sanctions into "disclosure rules for personalities." That is a stretch, because those duties largely fall on institutions, not on presenters. - **The "adds nothing" horn:** PRO answered it in A4.1. Current influencer disclosure is patchy etiquette, with vague "collab" tags and buried hashtags. Parity turns it into an enforceable legal baseline. CON's own A2 then conceded the operative standard: a clear "paid ad" label at the first point of persuasion. PRO's closing turned that concession into convergence on the motion. CON's evasion and over-labeling concerns were reasonable but stayed speculative. PRO's comparison weighed them against the core harm of millions mistaking bought praise for a friend's advice, which was cleaner. CON was rhetorically sharp, but its case increasingly depended on its own expansive reading of "strict."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was the second Pressure Questions round. PRO asked CON to name a disclosure duty a large influencer cannot meet and to state its own standard. CON answered with a pre-claim "paid ad" label that matched PRO's standard. PRO's closing then exploited this: the disagreement was about infrastructure the motion does not require, not about the disclosure duty itself.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO won by holding the motion to advertising-disclosure rules themselves—clear, prominent, pre-pitch labeling with accountability—rather than the institutional machinery CON imported.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 and closing, which answered both pressure questions and then used B4.3’s own “paid ad” at first persuasion to show CON had conceded the operative disclosure duty while attacking an institutional reading the motion does not require.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A gave the more natural reading of the motion: parity in disclosure substance, not transplantation of a network’s entire corporate structure. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): PRO wins because it showed that strictness in disclosure, not identical production machinery, is the standard the motion actually sets. CON's strongest weapon was a dilemma: either parity is trivially a one-tap label, or it imports broadcast-style compliance burdens and mislabels embedded testimony. That was an original and sharp line, and it forced PRO to get specific. PRO answered directly in A1: it named a concrete stricter duty, sponsor identification placed at the moment of endorsement and kept persistent through long content, with no pre-clearance required. CON's own A1 then endorsed payer-specific, point-of-recommendation, repeated labels. That is essentially a strict, broadcast-equivalent standard. CON's insistence that this is "not parity" leaned on reading "as strict as" as "identical format," which the motion does not require. PRO's closing exploited this cleanly and also seized the gap CON left by limiting its trigger to "discrete paid promotion": affiliate asides and casual mentions are exactly where hidden payment exploits parasocial trust. CON's compliance-trap and wallpaper points were plausible, but PRO's "the trigger is already known" response largely neutralized the burden claim. Both sides avoided fake precision and stayed well grounded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Pressure Questions exchange decided the debate. CON asked PRO to name a stricter traditional-media duty or concede collapse. PRO answered with payer identification plus contemporaneous, persistent placement. CON's own A1 then adopted nearly the same standard, which let PRO's closing convincingly frame the remaining dispute as vocabulary. It also showed that CON's narrower "discrete paid promotion" trigger leaves affiliate-laden testimony exposed.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side B won by keeping the motion’s actual measuring stick in focus. It showed that “as strict as traditional media” either misfits continuous, mixed creator speech or collapses into the prominent, relationship-specific disclosure rule Side B already accepted. Side A made a strong case for clear disclosure and answered the compliance-trap objection, but never fully justified why traditional-media parity remained necessary once it abandoned production burdens, and its closing dismissed a live trigger difference as semantics.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: after specifying payer- and relationship-specific labels, it showed Side A’s own answers had moved toward a targeted regime while still leaving post-by-post boundary and archive burdens unresolved.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): A made the cleaner case that “as strict as” concerns the stringency of disclosure, not importing broadcast production machinery or using one indiscriminate label. B’s fit-versus-parity framing and collapse dilemma were incisive, but B increasingly accepted payer-specific, prominent, repeated disclosure while overstating A’s position as uniform “ad” labeling. A also identified a remaining substantive gap: B’s narrower trigger could leave embedded affiliate and monetized recommendations outside strict disclosure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s challenge that A’s rule either imposed unworkable parity or collapsed into B’s targeted regime was the strongest attack. A answered directly with payer identification, prominent placement, and persistent livestream disclosure, then used B’s acceptance of those measures to expose the narrowing difference between the sides.
