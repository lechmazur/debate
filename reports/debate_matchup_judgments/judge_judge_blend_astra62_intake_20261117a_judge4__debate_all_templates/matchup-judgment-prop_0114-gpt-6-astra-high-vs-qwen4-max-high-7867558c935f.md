### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON (A) was the sharper and more inventive debater. Its timing objection, the pension-floor targeting problem, the verification-gatekeeper point and the "wage-gap trigger" critique repeatedly forced PRO to tighten its design. The trouble came at PRO's Q2. CON answered honestly that a raised floor and home help do not restore a residual caregiver's lost pension. It then offered a "care-recognized pension supplement" that grants retirement entitlement for periods of care. That instrument is substantively a pension credit for unpaid care, which is what the motion asks for. PRO's closing exploited this cleanly. CON's reply that the motion "names a mechanism inside contributory insurance" leans on the PRO design's particular trigger rather than on the motion as written. The motion only requires pension or social-insurance credits for care. PRO also defended its trigger coherently: credits insure a lost pension entitlement, and the full-time worker does not suffer that loss. PRO further showed that CON's own instrument also needs proof of care. CON's rhetoric ("recognition she earns by losing") was memorable but contests a design detail more than the proposition. PRO wins narrowly-to-clearly: after the full exchange, its core principle survives and CON's alternative converged onto it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Q2 and CON's A2 answer. CON conceded that floors and services leave the residual caregiver's pension loss unrepaired and proposed a care-based pension supplement. PRO's closing then reframed that supplement as acceptance of the motion's core principle, neutralizing CON's "credit versus supplement" dichotomy.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a tight, well-fought debate. A's CON case was sharp — the timing objection, the pension-floor offset, the verification gatekeeper, and the "rewards payroll absence, not care" trigger attack were all genuinely damaging and beautifully phrased. But B's answers held: the timing point was reframed correctly (rejecting credits for not buying respite is like rejecting pensions for not paying rent), the floor problem was met by flat credits coordinated with floors, and the full-time-daughter objection was answered with the coherent insurance logic that differential losses justify differential compensation. The decisive exchange came when A's own fallback — a "care-recognized pension supplement" that delivers retirement entitlement for care periods — conceded B's central principle, and B exploited this in the closing: CON disputes the name of filling the hole while accepting it should be filled. A's counter that the motion names a specific mechanism (contribution-gap-triggered credits) is real but narrow, since a care-triggered pension supplement is still recognizably "pension or social-insurance credits for unpaid care work" under the motion as written. A's superior rhetoric and the father-consent gatekeeper point kept this competitive, but B left the cleaner live route to victory: someone above the floor who must cut hours loses retirement security, and only credits (or A's concession dressed as a supplement) repair that.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1's dilemma — that coordinating credits with floors either collapses into a care-recognized supplement (conceding A) or stays contribution-shaped and pays most where earnings were strong — was the sharpest single move of the debate. B never fully escaped it, but B4.5/B5.2's response (a flat credit for qualifying care periods can accrue independent of salary, and if A's supplement provides retirement entitlement for care, A has accepted the motion's principle) turned A's trap into a concession that ultimately decided the debate.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B built a far more coherent and resilient case from start to finish. Side B cleanly separated immediate care support from social insurance against permanent retirement loss, neutralized Side A's regressivity, spousal-wealth, and verification objections, and used its Pressure Questions to prove that Side A's Opening alternatives (immediate services, cash, and pension floors) fail caregivers who must still cut paid hours and remain above the minimum floor. On the remaining dispute over Side B's gap-filling trigger, Side B persuasively explained why social insurance targets actual lost pension accrual while immediate support addresses burdens shared by full-time working caregivers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's sequence in [B2.1], [B3.2], and [B4.6] exposing the gap in Side A's "support today plus a pension floor" counter-model: Side B showed that rejecting credits because they cannot buy Tuesday's respite is like rejecting pensions because they cannot pay today's rent, then trapped Side A on the caregiver who cuts hours despite respite and lands above the floor—forcing Side A's candid concession in [A4.3] and late pivot to its own retirement supplement.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins a clear but competitive debate. The motion asks whether care should earn pension or insurance credits rather than stay invisible. It does not ask whether credits must outrank every other form of support. PRO tracked that question better. PRO set up a concrete, memorable case: the daughter's nine-year hole in her contribution record. It framed credits as correcting a measurement error. It also showed they are not fiscally novel, since systems already credit childrearing, illness and unemployment. Its "do both" answer, sequencing respite first and then capped credits, fits within the motion. PRO's argument that CON's need-based logic "proves too much" against the contributory tier was a strong turn. CON's reply that contributory pensions are financed by payroll contributions was weakened by its own earlier concession that non-contributory credits already exist. CON argued carefully throughout. Its strongest points were opportunity cost, imperfect targeting, the counterfactual on care savings, and turning "respite first" into a concession. Its closing also landed a fair point that capped credits do not equalize the daughter with her sister either. But CON's case mostly shows that credits are not the only or highest priority. That falls short of defeating the motion, and CON's floor alternative never answered the recognition and record gap PRO kept pressing. Grounding was cleaner on CON's side, though neither side relied on fake precision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was PRO's Q1 and its Rebuttal 2. PRO forced CON to admit that its individual minimum pension does not pay the caregiver more than a sibling who never cared. PRO then argued that a pure-need metric would delegitimize the contributory tier CON defends. CON's answer, distinguishing paid contributions from tax-funded entitlements, sat uneasily with existing credits for childrearing and unemployment, which CON had already conceded. CON's best counter was turning "respite first" into an admission of priority, but that does not negate the motion.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a competitive but clear debate. Both sides built coherent cases: B framed credits as correcting a measurement blank in a contributory system; A framed them as one spending choice among better alternatives (respite, income support, a retirement floor). The decisive clash was the daughter-vs-sister comparison. A's floor protects need but, as B hammered in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, pays the caregiving daughter nothing extra and leaves a "tie at the bottom." B's consistency trap — that A defends wage-linked entitlements above need, so A's real objection was never need but work without a payslip — was never fully neutralized; A's "paid contributions differ" reply was partly question-begging since the motion is precisely whether care counts as contribution. A landed real hits too: B's "respite first" concession and the contradiction between "credits don't withdraw" and later phasing them down for high earners. But B absorbed these as sequencing and design rather than retreat, and A's A4.1 answer ("neither do capped credits") dodged the relative-compensation point. B's answers to pressure questions were more direct and damaging, and the closing crystallization (raise the floor and it swallows the contributory tier — a price A won't pay) made the choice sharper than A's closing. B was also more memorable rhetorically without sacrificing substance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2: answering A1/A2 directly, then landing the "tie at the bottom" point — the floor lifts both sisters to the same line and leaves the nine-year gap intact — plus the consistency argument that A accepts contribution-based rewards above need, exposing that the objection is to payslip-less work, not to non-need-based entitlements.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side A won a high-level debate by dismantling Side B's central claim that pension credits are self-financing "bookkeeping" rather than a competing tax-funded spending choice. Side A cleanly showed that even where family care avoids public nursing-home bills, those baseline savings exist with or without credits and are not earmarked for deferred pension liabilities over present respite, current income support, or a broad retirement floor. Under pressure from Side A's questions, Side B conceded "Respite first" and proposed phasing credits down for high earners, undermining its earlier stance that credits are unconditional, non-withdrawing earned rights. Side A also neutralized Side B's closing attack on the retirement floor by pointing out that Side B's own capped, phased-down credits would not equalize the caregiving daughter with her working sister either.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A3.1], Side A forced Side B off its "fund all of it" stance by pitting pension credits for a secure former caregiver against respite keeping a low-income caregiver employed today. When Side B answered "Respite first" and proposed phasing credits down for high earners ([B4.1]), Side A capitalized in [A4.4], showing that Side B had conceded record-correction is not an overriding obligation and had contradicted its earlier promise ([B2.6]) that credits "don't withdraw" with income—proving credits are a discretionary distributional choice, not neutral bookkeeping.
