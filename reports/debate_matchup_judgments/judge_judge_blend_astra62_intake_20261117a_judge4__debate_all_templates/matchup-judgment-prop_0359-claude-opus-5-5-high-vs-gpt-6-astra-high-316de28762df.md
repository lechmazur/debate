### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins a high-level, competitive debate by proving that an information gap is not automatically a welfare improvement and demonstrating a concrete competitive distortion from compulsory default display. Side B argued memorably for symmetry in a three-party market, answered the multi-trip bonus trap practically with a pending-bonus line, and put sharp rhetorical pressure on Side A's allowance of optional breakdowns. However, Side B never established how knowing the rider's fare helps a driver choose better-paying work (given $20 of $40 beats $12 of $15) when pooled costs, demand rationing, and cross-trip incentives break single-trip pass-through. Side A systematically dismantled Side B's "real-time auditors" claim, showed that elevating single-trip gross share to the universal fairness benchmark penalizes platforms that use busy-trip cuts to fund off-peak driver guarantees, and proved that public claims can be audited by regulators without mandating a distorting per-trip benchmark on every screen.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A4.3]-[A4.5], Side A dismantled Side B's claim that adding a screen line ("Part of this fee funds guaranteed hourly minimums") lets drivers verify surge use in real time, pointing out that a peak-hour driver's receipt cannot show whether that fee funded another driver working off-peak—forcing Side B back onto the very regulatory accounting it had dismissed as a "filing cabinet."
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won narrowly by preserving the decisive distinction between allowing access to fare information and making an incomplete gross-share metric prominent by default. Its cross-trip bonus and off-peak-guarantee arguments identified a concrete competitive distortion and showed why screen labels cannot substitute for verification. Side B was more rhetorically forceful and offered practical display answers, but its closing reduced A’s objection to fear that people would act on information and did not fully establish that compulsory prominence improves decisions rather than merely attracting attention.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: a driver’s busy-trip receipt cannot verify guarantees paid to another off-peak driver, and labeling or later updating a bonus does not resolve its allocation. This exposed the limits of B’s “real-time auditors” claim and restored the need for the broader accounting B had disparaged.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): B won by narrowing the decisive clash to default salience and turning A’s opt-in concession into a cost: if the split is computable and not dangerous, hiding it by default mainly protects platform opacity. A raised strong complexity and distortion concerns, but B’s labeled bonus-progress and public-explanation answers blunted them enough, while B’s concrete airport-run harm remained more vivid.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 answer to the bonus question—show settled trip pay plus a clearly labeled bonus-progress line—defused A’s strongest implementation trap and kept the debate focused on default disclosure rather than optional access.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): In a high-level debate from both sides, Side A (CON) won by systematically dismantling the practical decision-making value of a per-trip default fare split while offering targeted alternatives (upfront guaranteed pay, itemized driver statements, and audited pass-through/take-rate reporting). Side B (PRO) argued with strong epistemic discipline and landed real hits—exposing Side A's overstatement on allocating insurance costs [B3.1] and its omission of platform profit compression [B3.2]. However, Side A's pressure forced crucial concessions that narrowed PRO's path to victory: Side B conceded that drivers should base trip acceptance on guaranteed pay rather than pre-bonus splits [B4.1], that a single ride cannot establish surge pass-through [B4.4], and that granular splits make rival monitoring easier [B4.2]. By the closing speeches, Side A convincingly showed that putting a provisional, gross-revenue percentage on every screen adds behavioral noise and duopoly coordination risk without answering the core accountability questions PRO set out to solve.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's two-step dismantling of PRO's core decision-utility claims across [A2.4], [A3.1], and [A4.2]: Side A proved a single trip lacks a counterfactual baseline to show riders whether surge raised driver pay (conceded in [B4.4]), and used Q1 on weekly bonuses to elicit Side B's admission in [B4.1] that drivers should still decide using guaranteed upfront pay—allowing Side A to reframe the default split as "a provocative number attached to a decision already made."
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): B won narrowly because its core case survived: gross fare, driver compensation, and retained platform revenue are checkable transaction facts even when they cannot explain surge causation or replace earnings summaries. A delivered the sharper attacks on provisional bonuses, limited decision value, and duopoly monitoring, but its behavioral and coordination harms remained comparatively speculative. Its alternatives also left riders and drivers without the transaction-specific distribution the motion concerns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s A2/A4 sequence most effectively pressured B by exposing later reconciliation and extracting a concession on rival monitoring. B’s direct A1/A2 answers and closing contained that damage by distinguishing gross retention from profit, separating contingent bonuses, and framing coordination as a risk rather than an established reason to withhold the facts.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won narrowly by keeping the focus on the proposition’s core: default visibility of the actual transaction split, with revenue/profit distinctions and reconciliation labels. Side A made strong objections about cross-subsidies, provisional allocations, and coordination risk, but those harms stayed somewhat speculative and did not fully defeat the presumption that riders and drivers should see their own fare division by default.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal sharply used B’s concession that drivers decide on guaranteed pay to attack the decision value of the split, but B preserved enough of the accountability case by separating decision relevance from transaction scrutiny.
