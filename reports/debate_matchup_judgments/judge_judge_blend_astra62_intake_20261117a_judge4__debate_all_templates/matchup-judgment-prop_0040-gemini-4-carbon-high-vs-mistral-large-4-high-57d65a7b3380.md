### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Both sides were competent, but PRO adapted better as the clash narrowed. PRO separated archiving and quoting from first-person mimicry. It argued that labeling cannot address harms where no one is deceived. Its pressure questions exposed two real gaps: the case of the sole heir who is also the wrongdoer, and whether an explicit refusal must be honored. CON conceded that explicit refusals must be honored, and PRO then pressed that concession effectively. If directives are verifiable, CON's vagueness worry about a "fog" weakens. The debate then reduces to whether silence defaults to yes or no. PRO also turned CON's point about people who died before AI existed: those people could not opt out either. CON's proportionality case was coherent, but its remedies became thinner under pressure. Its claim that "platforms themselves can sue" was hand-wavy. Its closing asserted that training inflicts no injury without engaging PRO's privacy and biometric-harvesting answer. CON also kept citing the literature-tutor and archival examples after PRO had shown they fall outside the motion. It overstated the motion as a "moratorium on memory and scholarship" and as criminalization. PRO bit the bullet honestly on private family memorials and gave a reason why that ban is justified.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The sole-heir standing exchange was most decisive. PRO's Q2 asked who sues when the estranged spouse is the only heir. CON answered with vague appeals to court-appointed representatives and platforms suing. PRO replied that grief-tech platforms will never sue their own paying users, and that an heir's authorization makes the upload "authorized" rather than misuse. That exposed the gap in CON's targeted-remedies alternative.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B built the stronger path to victory by making nonconsensual training itself the core injury, rather than only downstream deception or monetization. Side A’s targeted-safeguards alternative was plausible and well-framed around proportionality, but it never fully resolved the sole-heir standing problem or justified why family-commissioned mimicry should proceed without the deceased’s consent. Side B also turned Side A’s concession that explicit premortem refusals must be honored into a decisive default question: if silence is not consent, opt-in follows. A had genuine overbreadth costs, but B showed the rule still preserves real memories and allows voluntary digital afterlife.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was Side B’s second rebuttal: once nonconsensual training is recognized as the injury, labeling, cooling-off periods, and estate licensing only govern after the harm has occurred, and the sole-heir scenario leaves no effective standing for targeted remedies.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A won by turning the debate on the default meaning of silence: because the dead cannot opt out afterward, A made explicit premortem consent the cleaner legal rule. A also gave a direct training-stage harm and showed why downstream labeling, cooling-off periods, and estate remedies are weaker when heirs conflict or companies profit from grief. Side B’s proportionality and overbreadth points were strong, but its alternatives often relied on speculative standing and it never fully answered why silence should permit mimicry.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal directly answered the pressure questions by conceding that private mimicking memorials would be barred, then reframing training itself as nonconsensual extraction and showing that labeling/estate remedies fail when a sole heir authorizes the upload or the platform profits from continued grief subscriptions.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): CON won by setting a precise counterfactual early and holding it: estate or next-of-kin consent combined with binding opt-outs made during life also operates before training. That alternative blocks the scraped profiles and unauthorized celebrity bots PRO cited. On that comparison, a mandatory opt-in before death adds little protection and mainly bans consensual uses. PRO never squarely engaged that comparison. Late in the debate, PRO claimed CON's regime leaves companies "free to build the exact simulacra that injure families," which misdescribes a regime that requires estate permission before training. PRO also shifted on history. It first spared the Lincoln exhibit as "public records," then conceded under questioning that public speeches are covered. CON exploited that reversal cleanly. CON's widow and film-studio comparisons exposed inconsistencies in PRO's line between "stewardship" and "simulation." Under PRO's rule, families may publish intimate letters, and studios may script invented Lincoln dialogue, yet a private encrypted keepsake or a labeled study tool is illegal. PRO's strongest asset was its closing frame, "whose consent counts," and the idea that personality is not estate property. That is a coherent principle, but it was asserted rather than defended against CON's point that intestacy and organ-donation norms already let survivors act when the dead left no instructions. Both sides used the A1/A2 format properly and both asked fair pressure questions. CON's answers were more candid, and its use of them did more to advance its case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B4.2–B4.3. CON clarified that "zero additional exploitation" compares a mandatory opt-in against estate consent, not against today's legal gap. That neutralized PRO's celebrity-litigation question. CON then turned PRO's A1 concession, that Lincoln-speech chatbots are banned, against PRO's own earlier "public records" carve-out and against its tolerance of film studios scripting invented dialogue for actors playing Lincoln.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): A built the cleaner comparative path after the exchange. PRO conceded under pressure that the rule bars even confidential family memorials and historical chatbots, while A showed estate/survivor consent plus living opt-outs can block unauthorized exploitation before training too. PRO's no-yes/no-voice principle was clear but bit too many bullets against private mourning and Lincoln-style education.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 turn on "zero additional exploitation" was decisive: it used PRO's Lincoln and private-vendor concessions to show the opt-in rule uniquely bans consensual, non-exploitive uses without preventing extra abuse.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won by exposing the proposition’s overbreadth and offering a clearer comparative rule: estate or next-of-kin consent plus living opt-outs can block abusive training before it happens without banning private memorials or historical interactive uses. A gave a principled autonomy case and clean slogans, but could not fully answer why pre-death opt-in adds protection beyond B’s alternative or why the harsh results in widow and historical cases should outweigh consent concerns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttal after pressure questions effectively conceded the widow/vendor example but turned it into the winning overbreadth point, then compared pre-death opt-in with estate consent to show both block law-abiding abuse while only the former bans consensual private and historical uses.
