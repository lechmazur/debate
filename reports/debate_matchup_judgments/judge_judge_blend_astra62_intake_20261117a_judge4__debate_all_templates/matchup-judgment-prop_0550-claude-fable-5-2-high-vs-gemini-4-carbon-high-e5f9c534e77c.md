### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B won the decisive exchanges. A's cleverest pressure (the poaching paradox, A3.1) was cleanly resolved by B4.1: portability across rival firms at twenty-two is not adaptability across forty years of technological change, and rival bidding only prices a task, never lifts a technician onto a bachelor's trajectory. B also flipped A4.1's concession that taxpayers fund the "portable share" into an erasure of A's co-investment advantage (B4.3), neutralized the dropout-debt framing by noting grant-aided early leavers carry no tuition debt (B2.1, B4.2), and landed the sharpest turn of the debate in B5.1: A cannot condemn college subsidies via unprepared dropouts while celebrating employers who screen those same teenagers out of apprenticeships. A's ceiling-times-odds framework was coherent and well-voiced ("he quoted the prize and never priced the ticket"), and A2.5's liquidity retort was strong, but A left B's college-door-reopening and adaptability points unanswered, reasserted the poaching contradiction after B had resolved it, and suffered three clipped turns as execution costs. B's completion-raising mechanism (work hours relieved by grants) gave a live causal route A only met with "hope," and B's closing crystallized the clash rather than widening it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1's answer to A's poaching paradox: distinguishing task-level portability (which invites poaching) from career-level adaptability (which protects against automation), then B5.1's turn showing A's embrace of employer screening contradicted A's own marginal-dropout case against college subsidies.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): A won a close debate by turning B’s screening defense into a targeting dilemma: unprepared students may receive no apprenticeship slots, while capable cash-strapped students risk diversion from higher-ceiling degrees. A also answered the portability-versus-automation challenge and explained how grants could improve completion. B’s expected-value framing and rhetoric were strong, but its closing assumed the dropout outcome grants were intended to change and never fully resolved employer scaling. B’s repeated clipping was an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal decisively separated portability within today’s occupation from adaptability across a career, then used employer screening to expose the unresolved tension in B’s treatment of marginal students.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B wins by answering A's marginal-enrollee ceiling-times-odds frame rather than ignoring it: subsidies make completion itself more likely by reducing work and debt, while apprenticeship expansion faces a selection dilemma. ### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Both sides were competent and clash-focused, but B built the cleaner route to victory. B's three pillars held up under pressure: (1) the long-run depreciation of specific skills versus compounding general skills, anchored in the marginal-admission evidence that already counts dropouts; (2) the selection dilemma — tight apprenticeship standards miss the marginal student, loose ones produce dilution; and (3) the evidence asymmetry — college estimates count their failures and still come out positive, while A's apprenticeship ledger counted only completers. A answered pressure questions directly and landed good counters (washouts keep wages and work history; a subsidy buys seat time, not a credential; European tracking not like-with-like), but A's attempt to supply long-run evidence in A4.1 was effectively dismantled in B4.7-4.10: career academies feed college, the dual-systems citation sat awkwardly next to A's earlier disqualification of European comparisons, and the journeyworker comparison repeated the survivorship error A itself had charged B with. A never squarely repaired that. B's closing crystallized the asymmetry and the degree-gated ceiling cleanly. B's execution costs — three clipped turns — are real but did not break the visible case, since each clipped turn lost only its tail. A wins points for format discipline and a strong closing reframe of the employer test, but B made the debate easier to decide on the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.6-4.11): the point-by-point dismantling of A's three pieces of long-run evidence, culminating in the ledger asymmetry — "the college estimate counts its failures and still comes out positive; the apprenticeship estimate counts only its successes" — was the decisive exchange that A's closing never fully answered.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B made a strong case on immediate income, completion risk, and employer discipline, but never established the decisive long-run advantage for scaled pathways serving marginal students. Side A directly pressed that burden, exposed B’s selection dilemma, and showed why positive marginal-college estimates counting dropouts were more probative than B’s completer-heavy comparisons. A’s closing distinction between employer demand today and earnings over forty years sharply resolved the motion. A’s clipped turns were an execution cost, but its core answers remained clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal dismantled B’s evidence answer: career academies often feed college, B invoked dual systems after disqualifying them as incomparable, and journeyworker completers versus college entrants reproduced the survivorship problem B had alleged. That left B’s long-run route substantially unsupported.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B won the long-run, expected-outcomes comparison. Its selection-versus-dilution dilemma, degree-ceiling concession, and point that college cutoff gains already count dropouts survived A's replies. A offered plausible paid-work and demand-matching mechanisms, but never supplied credible scaled evidence that marginal apprentices out-earn comparable college entrants by mid-career.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 dismantled A's three late evidence claims: career academies feed college, dual systems were cited after being disqualified, and journeyworker completers were compared with college entrants—the survivorship error A had charged against B.
