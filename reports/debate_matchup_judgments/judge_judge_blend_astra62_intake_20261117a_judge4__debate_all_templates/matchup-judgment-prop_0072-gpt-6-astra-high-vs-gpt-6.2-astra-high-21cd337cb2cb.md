### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): PRO wins. The decisive issue was whether a mandatory floor is justified when omissions can persist quietly without suppression or false statements. PRO showed that CON's transparency and teacher-protection safeguards do not trigger when a school simply passes over major abuses, and that the students least equipped to object are the ones most likely to be affected. CON's strongest counter—that a broad category and teacher choice invite safest certified cases and may displace inquiries into current institutions—was real but narrower. PRO's factual-substance test meaningfully reduced the whitewashing risk, and studying even settled atrocities still gives students evidence with which to question redemption narratives. CON never showed that the safer-selection incentive outweighs the guaranteed exposure PRO establishes; the alternative remains dependent on a willing teacher.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was PRO's rebuttal that a school could omit Japanese American incarceration while violating none of CON's safeguards: omission itself is a curricular judgment, so a public requirement makes that judgment contestable. B pressed safe compliance well but could not erase that baseline point.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a very high-level debate by establishing a clear comparative baseline advantage that Side A (CON) could never overcome. Side B demonstrated that national atrocities routinely vanish from classrooms without formal censorship because timetables are crowded and schools avoid local controversy—a gap Side A conceded its alternative of teacher protections and syllabus transparency fails to fix when nobody at school wants the fight. Side A mounted a sophisticated critique that compliance rules incentivize selecting "safe," settled atrocities over disputed abuses by currently powerful institutions. However, Side B persuasively weighed the trade-off: without the requirement, reluctant schools can omit past and present abuses alike, whereas guaranteeing every student primary evidence of major historical harms equips ordinary pupils across all districts to question state power rather than leaving them dependent on a volunteer champion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal in [B4.2]-[B4.4]—building on [B3.1] and forcing Side A's concession in [A4.1]—decisively neutralized Side A's dilemma that enforcement either tolerates patriotic "redemption" whitewashing or polices teacher ideology. By grounding compliance in whether student work examines concrete evidence of who exercised power and what harm resulted (such as reading an exclusion order alongside family testimony), Side B showed that factual evidence itself arms students to question a redemption gloss ("did anyone give it back?") whereas omission leaves them no reason even to ask.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): A wins narrowly because it kept the debate focused on the students who would otherwise never encounter omitted abuses, offered a plausible factual-substance compliance test, and weighed the requirement against the status quo. B’s safe-compliance and displacement objections were sharp and not fully eliminated, but remained more speculative than A’s concrete omission and equity case.
