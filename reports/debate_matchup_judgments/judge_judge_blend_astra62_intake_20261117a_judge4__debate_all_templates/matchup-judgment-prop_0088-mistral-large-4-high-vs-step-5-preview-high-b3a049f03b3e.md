### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): B more convincingly connected staffing continuity and fiscal stability to learning itself. A largely assumed fewer school days meant less learning, then conceded that longer-day fatigue could be managed. Its insistence that schools must prioritize instruction did not resolve the comparative tradeoff. A’s equity and access objections remained substantial, while B overstated savings and portrayed five-day alternatives as uniformly dysfunctional. Those weaknesses limit B to a clear but competitive win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B directly answered A’s claim that pay and class size drive burnout: schedule flexibility need not cure every staffing problem to improve retention, and teacher continuity itself supports learning. A never adequately answered that marginal-benefit argument, instead repeatedly separating the educational mission from the staffing needed to deliver it.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by establishing the decisive real-world counterfactual and linking staffing retention directly to student learning. Rather than defending four days against an idealized five-day calendar, B showed that districts adopt compressed schedules under fiscal and labor distress, making the actual alternative five degraded days marked by vacancies, rotating substitutes, larger classes, and program cuts. B also convincingly argued that schedule flexibility acts as a non-wage retention lever that preserves instructional continuity. Side A built a strong equity case around low-income students losing guaranteed Friday supervision and meals, smartly noted how district averages can mask subgroup losses, and exposed tension in B's claim that budget-strapped rural districts can fund targeted Friday services. However, A had no convincing answer for structural deficits once levies fail, dismissed retention too simplistically, stumbled verbally in closing, and undercut its own extended-day fatigue argument in [A4.1] by calling longer-day fatigue a bounded design problem solved by front-loading core instruction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's "fix the finances" alternative across [B2.1], [B3.2], [B4.2], and [B5.1] was decisive. After A claimed districts could simply pass levies or find targeted cuts [A2.5], B used Q2 to ask what happens when levies have already failed and overhead cuts are exhausted; when A retreated in [A4.2] without defending the staffing fallout of an underfunded five-day week, B cleanly framed the live trade as four days with experienced teachers versus five days with vacancies, substitutes, and overcrowded classrooms.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side B built the stronger comparative case by insisting that the relevant alternative is often an understaffed, unstable five-day week rather than an ideally funded one. B connected savings and retention directly to learning conditions and answered both pressure questions with a coherent staffed-four-days-versus-unstable-five-days comparison. B sometimes overstated how representative that counterfactual is, keeping the win competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal decisively challenged A’s idealized counterfactual and “fix the finances” response. A’s later list of administrative remedies did not fully resolve the exhausted-deficit scenario, while B’s second rebuttal sharpened the choice between four staffed days and five days weakened by vacancies, overcrowding, and program cuts.
