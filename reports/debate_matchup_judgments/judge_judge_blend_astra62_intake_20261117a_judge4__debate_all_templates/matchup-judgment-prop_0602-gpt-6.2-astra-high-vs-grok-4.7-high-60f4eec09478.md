### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO wins on adaptation. Across the rounds, PRO converted CON's strongest pressure points into concrete design answers. It kept the official store as the default and offered a reset-proof store-only lock at setup. It let platforms block detected threats immediately while still reviewing pretextual blocks. In answer to CON's Q2, it added a funded refund guarantor. CON's best line was that the mandate deletes the sealed phone and that baseline standards lag attackers. That line was sharp and memorable. After A1, however, CON conceded that a sealed phone cannot be talked open later, which shrank the remaining threat to bad advice in the "setup minute." CON's claim that PRO "abandoned the motion" overreaches. "Baseline security standards" can plausibly include an owner-chosen permanent lock, and the motion requires that the OS permit sideloading, not that every owner keep it enabled. CON's chilling argument about block review had some force. Its reply to the guarantor ("never gives back the contacts") shifted ground rather than answering the refund point. PRO's closing framed the comparison cleanly: an irrevocable no chosen by the parent versus a platform no imposed on every buyer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2 was the decisive exchange. The reset-proof seal answered CON's social-engineering lock attack, and the funded guarantor answered CON's question about who returns the money. This forced CON to retreat to the narrower setup-minute risk and to a textual objection that the seal is not in the motion.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): B made the clearer case that mandated capability need not mean compelled exposure: default-off installation, an owner-selected permanent seal, continuing malware blocks, and an enforceable refund backstop answered A’s principal harms. A’s sealed-phone framing and setup-time social-engineering argument were strong, but its repeated claim that the motion necessarily abolishes a sealed option was not established. A also lost the payment-refund exchange after B supplied a concrete guarantor mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions: no later path would reopen a permanent owner-selected seal, and an independent funded guarantor could refund buyers when a processor refused. A’s response narrowed the dispute to setup-time advice but relied too heavily on asserting that these safeguards were outside the motion.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): A narrowly wins because its baseline-standards model preserved the motion’s core benefit—opening payments and distribution—while giving credible security answers: default-off sideloading, reset-proof optional locks, immediate threat blocks, and a refund-guarantee mechanism. B’s sealed-phone critique was sharp and memorable, but it overclaimed by treating every residual setup risk as decisive and underweighed the competitive harms of forcing users to change devices. A’s final frame—that the state can require capability without forcing users to abandon it—was enough to carry the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over the reset-proof lock: B used pressure and Rebuttal 2 to argue that A’s seal was an add-on the motion does not require and that the setup moment remains exploitable; A answered directly that the mandate need only require capability, not leave it enabled, keeping the benefits of openness while allowing an irrevocable parental lock.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): PRO had the larger stakes story: monopoly rents, gatekeeper barriers to entry, and the platform acting as "merchant and judge." It also exploited CON's late concession on billing to good effect. But the motion is conjunctive. CON's core claim was never squarely neutralized: under the mandate, the developer rather than the user chooses the channel, so "can remain" and "just disable sideloading" do not preserve a family's option to rely on a closed phone. PRO's answers moved through optional reporting, source-specific trust and "procurement," and CON answered each in turn. Optional reporting is not authorization before a charge. A signature identifies the signer, not the school's choice. A parent who declines still goes without the app. CON also gave a narrower alternative for gatekeeping abuse: enforceable admission deadlines and an independent regulator. That blunted PRO's "only door" argument, though PRO's point that the owner still defines qualification and controls timing kept some force. PRO's later speeches leaned on repeated toll-booth rhetoric rather than resolving the access-loss point. CON's precision and adaptation across rounds give it a narrow edge, offset partly by its billing concession and thinner engagement with PRO's competition harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over PRO's closing line, "Neither parent picks the channel today either." CON turned it directly: precisely because the school chooses the channel, the platform rule determines whether access can be conditioned on approving an outside installer. That exposed the limit of PRO's user-switch answers.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): A won a close, high-quality debate by identifying a cost that survives even perfect baseline security: a developer or school can make outside distribution exclusive, leaving users unable to retain a reliable no-sideloading phone while accessing that service. B’s verified, source-specific installation answers malware and impersonation concerns, but not this loss of user choice. A also answered both pressure rounds directly and offered narrower remedies—open billing, public qualification criteria, deadlines, and enforceable admission. B made a strong competition and gatekeeper-bias case, but never fully explained why those remedies leave an installation mandate necessary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it granted genuine signatures, school confirmation, and passing security checks, then showed that the outside-only school app still defeats the family’s chosen installation policy. That shifted the clash from manageable security risk to who controls distribution, which B’s closing did not squarely neutralize.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B wins narrowly by keeping the decisive clash on user choice rather than security alone. A showed convincingly that baseline standards can reduce sideloading risks and that gatekeeper exit can discipline fees, but B maintained that the mandate can make a developer’s chosen distribution channel binding on users, especially in the school/parent example, even when security checks pass. B also preserved a less-drastic path: open payments and enforceable fair-access rules without making outside installation a universal requirement. A’s source-specific sideloading answered some security pressure, but did not fully restore the user’s ability to rely on a no-outside-install guarantee when a required app is only available externally.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 response to source-specific sideloading: a signature can identify the distributor without proving the school chose it, and a parent who refuses that channel still loses access, keeping the user-choice harm live.
