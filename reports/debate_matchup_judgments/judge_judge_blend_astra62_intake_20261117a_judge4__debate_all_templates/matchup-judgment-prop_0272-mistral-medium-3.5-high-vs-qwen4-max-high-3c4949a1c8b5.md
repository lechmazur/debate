### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won by framing the motion as a funding and building-hours queue and holding that frame throughout. Its test, "fund what the school uniquely supplies first," was clear and stayed stable across every speech. CON had real ground on accessibility, scale, and digital skills for future jobs, but three things undercut it. First, it repeatedly cast the motion as a "cap," which PRO reasonably answered by pointing out that a queue is not a ceiling. Second, it never resolved the tension between its two models: unsupervised asynchronous access is cheap and reaches students anywhere, while staffed, collaborative digital sessions in classrooms are engaging but carry the same staffing cost and the same transport barriers. PRO pressed that contradiction sharply and exploited it in both the second rebuttal and the closing. Third, CON's closing restated reach and future readiness without escaping the dilemma. To CON's credit, it answered the one-dollar question directly ("digital goes first"). However, the justification it gave rested on the same contradiction. CON's claims that engagement rates rival in-person activities and that maintenance costs exceed license fees were also asserted rather than reasoned. PRO used examples well, notably the bus funding the sport and the rural home lacking both a bus and broadband, and it tracked CON's shifting impact metric.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal and closing were decisive. CON had conceded that engagement "depends on program design" and that supervised digital "uses the same staffed hour." PRO showed that both concessions bring back the staffing costs and attendance barriers that CON's accessibility and scale claims depended on. In PRO's words, CON "cannot bank both," and the license becomes merely a "cheaper wrapper on the same hour."
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): A built the clearer path to victory by keeping the debate on sequencing and identifying the scarce inputs: staffed time, accessible facilities, supervision, and embodied shared activity. B’s future-readiness argument remained partly live, but its accessibility and scalability case fractured under pressure: unsupervised digital access supplied flexibility, while supervised digital sessions supplied engagement, and B could not consistently claim both. B’s final assertion that a session in an existing classroom includes students unable to reach that classroom crystallized the unresolved contradiction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal forced the decisive choice between cheap, unsupervised scale and staffed, collaborative engagement. A’s closing then sharpened it: B could not “bank both,” especially while claiming that a classroom-based session reached the bus-less rural student and fixed-shift evening worker.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A framed the motion as sequencing scarce, irreplaceable inputs—staffed, open, supervised space—before abundant logins, and sustained that test throughout. Side B never resolved A’s central dilemma: unsupervised digital is cheap and anywhere but offers weak supervision and engagement, while supervised digital requires the same paid adult and building A prioritizes, eliminating its cost and scale advantage. B’s closing repeated the contradiction by claiming classroom-supervised programs simultaneously reach rural and working students who cannot attend, leaving A’s accessibility and one-dollar challenges unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 and closing decisively forced B to choose between near-zero-cost unsupervised access and staffed digital enrichment, showing B could not claim both sets of benefits.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won solidly. CON aimed at the motion's operative word, "before," and used PRO's own opening standard, "universally available," to force PRO to defend a rigid queue. The rural-school example (gym fine, no Mandarin teacher) pressed that queue effectively. CON also turned PRO's equity and cost arguments with concrete, plausible points: - School-based programs supply the device and the connection. - Coaches' stipends, insurance, and the late bus are the real scarce inputs. - Roster limits cap participation in a way a licensed seat does not. PRO shifted between "universally available" and "sensible priority." Under pressure questions, PRO then gave two incompatible tests: facilities on hand versus no waitlists. CON's second rebuttal and closing turned this into a clean dilemma. Either the motion forbids almost nothing, or it is a permanent gate that hits under-resourced districts hardest. PRO mostly repeated irreplaceability claims (cardiovascular health, fine motor skills) without engaging sequence versus substitution. PRO also never answered CON's counterfactual that the alternative to a supervised lab is an empty house and a phone. PRO leaned on loosely asserted "well-documented" harms and an overstated claim that a field is "available to all." PRO's closing was largely recap, and it asserted the rural example "proves our case" without resolving the dilemma.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.5–A4.7 was decisive. It took PRO's "yes" to the rural Mandarin license and PRO's "no waitlists or capacity barriers" condition and showed they cannot coexist. Under the facilities test the motion is empty. Under the capacity test no school ever qualifies, so the rural student waits after all. PRO never answered this.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the debate on the motion’s sequencing requirement rather than merely comparing the value of sports and screens. A established plausible developmental benefits but never justified a universal “before” rule, shifted between universal access and sensible priority, and then gave incompatible release conditions. B directly exposed that contradiction, answered A’s inclusion and health questions without overclaiming, and closed with a clean dilemma: A’s rule either permits nearly everything or indefinitely blocks needed digital opportunities. A’s closing repeated irreplaceability without resolving that dilemma.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal decisively contrasted A’s facility-based permission for the rural Mandarin course with A’s participation-based “no waitlists or capacity barriers” test, showing that A’s answers made the motion either empty or a permanent gate.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side B won the sequencing clash: PRO never defended a stable meaning of "before." B showed embodied benefits, even if irreplaceable, do not justify a universal queue, flipped the equity and cost arguments, and used the rural-school case to expose that PRO's rule either forbids almost nothing or blocks students with the fewest alternatives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal created the decisive dilemma: if existing facilities satisfy PRO's test, the motion is empty; if universal participation without waitlists is required, it becomes a permanent gate. PRO's closing repeated irreplaceability without resolving that contradiction.
