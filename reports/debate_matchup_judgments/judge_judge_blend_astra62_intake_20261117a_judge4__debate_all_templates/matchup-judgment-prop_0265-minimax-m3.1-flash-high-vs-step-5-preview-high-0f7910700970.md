### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO wins narrowly on the core clash. It held that the motion requires the displayed price itself to show the amount payable. It then neutralized CON's best substantive objection, that an all-in price hides cost structure, by showing components alongside the total. CON's "regulate timing, not arithmetic" frame was coherent, but it never fully explained why requiring diners to combine a food price with a separately stated condition beats giving them the combined figure. CON's late "unstable headline" attack also has limits. It applies equally to CON's own percentage-based conditional disclosure, since the cost also rises when a sixth diner joins. CON also slightly misdescribed B4.2, which withdraws the £30 total and replaces it with £36, not the reverse. PRO's B5.3 explicitly pre-empted this attack. The margin is thin because PRO's rebuttal, second rebuttal and closing were all clipped. That is a real execution cost: PRO lost its final weighing. CON's closing, by contrast, was clean and rhetorically sharp.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange began with CON's Q1 asking whether the "menu price" is £30 or £36. PRO answered directly in A1 (B4.1): the £36 is the displayed payable price, with the £30 dish price and £6 charge itemised alongside it. This defused CON's claim that incorporation conceals cost structure. It also reduced CON's objection to a terminology dispute, which PRO named as such in B5.2.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a close, well-matched debate. B's decisive move was arguing the motion as written—displayed price must state the amount payable—while conceding itemization alongside a prominent total, which absorbed CON's "structure transparency" attack almost entirely. Side A (CON) skillfully exposed the composite-total tension (is the menu price £30 or £36?) and the provisional-price problem when party size changes, and its rebuttals were sharper and cleaner rhetorically. But B answered the pressure questions directly and honestly (B4.1–B4.2), and showed that conditionality burdens both regimes symmetrically: a disclosed conditional total is still a disclosed price, while CON's own rule also requires diners to track conditions. A's persistent weakness was substituting a "timing of disclosure" rule for the proposition and never fully justifying that swap; B hammered this repeatedly and A's closing overclaimed ("knows the applicable cost with certainty") when its own regime equally involves conditional variation. B's execution cost—three clipped turns—narrowed the margin, but its argumentative core survived the exchange more intact than A's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The pressure-question exchange over the composite price: A's Q1/Q2 forced B to declare the £36 total "the menu price" and to require withdrawing/updating it when a sixth diner joins; B answered directly in B4.1–B4.2, and A's follow-up in A4.3/A5.1 ("a price that can be revoked is a temporary estimate") was the sharpest CON moment. B's counter—that conditional prices are still disclosed prices and prices changing is not prices being hidden—largely neutralized it, since CON's own regime also turns on the same party-size condition.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by arguing the proposition as written and winning the central comparative clash even on Side B's preferred ground. Side B refused to defend revealing charges only at the bill or checkout stage, substituting a pre-commitment notice rule while still forcing diners to perform mental percentage math (such as adding 12.5% to £32) on every dish and leaving universal surcharges undefended. Side A cleanly neutralized Side B's cost-structure transparency critique by showing that menus can itemize the food price and service fee right alongside the payable total, exposing Side B's complaint about redefining the menu price as purely terminological. Side B's sharp pressure on conditional party-size surcharges and cleaner delivery—paired with Side A getting clipped at the end of three speeches and making minor R1 reference/attribution slips (C1.1 and [B1.5])—narrowed the margin but did not overturn Side A's decisive substantive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's sustained rebuttal ([A2.2], [A3.2], [A4.1], [A5.2]) showing that displaying "Dish £30, Mandatory Service £6, Total £36" gives diners both full cost-structure visibility and an immediate payable figure, reducing Side B's "concealment" objection to a semantic dispute over the label "menu price."

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a close debate. It framed the decisive clash memorably: does notice before ordering require the number the diner actually acts on, or is a disclosed rate enough? CON's counter-model of prominent advance disclosure was coherent, its arithmetic example was accurate, and its "all" objection about conditional large-party charges was a real pressure point. However, CON's alternative concedes most of PRO's core harm, since CON agrees that hiding charges until checkout is wrong. Its A2 answer then admitted that bundling with a plainly stated policy "solves little beyond preventing miscalculation," which is exactly PRO's anchoring and cognitive-load harm. PRO's tax distinction (a government levy versus the restaurant's own pricing choice) blunted CON's opening hook. PRO's Q2 also neutralized the claim that bundling "removes information." PRO's move to classify triggered party-size charges as conditional rather than mandatory for the menu reader was somewhat convenient, and CON rightly flagged it as narrowing the motion. PRO's closing also included sloppy bare reference IDs. On CON's side, the first rebuttal was clipped, a real execution cost. On balance, PRO left the cleaner live route: disclosure-only leaves an anchoring gap that inclusion closes at little cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 and the A2 exchange were decisive. PRO showed that an inclusive price can sit alongside a plain-language service policy, so no breakdown is lost. That forced CON to concede that bundling's main effect is preventing miscalculation, which is the very harm PRO was targeting.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) was rhetorically superior, with memorable framing ("truth that arrives too late is documentation," "a worksheet, not a price") and a clean emotional core around anchoring and cognitive load. But Side A (CON) did the stronger argumentative work on the motion as written: A pressed the scope problem repeatedly—conditional/party-size charges are mandatory once triggered, so the proposition's "all" cannot be satisfied by any single truthful item price—and B's response amounted to redefining "mandatory" to cover only universal charges, effectively narrowing the proposition while claiming to defend it. A also neutralized B's core cognitive-tax argument by noting that diners can never compute a final total from item prices alone (drinks, quantities, tax, companions), so inclusive pricing changes presentation, not decision-relevant information, and by showing that a prominently stated rate changes the anchor to the real obligation. B's best unanswered moment—the receipt arriving after commitment—was blunted because B conceded advance disclosure prevents checkout surprise. A's clipped first rebuttal is an execution cost, and A's persistence in pressing the scope clash across all five speeches gave it the cleaner live route to victory. B wins on style; A wins on the argument. The margin is thin because B's timing-of-truth framing remained genuinely compelling.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's pressure question on conditional charges (what single menu price can truthfully include an unknown future rate?) followed by B's Rebuttal 2 answer that conditional surcharges "are not mandatory for the diner reading the menu." That answer let B keep its universal-charge case alive but at the cost of conceding that not all mandatory charges belong in the item price—which A then hammered in Rebuttal 2 ([A4.4]) and the closing ("PRO therefore narrows the debate to universal charges, abandoning the proposition as written"). B never fully repaired this breach, and it proved the motion's fatal overreach.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won the central comparative clash over how diners actually evaluate prices. Even when Side B pivoted from checkout-only revelation to upfront percentage disclosure on the menu, Side A convincingly showed why unbundled item prices still distort choice: diners anchor on the lower sticker price ($28 rather than $33.60), struggle to compare variable fee structures under social dining pressure, and receive the itemized receipt only after commitment is irreversible. Side A also cleanly neutralized Side B's claim that bundling hides information by pointing out that menus can display inclusive prices alongside a plain-language service policy, and flipped Side B's demand-deterrence argument by noting that lower ordering under honest prices proves unbundled quotes were inflating demand. Side B kept the round competitive by pressing a genuine textual and practical problem with embedding conditional large-party gratuities under the motion's word "all" and noting that item prices never eliminate order-total arithmetic, while minor execution slips on both sides (Side B's clipped first rebuttal; Side A's premature claim in Turn 7 that [A3.2] went unanswered and bare reference tags in closing) roughly offset.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's "bundling removes information" argument ([B2.3] answered in [A3.2], [A4.2], and [A4.5]): Side A showed both that diners have no need to separate mandatory service from food any more than rent from the price of a steak, and that stating the service policy alongside inclusive prices preserves full transparency without outsourcing arithmetic—forcing a weak concession in [B4.3] that collapsed Side B's main harm against bundling.
