### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO built a coherent, adapting framework (notice-and-cure, proportional fines keyed to resources and duration, remediation-focused enforcement) and used it to answer CON's strongest example (the rural clinic) with concrete distinguishing criteria rather than bare assertion. Crucially, PRO's pressure question exposed a structural hole in CON's case: CON never offered a workable alternative consequence for a dominant provider that simply ignores warnings indefinitely, and CON's answer ("enforce existing binding remediation plans") was circular, since an unenforced "binding" plan has the same toothlessness problem CON criticizes in fines. CON's core intuition—that fines drain budgets serving disabled users rather than punishing decision-makers—was vivid and repeated effectively, but across rebuttals it mostly reasserted this point rather than engaging with PRO's scaling and notice/cure mechanics, and it never resolved the bad-actor gap that PRO kept pressing. PRO's answers to pressure questions were properly labeled and substantive; CON's answers, while labeled correctly, doubled down on unfalsifiable claims ("no regulator can ever know") without engaging PRO's proposed evidentiary criteria. PRO's closing crystallized the decisive clash (toothless alternative vs. enforceable backstop) more precisely than CON's closing, which mostly repeated the budget-diversion point without addressing the dominant-offender problem at all.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to A2 (distinguishing the rural clinic's genuine capacity constraints from indefinite refusal using duration, documented triage, and interim access) directly neutralized CON's central example, while CON's parallel answer on the dominant provider (relying on unenforced "binding" remediation plans) failed to escape the same toothlessness critique CON leveled at fines.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B won by defending a limited regime of notice, cure periods, proportionality, and escalation while showing that Side A's alternative lacked any credible backstop against persistent refusal. Side A offered a memorable budget-incidence argument, but relied on unsupported absolutes that fines never induce remediation and inevitably cut disabled support. It also failed to explain how its binding remediation plans would bind dominant providers without consequences. The clipped opening slightly hurt B, but not enough to offset its superior comparison and adaptation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered both questions, conceded that no fine can guarantee zero budget effect, and reframed the comparison as early compliance versus prolonged neglect followed by avoidable sanctions. It then exposed Side A's inability to impose any effective consequence on a provider that ignores warnings.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): B wins on the central enforcement gap. Despite clipped opening, PRO established notice/cure, proportionality, and that fixing also costs, while CON repeated that fines tax disabled budgets but never answered the dominant provider that ignores warnings. B's pressure questions exposed permanent immunity; A's A1 conceded no evidence could ever justify enforcement and A2 offered a consequence-free plan. That weighing of certain exclusion vs speculative cuts is decided for PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 answers to A's Qs plus dismantling of "no regulator can distinguish" — showing duration, steps taken, interim access as workable tests and forcing CON into admitting it would impose no consequence even on wilful defiance.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON built a consistent, escalating case that fines punish without guaranteeing remediation, and pressed a genuine structural gap: nothing in the motion text itself secures cure periods, proportionality, or anti-withdrawal protections—PRO had to keep calling these "implied" rather than showing they're in the motion as written. CON's alternative (launch/procurement conditions tied to verified fixes) was a sharper causal mechanism directly answering "how does punishment convert into access." PRO's strongest move was the ramp analogy and the "20 years of failure" framing, but when pressed (A3.1/A3.2), PRO's answers leaned on "nothing prohibits also banning retreat" and "implied by the terms," which CON correctly flagged as conceding the safeguards aren't actually written into the motion. PRO never adequately rebutted the core asymmetry CON pressed repeatedly: a fine can be paid without producing a working service, especially for public agencies paying from the same budget needed for repairs. Both sides suffered word-limit clipping in their final rebuttals, but CON's clipped turn had already made its key point before the cut, while PRO's closing mostly recapitulated the "zero cost" framing without directly rebuilding the access-guarantee. CON's closing crystallized the clash cleanly ("cost without secured remedy") while PRO's closing was more assertion than fresh resolution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1-A2.5 (CON Rebuttal 1) pressing that "meaningful/repeated/basic" are liability thresholds, not an enforcement architecture, forcing PRO in Rebuttal 2 to admit the safeguards are merely "implied" rather than specified in the motion.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): B won narrowly by showing that fines need not independently diagnose or repair barriers; they supply consequences that make existing remedies a priority and can coexist with testing, deadlines, and anti-withdrawal rules. A sharply exposed B’s unsupported claims and implementation gaps, but too often treated the motion as requiring fines to be the sole or guaranteed remedy. A’s two clipped turns were an additional minor execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer that “a fine does not write code” mistakes the function of enforcement less than A’s critique suggested: the penalty changes organizational priorities, while complementary rules perform diagnosis and remediation. This directly weakened A’s central demand that the fine itself secure access.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON wins the central enforcement clash. PRO relied on status-quo failure and the physical-access analogy, but never closed the gap between paying a fine and getting a usable service. CON's retreat objection - that you can end "repeated failure" by removing the feature - was pressed in Q1 and never answered except with "nothing prohibits also banning" [B4.1], conceding the motion alone doesn't secure access. CON also showed meaningful/repeated/basic are liability labels, not a scheme for who tests, what cure period exists, or how a small charity is protected, and PRO's reply that safeguards are "implied" [B4.3] left the vagueness live. PRO's Q1 about what consequence will force prioritization was answered directly with launch/procurement conditions [A4.1], while PRO sidestepped CON's pressure. Verified-access vs budgeted fine becomes the cleaner voter.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 + Pressure Q on retreat: shows fines charge after exclusion, can be budgeted by agencies, and reward removing material to avoid "repeated failure," while PRO's version needs cure periods and proportionality not in the text - exposing that the decisive protections are added in speech, not in the motion.
