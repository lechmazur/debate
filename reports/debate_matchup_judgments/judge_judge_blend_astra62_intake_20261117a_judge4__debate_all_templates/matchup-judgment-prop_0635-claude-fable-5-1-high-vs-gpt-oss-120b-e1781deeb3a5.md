### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): A (CON) won by turning PRO's own pressure answers into concessions. When pressed, PRO said one wrong dosage in ten million is a defect and that open release without safety scaffolding is itself a defect. A showed this collapses the proposed "reasonable safeguards" limit into strict liability for probabilistic errors and an effective ban on open-weight models. A also offered the sharper comparative story: liability should track contextual knowledge, so the therapy deployer and police user bear responsibility, not the provider who only ships tokens. B's car and pharmaceutical analogies were answered cleanly by A's Toyota/driver distinction, and B's consolidation response accidentally strengthened A's point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 seized on PRO's answers to the pressure questions: B admitted liability does not hinge on a statistical threshold and that open release itself is a defect. A converted those direct answers into proof that PRO's standard demands unrealistically safe outputs and bans open-weight release, making it the decisive exchange.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): Side A (CON) won solidly by showing that grafting finished-goods product liability onto general-purpose models misplaces responsibility onto upstream providers who lack deployment context while collapsing the motion's "foreseeable" and "reasonable safeguards" limits. Side B (PRO) undermined its own case by claiming negligence duties are too vague for novel AI while asserting "reasonable safeguards" are objective benchmarks, conceding that even a one-in-ten-million medical error constitutes a design defect, and making technically implausible claims about fine-tuning and open-weight guardrails. Despite minor blemishes from Side A (a few unbracketed P-prefix IDs and slightly clipping a qualifier from B4.3 in closing), Side A answered Side B's pressure questions directly, turned Side B's car, pharma, and therapy-chatbot analogies, and won the comparative weighing on real-world user safety.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Turn 4 and Turn 8 dismantling of Side B's liability standard: Side A first caught Side B's internal contradiction in calling deployer negligence "too vague" while proposing an equally vague upstream "reasonable safeguards" standard [A2.8], then used Q1/Q2 to get Side B to label a one-in-ten-million error a defect [B4.1] before proving why fixed-chemistry drug batches differ from probabilistic answering and why licenses, watermarks, and removable weight terms do not stop open-weight modification [A4.4-A4.6].
