### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A won both prongs of the motion with cleaner logic and stronger institutional mechanics. Yet Side B repeatedly conflated a default-settings mandate with strict liability for any bad interaction reaching a minor, whereas Side A met the friction concern head-on with statutory UX standards (a plain-language, one-screen opt-in) enforced via objective funnel telemetry and demographic surveys that do not rely on individual teen complaints.

A never fully neutralized those automatic audits or justified raising “reduce harm” to net elimination of peer harm plus unmeasured speech loss.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by dominating the measurable harm-reduction prong and persuasively holding the line on what counts as a significant increase in censorship. Side A established concrete harm pathways—unsolicited adult stranger DMs enabling grooming and engagement feeds pushing self-harm or eating-disorder content—backed by plausible rollout outcomes and rigorous population-level tracking metrics in A4.1. Side B offered a smart, creative framing of state-mandated sticky defaults as censorship by design and made a strong point in B4.3 that contextual prompts do not replace passive discovery for isolated teens. However, Side B dropped its early content-classification overblocking claim after A2.3 pointed out that account-level defaults are content-neutral, stretched significant censorship to cover even a transparent one-tap opt-in, and lost the risk-displacement clash when it claimed marginal migration to smaller apps would suffice to negate large-platform harm reductions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In Q2 [A3.2], Side A exposed a sharp tension in Side B's case: claiming teens are too bound by friction to tap one clear opt-in prompt on major platforms, yet active enough to migrate to smaller unmoderated apps. Side B's reply in B4.3—that friends move socially and marginal displacement is enough to defeat measurable harm reduction—failed to explain why friend groups would abandon major platforms where peer connection still works by default, and conceded away net harm weighing since marginal spillover cannot erase large reductions where most teens actually are.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by holding the motion to its dual test. Contact drops and easy opt-out rhetoric never became independent harm reduction, while government-mandated sticky defaults still hide lawful support speech from minors who never discover or dare the prompt. Side B’s narrower “no deletion” definition and status-quo comparison did not neutralize that structural censorship cost or the displacement problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal, which separated corporate curation from a state-written opt-in rule and showed why sticky defaults plus exempt-app displacement defeat both prongs at once.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by keeping the motion’s dual burden central: A established a plausible reduction in unwanted contact, but its claimed harm reduction rested heavily on unsupported platform-rollout results and contact proxies. B also gave the more developed account of how sticky, state-mandated defaults could suppress lawful support communities without deleting posts. A’s status-quo double-standard argument and contextual opt-in prompt were strong responses, but did not fully answer the undiscovered-prompt problem or independently establish net harm reduction across platforms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions, distinguished marginal social migration from deliberate risk-seeking, and turned A’s proposed independent outcome tracking into an admission that existing contact drops did not yet prove the motion’s harm claim.
