### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined, avoided fake precision, and used the A1:/A2: format correctly. PRO drew a clear line: banning inferred emotional profiles as a targeting criterion while allowing ordinary demographic targeting. It answered both pressure questions directly and landed a sharp closing on CON's admission that a truthful appeal selected by a "grief-prone" score should not be stopped. CON built the more resilient comparative case, though. Its asymmetry point carried through every speech: the ban targets a selection method rather than manipulation, so it blocks truthful reassurance while leaving broadcast fear appeals lawful. PRO's reply that existing deception rules cover broadcasts was turned back on it: then the motion adds nothing against manipulation and only removes one channel. CON also exploited PRO's extension to "proxy variables documented as emotional predictors" to show the boundary blurring. It pressed PRO to name the independent wrong in selection itself, and PRO kept asserting exploitative intent ("easier to move") rather than proving it. CON's late turn was also effective: PRO's own fix of targeting statements and audits shows that disclosure-based regulation can address the secrecy harm without a prohibition. PRO's dignity and consent harm remained live but was partly answered by CON's collection limits. The result is a competitive but real edge to CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.5–B5.2 sequence was decisive. Taking PRO's own A4.3 frame of holding deception rules constant, CON showed that the motion adds nothing against broadcast manipulation and only removes a selection method. CON then argued that PRO's proposed audits and targeting statements prove the secrecy harm can be regulated rather than banned. This narrowed PRO's remaining case to the bare claim that emotion-based selection is inherently wrong.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): A wins the central justification clash: B repeatedly equated inferred emotional selection with exploitation without adequately establishing why truthful, relevant targeting remains impermissible under disclosure, correction, opt-outs, and data-use restrictions. B effectively defended ordinary targeting alternatives and explained why broadcast manipulation does not invalidate a narrower prohibition. But A more clearly exposed the prohibition’s overbreadth and challenged B to establish an independent harm beyond secrecy and invasive data practices. That burden remained insufficiently answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A turned B’s proposed targeting statements and audits against its claim that emotional profiling cannot be made visible or contestable. B then treated A’s willingness to permit a truthful “grief-prone” appeal as a concession; A correctly answered that whether such selection is inherently exploitative was precisely the disputed premise.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the decisive clash clearer: whether selection by inferred emotional vulnerability is a distinct democratic harm that transparency and content rules cannot cure. Side A pressed the overbreadth and workability points effectively, but B gave a workable line based on targeting basis, affect scores, proxies, and campaign-side documentation, and used A’s own answer about truthful grief-prone targeting to show that A’s regulatory package did not stop the exact practice at issue.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 and closing turn on A’s grief-prone score answer: B argued that if truthful emotional targeting based on an inferred grief score is not stopped by deception rules, that exposes the precise harm the ban targets.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): PRO wins because it turned CON's best line-drawing attack into a symmetry argument. CON's alternative of audits plus limits on sensitive inferences needs the same records and model inspection it says make PRO's ban unworkable. In A1/A2, PRO gave a concrete categorical line: conduct-based turnout propensity scores stay permissible, while inferred mental states such as civic efficacy, shame, or anxiety are prohibited. PRO also bit the bullet on low-efficacy GOTV and named alternative outreach channels. CON's strongest material was the vagueness and overbreadth critique, along with the challenger/megaphone point. Both lost force once PRO left conduct-based voter-by-voter targeting lawful. In Rebuttal 2, CON conceded that fear, grief, shame, and distress inferences could be banned outright. That narrowed the gap between the sides and let PRO argue that CON's purpose-and-use test is the harder, after-the-fact judgment. CON's closing misdescribed PRO's test as sweeping in turnout-propensity models, which PRO had explicitly excluded. That weakened CON's final crystallization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 and A4.4 were decisive. They pressed that CON's audit-and-restrict alternative depends on the same records it calls unworkable for a ban. PRO then argued that CON's "exploitation" test requires a harder judgment than PRO's method-based test. CON's A1 conceded that the same records support narrow restrictions and never showed a unique administrative advantage for its alternative.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): B wins by answering the enforcement pressure directly and distinguishing psychological profiling from conduct-based targeting and responsive canvassing. A’s benign civic-efficacy example raises a genuine overbreadth concern, but its participation argument repeatedly treats the prohibition as eliminating individualized outreach despite B expressly preserving alternatives. B also establishes a comparative advantage: A’s proposed restrictions share the audit burden while requiring an additional judgment about exploitation. Some classification uncertainty remains, keeping this competitive, but A never sufficiently establishes that the ban’s incremental costs outweigh that protection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal turns A’s audit alternative against its enforcement objection: both policies inspect targeting records, while restricting only exploitative uses introduces another contested threshold. A distinguishes narrow from broad restrictions but does not adequately answer that additional enforcement burden.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the central clash clearer: whether the state should ban a covert method of psychological sorting before persuasion begins, or rely on narrower after-the-fact rules against exploitation. B gave a workable core distinction between conduct-based turnout targeting and inferred mental or emotional profiling, answered the pressure questions directly, and turned A’s acceptance of audits and sensitive-inference limits into support for enforceable prohibition. A raised credible line-drawing and challenger-outreach concerns, but never fully answered why a purpose-and-use rule would catch invisible psychological targeting more effectively than B’s method ban.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttal 2 was decisive: it answered the turnout-propensity and relabeling questions, conceded only borderline ambiguity while preserving a clear core, and showed that A’s own audit-based alternative required the same evidentiary machinery B’s prohibition would use.
