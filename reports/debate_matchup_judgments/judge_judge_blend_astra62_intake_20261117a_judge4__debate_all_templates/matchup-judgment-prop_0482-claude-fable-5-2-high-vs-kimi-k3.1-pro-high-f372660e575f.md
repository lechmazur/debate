### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined and grounded, but Side A controlled the decisive clash. Side A's core asymmetry — that CON's entire remedy (mandated manuals, parts access, repair licences) is openness extracted by negotiation from rights holders who can walk away, while open design makes repair and supply the default — was never squarely neutralized. Side B's answer that "an open workshop can also refuse an unprofitable order" was directly answered in A's closing: when the patent holder refuses, no one else may build; when one open producer refuses, anyone qualified may step in. Side B's best remaining ground (fragmentation, certification limits, "legal possibility is not clinical capacity") stayed live but speculative, and A's weak-dominance framing ("if only one maker is viable, only one enters, and we are no worse off") blunted it. Side A also dealt with the pressure questions more decisively, directly answering who finances manufacturers and how lives-saved is measured, whereas Side B's A1/A2 answers leaned back on the very leverage argument A had already undermined. Execution cost for Side A: three clipped turns (opening, both rebuttals), which blunted some flow but never broke the argument spine. Side B was cleaner on format and epistemically careful throughout, which keeps this clear but competitive rather than a rout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing turn on B's concession that "removing repair lock-in captures the graveyard benefit." Once B admitted the graveyard's lifesaving value lies in openness, A reduced the debate to how a poor system obtains that openness: by permission from firms with no incentive, or by default. B's reply that qualification requires real capacity, not legal possibility, was its strongest counter, but it left B defending a pipeline whose own fixes depend on leverage B admitted is "sometimes absent."
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins a sharp, competitive debate through superior institutional symmetry analysis, stronger medical-device regulatory mechanics, and cleaner execution (Side B had three speeches clipped at the word limit). Side B built persuasive pressure around the "equipment graveyard" and argued that Side A's procurement remedies still depend on permission from disinterested IP holders. However, once Side B relied on ministry/pooled tenders to fund tooling and strict regulatory certification to filter unsafe output, Side A exposed the core flaw: where thin markets cannot fund support, free files create no qualified producers, and where funded tenders exist, buyers can secure manuals, parts, and repair rights without surrendering scale economies, validated processes, and unified recall accountability. Side A also convincingly defeated Side B's claim that open entry is costless, showing that thin markets either support no added suppliers or split scarce budgets across duplicated validation and compliance costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B4.2]-[B4.4] and [B5.4], Side B argued that openness "adds suppliers... and removes none," allows another shop to make parts "on Monday" if a producer fails, and self-corrects against fragmentation. Side A dismantled both claims in [A4.5]-[A4.6] and [A5.3]-[A5.4]: medical certification attaches to a defined manufacturer and process rather than parts fabricated from a public repo ("A repository survives a firm's exit; a warranty, trained technician and traceable production system do not"), and thin markets face a two-horned dilemma between single-supplier fragility and budget-draining duplication of fixed compliance costs.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by holding the motion to more safe device-years, not cheaper files or repair slogans. A separated repair access from unvalidated cloning, showed free designs do not create technicians, validation, or solvent service, and blocked B's attempt to treat repair reforms as a concession. B landed the sharper hit that IP support often depends on rights-holders who can walk away, but overreached on that concession and left three major turns clipped.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal and closing refused to let "removing repair lock-in" concede open manufacture, then weighed thin-market duplication against B's claim that openness only adds suppliers.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B won the decisive clashes. B's pressure question demanding a single scaled open-source device forced A into a candid concession ("I cannot responsibly name one"), and B leveraged it repeatedly as proof that the "would save more lives" burden was unmet. B also shrank A's case by mapping its concessions: local adaptation withdrawn, same manufacturers and costs conceded, funding resting on donors A itself called unreliable. A's best surviving route—continuity of service and "device-years per dollar"—was real but blunted by B's design-escrow counter showing the same hedge is purchasable inside the IP system, plus the unanswered cost of re-qualifying a second manufacturer. A answered pressure honestly and with good epistemic discipline, and its "insurance policy is lifesaving when the asset is oxygen equipment" reframe in the closing was its best moment, but it never resolved who pays to exercise the option. B's three clipped turns are a modest execution cost, yet the visible argumentation consistently created and pressed comparative pressure. B made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal [B4.1]-[B4.8]: answering the "what does secrecy add" question with "exclusivity pays for the expensive part of a device's life," then cataloguing A's concessions (no scaled example, adaptation withdrawn, same manufacturers, unreliable donors) reduced the open case to a purchasable hedge—matched only by A's honest A1/A2 answers and its continuity reframe in [A4.3]-[A4.4].
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won solidly by proving that factory-specific regulatory validation, manufacturing scale, financing, and accountable service—not access to design files—are the binding constraints on saving lives in low-income health systems. Side B argued thoughtfully, drew a valid distinction between off-patent principles and open production documentation, and showed exemplary epistemic discipline in [B4.1] by refusing to invent a scaled open-source success story under pressure. However, Side A's relentless institutional analysis forced Side B to concede both local part-swapping adaptation (which voids validation) and cheaper small-batch production (since high-volume manufacturers already hold scale). That narrowed Side B's entire case to preserving continuity when a vendor exits, which Side A cleanly neutralized by pointing to third-party design escrow clauses, the prohibitive cost of re-tooling and factory re-clearance for small discontinued fleets, and B's own claim that donor funding is unreliable. Side A takes a small execution penalty for minor end-of-sentence clipping on three turns, tempering an otherwise dominant substantive performance into a solid win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A4.1]-[A4.8], Side A directly answered Side B's pressure questions—clarifying that patents provide exclusivity to fund certification and recall liability rather than secrecy—deployed design escrow and re-tooling/re-clearance economics to neutralize B's vendor-exit argument, and cataloged B's own retreats on adaptation, production cost, and donor reliability.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A won by forcing the comparison down to a continuity hedge and then showing that hedge does not establish the motion. PRO never named a scaled open device that outperformed generics or proprietary products, conceded away local adaptation and unique cost advantages, and left second-source continuity dependent on the same scarce, unreliable funding it had criticized. CON's escrow, service-contract, and delivery-before-continuity replies left that route weaker than the existing pipeline's record of actually putting validated devices at bedsides. Clipped turns were an execution cost for A, but the visible concessions and closing still made the vote clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal, which extracted PRO's no-example admission and collapsed adaptation, cost, and funding claims into an insurance policy already available through procurement clauses.
