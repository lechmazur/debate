### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins because its central test was content mismatch, and it was never squarely answered. A braider is tested on cuts, chemicals and coloring she does not perform. A designer needs a degree and apprenticeship to give advice that codes and licensed trades already govern. That surplus, written by incumbent boards, is what makes the license primarily a fence. CON scored real points. Calling the state-to-state comparison an ecological fallacy was sharp. Its account of diffuse, untracked harms was plausible. PRO then turned those points well. It argued that CON's framing makes the safeguard claim unfalsifiable, since quiet states count as hidden harm and any mandatory module counts as a license. CON's falsification answer set a measured-divergence bar its own side also never met. CON's "refuse or refer" judgment line was its best late answer. It still never explained why that judgment requires the full cosmetology curriculum. By calling board capture and grandfathering "calibration error," CON effectively conceded that the exclusionary features exist. That left PRO's narrow-rule-plus-surplus framing standing on the word "primarily."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answered CON's question about which credentialing feature distinguishes incumbent protection. PRO said the marker is not cost but a gate whose tested content does not match the hazard, written by people who already hold it. It then showed that CON's "untracked harm" and "any mandatory rule is a license" moves together make the safeguard claim impossible to fail. This exchange shifted the burden decisively, and CON's closing claim that PRO was "subtracting first" restated its position rather than repairing it.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won solidly by proving that the defining features of licensing in these fields track incumbent exclusion rather than actual public-safety risk. Side B mounted a clever mid-debate pushback—arguing that state-line comparisons ignore confounding variables and diffuse harms, and reframing Side A's openness to short sanitation rules as a dispute over "scope" or "calibration error" rather than purpose. However, Side A decisively dismantled both moves in the second half. First, Side A showed that when narrow sanitation rules, permits, inspections, and building-trade licenses already address the nameable hazards, the massive remaining credential—forcing braiders to train in cuts, coloring, and chemicals they never use, policing interior-design job titles and layout advice, and licensing florists or threaders (which Side B dropped entirely)—is exclusionary surplus. Second, Side A identified structural hallmarks of incumbent capture—practitioner-dominated boards, grandfathering insiders, and blocking out-of-state practitioners with clean records—that cannot be brushed aside as incidental calibration errors. Side B also stumbled in closing [B5.3] by misattributing its own onerous falsification test [B4.1] to Side A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A4.1]-[A4.2], answering Side B's sharp pressure question [B3.1] on what separates incumbent protection from ordinary verification besides cost, Side A pinpointed unrelated exam content (forcing braiders to learn cuts, chemicals, and coloring), practitioner-controlled boards, grandfathering insiders, and denying interstate reciprocity—cleanly defeating Side B's attempt to dismiss licensing barriers as mere calibration scope.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A made the proposition easier to decide by tying “primarily” to mismatch between real risks and imposed credentials, incumbent-controlled boards, and the availability of narrower rules. Side B’s confounding and hidden-harm points dented the state-line comparison, but they never explained why the surplus credentialing was mainly safety rather than exclusion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The strongest exchange was A’s pressure on falsifiability and B’s answer requiring a measured, controlled harm divergence. B made the map less decisive, but A then turned B’s own concession into the key weighing point: if narrow rules cover the nameable risks, the remaining long credential looks like a barrier.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO began with a strong, intuitive case. Its central evidence was the mismatch between risk and requirement: hundreds of cosmetology hours for braiders, plus insider control of the boards. CON did the more effective adaptive work. It conceded that surplus hours exist and that schools and incumbents gain from them. It then argued that overbreadth is a calibration flaw rather than proof of primary purpose. It also neutralized the board-capture point by noting that PRO accepts the same practitioner-board structure for engineers and electricians. Its most damaging move was showing that PRO's own preferred alternative, a narrow sanitation certificate, is still a pre-service license aimed at the same harm. That concedes the safeguard function and leaves only a dispute about its width. CON answered both pressure questions directly. PRO's A1 shifted the motion toward a least-restrictive-means test, and CON correctly flagged that "primarily" does not mean "least restrictive." PRO's later speeches leaned heavily on repetition: the "business model" line appears in both its first rebuttal and its closing. PRO also kept asserting that the license "excludes far more than it safeguards" without new support. On the merits, the motion is fairly PRO-friendly, and CON's concession on surplus hours gave PRO some leverage. But PRO never cleanly converted that concession into a demonstration that exclusion dominates the safety function. That makes CON the clearer, though not dominant, winner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's A4.4–A4.6 reply to PRO's A2. PRO's evidence of protectionism rested on the refusal to issue narrow, risk-matched certificates. CON answered that a short sanitation certificate is still licensing aimed at the same harm, so PRO's alternative concedes the safeguard and disputes only its width. PRO never answered this; its closing restated the bundling critique instead.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B won a competitive, well-argued debate by neutralizing Side A's non-licensing alternatives and framing the burden of "primarily" much more accurately. Side B established concrete, unseen pre-service hazards in both hair braiding and interior design, then systematically proved why ex post liability, reviews, and building permits fail—most notably that permits never review unpermitted room refreshes and lawsuits arrive after irreversible harm while failing against judgment-proof practitioners. Side A scored real points pressing the bloat of unreformed cosmetology hours in force, but dropped Side B's rebuttals on unpermitted interior work, judgment-proof defendants, and practitioner seats on legitimate engineering/electrical boards, while retreating to a narrow sanitation certificate that Side B shrewdly exposed as conceding the need for an ex ante license.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B2.5-B2.8] and [B4.3-B4.6], Side B dismantled Side A's claim that permits, liability, and narrow certificates make licensing redundant—showing permits miss unpermitted interior refreshes, tort remedies fail low-income clients facing judgment-proof practitioners after first-contact injury, and Side A's own proposed sanitation certificate [A4.2] is itself a narrower license that concedes the safety screen and disputes only its width.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): B won by making the decisive clash clearer: it distinguished overbreadth from primary protectionist purpose, gave plausible pre-service safety harms that reputation, permits, and liability do not fully address, and showed that A’s narrower-sanitation-certificate alternative is still a safety screen. A had memorable rhetoric and a strong point about actual overbroad requirements, but leaned too heavily on least-restrictive-means reasoning and did not fully prove that incumbent protection was the primary function rather than an overbroad calibration problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttal 2 was most decisive when it treated A’s sanitation-certificate alternative as a concession that competence screening is still licensing, while arguing that permits and liability arrive too late or miss much of the relevant work.
