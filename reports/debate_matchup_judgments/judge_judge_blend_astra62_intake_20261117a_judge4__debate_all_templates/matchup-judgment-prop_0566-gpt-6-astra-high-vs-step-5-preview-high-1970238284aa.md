### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO built a clean causal story: a preference only matters when it changes outcomes, and when it does, another qualified applicant loses a place. Against that definite cost, PRO set a contingent and unquantified financial benefit. PRO kept pressing this comparison and answered CON's burden-of-proof challenge directly by conceding uncertainty while explaining why it does not justify routine inherited advantage. CON's best lines were the "record, not surname" reframe and the parallel to athletics, geography, and full-pay capacity. PRO neutralized the first with "whose record?" and made the decisive move with Q1, which forced CON to specify a ceiling. CON's answer allowed no point boost, no weighting, no rescue below threshold, only an audited tie-breaker among substantially indistinguishable candidates. That answer effectively conceded a sharp limit. CON's closing then claimed its proposal "keeps" routine legacy boosts, contradicting its own A1 answer. CON also mislabeled references (P2.x) and misstated that PRO dropped the "state-mandated purity" point. CON's burden-shifting on giving was reasonable but never quantified, so it did not overcome the displacement argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 asked CON to name a concrete ceiling on its "bounded preference." CON's answer was a narrow, audited, no-boost tie-breaker. PRO's closing then argued that this proposal is itself a sharp limit, leaving only a residual exception in dispute. That exchange collapsed CON's opposition to the motion as written, and CON's "word game" reply contradicted its own stated ceiling.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B wins because it kept a clearer decision rule: a preference that changes who gets a scarce place imposes a definite fairness cost, while the claimed fundraising return stayed contingent and unproven. Side A’s stewardship case was coherent, but its best defense retreated to an audited, no-points, indistinguishable-only tie-breaker. Side B showed that this ceiling is itself a sharp limit, not a reason to reject the motion, and that a parent’s giving is not the applicant’s achievement. Side A never established that relational generosity requires inherited priority, and its closing mostly relabeled that concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Q1 and closing were decisive: Side A’s concrete ceiling—no boost, quota, weighting, or below-threshold rescue, only an audited tie among indistinguishable finalists—was the sharp limit the motion requires, so defending that exception could not justify voting against substantial restriction.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the debate centered on the fairness cost of inherited or donor-linked advantage and by handling the financial pressure with a clean comparison: a definite distortion in admissions versus an unproven, contingent fundraising benefit. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): PRO opened with a vivid moral frame: seats should not be sold, and lineage displaces merit. Its later "door is not for sale" and "toll on the admission line" lines were memorable. But several of its load-bearing claims got weaker as the debate went on. - **Degree discount:** CON showed that PRO's claim that "every degree" is discounted conflates admission with graduation. PRO conceded in A2 that the degree still certifies completion. - **Inconsistent example:** PRO's "better-qualified stranger" language contradicted its own identical-applicant example. - **Hypothetical:** When asked to assume a preference demonstrably expanded low-income access, PRO declined the premise and said it would still "curtail the exchange itself." CON exploited this answer effectively. CON's case was more disciplined. It framed its scholarship example as explicitly conditional and conceded where PRO was right. It then turned that concession into a positive standard: scrutiny and accountability. It answered both pressure questions directly with a falsifiable, policy-level test, rather than claiming dollar-by-dollar tracing. PRO's best counter was that the counterfactual cannot be verified and audits become self-justifying. That was a real point, but CON squarely answered it by proposing policy-change comparisons, aid audits, and enrollment checks. PRO never clearly argued that CON's accountability regime is effectively a weaker standard than the motion demands, even though that was its strongest available line. Overall, CON made the decisive comparison clearer: should a categorical rule override demonstrated access benefits? PRO's strongest moral principle survived, but in a narrower form.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's B4.4 and B5.1. CON took PRO's A1 answer, which would still curtail preferences even after additional low-income access was proven, and showed that PRO's rule sacrifices demonstrated benefits for the sake of mechanism purity. PRO's closing reasserted that the counterfactual cannot be verified but never squarely addressed that concession.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won a competitive debate by keeping the access tradeoff live. Asked to assume a preference truly expanded low-income enrollment, Side B still insisted on curtailing the exchange and never showed that naming rights or other gifts would replace that marginal support. A's scrutiny rule—test aid, enrollment, and realistic alternatives, then drop the preference if the justification fails—answered the pressure more directly than B's unverifiability claim, which partly evaded the hypothetical. B's integrity principle and cleaner slogans remained forceful, but they did not fully weigh the educational opportunities a sharp categorical limit would cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal: even if additional funding and expanded access were proved, B would still "curtail the exchange itself," and calling alternative fundraising tools available does not show they are equivalent.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by making the decisive clash about real educational opportunity rather than only the purity of the admissions signal. It offered a plausible, accountable decision rule for when preferences might be justified, answered pressure questions directly, and exposed that Side B would prohibit even a proven access-expanding preference. Side B was more rhetorically memorable and kept the fairness harm live, but its claims that the counterfactual is wholly unverifiable and that every audit would be self-justifying were too sweeping to neutralize A’s practical tradeoff.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal gave a concrete policy-level evidentiary test and then turned Side B’s own answer against it: even if additional funding expanded low-income access, PRO would still ban the exchange, making fairness of process outweigh actual opportunity.
