- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): A built the clearer normative and practical case: safeguards should follow consequential EU decisions, while responsibility among authors, modifiers, suppliers, and deployers can still vary with knowledge and control. ### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B wins because it converted the debate from abstract jurisdiction to practical leverage. A's impact-based rule was principled, but B showed direct sales are already regulated by market entry and that a vanished supplier is not made reachable by a patient's location. The cross-border referral example remained decisive: A could not show how a domestic-only developer would get advance notice or how an impact rule would prevent first harm, while B's requirement that the French hospital secure validation before relying on the opinion gives a pre-use safeguard. A's rebuttals repeated the same provider-control point and did not fully answer the pressure questions, especially why impact adds assets or notice that the direct sale did not already provide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response that a direct sale is market entry even when unlawful, and that if the vendor has no EU assets the patient's location adds none, was the decisive exchange because it undercut A's vanishing-vendor loophole and shifted the focus to who Europe can actually reach before harm.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won decisively by isolating where impact-based jurisdiction actually diverges from combining headquarters supervision with market-entry and deployer rules, then winning both resulting clashes. On direct cross-border sales, Side B showed that market-entry rules give Europe ex-ante leverage at the purchase order via mandatory local representatives, whereas impact-based jurisdiction adds zero enforcement reach if an online vendor vanishes without EU assets. On indirect impacts—illustrated by an overseas specialist using domestic software on a referred French scan—Side A first misread the scenario twice and then conceded in [A4.1] that purely domestic foreign developers must comply with EU rules without advance notice, allowing Side B to show that only clinical reliance checks by the referring EU hospital can protect the patient before treatment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's "vanishing online vendor" argument across [B2.1]-[B2.2], [B3.2], and [B4.1]-[B4.2]: Side B pointed out that declaring a vanished vendor subject to EU law based on patient location does not materialize EU assets that a direct sale did not already provide, whereas conditioning clinical use on a local representative stops unvetted tools before the first scan and keeps the non-compliant clinic accountable in France.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B built the stronger preventive and enforceability case. A’s direct-sale and vanishing-vendor examples were largely answered by B’s market-entry conditions, while A never squarely solved the domestic-only developer’s lack of advance notice in the referral case. A also repeatedly treated B’s hybrid alternative as headquarters-only regulation and overstated the hospital’s documentation burden. B consistently distinguished direct sales from unforeseen referrals and explained who could require safeguards before the first patient was harmed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal decisively answered both pressure points: a direct sale already supplies market-entry jurisdiction and whatever asset reach exists, while marketing to EU hospitals confirms notice under B’s rule. The two-scan referral example then exposed why impact alone cannot tell a domestic-only developer which validation regime applies before deployment.
