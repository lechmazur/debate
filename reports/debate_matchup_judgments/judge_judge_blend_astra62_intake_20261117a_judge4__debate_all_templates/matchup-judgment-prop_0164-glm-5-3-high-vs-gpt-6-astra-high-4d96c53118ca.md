### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): PRO built a vivid case that replication is under-supplied because it is a collective good that individual researchers have no incentive to provide, and that discretion has failed. Its strongest later line was the asymmetry argument: quota failures are visible, while discretionary failures are silent. CON, however, kept the debate on the comparative question the motion actually poses: why a fixed share, rather than replication funding as such. CON offered a concrete binding alternative: validation must be funded before any dependent follow-on investment proceeds. That answered PRO's downstream-waste mechanism without a percentage. CON also showed that both schemes still rely on reviewer judgment to separate consequential checks from trivial ones. PRO's answer to CON's Q1 conceded that reserved money stays locked even when the remaining replications offer less value, and CON pressed this effectively. PRO justified the concession only by asserting one-directional bias, not by showing the floor buys more value. PRO's closing misdescribed CON's rule as an obligation that "never spends money," which CON had already explicitly rejected. PRO's opening was also clipped for length, and it leaned on overbroad claims such as "every mature accountability institution." CON was cleaner on burden and comparison, so CON takes a clear but competitive win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came in Rebuttal 2. Answering PRO's Q1, CON turned "who decides what is load-bearing" around with a documented dependency test, creating the very paper trail PRO said could not exist. CON then noted that PRO's quota equally depends on reviewers judging whether funded replications matter. That neutralized PRO's visible-versus-silent failure asymmetry, while PRO's own A1 conceded that money stays locked away even from higher-value novel projects.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B wins a clear but competitive decision. A built a strong case that replication is structurally underfunded and that a visible floor beats quiet discretion. But B pressed the decisive cost: a fixed percentage tracks budget size, not scientific risk, and A conceded reserved money stays locked even when a marginal novel project offers greater expected benefit after accounting for error costs. A retreated to broad distrust of reviewer judgment, while B showed A’s own scheme still depends on reviewer judgment to select consequential replications. B’s binding dependency rule—no adequate check, no follow-on funding—directly addressed A’s downstream-waste concern without arbitrary rigidity, and B partly neutralized the visibility asymmetry by noting expenditure is not evidence that an important uncertainty was resolved. That left B with the cleaner, more efficient route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure question and follow-through were decisive: B asked whether reserved money must remain unavailable when marginal replication has lower expected benefit, and A conceded it must. B then turned that into the fixed share’s distinctive cost, arguing that novelty bias justifies enforceable checks, not an arbitrary percentage.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): PRO showed replication is structurally underfunded, but CON preserved a binding alternative: fund mandatory validation of load-bearing claims before costly follow-ons. PRO never justified fixing the amount independently of the value and cost of needed checks, and conceded the money stays locked even when a novel project offers greater expected benefit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's first pressure question forced PRO to accept that reserved funds remain unavailable even when further replication is less valuable than displaced discovery; CON then converted that concession into the decisive distinction between guaranteeing spending and guaranteeing consequential protection.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): PRO built its case on an incentive asymmetry. Replication's benefits are diffuse, while a novel proposal has a visible claim on the budget, so discretion systematically underfunds verification. That route survived the whole debate. CON had the sharper rhetoric. Its "quota breeds box-ticking" line, the convergence charge, and the hoard/waste/abandon trilemma were vivid. However, its key move rested on an assertion: that discretionary commissioning would fund "every replication the fixed share would fund." PRO's Q1 pressed exactly that assumption. CON's answer was that an agency-level standing commitment would protect commissioning. This undercuts CON's own position, because a durable, institutionally protected replication program is close to a reserved share. CON never showed why it would be more resilient than an explicit budget allocation. PRO's closing correctly noted that cutting an explicit reserve is costlier than quietly letting discretionary commissions lapse. CON's "concession" framing in B2.1 overreached. A funder-wide fixed share that is targeted across disciplines is still a fixed share, and CON itself conceded the point's formal coherence in A2. PRO answered both pressure questions directly. It chose carryover with the funds still ring-fenced, and argued that new research keeps regenerating the replication queue. PRO also openly accepted the opportunity cost. CON's trilemma lands only if worthwhile checks are frequently exhausted, which CON asserted rather than established. CON's second rebuttal was clipped for exceeding the word limit, which is an execution cost. On balance, PRO wins narrowly but clearly on substance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over protection. PRO's Q1 and A4.5 asked what actually shields discretionary replication funding from novelty-driven panels. CON replied (B4.1) with an "agency-level standing commitment." That answer implicitly concedes the need for a protected institutional allocation, and PRO's closing exploited the gap: an explicit budget commitment is harder to erode than discretionary goodwill.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Even accounting for the clipped rebuttal, B won narrowly. PRO's protection rationale was strong, but CON's parity attack—a fixed share needs the same institutional commitment as targeted commissioning—undercut its necessity. PRO's carryover answer avoided low-value spending but opened the deadweight trap: hoarded funds, wasteful spending, or abandoned protection. B's flexible triage alternative remained the cleaner mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal that the quota presupposes the same agency-level commitment needed for discretionary commissioning, then adds only a rigid spending obligation, was decisive. PRO's carryover answer sharpened it into the "hoarded, wasted, or abandoned" trilemma.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): PRO won the central clash over mechanism: discretionary commissioning assumes replication funding survives novelty-biased competition despite dispersed benefits, while an explicit fixed share changes the default. PRO's carryover answer and regenerating queue substantially blunted CON's thin-queue and forced-spending dilemma, and PRO distinguished silent erosion of discretion from repealing an explicit reserve. CON's rigidity and idle-funds costs remained real but less decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: A1's ring-fenced carryover answered the forced waste objection without abandoning the fixed share, and A2's regenerating queue answered the finite-backlog challenge.
