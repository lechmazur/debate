### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins a competitive debate. It built a clear causal story: live matching puts unverified algorithmic hits straight onto the street, while post-incident searches leave time for corroboration and judicial oversight. CON (A) had real strengths. Its emergency-use argument ("let the kidnapping happen first") was memorable. Its one-to-many point was its best line: a warrant particularizes the probe image, not the searched population. That point was never fully neutralized. PRO's reply that a warrant limits police to a "static reference database" partly assumes limits the motion does not specify. However, CON's position ran into a contradiction that PRO exploited cleanly. CON condemns warrant-backed one-to-many matching as mass surveillance while defending warrantless live crowd scanning. CON's retention/duration answer was reasonable but came late and only partially defused this. On verification, CON conceded that officers cannot reliably confirm a match in seconds. That made PRO's time-buffer argument the more intuitive weighing. CON's alternative ("scope, minimization, retention") stayed vague throughout. CON's first rebuttal was also clipped for length, an execution cost. Both sides cited nonexistent C-prefixed reference IDs, so that issue roughly cancels out. Overall, PRO made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B4.3/B5.2 contradiction turn decided the round. It argued that if one-to-many matching under a judge's eye is too intrusive, then CON's warrantless live one-to-many scanning of transit crowds is worse. Combined with CON's concession that live hits can't be reliably verified in seconds, this undercut CON's core emergency carve-out. CON's best counter was A2.5/A5.1: the warrant reviews the probe, not the searched population. That counter remained partly live but was outweighed.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A (PRO) won by making the timing line coherent and answering every major CON attack. A's core argument—that live matching necessarily biometrically measures every passerby while a warrant-backed post-incident search is gated by time, particularity, and corroboration—survived the exchange. A's pressure questions exposed a real contradiction: B condemns warrant-supervised one-to-many searches as dragnets while demanding warrantless live one-to-many crowd scanning, and B's answer (live alerts are narrow in purpose and duration, create no lasting index) only partially resolved it, since the live scan still measures everyone present. B's best surviving route—the particularity asymmetry (a judge reviews the probe image, not the searched population, enabling retrospective sweeps)—was genuinely strong and original, but A's reply that a particularized warrant limits the probe image and forbids indexing neighborhood CCTV was a plausible, legally grounded answer, and B's alternative regime of "scope, minimization, retention" stayed vague. B also carried execution costs: a clipped Rebuttal 1 and the weaker side of the verification asymmetry (trusting seconds-long visual confirmation during a crisis while distrusting days of detective corroboration). Both sides used some nonexistent [C...] reference IDs, roughly symmetric. A gave the cleaner reason to vote; B was original and rhetorically sharp but defended the harder position without fully neutralizing the live-dragnet problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: answering B's pressure questions directly (A1: raw footage is not biometric processing; A2: identifying a suspect via a specific probe image and static database is not tracking the public), then landing the contradiction—B condemns one-to-many matching under a judge's warrant while demanding warrantless one-to-many live crowd scanning—which B never fully escaped, and which A crystallized again in the closing.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): A won by framing the cleaner comparative safeguard: judicial oversight and time for corroboration versus warrantless, time-pressured live matching. B's probe-versus-population warrant critique was the strongest counterweight.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins because it pressed two routes that remained live through the closing. First, the emergency-exception route. CON used the child-abduction and active-shooter examples to argue that a narrow, one-face, life-threatening carve-out avoids the dragnet harms PRO describes. PRO's reply was that the architecture still scans every passerby and that watchlists will creep. That is a coherent point, but it rests mainly on a slippery-slope prediction. PRO's B4.5 line, "even if emergency real-time matching could save a life," then handed CON a concession it exploited effectively. Second, the warrant route. PRO's B1.3 framed post-incident warrants as tied to "a named suspect." PRO later retreated to a gallery-search warrant and then denied it had ever made the named-suspect claim. CON caught the shift. PRO's fingerprint analogy also backfired: CON showed that comparable database lookups normally need no warrant, which makes PRO's warrant requirement look like delay rather than meaningful protection. PRO's answer, that face recognition can "scan strangers as they walk," does not apply to post-incident mugshot searches, a point CON landed cleanly. CON's contradiction argument tightened the case. If courts can police post-incident gallery creep, why can't they police real-time emergency watchlists? PRO's closing distinction was that post-incident searches are anchored and real-time ones are vague. CON plausibly answered that an abduction also involves a specific crime, a recorded image, and a one-face gallery. PRO stayed thematically consistent and its "panopticon vs probe" framing was memorable. However, it was more reactive in the later rounds and never fully neutralized the life-saving impact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's A4.3 and A5.2 against PRO's B4.1, B4.4, and B5.3. CON exposed PRO's asymmetric trust in legal guardrails: they are powerless before a crime yet reliable after it. CON then showed that PRO's proposed distinction (recorded image, specific crime, defined gallery) also applies to a live abduction search. This left PRO's ban on emergency use resting on a slippery-slope claim plus a conceded cost in lives.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): An exceptionally close, high-quality debate. A landed the best single analytical move of the round: dissolving CON's "warrant Catch-22" by showing a warrant can authorize a narrow gallery search of a recorded crime-scene image without naming a suspect, and A's closing scope-visibility distinction (recorded image, place, gallery vs. vague fast emergency authority) partially answered B's guardrail-symmetry attack. But B won the cumulative exchange: B demolished A's manual-alternative claim (no human team can scan dozens of rush-hour cameras, never answered), forced and exploited A's explicit concession that life-saving narrow matching would still be banned, offered more concrete mechanisms (ephemeral volatile-memory matching, hard-coded limits, wiretap-style sign-offs, command-center analyst verification, which A dropped after A2.5), and delivered the sharper final blow in B5.2 ("a crime has already occurred: a specific crime, a recorded image, and a gallery of one face"), leaving A's review asymmetry only partially intact. B also covered more of A's points than vice versa, and B's reframes (officer-with-a-photo analogy, tool-vs-abuse framing) were more original than A's standard panopticon framing. Razor-thin edge to B on coverage, unanswered pressure, and last effective strikes, despite A clearly winning the warrant-prong clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Two decisive exchanges: A2.3's dissolution of the warrant Catch-22 was the round's best single repair, but B's B4.3 contradiction charge ("if legal guardrails work post-incident, they can restrict real-time watchlists") — only partially met by A5.3 and then re-cut by B5.2's observation that emergencies are equally particularized — was the pressure A never fully escaped, especially combined with B2.3's unanswered scale rebuttal of manual monitoring.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B built the stronger path to victory by attacking both halves of the motion: narrow emergency searches need not become population identification, and a warrant for initial facial-lead generation may add delay without meaningful privacy protection. B also exposed A’s retreat from the “named suspect” formulation and the asymmetry in trusting courts post-incident but not during emergencies. A offered a memorable surveillance-versus-investigation frame, but relied too heavily on inevitable watchlist expansion and did not neutralize B’s targeted, ephemeral emergency alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal decisively pressed the legal-guardrails contradiction, and its closing sharpened the point: active abductions and shootings can already supply a specific crime, image, and one-face gallery, undermining A’s claim that only post-incident review can be sufficiently particularized.
