### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A (PRO) won by turning the debate's hardest clash — family consultation — into a concrete, grounded comparison between being heard and having to authorize. A's key move in Rebuttal 1 (if the patient's affirmative act were what permits recovery, no one would ask relatives under opt-in) undercut B's moral-inversion framing, and A answered both pressure questions directly with the required A1:/A2: structure. A's Q2 pressure was decisive: fewer registrations do not equal fewer completed donations, and B's rebuttal relied on a speculative compounding chain (registrations → conversations → trust → unreachable donors) that was asserted rather than supported. B also twice misattributed the "modest gains" concession to PRO when B introduced that word in its own opening. B's structural argument — that silence-as-consent redefines authorization and thus fails the ethical prong by itself — remained live and was well crystallized in the closing, but B leaned on speculative institutional-momentum mechanisms and assumed-away clinician conduct, while A offered specific bedside safeguards (refusals honored, doubt paused, opposition stops). Both sides argued the motion as written; A made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer to B's registration-erosion attack (A4.3-A4.4): a willing donor who no longer signs up has not withdrawn an organ from the pool, so cancelling the gain requires distrust to cost actual completed donations, a burden B never met beyond conjecture. Combined with A2.1's inversion point — opt-in also recovers organs without any patient authorization — this broke B's core ethical and fragility routes.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange exposed that opt-in already relies on relatives when the patient is unregistered, then separated being heard from having to authorize donation.
