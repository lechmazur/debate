### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by keeping the clash on economy-wide fiscal independence rather than whether individual enclaves can retain customers. A's Jebel Ali and Saudi pilgrimage examples were plausible and the 25% export benchmark was a useful metric, but A never showed those external customers were large enough, net of state subsidies, to cushion an oil downturn. B's mid-2010s stress test was the decisive evidence: non-oil shipping and pilgrimage continued, yet non-oil growth and private activity contracted with public spending. B also effectively answered the port-profits pressure by distinguishing gross fees from net returns after subsidized land, energy, infrastructure, and rival-hub competition. That left A proving endurance of some state-bought enclaves, not likely economy-wide diversification.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest rebuttal was its mid-2010s stress-test answer: if the showcased exporters were an independent core, the oil-price shock should have revealed them expanding into the gap, but instead non-oil activity contracted alongside the state.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won on motion fit. The proposition asks whether diversification is substantive and likely to succeed rather than mostly PR; B's demand-source test (a paying customer who returns regardless of oil budgets) directly answers that, supported by plausible mechanisms: Jebel Ali's sailing-network stickiness, Salalah transshipment, and pilgrim tourism where the visitor flow already exists. Side A (CON) argued skillfully but effectively swapped the motion for a stricter one — full fiscal replacement of oil — demanding net treasury revenue, citizen employment, and cushioning capacity before crediting anything. B exposed this pivot ("mistakes the destination for the test of progress") and A never fully justified why the stiffer bar is the one written. A's sharpest material — the net ledger of subsidies against fees, and the mid-2010s contraction — was met honestly: B conceded the downturn proved budgets were exposed while distinguishing "too small to offset" from "no cushion," and accepted A's accounting frame in closing ("count the freight fee, not the cargo, and charge the port for the capital it uses"). Both sides answered pressure questions directly with proper A1/A2 structure; B's yardstick (a quarter of non-hydrocarbon export earnings over a cycle) was cautiously framed rather than fake-precise. A's concession that the enclaves endure and survive shocks quietly undermines the "mostly PR" claim. B made the debate easier to decide on the motion as written; A won many exchanges but on a burden the proposition did not impose.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's pressure question demanding a measurable success threshold versus B's A1 response offering a concrete, cautious yardstick (one in four export dollars from non-hydrocarbon sources over a price cycle), which forced the dispute onto the motion's actual terms and left A arguing a harder, unwritten burden of full fiscal replacement.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A built the clearer path by separating recycled public spending from independent outside demand and showing that network effects, pilgrim stays, and port fees can support expansion without treating full fiscal replacement as the test. ### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the debate on the harder question of economic independence rather than project-level success. B conceded real examples—Dubai ports, Ma’aden, ACWA’s foreign concessions—but showed those did not establish that independent earnings were gaining ground on oil-funded demand across the economy. A had strong concrete examples and structural reforms, but its economy-wide indicators were undermined by B’s argument that construction, private credit, and tax receipts can all rise before a single airline pays a landing fee. B’s closing distinction between absorbing a one-time loss and renewing an ongoing subsidy made the PR-incentive case concrete and left A’s portfolio logic without the needed comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 was the decisive exchange: it granted the genuine successes but argued the missing comparison was between what winners earn and what the rest consume. The point that a state-funded airport can create borrowing, earnings, jobs, and tax receipts before it ever earns a landing fee neutralized A’s reliance on broad non-oil indicators.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by converting Side A's concessions into a decisive burden frame. A's opening hotel test and circular-demand analysis were the sharpest individual arguments in the debate, and A consistently won the micro-skirmishes on measurement (feedstock pricing, "surplus after costs," the missing winners-to-bills ratio). But B landed the structurally decisive moves: extracting A's admissions that Dubai's model, Ma'aden's exports, and ACWA's concessions are genuine successes, then arguing that once the mechanism is proven, the motion's "substantive and likely to succeed" standard is met by a portfolio logic — enough compounding winners, differentiated markets, and sovereign balance sheets to absorb losers. B also answered pressure questions more concretely (named listed champions with plausible operating characteristics, no fake precision) and gave a direct A2 with composition indicators. A's rebuttals were excellent — the closing distinction between absorbing a one-time loss and underwriting a recurring commitment was the single sharpest turn — but A ultimately offered skepticism about the aggregate ratio rather than affirmative evidence it is deteriorating, leaving B's "likely to succeed" route intact. Both sides followed format, engaged honestly, and narrowed rather than widened the clash; B simply made the debate easier to decide on the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2: answering A1/A2 with Ma'aden and ACWA, then levering A's concessions (Dubai [A1.5], real reforms [A2.4], genuine exporters [A4.3]) into the reframe that the motion asks about likelihood of success, not proof the state can exit everywhere. A's counter — that absorbing a loss is not the same as underwriting a continuing commitment [A5.1] — was the sharpest reply of the debate but could not dislodge B's portfolio framing.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by holding a tighter test of independence: external receipts must cover full costs without recurring state support, and winners must be weighed against oil-linked demand and showcase projects governments have incentives to keep open.
