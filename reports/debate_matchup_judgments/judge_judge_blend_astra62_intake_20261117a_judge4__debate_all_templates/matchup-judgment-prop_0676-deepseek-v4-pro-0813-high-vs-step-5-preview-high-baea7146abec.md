### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO wins because it secured the policy core of the motion, and CON's own answers conceded most of it. PRO framed "heavy restriction" early as a pre-deployment gate: validation, audit, disclosure and human review before use. It then pressed CON with a dilemma: either the small employer is barred from unvalidated tools (a de facto restriction), or it may keep using them (conceding the harm). In Rebuttal 2, CON answered honestly but conceded the decisive points: - an unvalidated tool "cannot operate until the evidence exists"; - refusal of disclosure or review means the use "is prohibited." That is functionally the heavy restriction the motion asks for. PRO capitalized cleanly on this in its closing. CON's last move tried to recast PRO as banning the whole category, including camera-based work samples. PRO had already answered this in A2 by explaining that validated task-scored samples survive a heavy restriction. That makes CON's distinction feel like a mischaracterization rather than a live difference. CON's referral-substitution argument was its best independent route. However, it applies equally to CON's own ex ante gate, and CON never weighed it against the hidden-scoring harms PRO emphasized. Both sides showed good epistemic discipline, with no fake statistics or citations. CON was rhetorically memorable ("amputate the instrument"), but PRO controlled the decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Q1/Q2 dilemma (cost of validation for small employers; whether refusal of disclosure prohibits the tool) and CON's Rebuttal 2 answers. CON admitted that unvalidated or non-disclosing tools are prohibited before deployment. That conceded that the regime PRO defends is necessary, leaving CON to fight mainly over labels.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by controlling the meaning of "heavily restrict" and demonstrating that Side A's regulatory alternative effectively conceded the motion. Side A built a strong comparative case around the invisible bias of unstructured human interviews and referrals, but proposed mandatory validation, independent bias audits, disclosure, and human review ([A1.5]). Through pointed pressure questions ([B3.1]-[B3.2]), B forced A to commit in [A4.1]-[A4.4] to an ex ante pre-deployment ban on unvalidated tools and mandatory explainability and human review—a policy package that clearly constitutes a heavy restriction on automated video and personality assessments and largely blunts A's own substitution-to-referrals critique. Meanwhile, B cleanly answered A's pressure on existing civil-rights law and task-scored video samples ([B4.1]-[B4.3]), whereas A's closing relied on mischaracterizing B as condemning any assessment conducted on camera ([A5.3]).
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's [B2.7] counterplan capture and [B3.1]-[B3.2] fork questions forced Side A in [A4.1]-[A4.4] to endorse an ex ante prohibition on unvalidated deployment plus mandatory disclosure and human review, conceding the core policy gate required by "heavily restrict."
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by making the decisive clash about who bears risk before an automated tool is proven valid, and by using CON’s own ex ante gate to show that heavy restriction is already necessary. CON kept the debate close with a useful category-versus-inference distinction and a real warning about fallback to referrals, but PRO’s discrimination, opacity, and burden-shifting case remained clearer and more motion-relevant.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s pressure questions forced CON to clarify enforcement, and CON’s admission that unvalidated tools cannot operate until evidence exists became the key concession PRO crystallized in closing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): The motion is "ban or heavily restrict," and that wording decides the round. CON's case improved across rebuttals. It offered a precise validity rule, vendor filing requirements as a condition of sale, mandatory human review, and a bar on any automated result alone rejecting a candidate. But each of these is itself a heavy restriction. PRO flagged this early in A2.6 and sharpened it in the closing: the validity rule is not an alternative to the motion, it is the motion. CON's main late attack was that PRO's carve-out for content-scored simulations meant PRO had abandoned the motion. That attack misreads the motion, which explicitly allows restriction short of a ban. CON's own A2 answer also conceded that eye contact, flat affect, and camera freezes are not job-relevant constructs. That concession gave PRO the substantive core of its case. CON did land real points. Its accountability point about structured, recorded evaluation was fair. It also correctly noted that weak enforcement capacity cuts against bans too. PRO had weak spots: its claim that human bias is "local, visible, challengeable" was thin, and its closing leaned on repetition. Still, PRO's framing of who bears the burden of error, combined with CON's concessions, leaves PRO the cleaner path to satisfying the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange began with PRO's Q2, which asked what job-relevant construct eye contact, vocal flatness, or a camera freeze measures. CON answered in B4.3 that none exists and such scoring should be barred. PRO then converted that admission in its closing (A5.2): once the construct is conceded absent, CON's strict validity regime amounts to the heavy restriction the motion calls for. This neutralized CON's "regulate, don't ban" route.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built the stronger path to victory under the motion as written ("ban or heavily restrict"). Side B dismantled Side A's initial light-touch regulatory model by showing that ex post disparate-impact audits miss equal-opportunity invalidity, asynchronous access does not require algorithmic personality scoring, and facial/vocal proxies lack valid underlying constructs. To survive that pressure, Side A conceded that eye contact, flat affect, and camera freezes fail validation and proposed pre-sale vendor filings, pre-use construct proof, banning affect variables, mandatory human review, and barring any automated result alone from rejecting an applicant. As Side B noted in [B2.6] and [B5.2], that regime itself heavily restricts these tools while gutting Side A's own volume-efficiency benefit. Side A kept the round competitive through sharp institutional comparisons on unrecorded human bias, a clever Q1 trap on content-scored simulations, and catching Side B's Turn 9 overstatement of who bears the burden under CON's shifted proposal, but Side B cleanly distinguished observable work samples from proxy inference ("recording vs. inference") and carried the central clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's equal-invalidity rebuttal [B2.2] and follow-up question [B3.2] challenging Side A to name what job-relevant construct eye contact, vocal flatness, or camera freezes measure. This forced Side A to concede in [A4.3] that those cues are invalid and must be barred alongside solo automated rejections, allowing Side B [B5.2] to show that CON's "targeted" validity filter effectively concedes the motion's call to ban or heavily restrict such assessments.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by turning the debate toward overbreadth and workable regulation. It gave a concrete targeted regime—pre-use validity proof, bans on environmental/affect variables, human review, and no automated rejection alone—while using Side B’s concession that job-content video scoring can be legitimate to argue that PRO was no longer defending the motion as written. Side B’s opacity and burden-of-proof case was rhetorically stronger, but its closing did not fully answer A’s final concession-based clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal: it conceded that eye contact, flat affect, and camera freezes are not valid constructs, then used that concession to argue a validity rule would exclude junk scoring while preserving legitimate automated simulations, setting up the decisive overbreadth point against PRO.
