### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO built a clean, intuitive case on information asymmetry and fair risk allocation, but CON developed more layered and original rebuttals that exposed structural problems in the proposed rule: biased complaint files that create a false "all-clear" for unreported-but-severe problems, and a remediation/stigma dynamic where landlords who fix problems are punished with permanent disclosure labels while untracked chronic problems go unflagged. PRO's pressure questions were decent, but its answers (A4.1-A4.3) expanded the disclosure duty to cover informally "known" conditions, which patched one hole while reopening CON's vagueness and administrability critique rather than resolving it. CON's answers to pressure questions directly engaged and sharpened its case, showing that CON's framework (resident-asking, reviews, fraud liability) preserves existing information-gathering tools that PRO's mandate would displace with an official-seeming but unreliable label. Both sides argued the actual motion without reframing, but CON's rebuttal sequence did more damage to the opponent's live route to victory and left PRO's closing restating asymmetry without fully dissolving the stigma/bias critique.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.3-B2.5 and B4.2 exchange on remediated buildings being stigmatized while unreported chronic problems escape disclosure (the "biased complaint file" argument) was the most damaging unanswered mechanism, directly undercutting PRO's core claim that landlord records reliably capture material risk.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO won because the decisive clash stayed who should bear known material asymmetry at signing, and CON never neutralized that route. A's core path—Friday-night parties, emptied package rooms, and landlord files invisible on a daytime tour—survived B's dynamic/behavioral category attack and became stronger once A flipped it into the reason tours fail. B's best live pressure (vague thresholds, remediation stigma, biased complaint files creating a fake all-clear) was real and sustained, but A answered the trigger and liability questions directly, kept the bar high, and converted remediation into “history plus cure” rather than a scarlet letter. B's alternatives (ask residents, reviews, fraud after the fact) stayed a post-commitment scavenger hunt and never restored pre-lease access to the landlord's actual records. Comparison stayed cleaner on A's side: imperfect disclosure with context still beats engineered silence for a year-long lock-in. Competitive, not a blowout—B made administrability costly—but PRO left the easier decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R1/R2 flip of B's category-error and remediation attack: invisible behavioral problems are exactly why disclosure is needed, and “past complaints + cameras/lockers on [date]” is information tenants can weigh, not a reason to suppress the file.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Both sides executed the format cleanly and engaged directly with pressure questions. CON's vagueness/administrability critique (no threshold, incentive to stop documenting, boilerplate risk) was sharp and exposed a real tension in PRO's "reasonable inquiry" standard. But PRO's comparative framing—warning before signature versus a post-hoc lawsuit after the tenant is already trapped—proved more durable, and PRO landed a telling counter-punch (A2.5) that CON's preferred remedy (fraudulent concealment/quiet enjoyment suits) suffers from the identical evidentiary problem CON raises against disclosure: proving landlord knowledge once records are gone. CON's answer that circumstantial evidence (neighbors, police logs) could substitute was serviceable but not fully convincing, since it doesn't establish what the landlord himself knew. PRO's safe-harbor concept, while imperfect, gave a workable content requirement (pattern, frequency, remediation) that CON's closing didn't fully engage with, instead repeating the "no format" charge. The debate stayed close throughout, but PRO's framing of pre-lease choice versus post-lease litigation gave the cleaner, more decisive reason to prefer its side of the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over A2.3/A2.5 (oral knowledge counts as concealment; fraud suits require reconstructing the same vanished knowledge) versus B2.4/B4.1-4.2 (oral knowledge is an invisible "swearing contest"; circumstantial evidence can still prove fraud) was the pivotal clash — it tested whether either side's enforcement mechanism actually survives the record-destruction problem each accuses the other of causing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): CON made the decisive clash clearer: the motion’s undefined “recurring/severe/chronic” terms create an unworkable classification duty that produces either useless boilerplate or litigation traps and defensive silence, so it does not deliver usable pre-lease information. PRO’s asymmetry/timing case and materiality/safe-harbor replies were strong on principle but never supplied an operational pre-lease line that neutralizes that administrability route; CON consistently showed why court-style reasonableness after the fact is not a workable disclosure rule before signing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 on why fraudulent-concealment proof is narrower than the motion’s pre-harm duty to label ambiguous complaints, plus why “reasonable tenant/materiality” remains a post-hoc standard rather than usable pre-lease information.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won the core comparison: timing and asymmetry. Tenant can't discover pattern during staged tour, landlord holds logs and is cheaper cost-avoider. A vague but material pre-lease warning that lets nurse rent elsewhere beats post-lease fraud suit where proof is harder without logs - the "worse swearing contest" point. CON's vagueness/record-destruction route was mitigated by B's reasonable-tenant/materiality standard, safe harbor "after reasonable inquiry," and willful-blindness bar, which matches existing habitability/disclosure law. CON never explained why this duty is uniquely unworkable versus bedbug/mold duties.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Pressure Q1 + B2.3/B4.5: exposing that CON's own fraudulent-concealment remedy requires proving the same hidden oral knowledge CON calls a "swearing contest," making post-hoc remedy worse than pre-lease disclosure.
