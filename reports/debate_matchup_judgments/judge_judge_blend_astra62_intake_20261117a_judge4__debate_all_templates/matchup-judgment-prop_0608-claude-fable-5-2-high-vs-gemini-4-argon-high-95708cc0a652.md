### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): PRO built the cleaner path to victory. Its pressure question forced CON to admit that a company with flat or rising emissions could lawfully call itself net-zero under a quality-only standard. PRO then turned that into a dilemma: if high-integrity credits are costly enough to drive in-chain cuts, the cap costs honest firms nothing; if they are cheaper, CON has no backstop against continued pollution behind the label. PRO also effectively answered greenhushing by showing firms dropping the claim were not cutting anyway and could still fund removals without the net-zero badge. CON's quality-not-location argument was strong, but PRO showed a strict quality standard shrinks usable offsets to a sliver, recreating the cap in practice. Despite two clipped A speeches, the visible content carried the decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was PRO's Q1 and CON's answer that flat or rising emissions can still be net-zero if balanced by verified credits. PRO then crystallized this in closing: CON's price safeguard either already produces mostly in-chain cuts, making the cap costless, or fails and leaves no requirement to reduce own pollution.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B made the stronger comparative case against a legal quantity cap. Once A conceded genuinely reliable removals, its scarcity argument did not establish that quality regulation and a small-share cap were equivalent. B explained why expensive credits could encourage feasible internal cuts while financing mitigation beyond them, and why restricting a voluntary credential need not force further reductions. A’s claim-integrity and investment-delay concerns remained substantial, and B overstated how universally firms would withdraw. Nevertheless, A never adequately answered the distinction between costly internal reductions and currently unavailable ones.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B directly challenged A’s claim that small shares of every corporate footprint would sustain removal demand: firms could earn the credential through those purchases only after completing the required deep reductions. That exposed an eligibility problem in A’s financing defense and sharpened the voluntary-participation clash.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate. A crafted the stronger label-integrity argument and memorably exposed B’s acceptance of net-zero claims despite flat or rising gross emissions. But A never squarely answered B’s central practical objection: under strict quality standards, making a voluntary credential unattainable can eliminate funding for real, verified mitigation without legally requiring any internal cuts. A’s response mostly assumed the lost credits would be cheap junk or appealed to semantic purity. B directly answered both pressure questions, distinguished net-zero from gross-zero, and explained why costly high-integrity credits can induce feasible internal cuts while financing additional mitigation where cuts are impractical. A’s repeated clipping was a further execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal turned A’s removal-demand and credential arguments against the cap: firms unable to eliminate most Scope 3 emissions cannot earn the label at all, so the associated boardroom capital and removal demand may disappear. A’s closing restated that few firms are genuinely net zero but did not answer this lost-mitigation mechanism.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): CON won by successfully reframing the debate as quality versus quantity. PRO's opening harms—burned forests, inflated baselines, cheap paper credits—are all bad-ton problems, and CON's quality regulation directly answers them. The decisive exchange was CON's pressure question: what is false about a claim backed by a ton mineralized in rock? PRO's answer redefined net-zero as a "sustainable business model" rather than atmospheric neutrality, which CON exposed as conceding the arithmetic. CON also resolved PRO's contradiction charge by distinguishing price-based toughness from prohibition, and showed the cap binds no one because the claim is voluntary—hardest sectors simply walk. PRO's scaling argument was real but CON answered it with other policy instruments and the standing-bill incentive. CON's clipped turns were an execution cost but did not remove key arguments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal of the contradiction charge and the "what is false" pressure question. CON showed abatement costs differ by firm, so expensive verified offsets and cheap internal cuts coexist without contradiction, and then forced PRO to admit a fully verified ton-in-rock claim is not false—leaving the cap forbidding true statements.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B wins the narrowed comparison: durable-removal requirements address A’s principal claim-integrity objections, while A does not sufficiently justify capping genuinely neutralized emissions. More importantly, B challenges the mechanism connecting a voluntary label restriction to compulsory industrial investment: companies can abandon the claim instead. A effectively answers the cement objection and establishes credible concerns about infrastructure lock-in, but never resolves that incentive problem. B’s clipped speeches and misleading equipment-ownership analogy reduce the margin without overturning its stronger comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B decisively answers A’s alleged cost contradiction: a company can value the badge enough to pay three hundred dollars per ton but not four hundred. That willingness-to-pay threshold explains differential walkaway and undermines A’s closing insistence that money available for removals must therefore finance costlier internal upgrades.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B made the decisive quality-versus-quantity distinction and defended it through the exchange. A gave a credible technology-lock-in and scarce-removals case, but never squarely explained why an audited, durable one-for-one removal makes a net-zero claim false, instead shifting toward industrial-policy preferences. B also answered the scale and walkaway pressures more directly: the voluntary cap can be abandoned, while expensive permissible claims can sustain annual demand. A’s cement answer was substantive, but B exposed the arbitrary distinction between onsite and contracted storage. B’s clipped endings slightly reduced execution, without defeating its core case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both pressure questions, contrasted a costly “ladder” with a prohibitive “wall,” and showed that A’s cap could forbid atmospherically valid claims while rationing removal by equipment ownership rather than climate effect.
