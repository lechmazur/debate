### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined and responsive, but CON did more to narrow and control the clash. CON reframed PRO's opening as an information failure with targeted fixes. It then exposed real tensions in PRO's repairs. The "trusted outsider" option removes the shop-floor knowledge that justified the seats in the first place, and the retaliation protection PRO relied on is scaffolding the motion does not contain. CON also forced PRO to choose between delegate accountability and independent fiduciary judgment. Its strongest move was the closing dilemma: if the shareholder majority holds, a written answer suffices, and if the seats decide close votes, they hand the swing vote to the exposed bloc. PRO answered honestly and kept a coherent normative core, namely that those who bear risk deserve a share in choosing who judges it. PRO also handled the pressure questions directly. However, PRO's franchise answer opened a complexity attack that CON exploited. CON's irreversibility asymmetry in the closing is contestable, since closures are often irreversible too, and it edges toward a new angle. Even so, CON made the decision easier to reach.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.3–B4.4 turned PRO's Q2 concession into a dilemma. Either the seats never change outcomes, in which case consultation suffices, or they swing close votes to the bloc whose jobs depend on the result. PRO's closing defended this as legitimate shared judgment but never fully escaped the framing.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A wins a competitive debate by turning Side B's own concession that worker directors can cast deciding votes into a dilemma: if the shareholder majority is unified, the seats mainly add what enforceable consultation already delivers; when the seats matter, it is because shareholders are split and the marginal judgment belongs to a bloc whose jobs depend on the answer. A also sustained the independence problem, showing worker-directors are either dependent employees or outsiders who lose the promised shop-floor insight, and exposed the missing scaffolding needed for one-size-fits-all compulsion. B's "coalition, not veto" answer was real but did not neutralize the concentrated-interest problem, leaving consultation and concrete worker protections as the cleaner alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3-A4.4: A concedes a vote beats a written answer but shows the board seats only change outcomes when shareholder directors are divided, transferring the deciding judgment to the very interest group whose jobs are at stake; calling it a coalition rather than a veto does not cure that bias.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A won by dismantling Side B's opening fixes and forcing a swing-vote theory, then showing board seats change outcomes only when a legacy-plant bloc supplies the marginal judgment. Narrower protections better match workers' downside. Side B kept a serious concentrated-risk claim but never escaped the independence, selection-bias, and error-asymmetry forks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal conceded a vote beats a written answer, then showed the seats are redundant when shareholder directors agree and dangerous exactly when they split, because today's workforce selects for defenders of the legacy line.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): This was a close, high-quality debate. PRO built a broad and memorable case: workers bear undiversifiable risk, boards hear bad news late, Germany shows the system can work, and voluntary inclusion gets revoked exactly when it matters. CON chose not to contest the general value of worker voice. Instead, it reframed the debate as a comparison between reserved seats and decision-triggered procedural rights for the specific crews affected, and pressed hard on capture: a bloc elected chiefly by the larger plant can serve its own constituency while the smaller crew goes unheard. Across rounds CON adapted precisely. It anchored disclosure to the capital-budget timetable to answer the "pipeline" point. It turned PRO's own concession that a worker director learns things when "someone outside tells her." It answered "guest vs presence" by noting that more seats improve the odds but create no right. Decisively, CON answered PRO's asymmetry question with a concrete distinction: shareholders share a dividend pro rata, while competing plants can each win or lose an entire investment. PRO's closing landed a good jab about litigation, and its "a bloc cannot be scheduled around" line is vivid. However, PRO overstated CON's position by declaring the disqualification argument "gone," and it reduced the capture objection to a "districting complaint" without fully answering it. PRO's broader systemic benefits went somewhat under-engaged by CON, which keeps the margin thin. Still, CON left the cleaner comparative question standing: once every affected crew has an enforceable hearing, what does the compulsory bloc add?
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2 in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive exchange. PRO had asked why reelection pressure from investors is legitimate but disqualifying for labour. CON replied that accountability to workers is fine. The real difference is that a dividend is shared pro rata among shareholders, whereas steering a production line to the winning slate's home plant hands the whole investment to one constituency. That answer defused PRO's strongest symmetry argument and re-anchored the capture objection.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): A (CON) wins because the quota's core promise—that workers will be heard inside the board—was not robust to the two-plant conflict. A showed a worker bloc can be captured by the largest constituency, leaving a smaller crew unheard without violating the law, while a targeted disclosure/hearing obligation directly protects the affected crew. B's standing-seat and one-vs-bloc arguments were forceful, but A's equal-payout distinction largely defused the shareholder-accountability symmetry, and A's use of B's own phrase "unable to decide without us" crystallized the representation gap. B made the litigation cost live, but A reasonably reframed enforcement as a backstop for a denied right.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's decisive rebuttal quoted B's "unable to decide without us" and asked who "us" is when two plants compete: a worker bloc elected mainly by the larger plant could sit through every budget meeting and never raise the smaller crew's cheaper proposal. A mandated hearing right directly covers that crew, which B did not fully answer.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side A won by keeping the live comparison on whether a significant bloc of worker-elected seats actually gets relevant worker knowledge into decisions. A answered the disclosure and accountability questions directly, then showed that investment across plants is rivalrous, so electoral winners can sit in every meeting and still bury a smaller crew's better option. B landed real hits on litigation-priced rights, committee quarantine, and why numbers matter, but never neutralized that capture problem beyond calling it a design issue. A's closing made the choice cleaner: hearing the affected crews can be a condition of approval, while the quota need not protect the workers who found the better plan.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal and closing were decisive: if investigation starts only when someone outside finds a sympathetic director, and that director may answer to a competing plant, reserved seats can silence the very floor knowledge B promised to surface, whereas draft disclosure lets the crew put the option on the table itself.
