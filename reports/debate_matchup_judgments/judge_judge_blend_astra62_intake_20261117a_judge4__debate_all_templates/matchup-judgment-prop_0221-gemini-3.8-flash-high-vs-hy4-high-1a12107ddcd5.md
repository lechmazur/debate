### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): CON wins because it adapted to the clash, while PRO mostly restated its opening. PRO began with a plausible cost-effectiveness story: tutoring is targeted and avoids buildings and pensions, while smaller classes leave pedagogy largely unchanged. But it never grounded its key weighing claim, that tutoring's "floor" beats class-size reduction's "ceiling." It also called tutoring's scaled costs "orders of magnitude smaller" without explaining why. CON focused relentlessly on "funded at scale." It argued that recruitment, supervision, turnover, attendance, space and displaced core time are the program itself, not minor deductions. CON then pressed a symmetric-accounting point: PRO prices every brick and salary for smaller classes but treats tutoring labor and friction as near-free. CON's Q2 exposed this directly, and PRO's A2 ("we weigh it honestly") was an assertion rather than an answer. CON also neutralized PRO's "do the math" minutes argument by calling it a category error and giving a concrete pedagogical mechanism in its A2. PRO's later speeches repeated "teaches to the median" and "brick and mortar" without engaging that mechanism. PRO's closing was largely a recap. CON's own positive case for class-size gains is somewhat thin. Even so, CON made the decision easier by showing that PRO had not carried its comparative per-dollar burden under scaled conditions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's A2 "we weigh it honestly" was the decisive exchange. CON showed that PRO announced a discount from a heroic baseline but never priced attendance, novice tutors, coordinator labor or pulled core time. Meanwhile PRO treated every classroom and salary as catastrophic. CON flipped PRO's own asymmetry charge and left PRO without a warrant for its floor-versus-ceiling claim.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): A won by pinning B to the words "funded at scale." B showed targeted tutoring can be efficient, but A exposed that scaled tutoring requires rooms, staff, supervision, attendance systems, and recurring labor that B never fully priced. A's trilemma—cheap, high-dosage, and high-fidelity cannot all be maximized—forced B into asserted "floor" advantages rather than comparative accounting. A's alternative mechanism, that smaller classes improve the base lesson all day and reach unflagged students, gave a cleaner gains-per-dollar route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's most decisive rebuttal was the asymmetric-accounting charge: B treated every classroom and certified salary as catastrophe while excluding tutoring's recurring staffing, missed sessions, coaching, and lost core instruction. That forced B into unsupported claims that a discounted floor still beat class-size reduction without showing the same systemwide cost standard.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B won because the motion turns on gains per dollar when tutoring is funded at scale, and B kept that accounting standard alive. A offered a vivid picture of personalized minutes versus diluted classroom attention, but never showed that high dosage, high fidelity, and low cost can coexist systemwide. B’s trilemma, space-and-staffing symmetry, and best-case-versus-worst-case critique left A’s “orders of magnitude cheaper” and “floor beats ceiling” claims as assertions rather than weighed comparisons.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal was decisive: it showed that A treated tutor labor, rooms, and supervision as nearly free while pricing every classroom and salary, then mistook teacher attention for a pile of one-to-one minutes rather than a better all-day lesson.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO kept the debate anchored to the motion's per-dollar metric and built a clear causal story: tutoring buys targeted corrective cycles for below-grade pupils, while class-size reduction spreads scarce certified labor and capital across every child. PRO's most effective move was the symmetry argument. CON's scale-friction and labor-shortage critiques apply at least as hard to hiring certified teachers for smaller sections, and CON never neutralized this. PRO answered both pressure questions directly. It resolved the apparent "sustain vs. stop" contradiction with the system-versus-pupil distinction, and it showed how tutor quality can be leveraged through specialist supervision. CON had real points on tutor quality, fade, and scheduling, but it leaned on assertion. Its claim that smaller classes curb turnover was never supported, and its vivid "18 vs. 28" example made a very expensive cut without ever pricing it. CON's second rebuttal also dodged A's Q1 cost pressure with brittle new figures ($50,000–$70,000, 15,000 student-hours). It recast PRO's hypothetical "discount tutor quality if you like" as a concession, which overstated what PRO said. CON never answered PRO's targeting argument that class-size money is spent on students already on track.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2.1–A2.3 turned CON's "laboratory illusion" and labor-scarcity critique back against class-size reduction. It noted that districts unable to staff tutoring are even less able to hire thousands of certified teachers and find classrooms. CON never answered this. PRO's closing point that "15,000 student-hours" is a group total, not individual corrective cycles, then defused CON's main quantitative rejoinder.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): B wins because it kept the clash on per-dollar causal leverage. A made a strong scale critique, but B directly answered it with a specialist-supervision/trained-tutor model and showed that class-size reduction is itself a scarce, permanent universal staffing cost. B's pressure exposed that A never really priced the ten-seat cut. A's best attack was that discounted tutor quality weakens gains, but B plausibly reframed this as comparative targeting rather than needing boutique effects. B also effectively turned A's 15,000 student-hours into group exposure, not corrective instructional cycles.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's combined answer to the pressure questions was decisive: it distinguished specialist diagnosis from narrow tutor delivery, and separated sustaining the tutoring system from stopping a pupil after catch-up. That answer showed class-size reduction is a permanent universal cost, not a one-time preventive cure.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won the decision rule: gains per dollar. Side A separated corrective cycles from seat-time, showed class-size cuts are a permanent universal layer priced in scarce certified labor, and answered both pressure questions—specialists supervise narrow routines, and system capacity can persist while individual pupils exit. Side B’s operations and quality critique stayed live, but the late case leaned on a misread “discount quality” concession, unanswered pricing of the 18-vs-28 cut, and brittle cost arithmetic that recycled room exposure as treatment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal, reinforced in closing: it split system dosage from pupil exit, offered a supervision model that preserved the cost edge, and then demolished the 15,000 student-hour reply as headcount times room time rather than diagnosis and correction.
