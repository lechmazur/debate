### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by defending the motion as a necessary floor rather than a full remedy. CON raised a sharp and credible objection—that audited disclosure can become performative or reward minimal claims—but PRO answered it with concrete audit mechanisms, comparability, plausibility red flags, and the turn that CON’s own preferred tools such as import bans and liability generally require mapped, audited records. CON’s best later push that many benefits were “borrowed” from other laws did not fully defeat PRO’s narrower claim: mandatory public looking is the precondition that makes those remedies workable. The debate was close, but PRO gave the cleaner third-party reason to vote for the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was turning CON’s alternatives against it: liability, import bans, and remediation all run on knowing origin and what the buyer knew, which is exactly the standardized audited record the motion compels. That made CON’s opposition look like rejecting the information base while depending on it.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): A won a competitive debate by better distinguishing audited disclosure from the evidence and remediation B repeatedly credited to it. A’s objections about non-engagement escaping disclosure and modest claims receiving assurance remained live. A also directly explained how bans and liability can generate information without this reporting mandate. B offered a credible public-comparability mechanism and concrete answers about audit failures, but its later speeches overstated reporting’s necessity and assumed remediation rather than adequately resolving the adverse incentives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B claimed import bans and buyer liability presupposed these reports. A answered that shipment-level traceability and litigation discovery generate evidence under different obligations, without requiring standardized public process reports. That broke B’s necessity claim; B’s closing largely repeated it instead of answering the distinction.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): A made a strong case that comparable public information is a precondition for enforcement and turned several of B’s examples against the status quo. B ultimately won the decisive comparison: A’s claimed consequences—border seizure, liability, remediation, and enforceable inspection—depended on duties outside the motion, while B explained why auditing reports alone rewards minimal claims, certifies ignorance, and can exclude vulnerable suppliers without any disclosable event. B answered both pressure questions directly and left A’s “floor” looking insufficiently connected to the promised benefits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal decisively separated evidence about goods under an import ban from a self-authored due-diligence narrative, then showed that A’s proposed audit failures penalize overclaiming while firms claiming little have little to contradict. This exposed A’s reliance on borrowed enforcement consequences and reinforced B’s downward-compliance incentive.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): CON won by turning PRO's best evidence: the Accord and existing commercial records show that consequences come from binding enforcement machinery, not from published paperwork alone. PRO never fully answered the self-incrimination problem—firms will not sign reports admitting unremediated abuse—or the Congo-style exit concern, where public comparability rewards dropping hard-to-document suppliers. CON used PRO's own admissions that auditors will not open every dormitory and that the motion creates no new liability to reframe the report as a compliance shield rather than a source of damning admissions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's reply that no general counsel signs a filing saying "we were warned and shipped anyway," combined with the point that bills of lading and commercial records already name factories for subpoenas, exposed the gap in PRO's claim that the motion "records knowledge."
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): B establishes the stronger incremental case: standardized disclosures can document corporate knowledge and support existing enforcement without themselves creating every remedy. A’s supplier-exit objection remains substantially unanswered, keeping this competitive. But A insufficiently establishes that reporting necessarily displaces stronger regulation or turns compliance into a liability shield. Crucially, A repeatedly converts B’s concession that auditors cannot inspect every dormitory into an admission that they verify only paperwork, overlooking B’s explicit sampling and unannounced-inspection answer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s distinction between records of goods and records of corporate knowledge directly answers A’s claim that commercial ledgers already provide the relevant evidence. A nevertheless lands the strongest unanswered counter on exit: a company that actually leaves a risky supplier can truthfully report reduced exposure, so B’s attack on dishonest “zero-risk” reporting misses that incentive.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): B won a close, high-quality debate by keeping the motion’s actual mandate—a standardized, audited public document—distinct from mapping, record retention, liability, and remedy. A’s strongest argument was that signed reports create evidence of corporate knowledge, but B answered that such reports may sanitize knowledge, certify process rather than conditions, and reward honest supplier exit. B also answered both pressure questions more squarely, while A’s detailed assurance model went beyond what the motion guarantees.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal decisively separated existing commercial records from the motion’s added publication-and-assurance apparatus, then reversed A’s exit argument: a firm that genuinely leaves a high-risk region can truthfully report a cleaner profile, replicating the Congo incentive without falsification.
