### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO's strongest route was that a bed people refuse to enter is not real capacity. That is an intuitive and reader-friendly frame, and PRO answered CON's pressure questions cleanly by defining the motion as default, designed accommodation with screening. CON, however, did more to damage that route as the debate developed. First, CON pointed out that under scarcity, a bed refused by one subgroup can still be filled by a single walk-in, a family, or a medical crisis. That directly undercuts PRO's "empty cots" premise, and PRO never answered it. PRO's closing simply restated the empty-bed claim. Second, CON reframed PRO's cost argument as a category error and stressed that partitions, kennels, and lockers scale with each bed. PRO did not defend its cost math after that challenge. CON did overreach. It claimed that PRO's concession on screening contradicts the motion, and it described PRO's plan as "commitment before safety." Both stretch PRO's actual position, since "allow far more" does not mean unscreened access. Even so, CON engaged the live clash more thoroughly across both rebuttals and its closing, while PRO's later speeches mostly repeated its opening frame. The result is a narrow edge to CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's A1 in Rebuttal 2. It answered PRO's "unused beds aren't capacity" question by arguing that unused beds are immediately fillable by other people in crisis when demand is high. This, combined with CON's point that redesign consumes beds at a linear rate, went unanswered. It left PRO's central empty-bed argument resting on bare repetition in the closing.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): B narrowly wins the unresolved capacity comparison. A persuasively explains why accommodation can improve uptake, but repeatedly asserts that redesign cannot reduce capacity without answering its space and staffing costs. B supplies the stronger marginal comparison: beds rejected by some applicants remain available to others. However, B wrongly treats planned accommodation as incompatible with individual screening and exaggerates the motion into universal admission, substantially limiting the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s direct answer to the empty-bed challenge was decisive: an unused cot can still shelter another arrival tonight. A’s closing repeats that these are beds nobody can use, failing to distinguish subgroup exclusion from universally wasted capacity.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side B presented a strong usable-capacity critique and answered both pressure questions directly, but relied on unsupported claims about widespread refusals, current shelter availability, and redesigns filling rather than consuming beds. Side A better preserved the emergency-triage comparison, offered practical layered alternatives, and exposed the unresolved space, staffing, and safety costs of B’s architectural default. B’s safeguards answered universal-access concerns but did not establish that “far more” accommodation produces net usable capacity; B’s closing largely asserted that point rather than proving it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s pressure questions forced B to distinguish “far more” built accommodation from CON’s screened exceptions. B answered clearly, but A’s second rebuttal kept the decisive burden on B: dedicated infrastructure retains screening while still consuming flexible emergency capacity, a tradeoff B never squarely neutralized.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): PRO narrowly wins on framing. It built the clearer metric: occupied and retained beds, not beds on a floor plan. "Capacity that people refuse to use is not capacity" stuck. PRO also blunted the safety attacks by proposing a continuum instead of mixed congregate chaos. It added managed pet zones, vaccination, and lockable storage, and argued that exclusion relocates risk rather than removing it. CON's strongest route was surge capacity: in cold snaps and evacuations, space is fixed and full, so carving it up turns people away that night. This stayed partly live. CON was right that the up-front space cost is certain while retention gains are less proven. But CON leaned on unsupported claims, notably that refusers are a "small subset" and that each accommodation costs two to three beds. It also partly conceded that reasonable accommodations are already standard, which shrank the gap between the sides. CON's safety points on allergies and bed bugs were largely answered by PRO's managed-risk response. CON's later speeches mostly repeated the capacity point without adapting. PRO had flaws too. Its A1 answer ("None, if the need is triage") was somewhat evasive, with a thin claim about vertical space. Its Q2 also wrongly said the motion itself includes screening, when that came from PRO's own case. Even so, PRO adapted better across the rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over net capacity. PRO's Q1 pressed CON to count the beds lost when separated households refuse entry or leave. CON answered that the square-footage loss is guaranteed while retention is speculative during surges. That was CON's best moment, but it rested on an unsupported claim that refusers are few. PRO's closing line, "A bed in a diagram is not a bed in service," kept its framing on top.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): B wins the central capacity comparison: when shelters are full and others await admission, improved household retention does not itself offset reduced bed capacity. A established plausible exclusion harms and sensible safeguards, but its answer to saturation—“none” followed by layout suggestions—never resolved that tradeoff. A’s closing also misrepresented B’s explicit acknowledgment that screening reduces risks. B’s unsupported bed-loss estimates and sweeping safety claims limit the margin, but its strongest argument survived the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to “CON counts beds; the motion counts people” exposed the missing comparison: displaced beds represent other people needing shelter, not merely administrative efficiency. Its full-capacity pressure question sharpened that objection, and A never demonstrated why retention gains would outweigh displaced admissions under those conditions.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B made the stronger practical case that usable capacity, retention, and managed risk matter more than nominal bed counts. Its flexible layouts and concrete safeguards answered much of Side A’s safety argument, while Side A too often reverted to unscreened partners, unvetted animals, and fixed bed-loss ratios. Side A’s surge-capacity and immediate-triage argument remained a serious counterweight, and it directly exposed B’s unquantified retention benefit, but its assertion that refusals affect only a small subset was likewise unsupported. B ultimately gave the cleaner account of why exclusion can reproduce both capacity losses and dangers outside the shelter.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal and pressure questions reframed the clash around net occupied capacity and forced A to answer a screened, contained proposal rather than unmanaged chaos; A’s immediate-versus-speculative-loss response was effective but did not fully repair its earlier mischaracterizations.
