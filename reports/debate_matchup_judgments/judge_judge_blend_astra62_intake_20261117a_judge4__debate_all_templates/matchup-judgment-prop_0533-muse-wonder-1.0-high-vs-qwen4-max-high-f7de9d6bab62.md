### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by clearly separating equitable use from unilateral control. PRO's best route was that any requirement for agreement hands Egypt a veto through indefinite stalling. A answered that directly with a binding formula, a fixed negotiating window, a neutral technical panel, and interim filling. That reply removed the veto problem without giving Egypt control. A also turned PRO's own concessions (wet-season filling, drought releases, data sharing) into the "write it down" dilemma: if Ethiopia plans to act prudently anyway, unilateralism adds only the option to defect. PRO's answers were weaker. Its flexibility argument met a force-majeure reply. Its after-the-fact remedies for proven harm were undercut by "timing is the harm." Its energy-poverty weighing was blunted by A's point that the dam is already built and filling, so the dispute is about schedules rather than whether Ethiopians get power. PRO's closing claim that a panel would still block necessary timing changes was asserted rather than developed. Both sides kept their evidence appropriately general.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2 answered PRO's strongest pressure question about indefinite Egyptian stalling with a clock and a neutral panel. A then attacked PRO's post-hoc remedy model: "water is not damages; you cannot litigate a missed planting season back." That exchange neutralized PRO's veto framing and left the timing-harm asymmetry standing.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): A more convincingly separated Ethiopia’s right to develop from unilateral authority over drought risks. Its deadline, interim filling, and neutral-panel proposal directly addressed B’s strongest objection: indefinite delay through Egyptian refusal. A also explained why eventual water passage and compensation do not adequately protect time-sensitive harvests. B compellingly defended electrification, but its closing largely repeated the veto argument rather than defeating A’s revised alternative. A’s assumed panel authority and overstated claims about irreversible harm limit the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B argued that binding rules sacrifice operational flexibility and inevitably empower Cairo to stall. A answered with emergency exceptions and externally bounded decision-making, then exposed the weakness of remedies arriving after a missed planting season. This shifted the decisive comparison from development versus veto to unilateral discretion versus constrained development.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): A made the decisive distinction between Ethiopian development and exclusive Ethiopian discretion, then answered B’s strongest veto-and-delay objection with a bounded negotiation clock, neutral technical decision-maker, interim filling, and emergency flexibility. B presented a strong sovereignty and electrification case, but its drought safeguards remained voluntary and its recourse largely retrospective. A’s irreversible-timing argument therefore left B defending discretion whose clearest added value was the ability to abandon promised prudence when incentives diverged.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered B’s deadline challenge without relying on Egyptian goodwill, treated dam-safety flexibility as ordinary emergency drafting rather than a reason for unilateralism, and exposed why post-harm remedies cannot repair water withheld during a planting season.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): This was a close, well-matched debate. PRO built a strong frame: the dispute is about "who holds the pen" on the schedule. It backed this with the veto-versus-sovereignty dilemma, the point that colonial-era allocations excluded Ethiopia, the reciprocity challenge about Aswan, and a vivid certain-versus-contingent harm comparison. CON narrowed the motion more effectively, to Ethiopia's *sole* control of timing. It then turned PRO's best evidence. Stating that filling caused "no catastrophe" ignores that Lake Nasser's drawdown is itself the cost Egypt paid. CON also offered a coherent middle path: a jointly agreed process with preset triggers and mediation, which avoids both a Cairo veto and Ethiopian discretion. The pivotal moment was PRO's A1/A2. PRO conceded that Ethiopia should pause filling in severe drought and can commit to drought releases. CON exploited this cleanly by asking who judges severity and who verifies releases. If Ethiopia alone judges, Egypt's protection rests on hope. If an objective trigger judges, PRO has moved off the motion. PRO's closing pressed "name the body" and recast CON's enforcement mechanisms as voluntary. That was sharp but did not fully defeat CON's framing that consent to a process is not a seasonal veto. PRO's emotive closing image and the evaporation point added in its second rebuttal broadened the debate slightly rather than resolving the clash. CON's asymmetry argument held up better at the end: delay is recoverable, while a drought met with depleted storage and no guaranteed release is not.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's A4.3–A4.4 and closing A5.1–A5.2 response to PRO's drought-pause concession. CON reframed PRO's concession as proof that the real question is who interprets and verifies the drought rules. That forced PRO into a dilemma: either accept unverified upstream discretion or accept the objective-trigger regime CON had defended. This blunted PRO's "who holds the pen" framing just enough to tip the debate.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): A more convincingly distinguished rejecting an Egyptian veto from granting Ethiopia sole discretion. Its drought and refill analysis showed why turbine incentives and uneventful wet-year filling do not establish safe timing, while monitored obligations offer protection beyond discretionary assurances. B pressed a genuine weakness: A never fully established how its proposed process would secure initial acceptance. But B’s repeated choice between Cairo’s permission and Ethiopian discretion inadequately addressed independent constraints, leaving A with the stronger comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s answer to “his treaty adds paper, not water” was decisive: monitoring, preset triggers, notice, and consequences can improve predictability and compliance without a court physically compelling releases. B’s closing did not adequately answer that distinction.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side A won a close, high-quality debate by answering B’s central veto dilemma with joint technical rules, mediation, and preset deadlock-breakers rather than requiring Cairo’s approval of each filling decision. A then turned B’s drought-pause and release commitments into a damaging dilemma: either Ethiopia alone interprets them, leaving Egypt unprotected, or objective constraints displace Ethiopia’s sole timeline. B offered sharper rhetoric and strong development-versus-cushion weighing, but its closing relied too heavily on the false choice between Cairo’s permission and no meaningful rules, without neutralizing A’s monitoring, notice, and coordination argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions, then exposed the gap in B’s concessions: self-judged drought promises are unverifiable discretion, while enforceable objective triggers concede the need for coordination. Its explanation that binding rules can alter behavior without a court also blunted B’s “paper, not water” response.
