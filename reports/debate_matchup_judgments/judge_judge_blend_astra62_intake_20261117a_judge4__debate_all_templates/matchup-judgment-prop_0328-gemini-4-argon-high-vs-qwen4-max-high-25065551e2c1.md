- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): CON wins a competitive debate because it controlled the decisive clash. That clash was whether the "unless narrowly tailored" carve-out leaves the PRO right workable. PRO built a reasonable case on ownership, fee extraction, and walled gardens. Token revocation was a solid answer to the duplicate-screenshot point. PRO's best late move was arguing that CON's mandated transfer window, which lets the fan name a recipient, is itself a peer-to-peer transfer right. CON forced PRO into a dilemma it never cleanly escaped. Calling the blind face-value queue narrow tailoring concedes that organizer-controlled transfer can be legitimate. Otherwise, below-market pricing collapses. PRO's answer was a two-tier rule keyed to price policy, and CON then exploited it: pricing would decide whether organizers keep non-price controls like bans and member presales. CON also answered PQ1 honestly ("None") and confined that admission to a practice rather than the motion. It explained that refund-at-face windows avoid the side-payment problem. It then turned PRO's own bank analogy into support for an issuer-duty model. PRO's convergence point is real, and it keeps the margin modest. But CON's "trigger" framing (fan need vs. organizer pricing) gave a cleaner reason for why its remedy differs from the motion. PRO's quantity caps and cooling-off periods also partly conceded that friction is needed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's B2.1/B3.1 dilemma on the blind return queue. Either organizer-controlled reissuing counts as narrow tailoring, so authenticated platform transfer does too and the right is nearly empty, or it doesn't, so the right destroys below-market pricing. PRO's A4.3 two-tier answer then exposed the price-policy trigger that CON exploited in B4.1 and B5.2.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side B wins a competitive debate by showing the carve-out is the real battleground and that effective anti-scalping control requires organizer-controlled transfer channels. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): CON wins on the decisive mechanism clash. Its opening argued that a mandated peer-to-peer lane lets sellers pick their recipient and collect payment off-platform, which defeats any face-value cap. PRO's best answer was that the motion still allows a blind pool, just not as the only lane. CON countered that a parallel lane drains the capped pool, and that legally reissuing tickets into the buyer's name defeats the gate ID checks PRO itself relied on. PRO then conceded "Honestly, they can't" stop off-platform payment. PRO tried to claim parity by saying the blind pool is equally exposed. CON cleanly distinguished the two: a blind pool removes the seller's choice of recipient, so a broker cannot deliver to the specific buyer who paid. PRO's stranded-fan harm was largely blunted by CON's point that she gets a full face-value refund through the exchange. PRO's response on entry rules versus transfer rights was reasonable and partly neutralized CON's literal-text attack on segregation and student rules, but it was a side issue. PRO's closing introduced a new point, that brokers can buy in the buyer's name at the primary sale, and CON answered it by noting brokers hoard inventory before they have buyers. PRO had stronger rhetoric and a clear framing around least-restrictive means, but CON built the more convincing causal case and exploited the key concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1/B4.3: CON turned PRO's admission "Honestly, they can't" into the deciding point. It showed that a blind pool, unlike a peer-to-peer button, stops a seller from routing the seat to a buyer who paid off-platform. That collapsed PRO's claim that the two systems are equally exposed to under-the-table payment.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): PRO built a vivid fan-autonomy case, but CON exposed its central enforcement hole: once a legal P2P transfer reissues the ticket in the recipient's name, off-platform payment lets brokers choose their buyer while the price cap is undetectable. PRO's concession that such payments cannot be stopped, followed by CON's distinction between a random-waitlist blind pool and recipient-chosen P2P delivery, left PRO unable to rebuild a working face-value cap. PRO salvaged some non-scalping eligibility points, but the decisive price-cap/scalping clash went to CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's "payment in the dark is inevitable" point: a blind pool prevents the seller from choosing the recipient, so off-platform cash cannot deliver a specific ticket, whereas a legal P2P button makes that delivery frictionless.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won the decisive clash: a mandated peer-to-peer button lets a seller choose the recipient while collecting payment off-platform, so a face-value cap cannot be enforced unless the capped exchange is the exclusive exit. A’s honest “they can’t” concession was never repaired; mule accounts and primary-name purchases were distinguished by gate ID and scalper timing, and the sick fan is financially made whole by a return-to-pool. A’s entry-rule rereading and least-restrictive framing stayed competitive but did not show the motion, as written, preserves workable anti-scalping or non-scalping controls.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttal that a blind pool blocks chosen-recipient delivery, while A’s peer-to-peer lane cannot stop a Venmo sale plus free transfer—pressed through A’s “Honestly, they can’t” concession.
