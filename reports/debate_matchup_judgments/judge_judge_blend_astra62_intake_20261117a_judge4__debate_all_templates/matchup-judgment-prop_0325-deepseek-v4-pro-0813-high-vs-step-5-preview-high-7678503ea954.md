### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins on the decisive economic clash. CON built its case on fixed rights fees and production costs. It argued that any cheap pass must either carry an "allocable share" of those costs or be subsidized by bundle buyers. PRO correctly showed that this treats sunk costs as marginal ones. A fan who would never buy the bundle adds contribution at any price above the tiny cost of serving one more stream. PRO also neutralized CON's best point, cannibalization, with product differentiation: a narrower pass with no neutral games, playoffs or archive limits downgrading. PRO further noted that the motion says "offer," not "replace." CON's closing line, "less access is not less cost," missed the point that price tracks value and versioning rather than production cost. CON repeated the fixed-bill argument without engaging the non-payer conversion mechanism. CON's piracy point, that free beats $60, was decent but left undeveloped. CON also lost its opening's ending to clipping. PRO has minor execution costs: it cited nonexistent reference IDs ([C1.5-C1.6], [C4.1-C4.2]), and its music analogy rebuttal was a bit loose. Overall, PRO gave the cleaner path to victory and answered pressure more convincingly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A2.4 and A4.3 against B4.3 and B5.2. CON posed a dilemma: a cheap pass either covers its full rights share and so is not cheap, or it is subsidized by bundle buyers. PRO answered that a narrower product can be priced lower because it delivers less inventory. It can still sit above marginal cost and include a per-subscriber rights contribution, while converting fans who currently pay nothing. That answer broke CON's fixed-cost framing, and CON never adequately rebuilt it.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A consistently won the central clash: whether a cheap single-team pass creates new revenue or merely rebates existing bundle buyers. A's pressure question (what bundle revenue is displaced from a fan who will never buy at any price?) put B in a genuine bind, and B's answers leaned on aggregate assertions ("displacement is measured across the whole base," "resets the reference price") rather than a net calculation. A also effectively conceded and managed the cannibalization risk by noting downgraders receive a deliberately narrower product, and the "offer, not replace" reading matched the motion's "rather than forcing" wording. B's fixed-rights-fee dilemma (below allocable share = transfer; full share = not cheap) was its strongest weapon and was well-executed in rebuttals, but it repeatedly conflated average and marginal cost without ever showing the net loss outweighs conversions from non-payers and pirates. B also suffered a clipped opening (execution cost), while A committed minor reference-ID errors ([C1.5-C1.6], [C4.1-C4.2]). Both sides answered pressure questions directly with proper A1/A2 structure, but A's answers were more responsive and A's closing crystallized the live dispute more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's Q1 in Pressure Questions ("what bundle revenue is displaced if the one-team fan won't buy the bundle?") against B's B4.1 answer. B could only assert imperfect separability and reference-price effects, while A's A4.1–A4.2 answer identified the actual marginal consumer outside the legal market and reframed downgrades as a real loss of access rather than a discount on the same product—defusing B's cannibalization route.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A built the stronger case for the motion as written: offering narrower passes alongside bundles, not abolishing bundles. A identified a live source of additional revenue among non-payers, differentiated the narrower product, and allowed repricing or withdrawal if demand disappointed. Side B landed useful pressure about fixed rights costs and downgrading, but repeatedly turned those risks into an unsupported guarantee that every cheaper pass must create a revenue hole. B never adequately explained why a differentiated pass could not be cheaper than a broad bundle while still making a positive rights contribution. B’s clipped opening and A’s occasional faulty reference labels were minor execution costs, but A ultimately left the cleaner route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s pressure questions and A1:/A2: answers directly exposed B’s two weakest assumptions: that cheap-pass buyers are necessarily downgrading bundle subscribers and that pricing above marginal delivery cost necessarily means shifting costs onto bundle buyers. B answered by restating aggregate fixed costs and allocable shares, but did not eliminate A’s profitable non-payer conversion scenario.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON wins because it built a clear causal structure and kept it intact through the debate: fixed rights and production costs must be funded by the subscriber base, and a cheap standalone pass invites existing bundle subscribers to downgrade. That turned the clash into a dilemma: either the pass is cheap and simple and cannibalizes the funding pool, or it is made additive through restrictions such as out-of-market, delayed, or blacked-out access. PRO never cleanly escaped that dilemma. PRO's replies were reasonable but mostly asserted rather than shown: - Its marginal-cost point was answered by CON's argument that the feed exists because bundle subscribers fund it. - Its zero-payer recapture and "$80-to-$0 churn" claims depended on fans who already left, without showing that downgraders would mostly be such fans. - Its out-of-market example was additive, but CON fairly framed it as a niche carve-out rather than the local team pass the motion promises. - PRO's "should vs. will" reframe had some force. However, CON tied substitution risk to the funding of smaller sports and coverage quality, which kept the issue normative rather than merely predictive. - PRO also cited nonexistent reference IDs ([C1.3, C1.4]) and let the ecosystem-subsidy point fade after its first rebuttal. PRO's opening and imagery ("sell the dish") were memorable but thin on mechanism. Overall, CON made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.5–A2.6 was decisive. It turned PRO's offer to design "genuinely additive" passes into a concession: the pass only works if it is restricted so that it cannot become the simple, cheap replacement the motion promises. That set up the cannibalization-or-scarcity dilemma PRO never fully resolved. PRO's out-of-market answer in B4.3 helped only partially, since CON fairly treated it as a niche exception.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides built a coherent economic clash: PRO on trivial marginal cost of digital viewers and the normative "should offer" framing; CON on fixed-cost recovery and cannibalization. The decisive exchange was the substitution ratio: CON pressed that one $80-to-$8 downgrade requires roughly ten new low-price buyers, and PRO never supplied evidence of that demand, instead reclassifying downgraders as churners who would have gone to $0—an assertion CON squarely flagged as unproven separation. PRO's out-of-market carve-out, while constructive, retreated from the motion's core promise of a cheap simple local pass, and CON exploited that retreat effectively in the closing dilemma. PRO's normative "should vs. will" reframing was its best weapon but did not fully neutralize CON's claim that the product itself cannot be cheap, simple, and revenue-stable simultaneously. Minor reference-id errors on both sides ([A2.3] citing "C1.3/C1.4"; [B2.1] citing "P2.1/P2.2") roughly cancel out. CON crystallized the choice more cleanly across final speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.2 and the closing: CON conceded the zero-payer adds revenue but showed the pass cannot be sold only to them, then pinned PRO's escape on an unshown separation between new fans and downgraders—the ratio PRO acknowledged but never supplied. This turned PRO's own arithmetic against it and exposed the churn-reclassification as assertion rather than evidence.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): B built the clearer practical route to victory: a generally available cheap pass cannot be confined to non-subscribers, so gains from new fans must be weighed against high-value bundle downgrades and the fixed costs those subscribers finance. A answered directly and usefully identified churn and out-of-market passes as additive cases, but never showed that these gains outweigh substitution, and its strongest example narrowed the proposed access to a niche. B sometimes overstated inevitability and underweighted consumer access, yet its central dilemma survived the exchange more intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s cannibalization exchange was decisive: it showed that converting zero-payers adds revenue only gross, while existing subscribers can also buy the cheap pass. A acknowledged the roughly ten-to-one illustrative break-even problem but answered mainly by reclassifying downgraders as already-lost customers, leaving the net-revenue burden unresolved.
