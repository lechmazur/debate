### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued with discipline and no invented sourcing, producing a genuinely substantive clash over whether standardized earnings records can bear the weight the motion places on them. B built a coherent, evolving mechanism (aggregate payouts, pro rata contributions, offsets, adjudication layered on top) and used the payroll-record analogy effectively to neutralize CON's "false precision" and "sequencing" objections, showing that payroll records also don't resolve misconduct/availability tests yet clearly "count." CON raised strong measurement and financing objections early, but by Rebuttal 2 retreated into a more abstract "legal consequence vs. data format" framing that conceded pooling was "never the disputed point," weakening the force of its own pressure question. CON's opening was also clipped for length, a real execution cost that blunted its initial framing before the exchange began. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions format correctly with clear A1/A2 answers, but B's answers more directly dissolved CON's dichotomy, and B's closing crystallized a sharper, more memorable asymmetry (recorded contributory claim vs. discovery-only-at-failure) than CON's closing, which largely repeated the overreach charge without fully answering the payroll analogy. This gives B a clear, if not overwhelming, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2) turning CON's "earnings records can't decide everything" objection back on itself via the payroll-wage analogy — "a payroll wage record never decides a discharge dispute either, yet no one says wages do not count" — was the most damaging single exchange, and CON's response partially conceded the pooling point rather than fully rebutting it.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): B won by clearly distinguishing standardized earnings records from the separate adjudication of involuntariness, availability, offsets, and program-specific rules. A identified genuine implementation problems but repeatedly treated the motion as requiring one record to decide every eligibility question or impose identical payroll treatment across programs. B also showed that A's preferred contributory self-employment schemes would themselves need aggregated, standardized earnings records, leaving A without a clearly distinct alternative. A's clipped opening was a minor additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response to the risk-allocation challenge was decisive: unemployment insurance pools risk, so contributions need not be traced to the particular app causing a loss, while other earnings can be handled through ordinary offsets and partial-benefit rules. This directly weakened A's claim that platform-specific causation had to be encoded in the earnings record.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully distinguishing between mere data collection (which both sides agree is possible via existing tax records) and the legal consequences of mandating that those records automatically generate qualifying credits for social insurance. Side A convincingly argued that Side B's approach puts the cart before the horse by mandating insurance entitlements before the underlying risks, offsets, and eligibility rules for platform work are actually designed. Side B's analogy to traditional payroll records was strong, but Side A effectively neutralized it by pointing out that payroll records operate within an already-settled legal framework of employment, which platform work currently lacks. Side A's answers to the pressure questions were particularly sharp, crystallizing the "legal consequence vs. data format" distinction and securing the conceptual high ground.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's payroll analogy by explaining that payroll records derive their meaning from an existing legal settlement regarding employment and separation, whereas Side B's motion attempts to reverse that sequence by compelling payroll-style credit first and leaving the actual insurance rules to later statutes.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): PRO built a clean fairness narrative (the courier excluded despite equivalent conditions) and pressed it into a concession CON could not fully escape: when a platform worker meets the same contribution/job-loss conditions as a payroll worker, there is "no principled reason" to discard her earnings. PRO then used that concession effectively in both Rebuttal 2 and Closing, framing CON's remaining objections (cost pass-through, surveillance, "false receipt") as speculative implementation choices not mandated by the text of the motion itself. CON's case was structurally strong—machinery-before-protection, who-owes-the-contribution, low-earner harm—and its pressure questions (Q1 on contributions/payer, Q2 on safeguards) were sharp and forced PRO to clarify that the motion itself sets no new rates or payers. But PRO's answers (A1/A2) met that pressure directly and turned it back into an argument that CON's net-benefit test "proves too much" since ordinary insurance never guarantees universal payout. CON's final closing reasserted that the concession was narrower than claimed, but this mostly repeated earlier framing rather than landing a new decisive blow. PRO's opening was clipped for length, a real execution cost, but the substance lost was minor and PRO's subsequent speeches compensated with consistently on-point format discipline (clear A1:/A2: labeling) and tighter comparative weighing. On balance PRO narrowed the dispute more effectively and left CON's strongest objections characterized as implementation hypotheticals rather than inherent features of the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2) turning CON's "who owes the contribution" pressure question back into the point that no social-insurance reform specifies financing in advance, paired with the closing's use of CON's "no principled reason" concession, was the most decisive exchange—CON's Closing response (the concession is "narrower than it sounds") reasserted its frame but did not substantively dismantle the concession's force.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B won by staying closest to the proposition: counting platform earnings toward eligibility is legal recognition, not a guarantee that every worker receives benefits. Side A’s deductions, surveillance, and pass-through harms depended largely on implementation choices the motion did not require. Most damagingly, Side A conceded there was no principled reason to discard the earnings of a platform worker who otherwise satisfies the same conditions, while never defeating B’s case that standardized records make that recognition administrable across apps. B’s clipped opening was an execution cost, but not enough to offset its substantially clearer comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response that conditional eligibility is still meaningful protection, and that A’s universal net-benefit test would undermine ordinary payroll insurance too, exposed A’s central burden as excessive. A’s later concession on equal treatment then reinforced B’s framing rather than escaping it.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by sharply dissecting the mechanics of the proposition. While Side B (PRO) established a strong moral premise that platform work should count toward eligibility, CON successfully demonstrated that the specific mechanism proposed—mandating standardized earnings records without defining the contribution obligor or rate—creates a "false receipt." CON's distinction between payroll systems (backed by employer withholding and liability) and the proposed platform mandate (which supplies a record but no accountable contributor) was decisive. PRO's reliance on existing schemes to "figure out financing later" failed to neutralize CON's proof that the mandate imposes immediate compliance costs, surveillance infrastructure, and pass-through harms on low-earning workers before securing any enforceable protection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled PRO's payroll analogy by highlighting that payroll insurance relies on an employer who legally owes and withholds contributions, whereas the motion mandates a government-verified record without an accountable contributor, thereby exposing the policy as a coercive apparatus that separates the record from the obligation.
