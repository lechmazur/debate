### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side B won decisively by dismantling Side A's two core claims: the limiting principle of what constitutes gambling, and the necessity of the gambling label to enforce spending caps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was masterful. B used A's own limiting principle (that a true gambling prize requires official developer-run cash-out mechanics) to prove that A's rule excludes the vast majority of cosmetic loot boxes, while simultaneously pointing out that legislatures can simply grant consumer protection agencies the exact enforcement teeth A claimed were exclusive to gambling regulators.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): CON won the necessity and scope clashes. PRO claimed only a gambling regulator can impose binding spend caps and exclusion, but never showed why a consumer/platform regulator could not be given the same power. CON offered a concrete payment-rail enforcement model that undercuts that necessity, and showed PRO's own monetised-prize limiting principle shrinks the motion to a narrow cash-out subset while the broad label would sweep in baseball cards and blind-bags. Targeted caps, odds disclosure, and parental auth therefore beat misclassification.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.3/B4.1 – CON shows PRO's desired safeguards are ordinary consumer-protection tools enforceable via app-store liability and statutory caps, not tools that legally require the gambling label, collapsing PRO's core necessity claim.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): Side A decisively dismantled Side B's core necessity claim. Side B's case hinged on the assertion that only a gambling regulatory framework possesses the legal authority to enforce binding spend caps and penalties. Side A effectively countered that legislatures can grant identical enforcement powers to consumer protection or child-safety regulators, and that payment-rail enforcement at the app-store level is a far more practical mechanism than game-by-game gambling licensing. Furthermore, Side A exposed a fatal flaw in Side B's definition: requiring a "monetised prize" inside a developer-run economy concedes that standard cosmetic loot boxes without official cash-outs are not gambling, meaning the proposition either overreaches to include ordinary collectibles or collapses into a narrow subset that Side A already agreed to regulate directly. Side B failed to adequately answer the pressure regarding why the specific "gambling" label was legally necessary, relying instead on unsupported assertions about the inherent limitations of consumer protection statutes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A1), where they introduced payment-rail enforcement and platform liability as a concrete, non-gambling mechanism for spend caps, directly and decisively neutralizing Side B's claim that only gambling licenses can enforce binding limits.
