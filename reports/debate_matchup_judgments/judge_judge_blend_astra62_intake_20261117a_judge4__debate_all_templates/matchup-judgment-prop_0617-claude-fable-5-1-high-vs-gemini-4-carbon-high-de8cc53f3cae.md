### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a close, high-quality debate. B's core framework—who bears the cost of uncertainty, the irreversibility of wrongly approved gatekeeper buyouts versus the availability of substitutes (non-dominant buyers, licensing, hiring) for wrongly blocked ones—survived the exchange and was never squarely broken. A's sharpest attack, that B substituted "merger-specific gains" for the motion's "substantial consumer benefit," was met with B's defensible answer that pre-merger "proof" always means likely effects shown with present evidence. A's second strong line, overbreadth (thermostat/robotics), drew B's ecosystem-entrenchment answer, which was imperfect but sufficient. A's best moment (A4.1: growth curves and "buy before they beat us" memos let enforcers prove harm) was partially answered by B's "memos now unwritten" and the counterfactual-burden distinction. A also incurred two execution costs: the opening and second rebuttal were clipped, losing the tail of the trap argument and A's closing summary. B answered both pressure questions directly with clean A1/A2 structure and closed with a memorable reframe (the defensive premium as monopoly rent shared with a founder). A's closing was sharp ("that assumption is the motion") but B's path to victory was cleaner end to end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1 versus B5.2: A argued that if benchmarks prove benefit, growth curves and threat memos let enforcers prove harm, collapsing the asymmetry; B's reply that an equal legal standard is not an equal task—and that the burden of proving a counterfactual standalone future is why such memos go unwritten—kept B's core rationale alive.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): PRO wins on the central burden clash. CON's symmetric-uncertainty and scope attacks were sharp, but PRO credibly distinguished proving a startup's counterfactual future as a rival from proving measurable gains when integrating technology into an existing platform. PRO also answered the founder-exit cost by weighing irreversible consumer lock-in above monopoly premiums and pointing to non-dominant buyers, licensing, and hiring. CON exposed real overbreadth and standard-lowering risks, keeping it close, but did not fully neutralize the error-cost and information-asymmetry case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 argued that if present evidence suffices to prove benefit, it also suffices for regulators to prove harm; PRO's decisive reply was that equal proof standards still involve unequal tasks—forecasting an independent rival versus documenting integration gains on a platform already used by millions.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): A wins narrowly because it kept the debate focused on the motion’s consumer-benefit burden and offered a plausible rebuttable standard: better-informed platforms can present objective integration evidence, while deals that cannot show substantial consumer benefit should be stopped. B’s symmetry-of-proof and overbreadth attacks were sharp, but A answered the pressure directly and closed more cleanly by treating founder payouts as distinct from consumer welfare. B’s two clipped turns also created a real execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttal 2 claim that prototypes/benchmarks for platforms should imply growth curves and internal memos for enforcers was the strongest pressure on A’s core asymmetry. A’s distinction between equal standards and unequal tasks, plus its consumer-over-payouts framing, was just enough to keep that route alive.
