### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a closely fought debate on the decisive clash. The core dispute became whether modest, equally divided checks can handle concentrated catastrophic costs. Side A's creative reframe — "pooling is a way of paying; provision is a way of supplying," with households buying private insurance — was the most original move of the debate, but Side B attacked it squarely: risk-based premiums, retail markups, and uninsurable ongoing costs like infant childcare would swallow a small stipend, and one check cannot simultaneously be rent, insurance, and daycare. Side A's closing restated the pooling/provision distinction without fully answering that pressure. Side B also answered both pressure questions directly and honestly (triage and phase-ins for the nurse shortage; bill elimination and workforce effects for the childless renter), even if the "nothing gets cut" answer was somewhat optimistic. Side A's rigidity and sunk-cost arguments were sharp and rhetorically superior ("cash lets supply follow people"), keeping the debate close, and B's phantom [C1.x] reference IDs are a real execution cost. But B's risk-pooling arithmetic and inelastic-market price-extraction case survived the exchange as the stronger live route, while A's insurance escape hatch was left partially blunted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B's closing attack on A's A4.1 insurance answer: B5.1-B5.3 argued that a diluted check cannot simultaneously buy catastrophe coverage, pay rent, and fund childcare, and that private premiums and uninsurable infant care would consume the stipend — A's closing (A5.3) restated rather than resolved this, leaving B's pooling arithmetic standing.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B more convincingly established that cash better fits radically unequal, time-sensitive needs and keeps every option open, including insurance and substitutes, while services pre-commit the same budget to forms many cannot use and still ration by queue or politics. Side A’s market-failure and capacity case was serious but only partly neutralized those points, and nonexistent reference IDs weakened its execution. The mismatch examples and the pooling-versus-provision distinction remained live and easier to vote on.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s first rebuttal reversed fungibility into a case for household choice and exposed pre-spending on unused capacity; its second rebuttal then separated insurance pooling from state provision, which Side A never fully closed.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate. A powerfully pressed monopoly pricing, concentrated costs, and the inadequacy of small checks, especially against B’s private-insurance answer. But B better dismantled the inference that risk-pooling requires public provision, exposed the pre-committed capacity and nonuser-value problems, and kept its heterogeneous-needs comparison alive through every round. A answered the required questions directly but increasingly repeated its budget arithmetic without fully resolving rationing or inflexible provision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal was most decisive: it showed that universal services spend on capacity before need arrives, turned A’s fungibility argument back toward household choice, and answered the supply-bottleneck charge with incremental private responses. B4.1 then sharpened this by separating pooling from provision, though A’s closing exposed its affordability weaknesses.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides were exceptional: same-budget fiscal discipline, no fake sourcing, and consistent A1/A2 compliance. Side A landed the sharpest technical hits, especially the fiscal double standard (A2.1) and the labor-bound care counter (A4.3) that damaged B's pooling-savings and "no cashable bonus" claims. But Side B's core route survived: cash cannot create supply where none exists, cash itself rations invisibly through prices while queues are visible and expandable, and the "survival trap" reframe in B4.4/B5.2 converted A's own concession (cash goes first to rent and food) into evidence that essentials stay unfunded until crisis. A's answer that this is rational triage was good but did not fully neutralize it, and A never convincingly showed how cash trains nurses or builds rural routes. B's closing crystallization ("building a floor vs funding a bid") made the choice clearer than A's equally skilled but more diffuse ending. Razor-thin edge to B on the live supply-creation and rationing-visibility routes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's B4.4, turning CON's fiscal objection back by arguing cash is also rationed — silently, by landlords, insurers, and prices — while a queue can be staffed and challenged, then crystallized in B5.2-B5.4. Side A's best was A4.3, showing that labor-bound care undermines B's "marginal additions" and "lowers unit costs" claims and that affluent switchers pocket a real cashable bonus.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): Side B won by pinning both policies to the same budget and showing that Side A's pooling story breaks once labor-scarce care and transit, affluent households shifting private bills onto the public, and rent-first survival are counted. Side A kept a live case that cash does not build missing routes or places and that price rationing hides need, but it never fully answered the windfall, mismatch, and triage points that remained decisive under the motion's fixed-money comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's same-budget rebuttal, completed by the point that extended hours and rural routes are labor-bound rather than cheap margins and that affluent switchers pocket a real cashable bonus when private bills move onto the public tab.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won a close debate by enforcing the fixed-budget comparison and exposing weaknesses in A’s claims that extended-hours care, rural transit, and affluent users could be absorbed cheaply. A supplied the stronger supply-side principle and a memorable price-versus-queue frame, but did not adequately answer B’s labor-cost, immediate-liquidity, and affluent-windfall objections. B more consistently explained why cash’s flexibility matters under the same fiscal constraint.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both pressure questions directly, then showed that childcare, nursing, and off-peak transit are labor-bound rather than cheap marginal additions, and that affluent households dropping private bills really do receive a financial windfall.
