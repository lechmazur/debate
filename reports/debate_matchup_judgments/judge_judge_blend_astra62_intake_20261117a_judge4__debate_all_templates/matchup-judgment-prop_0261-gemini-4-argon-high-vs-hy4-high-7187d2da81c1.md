### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): CON won by sustaining the legal and practical distinction between a chattel and a non-transferable license. PRO's strongest intuition—that a purchased digital library should not vanish at death—landed emotionally, especially when CON admitted an explicit parent still could not bequeath a single e-book. But CON absorbed that pressure by arguing the parent never owned a transferable right, and by offering narrower remedies such as disclosure, refunds, data export, and consensual rights-clearance. PRO's near-zero marginal cost and one-to-one footprint arguments failed to answer the open-ended successor-chain and ongoing service duties, and its limiting principle was exposed as inconsistent when perpetual software licenses were included but commercial software seats excluded. The privacy concern also remained live because default passage to next of kin reaches no-will estates, estranged kin, and creditors. Overall, CON made the comparative case for proportionality and legal coherence, while PRO relied on the poster-family example too heavily.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.4 turned PRO's own limiting principle against it: PRO tried to exclude commercial software seats as subscriptions but then protected perpetual software licenses, collapsing the line between purchased assets and personal credentials.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B won a closely fought, high-quality debate. Side A built a compelling fairness case (full "Buy" pricing, one-to-one footprint, the willing-parent trap) and landed a real concession in B4.1, but Side B absorbed that concession coherently: the "no" only matters if the e-book was property, and tailored consent reforms (disclosure, exports, opt-in designations) address the sympathetic case without a universal entitlement. B's sharpest hits went unanswered: the catch that A's own limiting principle includes "perpetual software license" (A4.1), which is exactly the software-seat case B had flagged, and A never resolved it in closing; B's poster-family-versus-default-rule weighing exposed that the motion as written requires statutory next-of-kin passage, reaching no-will estates, estranged kin, and creditors. B also defused the 85-year-old pricing question (uniform prices show sellers ignore life expectancy; the cost is legal substitution, not bandwidth). A's strong points—the windfall framing, the concession, the diary/privacy answer—kept this competitive, but A left the software-license inconsistency and the successor-chain duty standing, and used nonexistent reference IDs ([C1.3], [C4.1], etc.), a minor execution cost. B made the debate easier to decide with cleaner conceptual discipline and better weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's B4.4 catch that Side A's limiting principle ("static good, one-time price, low marginal cost") explicitly includes perpetual software licenses—the exact case B's pressure question targeted—was the decisive unanswered blow, compounded by B4.1's honest concession paired with the pivot from the poster family to the default next-of-kin rule the motion actually requires.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing the guarantee would create new successor rights and ongoing duties rather than pass existing property, and that Side A had no stable limiting principle once perpetual software licenses were included. Consumer remedies for misleading “Buy” labels fit the problem better than universal heritability. Side A’s fairness case was forceful but did not close those burdens, and it cited nonexistent reference IDs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal collapsed Side A’s asset-versus-credential line by flagging the included perpetual software license, and separated the sympathetic explicit-will case from default passage to next of kin.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): PRO won by keeping the guarantee narrow: one successor, one account, no new copies, no resale, and the same delisting risk. CON raised real concerns about cost, privacy, upstream rights, and probate division, but PRO answered most decisively with estate assignment or decline, title-blind acceptance, and the point that seller-side clearance failures should not penalize buyers. CON’s economic collapse prediction remained plausible but more speculative, and it did not neutralize PRO’s core claim that death-only forfeiture lets a seller keep the price while the purchased library disappears.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s A1 in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive: it defused CON’s unmarketable-license buyout problem by showing no valuation is required when probate assigns one indivisible account, leaving the platform still servicing only one account.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side A (PRO) won a competitive but clear debate. A's narrow rule—heir stands in the deceased's shoes, one account, no new copies, same delisting risk—survived B's successive attacks and gave A a consistent fallback position each round. A answered both pressure questions directly with required A1/A2 labels, gave a clean answer to the valuation trap ("no cash buyout is required," assign or decline), and resolved the market-collapse threat with the sharp distinction that "retirement for everyone is allowed; death-only retirement is not." B landed real hits: the pension/annuity counter to A's bank analogy was genuinely damaging, the privacy-inspection-before-decline point in Rebuttal 2 was original pressure, and B effectively flagged A's pivot away from sibling compensation. But B's core economic case rested on speculative predictions of subscription conversion that A plausibly reframed as an evasion threat that could defeat any consumer protection, and B's privacy case circularly defended the very death-expiry contract term A's mandatory-inheritance analogy had put in question. A's closing weighing (death alone doing what copyright, privacy, and resale limits do not) was the more decisive frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 was the decisive exchange: facing B's pressure on unmarketable-license valuation and the subscription-retaliation threat, A answered directly that no cash buyout is required and that "retirement for everyone is allowed; death-only retirement is not," collapsing B's core objections into seller-side friction rather than family-side harms. B's best counter was the pension analogy in Rebuttal 1, which turned A's own service-relationship example against the proposition.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B more cleanly showed that the guarantee collides with single-life pricing, upstream licenses, multi-heir allocation, and privacy inspection, and turned A's own concessions—that sellers may stop offering durability and that retirement for everyone is allowed—into a live reason the mandate would shrink rather than preserve buy-to-keep libraries. A's principled attack on death-only forfeiture remained coherent, but the later probate patches (no buyout, then title-blind acceptance) arrived as adaptations that B exploited without being fully neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal, which treated A's answers on valuation and upstream clearance as concessions that one heir takes an unmarketable bundle while platforms can abandon durability and retire catalogs for everyone.
