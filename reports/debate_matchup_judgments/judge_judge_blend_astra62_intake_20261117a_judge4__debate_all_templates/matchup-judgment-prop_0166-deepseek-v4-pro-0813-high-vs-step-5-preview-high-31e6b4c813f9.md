### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): CON wins a competitive debate by holding PRO to the motion as written. PRO opened by calling the trade-off "false," even though the motion stipulates real diversion. CON exploited that immediately. PRO then had to retreat to a net-return framing and shift its case across rounds: first "new money," then dual-use tools, then conditional co-funding authorization. CON's core distinction was never fully neutralized. Private biotech capital may be new, but it is not fungible with the scarce public inputs that stop extinction: land, regulators, veterinarians, and predator control. CON also answered PRO's strongest control argument, that refusal pushes the work offshore and unregulated. Governments can prohibit release, regulate tissues and facilities, and enforce biosafety without funding or legitimizing revival. PRO's closing asserted that restriction "cannot direct" offshore work but never engaged the point that releases into domestic ecosystems can still be blocked. PRO's co-funding mechanism was a creative and fair answer to CON's Q2. However, CON plausibly framed it as conceding the diversion and taxing it only after the public capacity had already been consumed. PRO also cited a nonexistent reference ID ([C2.2-C2.3]) and loosely characterized CON's claims in its Q1. CON had weaknesses of its own. Its moral-hazard claim was speculative, and it never quantified the net comparison PRO asked for. Even so, CON's case stayed more consistent and better anchored to the motion's burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A2/B4.3–B4.4. PRO pressed that refusing to fund or permit leaves offshore work unaccountable. CON answered that governments can control release, tissue movement, and facilities through biosafety law without funding de-extinction. CON also turned PRO's conditional-authorization plan into an admission that diversion happens first and is only partially recouped afterward. That undercut PRO's main remaining route to victory.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a closely fought debate by holding the motion's own price tag against Side B: the proposition concedes diversion, so PRO needed a proven net gain, and CON showed the net-gain story rests on non-fungible inputs. CON's pressure question forced PRO to admit no reserved donor dollars were actually diverted, and CON pivoted cleanly to the decisive metric—conservation output per unit of scarce public capacity (staff-years, permit review, veterinary and quarantine capacity)—arguing private biotech money cannot buy back hectares, water flows, or enforcement. PRO's best route was the conditional-authorization co-funding schedule (B4.2), an original and concrete mechanism, plus the offshore-leakage argument for public control. But CON's rebuttal that the schedule taxes the spectacle only after the capacity and public urgency have already been consumed, and that the same fence cannot save a quoll lost to clearing elsewhere, was never squarely answered. CON also gave a workable prohibition alternative (biosafety, import/export, facility permits), blunting the "unaccountable offshore work is inevitable" claim, which PRO asserted more than demonstrated; PRO's closing claim that restriction means "the diversion happens and living species receive nothing" was internally shaky since offshore private work would not drain public conservation budgets. Both sides followed format (proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2), stayed on the motion, and avoided fake sourcing. CON's slightly sharper cross-examination and cleaner weighing of proven protection versus speculative leverage gave it the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1–A4.4: CON's answer to PRO's pressure questions defined the decisive metric (public capacity per conservation outcome), showed private money is not fungible with hectares and regulatory hours, and dismantled PRO's conditional-authorization plan as a tax arriving after the scarce inputs were already consumed—directly damaging PRO's main live route to victory.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): A kept the debate focused on the motion’s conceded trade-off and made the stronger comparative case: private biotech investment and localized ecological co-benefits do not replace diverted public funds, regulatory attention, habitat, or specialist capacity. A also answered the offshore-work challenge with concrete restrictions. B’s conditional co-funding proposal was an ingenious countermove, but its claimed net conservation gain and assumption that harmful offshore diversion would otherwise continue were never adequately established.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered both pressure questions, distinguished non-fungible public conservation capacity from new private money, supplied a plausible prohibition alternative, and showed why B’s co-funding schedule could not restore staff-time and public attention already consumed.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): CON wins mainly by holding PRO to the motion as written. The proposition stipulates that de-extinction funding happens "even when that diverts money and attention" from living species. PRO opened by calling that trade-off a "zero-sum fantasy," which fights the premise rather than defending it. PRO later shifted to "diversion is not automatically waste," but by then CON had already fixed the frame. PRO's strongest lines were the flagship and leverage argument and the claim that denied funds may never reach conservation. CON neutralized both: - On the counterfactual, CON argued that even if denied grants don't automatically flow to conservation, the right response is to fund living species directly, not to take a costlier detour. - On spillover, CON showed that PRO's benefits depend on what permits "can" require, while the motion guarantees only the diversion. - On the "governments failed to fund directly" question, CON answered that living flagships such as tigers, condors and rhinos already carry fencing and breeding programs, which undercuts the claim that only extinct animals can mobilize support. PRO's climate argument also drifted from "planetary stakes" in the opening to a "plausible upside" in the second rebuttal. CON's pressure question exposed that retreat. PRO was rhetorically vivid and answered its pressure questions with clear A1/A2 labels. However, its closing largely restated earlier points and never overcame the "fund the tool directly at the living-species site" comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange is CON's B2.3 and B4.4 against PRO's spillover case. B2.3 argued that cloned ferrets and kakapo genetic rescue show endangered species themselves can be where the tools are developed, so a mammoth platform is unnecessary. B4.4 then pointed out that PRO's shared-infrastructure benefits rest on conditions permits "can" impose, while the motion guarantees only the diversion. Together these exchanges turned PRO's best evidence against it and left the leverage claim conditional.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by exploiting the motion as written: the proposition stipulates that funding de-extinction diverts money and attention from living species, and CON relentlessly pinned PRO to that concession. PRO's escape routes were all conditional — permits "can" require shared infrastructure, spillover "could" benefit numbats, denied funds might vanish — while the diversion is the one guaranteed effect. A's symmetry turn in Rebuttal 2 (if no mechanism guarantees reallocation, no mechanism guarantees spillover either) and the catch of B's retreat from "planetary stakes" [B1.3] to "not a climate plan" [B2.5] were the decisive blows. B argued well: the counterfactual pressure question was genuinely uncomfortable, the "extinct animals have no lobby / revival builds a constituency" frame was original, and the rhetoric was consistently punchier ("austerity dressed as virtue," "speculative austerity against leverage"). But B never neutralized the core problem that its promised benefits were speculative and unbuilt while the motion itself concedes the harm, and B's own admission that "PRO does not deny diversion" left the weighing to A's triage framing. Clear but competitive: B kept live routes open throughout and forced honest concessions, but A made the debate easier to decide on the motion's own terms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.4): honestly conceding no automatic reallocation mechanism, then turning that same no-guarantee logic against PRO's conditional spillover, exposing that B4.1's "permits can require" falls short of the motion's requirements, and nailing B's downgrade of the climate case from planetary stakes to a side effect.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A kept the debate anchored to the motion’s admitted diversion and showed that PRO’s benefits were conditional, speculative, or achievable through direct conservation and living flagship species. A’s second rebuttal exposed the central asymmetry: B demanded a guarantee that denied funds would reach conservation while offering no comparable guarantee that de-extinction would produce conservation spillovers. B’s shared-tools and infrastructure case was creative and plausible, but its closing repeated the weakened reallocation premise instead of answering that symmetry argument or proving net benefits despite the diversion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both pressure questions directly, turned B’s no-guarantee argument against PRO’s own spillovers, and stressed that permits merely “can” attach conservation conditions while the motion does not require them.
