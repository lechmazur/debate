### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): A (CON) won by resolving the central trade better. It turned PRO’s “freed wages” argument into a cash mechanism, exposed the tension between cash being both inflationary and too small, neutralized rent capture by showing service-freed wages also raise housing demand, and — most decisively — showed fixed-cost service capacity targets a planner’s forecast rather than the person’s actual emergency. PRO’s healthcare pooling point was real, but A plausibly contained it to one item of a package.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s rebuttal that empty childcare slots and unsubscribed fibre still cost the same broke PRO’s claim that services spend on actual need, and the counter that landlords capture service-freed wages too undermined PRO’s cash-inflation case.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side A won a razor-thin decision on the two decisive live routes: the healthcare concession, which B never contested as the largest item and answered only with the evasive "a pool is rules, not a spending line" under a motion that explicitly forces a same-money trade; and the flat-versus-stacked shape argument, which directly resolves the motion's "same money" comparison and survived B's forecast/capacity counter (unused capacity is a margin, not the core). A also resolved B's rent-versus-rounding-error contradiction pressure cleanly with "small per household, enormous per market." B was sharper turn-for-turn — the freed-wages flip, the floor-zero point, the mirror on landlord capture, and the empty-slot closing rebuttal that landed on A's "spends nothing on them" claim — and B's planner-versus-person frame ("fund her, not the forecast") was the most memorable crystallization. But B's own final weighing conceded the catastrophe domain, which A credibly extended beyond healthcare, and B's package re-weighing rested on the unexamined claim that the other three items dominate the budget. B's superior rhetoric and rebuttal craft narrow the gap without overturning it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's reconciliation of the rent-inflation contradiction under direct pressure: the cheque is "small per household and enormous per market" — every tenant receives the same sum the same day against fixed housing supply, so both claims hold at once. B's mirror (services-free wages are equally capturable income) was clever but only neutralized the clash, while A's resolution resolved a live burden.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): In an exceptionally crisp and responsive debate, Side B built the stronger comparative case under the motion's equal-budget constraint. Side A argued persuasively that cash is fungible for idiosyncratic emergencies and landed a clever counter-rebuttal showing that disposable income freed by services can also be captured by landlords in fixed-supply housing markets. However, Side B won the central structural and mathematical clash: dividing a fixed budget equally across every adult yields a modest flat cheque that cannot conjure supply-constrained network goods or cover major household costs, whereas universal services concentrate resources where high-cost needs actually fall. Under pressure, Side A largely conceded healthcare—a massive share of universal basic services, which cannot be expanded by "rules" alone without spending—and had to retreat from overclaiming what a monthly rural cheque could buy. Side A's closing objection that services pay for empty slots upfront did not overturn Side B's concentration logic, since capacity is sized for relevant cohorts rather than built for retirees and childless adults.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: When Side A argued that equal budgets make service expansion just as small as cash [A4.3] and that childless adults never use childcare [A4.7], Side B turned the second claim against the first [B5.2]-[B5.4]: precisely because non-parents do not use childcare, services stack a fixed budget onto families with acute need instead of diluting it across everyone—and earlier in [B4.1]-[B4.2] used Side A's own broken-transmission example to show why non-users still gain fallback security.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Side A built the cleaner path after the exchange. It conceded the narrow catastrophic-health exception but showed that one sector does not justify transit, internet, and childcare over cash. It also turned B's own smooth/scheduled test against monthly bills and exposed B's concrete service benefits as effectively conditional cash. B's capacity and risk-pooling arguments were real, but they never neutralized A's fit/exclusion examples: the rural worker, the grandmother behind on rent, and the parent who prefers an aunt.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal was decisive: when B was pressed to name what the grandmother, rural worker, and parent receive, the answer was "money back" or "gets paid." A leveraged that into the argument that services work like cash, but cash with conditions—making the direct transfer the cleaner alternative.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B won the exchange on fit and distribution. B's sharpest moves: (1) exposing that A's "smooth and scheduled" rule [A2.6] sank three of the four services, then noting A quietly rewrote it as "bounded exposure and the supply exists" [A4.1]; (2) flipping A's answer to the grandmother pressure question — when pressed to name what real people receive, A's answer was "money back in her pension," which B correctly characterized as cash with conditions [B4.5-4.6]; (3) holding the rural-worker mismatch as permanent (no transit route follows a home-care worker between clients), which A conceded in effect [A4.4]. A had real strengths: the rent-elasticity turn [A2.1], the "subscription to scarcity" framing, and the closing on inherited capacity. But B neutralized the rent turn (cash is not a voucher; can clear arrears or fund a move) and answered the cancer question honestly while scoping the concession to one corner of the motion. B's asymmetry argument — services must answer for off-list needs like rent because the motion spends "the same money" — resolved the opportunity-cost clash cleanly. Both sides had strong grounding and format compliance (Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 all present). B made the debate easier to decide by narrowing A's surviving case to catastrophic health insurance, which is not the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 [B4.5-4.6]: when forced to name concrete deliverables, PRO's answer for the grandmother and the aunt was "money back" and "the aunt gets paid" — B's observation that PRO's best case for services is that they function as conditional cash was the most damaging turn of the debate, and PRO's closing ("her rent gets paid either way") only confirmed it.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO)): Both sides delivered disciplined, rhetorically sharp cases, and Side B maintained real traction on collective bargaining, network infrastructure like broadband, and public capacity outlasting private consumption. Side A won clearly, however, through superior weighing on the marginal dollar and cleaner victories in the concrete clashes. Side A disarmed Side B's strongest point—catastrophic health risk—by conceding risk-pooling upfront while isolating it as one fraction of one service that cannot justify free buses, broadband, or universal childcare over cash. Furthermore, Side A defeated Side B's attempt to reduce the CON case to a temporary timing objection by proving a permanent mismatch of fit: poor households' largest squeezes (rent, heat, debt, deposits) are off the motion's menu, rural workers driving between scattered clients will never be served by fixed bus routes, and many families prefer informal or parental care without licensing hurdles.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's two-step takedown on rent and fungibility ([A2.3], [A4.5]-[A4.6], [A5.3]): after Side B argued that cash stipends just get captured by landlords ([B2.1]) yet later claimed free prescriptions pay the grandmother's rent by freeing up her pension ([B4.3], [B5.5]), Side A showed first that the landlord-capture claim proved too much (applying equally to pensions and income freed by free services) and second that Side B's own defense reduced services to conditional cash that only helps if a household's prior bills happen to match the state's menu.
