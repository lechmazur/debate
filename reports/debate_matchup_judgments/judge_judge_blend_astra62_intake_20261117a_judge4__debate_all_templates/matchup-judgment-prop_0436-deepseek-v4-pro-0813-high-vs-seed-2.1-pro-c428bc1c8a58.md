### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built the cleaner path to victory. B consistently framed the clash as "defaults are the product" — friction, not deception — and answered every CON escape route: the funding argument was met with a false-choice rebuttal (commissions, other ad formats, safe-harbor labeled slots), the filter defense with the observation that labels don't reveal what's missing below, and the vagueness attack with a workable outcome-based design standard (cheap nearby independents not displaced from the first screen solely for non-payment). B's pressure Q1 extracted a real concession from A, who admitted a near-fully-sponsored above-the-fold feed is indefensible but then leaned on market incentives — a soft, after-the-harm limit that B crisply dismantled in closing. A (CON) argued capably: the regressive cost pass-through weighing, one-tap sort friction, and pay-per-conversion auction math were genuine counterpressure. But A's case relied on several confident but shaky specifics (independents dominating listings, auction bids reliably tracking relevance, exploratory boosts for new listings) and never fully resolved the core burden: why price-sensitive users on a default feed should have to outwork the ranking. B answered pressure directly with clean A1/A2 structure; A did too, but A1 effectively conceded the target outcome and substituted a weaker mechanism for the rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q1 ("would you defend a feed where nearly all above-the-fold results are sponsored?") forced A into a concession at [A4.1], and B's closing [B5.1] converted that concession into the decisive weighing: market pressure operates only after a user has already overpaid, while the proposition targets the burden before it lands.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): B wins by defending meaningful discoverability without banning sponsorship. A offered a relevant revenue-versus-friction comparison, but asserted rather than established inevitable fee increases and overstated how reliably advertising auctions favor independents. B answered the labeling defense and effectively exploited A’s refusal to defend a nearly all-sponsored first screen. B’s threshold remained somewhat blunt, and preserving some advertising does not eliminate marginal revenue losses, but A never established that those costs outweigh the discovery harm targeted by the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s distinction between recognizing advertisements and discovering the cheaper alternatives they displace directly damaged A’s transparency defense. Its pressure question then secured A’s rejection of a nearly all-sponsored first screen, allowing B to crystallize the remaining dispute as effective discovery versus reliance on market pressure and user workarounds.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by turning Side A’s concession that an overwhelmingly sponsored default is indefensible into the decisive clash: market exit and user workarounds arrive too late and do not stop budget users from struggling to find cheaper nearby independents. B kept the proposition modest—paid slots may remain if they do not displace relevant cheap options—and adequately answered the funding and vagueness pressures with an outcome-based line. Side A landed real hits on enforcement fuzziness, fee pass-through, and auction incentives, but never fully showed why those costs outweigh bad defaults or why labels and one-tap sorts erase the targeted struggle.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 and closing, which converted A’s refusal to defend a nearly all-sponsored above-the-fold feed into proof that market pressure plus filters is too weak a check.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by keeping the debate anchored to the motion as written. The proposition only requires that sponsorship not be so heavy that users struggle—B explicitly disclaimed banning ads in the opening and later supplied a workable threshold (filters must bind; at most one labeled sponsored slot before the first matching organic result). Side A's central gambit was to claim B "abandoned" the proposition by reducing it to filter integrity, but this misdescribed B's position, which never demanded neutrality—only that ad bids not override explicit user search intent. A also leaned repeatedly on the fee-hike/revenue-loss burden, but B answered it in B4.2 (better alignment grows volume and platform value), and A's closing merely reasserted the cost as "unanswered" without engaging that response. A did land genuine hits: the supermarket shelf-space analogy answering B's Q2 was crisp, and the hyperlocal-bidding rebuttal to the auction-asymmetry point was solid. But A's own ground kept retreating toward "binding filters plus labeling," which B successfully co-opted as exactly what the proposition demands, leaving A defending little beyond the status quo's remaining sponsored prominence. B's pressure questions exposed real burdens, and B answered A's pressure questions directly and cleanly with the required A1/A2 structure. Both sides were disciplined and grounded; B made the debate easier to decide and controlled the decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The pivotal exchange was B4.1/B5.1 versus A4.3/A5.1: B operationalized "too heavy" via filter-binding plus a one-slot cap, then argued CON's preferred remedy is identical to the proposition; A countered that this concession means PRO abandoned the motion. A's reframing was rhetorically sharp but strained, since B never advocated banning sponsorship—so B's co-optation of CON's fix stood as the cleaner resolution of the round.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): B wins on the proposition as written, which permits sponsorship while opposing promotion that obstructs affordable local search. A repeatedly attacks a broader anti-sponsorship mandate and ultimately endorses binding filters—a remedy compatible with B’s principle, not a decisive alternative. A offers useful counters about independent advertisers and the distinction between influence and obstruction. B’s arbitrary one-ad threshold and unsupported promise of revenue offsets keep this competitive, but A does not establish that preventing the targeted obstruction requires the sweeping revenue losses its case assumes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest rebuttal was that enforcing user-selected filters implements the motion rather than defeats it. This directly undermined A’s closing claim that accepting some sponsored placements meant abandoning the proposition: the proposition never required eliminating sponsorship.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side B kept the motion's actual conditional standard in view: not a ban on sponsorship, but a limit on privileging ads so heavily that users cannot readily find cheaper nearby independents. Side A made a strong marketplace-cost case against burying ads, yet spent too much of the exchange opposing a broader mandate Side B had disclaimed from the opening. Pressed for a line, Side B gave a concrete one-slot metric and showed that Side A's preferred fix—binding filters and clear labels—implements rather than refutes the proposition. The late "abandonment" charge did not neutralize that comparison, and the fee-hike warning was never recalibrated to the modest standard actually defended.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal and closing, which answered the metric and revenue questions directly and then collapsed Side A's filter-integrity remedy into the proposition itself.
