### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B wins a clear but competitive debate. PRO’s best pressure forced B to admit that, under its regulated regime, the worker has no personal veto over a lawful bounded companion. But B recovered by turning PRO’s own power-asymmetry premise against the remedy: if the picker cannot negotiate, she cannot meaningfully refuse an “optional” companion either, and once she opts in, PRO leaves her to data-protection law—largely the safeguards B would require anyway. B also converted PRO’s acceptance of the fatigue monitor and drug-testing “fencing” into a concession that the real work is regulatory line-drawing, not categorical prohibition. A never fully restored a distinct harm of observation alone after B showed data limits, no discipline, and transparency could strip the performance-judgment dimension. The clipped sections were a real but minor execution cost; B’s visible argument still controlled the comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s decisive move was using PRO’s own coercion premise against the right of refusal: a refusal the picker cannot afford to use protects no one, while B’s safeguards bind the employer whether the worker says yes or no.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B won by making the choice easier to decide. A built a strong dignity case around coerced accompaniment and a memorable right to say no, but pressure forced A to let hospitals require always-on checking tools under a functional test and to leave opt-in logging to ordinary data law. Those concessions turned A's bright-line ban into the regulation B was already defending. B then used A's own power-asymmetry premise against the refusal remedy: if the picker cannot negotiate a job condition, a formal no she cannot afford does not protect her or her pressured colleagues, while limits on profiles, scoring, and discipline bind either way. Clipped turns cost B some completeness, but not the live comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal and closing after A said the hospital may require medication-checking: B showed that test was regulation, not a prohibition, and that A's own claim that the picker cannot bargain made the unaffordable refusal weaker than rules that bind regardless of consent.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): PRO won narrowly by drawing a workable line between bounded safety tools and continuous productivity companions, then crystallizing the decisive clash: CON’s safeguards could regulate monitoring but never gave the worker a protected right to refuse compelled accompaniment. CON’s overbreadth and voluntary-coercion arguments were strong, but they leaned heavily on hybrid safety examples and did not fully defend mandated productivity companions on their own terms; repeated clipping also hurt.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s pressure questions and follow-up forced CON to admit there was no universal off-switch and no blanket worker veto, which PRO turned into the central “power to say no” advantage.
