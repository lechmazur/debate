### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won the decisive clashes. It answered CON's pressure questions directly with the required A1/A2 format, offering statutory eligibility, suspension powers, and sunset review against the erosion objection, and effectively distinguished supervised center-based dosing from take-home opioid prescribing. Its concession that registry data cannot prove efficacy, paired with the trials-for-efficacy / regulated-access-for-safety division of labor, neutralized CON's strongest evidentiary objection. PRO also broke CON's population dilemma: severe treatment-resistant illness without psychosis, mania, or instability plainly exists, and CON's claim that high-risk comorbidity is "the norm" was asserted rather than supported. Finally, PRO's proportionality asymmetry—CON treating denial of care as risk-free—was never weighed or answered by CON, whose closing recap-style speech repeated earlier points without engaging the tradeoff. CON built a coherent cautionary case and answered pressure questions, but relied on universalized empirical claims and left the inaction-risk framing unaddressed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 (Rebuttal 2): PRO's A2 answer conceding that uncontrolled registries cannot establish comparative efficacy while assigning that job to continuing blinded trials—reframing regulated access as a safety-and-accountability question rather than an efficacy substitute—dismantled CON's central "no evidence outside trials" route to victory, and the same speech's opioid-analogy rebuttal (supervised episodic dosing vs. dispersed take-home prescribing) sharply undercut CON's erosion story.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A built the much cleaner path to victory through precise institutional design, smart concessions, and superior comparative weighing of status-quo harms against supervised, reversible risk. Side B raised serious clinical pressure regarding comorbidity exclusions, lack of a safety buffer, and potential trial siphoning. However, Side A systematically neutralized B's core objections by separating the epistemic role of randomized trials from the safety role of regulated care, adopting salaried public or nonprofit specialist centers with direct patient-reported registries, and breaking the opioid analogy. By contrast, Side B overreached in claiming that excluding psychosis and mania leaves only a low-severity cohort and leaned too heavily on asserting that non-trial protocols are inherently unenforceable paper rules.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's attack on missing control groups and provider underreporting ([A2.1], [A4.3]-[A4.6]): Side A conceded that registries cannot replace blinded trials for proving efficacy, then established a sharp division of labor where trials continue testing comparative efficacy while salaried specialist clinics use direct patient-reported follow-up to monitor safety, before dismantling B's opioid analogy by distinguishing supervised episodic in-clinic dosing from take-home prescribing.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): PRO won the central clash by separating safety regulation from efficacy proof: trials test comparative benefit, while licensed, screened, reversible access can provide accountable care. PRO also answered the comorbidity dilemma with severe patients who are stable enough to exclude specific risks, whereas CON largely asserted that high-risk traits are universal and that all safeguards must fail. CON's incentive and expansion concerns remained relevant but became less decisive after statutory limits, salaried provision, independent follow-up, and suspension powers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 division-of-labor answer to the placebo/control-group question was decisive: conceding registries cannot replace randomized trials, while retaining them for safety monitoring, neutralized CON's strongest evidence objection without overclaiming.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a clear but competitive debate. The central clash was the evidentiary standard versus status-quo triage framing. PRO's strongest route—patients already using unregulated substances while trials lock them out—was directly engaged: CON showed the trial-excluded patient is not automatically assigned to the black market, that regulated access may add a second harm pathway, and that PRO's displacement claim was conceded as unevidenced. CON also dismantled PRO's specific empirical assertions (comparable adverse-event populations, "markedly rarer" harms) by pointing out screened licensed clients and self-selected unguided users are different populations with unreliable denominators—pressure PRO never fully repaired. CON's answers to pressure questions were honest (admitting no displacement evidence while correctly placing the burden on the side proposing expansion), whereas PRO's answers leaned on asserted specifics and a surgery analogy that begged the question. PRO's urgency framing was rhetorically effective and kept the stakes vivid, but CON consistently held that desperation changes the urgency of research, not the burden of proof, and never let PRO identify a demonstrated net-benefit population. Execution was clean on both sides (proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 structure); the difference was epistemic discipline and sharper comparative rebuttal work from CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3–A4.4: CON's dissection of PRO's adverse-event comparison (non-comparable populations, missing denominators, acute-safety data not establishing durable benefit) and the surgery-analogy counter (surgical protocols are normalized after outcomes are established, not before) materially broke PRO's main escape routes from the burden of proof.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side B won solidly by dismantling each pillar of Side A's comparative harm-reduction case while maintaining superior epistemic discipline. Side A framed an urgent choice between supervised access, dangerous black-market use, and untreated suicide risk, and smartly defended intensive therapy in [A4.2] as an intrinsic component of the treatment rather than a confound. However, Side B showed that safer acute administration does not establish durable net benefit when trials remain small and functionally unblinded, trapped Side A in a dilemma on trial exclusions (expanded accessible trials solve scheduling and geographic barriers, whereas excluded comorbidities lack safety and efficacy data), and explained why screening refusals, high clinical costs, and newly induced exposure undercut net black-market displacement. Side B also candidly handled loaded pressure questions while exposing Side A's unsupported rate comparisons from Oregon and Australia, whereas Side A grew repetitive and left key forks unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side A claimed in [A2.3] and [A4.1] that early rollouts proved far lower serious adverse-event rates than unguided use by comparing mandatory session and integration reports against local poison-control and emergency department data, Side B ([B2.4], [B3.1], [B4.3]) decisively showed that screened clinic clients and unscreened illicit users are non-comparable populations, unguided use lacks a valid denominator or follow-up, and fewer acute crises do not establish durable clinical benefit.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): PRO framed urgency and status-quo harm well, but never established durable net benefit or displacement of unregulated use. CON exposed the expectancy/selection problem, showed screening and registries do not prove efficacy, reframed waiting as expanded trials plus care rather than abandonment, and kept the burden of proof on the proposed expansion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure questions and Rebuttal 2 dismantling PRO's claimed lower adverse-event rates: screened licensed clients and self-selected unguided users lack comparable populations, denominators, and follow-up, and acute safety alone does not prove benefit.
