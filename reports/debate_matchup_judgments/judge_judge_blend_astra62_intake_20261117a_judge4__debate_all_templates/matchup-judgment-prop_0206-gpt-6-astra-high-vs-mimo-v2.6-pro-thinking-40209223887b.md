### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON had the more vivid framing: averages conceal unequal longevity, and a later eligibility age imposes irreversible, concentrated losses while taxes spread costs. That framing kept CON competitive. PRO, however, built the cleaner comparative path to victory and handled pressure more honestly. PRO's Q1 exposed a real double standard: CON credited payroll taxes with exemptions and annual redesign while treating pension safeguards as illegitimate add-ons. CON's A1 reply, that a rate is "the instrument itself" while early exits form a "second program," only partly answered it. PRO's Q2 forced CON to admit that payroll taxes face limits. CON's answer moved to general revenue and capital income, which drifts away from the motion's payroll-tax side. CON's claim that PRO's concession "decides the debate" was overstated, and PRO's closing answered it directly: safeguards reduce net savings for displaced workers but do not erase the gains from people who can work longer. PRO also answered the ratchet worry concretely, saying the share of each longevity gain is set by legislators and cannot be raised without another legislative decision. CON's opening and first rebuttal were both clipped for exceeding the word limit, which is a further execution cost. The result is a clear but competitive edge for PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Q1/Q2 pressure followed by CON's A1/A2 answers. PRO's symmetry challenge on design additions, plus its question about what happens when payroll taxes hit their limits, pushed CON toward general revenue and a strained distinction between tuning a tax and adding a program. PRO's closing then turned CON's "savings shrink" point into "savings shrink but do not vanish," neutralizing CON's claimed knockout.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a competitive, well-argued debate through stronger comparative logic, honest fiscal weighing, and cleaner execution. Side A (CON) mounted a vivid distributional critique that indexing retirement ages to average life expectancy penalizes shorter-lived, lower-paid workers who fall through administrative cracks. However, Side B mitigated much of that risk via advance notice, long-contribution-history exits, and bridging support, noted that worn-out workers before today's pension age already face disability screening, and decisively showed that even if protecting vulnerable workers yields no net savings from that subgroup, raising the general age still generates real gains from healthier, longer-lived workers remaining employed. Side B's pressure questions also forced Side A to retreat from "mainly higher payroll taxes" toward capital and general taxation after conceding payroll limits, while Side A incurred minor execution costs from two clipped speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pressure questions [B3.1]-[B3.2] coupled with [B4.4] and [B5.2]: Side B exposed Side A's double standard in allowing low-wage exemptions for taxes while disallowing early-exit safeguards for pension ages, trapped Side A on its own admission that payroll taxes hit real limits (forcing Side A in [A4.2] to pivot outside payroll taxes to capital and general revenue), and refuted Side A's late turn that safeguards erase fiscal savings by distinguishing protected workers from healthier cohorts who work longer.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): This was close, with both sides competent and well-matched rhetorically. CON (A) edges it on rebuttal precision and on how the decisive clash was framed. PRO (B) had a memorable core frame: taxing work shrinks the work that funds pensions, and a payroll rate "cannot see" the vulnerable worker. Its strongest moment was the incidence point in Rebuttal 2. A gradual rate hike still lands on the cleaner from her twenties onward, whether or not she ever reaches the raised age. CON did more to narrow and resolve the debate: - It pinned PRO's own admission that savings come from "shortening" the benefit period, meaning cutting entitlement. - It challenged the automaticity of job losses. - It answered the "no targeting knob" claim with earnings-cap extension. - Most decisively, it turned PRO's bridge-income answer into a concession. If PRO must guarantee income to those unable to work, life expectancy alone cannot set when a pension is needed, and those payments cut into the claimed savings. PRO answered that with an unsized "affordable because targeted" assertion, the same flaw it attacked in CON's cap proposal. CON did make costly honest concessions. It admitted cap removal is only supplementary and that the cost may fall on low-paid wages. It recovered with an insurance-timing argument: losing some wages while employed differs from losing a pension when no paycheck exists. PRO's closing largely restated "the tax cannot target" without engaging the insurance framing. It also claimed CON offered "no mechanism," which overlooks CON's insurance and cap points. On grounding, PRO leaned on asserted employment effects, while CON's caveated mechanisms were more disciplined.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 response to PRO's bridge-income answer was the most decisive exchange. It argued that promising support where no suitable work exists concedes life expectancy cannot determine need, reduces the age link's savings, and that calling relief "targeted" is not a funding calculation. That turned PRO's own sizing demand back on it and left PRO's fiscal-superiority claim unresolved.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a sharp, high-level debate by building the more resilient comparative framework around social insurance and default design. Although Side B landed strong pressure questions forcing Side A to concede that lifting earnings caps is only supplementary and payroll hikes pass through onto low-paid wages, Side A integrated those concessions cleanly and persuasively weighed modest contributions across employed years against losing pension income entirely when work ends. Side A also neutralized Side B's predictability and demographic-ratio claims while trapping Side B's carve-out model in an unresolved dilemma: narrow disability or occupational exemptions miss workers with mixed careers or chronic pain, whereas Side B's Turn 7 expansion to early access whenever no suitable job exists undercut life expectancy as a governing rule and eroded its claimed savings.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Turn 6 pressure question (A3.1) and Turn 8 follow-up (A4.2-A4.6): Side A forced Side B to promise bridge support and early access whenever "no suitable work exists" (B4.1), then exposed how that expansion conceded life expectancy cannot track retirement need, shrank Side B's unsized savings, and still forced vulnerable workers to prove eligibility—while reframing Side B's wage-incidence attack as paying ongoing premiums for insurance rather than being denied coverage when earnings stop.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side B won because it made the central clash clearer: a retirement-age rule that can be adjusted and paired with targeted exemptions versus payroll-tax increases that Side A admitted could pass through to low-paid workers and were not sufficient through cap-lifting alone.
