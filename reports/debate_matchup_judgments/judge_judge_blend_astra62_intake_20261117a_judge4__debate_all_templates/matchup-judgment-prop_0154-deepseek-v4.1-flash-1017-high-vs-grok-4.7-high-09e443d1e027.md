### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): B built a more durable structural attack: training compresses works into weights in a way fundamentally unlike discrete-use licensing (radio, cable, mechanicals), so no non-arbitrary price exists, and the fair-use fork (if lawful, a tax; if infringing, a forced buyout that pre-empts the owner's actual remedy) was never cleanly resolved by A. A's rebuttals leaned on escrow/sampling and "a claim is better than nothing," but B's closing sharply caught A's pivot from "can this be priced" to "who holds a claim" as an implicit concession that the pricing/attribution problem was unsolved. B's pressure questions (identification vs. consent; fair-use fork certainty) exposed real gaps that A's answers softened but did not neutralize. A's case remained coherent and emotionally resonant (the "many vs. the litigious few"), but B's comparative framing—"a receipt is not compensation"—gave the cleaner, more decisive reason to vote against the compulsory scheme as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A2) and Closing, where B explicitly names A's shift from pricing mechanism to "who holds a claim" as a concession, directly dismantling the core promise of "payment to rightsholders" in the motion.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive decision by establishing that individual consent and litigation cannot compensate the long tail at training scale, then offering a plausible collective mechanism using inclusion records, sampling, audits, and escrow. Side B powerfully defended refusal and exposed imperfect attribution, but its alternative chiefly served identifiable owners able to bargain or sue. It never showed why rough compensation was worse than leaving most creators without an effective claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange followed A’s pressure question about paying millions of creators. B relied on voluntary licenses, injunctions, settlements, and walk-away power; A’s closing correctly weighed that these tools produce leverage for recognizable catalogs but no scalable compensation mechanism for the long tail.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A won by effectively dismantling Side B's core premise—the "right to refuse." A persuasively argued that for the vast majority of creators, this right is practically unenforceable, making the status quo a regime of free extraction. A also successfully defended the mechanics of compulsory licensing by drawing on established copyright precedents (e.g., mechanical licenses, escrow for orphans) and correctly noted that legislatures can define new compensated uses, bypassing B's fair-use trap. B's rhetoric about a "receipt" versus "compensation" was catchy but failed to offer a realistic alternative for the long tail of creators, ultimately conceding that their model leaves most authors with nothing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it cleanly separated the problem of identification from the problem of unanimous consent, and explained how legislative action can define a compensated use without waiting for a fair-use court verdict, effectively neutralizing Side B's strongest structural and legal objections.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued with unusual precision and sustained clash on the no-catalog author and the proxy/distribution problem, with clean adherence to the Q/A format. PRO's structural case (transaction costs make bargaining fictional for most creators, so a "right to refuse" is theater while a statutory claim is at least enforceable) was pressed hardest by its Q2 pressure question: if fair use eventually holds training is lawful, CON's status quo pays untraceable creators nothing. CON's answer (conditioning safe harbors or public procurement on negotiated licenses) was the weakest link in its case — it is narrow, largely inapplicable to private commercial labs, and does not actually solve the holdout problem CON's own voluntary-collective alternative depends on. CON's best counters — the "no general opt-out" concession and the closing point that "inclusion is not contribution" — were genuinely sharp and exposed real distributional weaknesses in PRO's scheme, but they attack the quality of compensation rather than rebut PRO's core claim that some enforceable payment beats an unexercisable veto or a zero outcome. PRO's closing kept the comparison anchored on the decisive case (the unrepresented author) and never let CON's alternative mechanism look like it actually delivers money in the fair-use-wins scenario, which is the harder case CON needed to solve. That unresolved gap is why PRO's path to victory survives the exchange more intact than CON's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 pressure question ("if fair use holds that training needs no license, your choke point pays nothing — what forces payment?") and CON's hedging answer (safe-harbor/procurement conditioning) was the most decisive exchange: it exposed that CON's voluntary-bargaining alternative has no clear mechanism to force payment from commercial labs once fair use is established, which is the scenario PRO's whole case is built to address.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by making transaction costs and mandatory payment decisive. Its distinction between setting the overall tariff by commercial success and distributing it by documented inclusion offered a workable, if imperfect, answer to B’s valuation objection. B powerfully defended consent and exposed the weakness of proxy allocation, but its alternatives—voluntary collectives, opt-outs, safe-harbor conditions, and litigation—never reliably produced payment when labs could refuse to bargain or prevail on fair use. A therefore better explained why compulsion was necessary rather than merely convenient.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s closing answer to voluntary collective bargaining was decisive: an optional bargain preserves the lab’s ability to refuse, so it cannot solve the core problem of making payment mandatory across fragmented rights. B’s consent objection remained important, but did not supply an equally scalable alternative.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side A won by grounding the debate in the practical realities of transaction costs and the unexercisable nature of consent for unrepresented creators. A's framing of the "veto" as "theater" and the choice between a "clumsy check" and a "perfect fiction" was highly effective and decisively shifted the burden. Side B made strong normative arguments about copyright's core and the difficulty of measuring marginal contribution, but failed to offer a realistic alternative for the vast majority of creators, relying on voluntary collectives and safe harbors that A successfully critiqued as inadequate replicas of the broken status quo.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it directly answered the pressure questions by conceding the lack of an opt-out to prevent holdouts, and explaining that the distribution split would track documented inclusion rather than publisher leverage, effectively neutralizing Side B's concerns about arbitrary distribution and passive claimants.
