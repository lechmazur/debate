### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side A won a tight, high-level debate by making the decisive clash cleaner. A's definitional squeeze — that B's own platform (hubs, links, services, care staff) requires place-based investment — was seeded in the opening, sustained through pressure, and never fully broken. B's best escape ("Inverness growth is concentration within a region"; "the stopping rule is the surrender") quietly swapped the motion's target from major metropolitan concentration to any regional clustering, which A correctly called out in closing: Inverness is a small city anchoring a rural region, exactly the motion's alternative. A also answered B's sharp failure-test pressure directly and credibly (Highlands example, concrete 15-20 year stopping rule), while B's answer to A's carer-staffing question ("care follows need"; "staff commute from that hub") was partial — it still required working-age residents in functioning places that B's relocation grants would drain. B's bounded-liability vs. annual-subsidy wager was its strongest live route and landed well, but A's response (care as decades-long rent when the town is emptied; consolidation into market towns is not metro concentration) held. B edged rhetoric; A edged argument and motion fidelity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Q1 exchange over the Highlands: B demanded one reversal case plus a failure test; A delivered Inverness/HIDB with a stopping rule; B countered that growth clustered around the hub ("my thesis with better scenery"), and A's closing reframe — that a state-built small-city hub hundreds of miles from any metropolis is precisely the motion's promise, and B's own carer plan depends on such hubs he won't fund — was the decisive turn of the debate.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): A wins by developing a motion-consistent alternative: viable regional hubs, not preservation of every settlement. However, its repeated claim that regional consolidation concedes the debate conflates local hubs with major metropolitan concentration. Its immediate-care comparison also does not establish why supporting those hubs is unnecessary. A leaves the stronger central route to victory intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response to the Inverness critique is decisive: growth concentrated around a small regional hub can sustain a rural region without concentrating it into a major metropolis. This also answers B's claim that consolidating unsuccessful villages automatically defeats A's proposal.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side A wins a close but clear decision by keeping the written choice intact: do more for viable regional centres rather than treat major-metro concentration as the default. ### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B won the decisive clashes. A successfully stripped away strict inevitability early, but B never needed that framing after explicitly disclaiming it and resting on cost-effectiveness. On the evidence, B's eastern-Germany counter (same toolkit, same transfers, youth still left) squarely neutralized A's one named success, Baden-Württemberg, and B plausibly defused A's replacements (Norway's permanent tax subsidy, thin wind-farm employment). A's cleverest trap — that granting rural clinics and colleges concedes the motion because hospital/school payrolls are the local economy — was met with the sharpest distinction of the debate: services sized to actual need versus payroll sized to keep a town alive, i.e., being cared for is not being made viable. A's closing metro-symmetry point (B's taper test exempts permanently subsidized metros) was a strong late weigh, but B's closing answer — public money following demand versus trying to summon it — plus B's scope discipline (polycentric city networks are not "smaller towns") left B's route more intact. Both sides executed format cleanly (proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2), used plausible hedged background knowledge, and crystallized well rather than recapping. A minor cost for A: the "nowhere, ever" characterization overstated B's stated rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Debate's most decisive exchange: A's pressure trap that in counties where the hospital and school are the largest employers, B's granted "decent services" already constitute viability — answered by B4.3–4.4's distinction between a clinic sized to remaining patients and a payroll sized to keep the town alive, reinforced by B2.2's eastern-Germany counter that dismantled A's flagship example.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B won the comparative spending argument: metropolitan housing reform addresses an identifiable constraint on existing jobs, while idle rural infrastructure does not itself establish employment demand. A's selective triage and objection to demanding subsidy-free rural success were substantial answers, but its payroll multiplier did not establish why additional place-based spending would outperform B's alternative. B sometimes inflated the motion into guaranteeing revival, which limits the margin; its stronger opportunity-cost comparison nevertheless carries the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to “payroll is jobs” was decisive: services sized to residents' needs do not necessarily sustain the wider employment base as population declines. A's closing repeated that nurses buy groceries without resolving whether that spending arrests decline or simply accompanies it.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): CON built the clearer path by forcing PRO’s case onto contested, limited examples and a triage rule that largely reproduces people-first policy for declining places.
