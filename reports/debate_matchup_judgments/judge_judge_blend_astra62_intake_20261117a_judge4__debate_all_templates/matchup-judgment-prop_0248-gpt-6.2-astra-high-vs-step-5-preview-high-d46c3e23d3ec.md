### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO wins on adaptation and a clearer route to victory. It built a careful mechanism: the aide is provided first, contributions are assessed later, affordability is protected, existing care is credited, and abusive or abandoning parents are excluded. In rebuttal it then neutralized CON's two main attacks. On regressivity, the aide-first design means the poor or childless parent is never left without care. On intrusiveness, PRO said acknowledged upbringing needs no inquiry, and that when upbringing is disputed, the agency must prove it or drop the claim. PRO's Q1, contrasting an adopted son with an abandoned biological son, forced CON to concede that conduct rather than choice is the relevant ground. That concession weakened CON's central "accident of birth" frame. CON's remaining line was that verification is always intrusive and that the duty amounts to a "surtax on parentage." This line stayed partly live but grew repetitive. CON also never squarely answered PRO's clean case: a son who admits he was raised, admits he can afford a share, and simply refuses. CON's closing cited nonexistent reference IDs ([P4.6, P2.3]), a small execution cost. CON was coherent and quotable ("monetizes birth," "the son does not deserve the subpoena"). PRO, however, made the debate easier to decide by narrowing it to the undisputed-refusal case and explaining why the public guarantee protects the mother without extinguishing her claim on her son.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2 ([B4.1]–[B4.4]) was the most decisive exchange. It placed the burden of proving upbringing on the agency and said that admitted upbringing requires no inquiry into why contact ended. It also separated evasion from refusal and showed that the honest sibling's share does not rise when another sibling hides income. That reduced CON's intrusiveness and evasion arguments to edge cases. It also exposed that CON's position gives the undisputed, affordable refuser the same exemption an evader obtains.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a close, high-quality exchange. Both sides honored format and avoided fake sourcing. B's path to victory ran through the special-claim argument: the son owes more than the stranger because the mother sustained him, grounded in conduct rather than birth. B's pressure question about the adopted son versus the abandoned biological son forced A to shift from "choice" to "conduct" as the ground, which played directly into B's "she raised me, but I'd rather not" framing. A's best route was the intrusion dilemma (verify upbringing = state sifts family history; skip verification = billing by biology) plus the "public guarantee makes the duty mere billing" reframe. B blunted both: in undisputed cases no inquiry is needed, and honest-sibling shares don't rise when others evade — leaving CON's rule granting the undisputed son the same exemption evaders earn. A's closing leaned on recap and used malformed reference IDs ([P4.6, P2.3] instead of the B-numbering), a small execution cost. B answered pressure directly and cleanly, made the decisive clash easier to decide, and never abandoned the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2: the A2 answer distinguishing refusal from evasion — noting CON's alternative gives the undisputed, capable son the exact exemption that only concealment could otherwise earn — combined with the point that the sister stopping care tomorrow would not end the brother's obligation, which defused CON's "surtax on cooperative children" framing.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): A won by narrowing the duty to cases where upbringing and ability to pay are acknowledged or provable, while pairing immediate public care with a contribution claim. This answered B’s intrusion worry enough: disputed or abusive cases can be dropped or exempted, and the agency bears the burden. B’s public-insurance frame and “surtax on cooperative children” line were strong, but never fully answered the clearest live case: an able adult child who admits he was raised and can pay, yet refuses. A’s closing crystallized that clash more decisively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal and closing neutralized B’s pressure by separating refusal from evasion, placing the burden on the agency to prove upbringing, and then presenting the decisive admitted-case scenario: “Yes, she raised me. Yes, I can afford to contribute. But I’d rather not.”

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON built one durable route to victory and carried it through every round. The two-nurse comparison showed that a family levy tracks which parent fell ill, not what the child received or what the child can pay. PRO never squarely neutralized that point. Its reciprocity answer in B4.1-B4.2 explains why children owe something, but not why one equally raised, equally situated child should owe more than another. CON also reframed public funding as mutual insurance, and PRO's "strangers pay" framing never recovered from that. PRO's other lines were turned against it. The "untouched heirs" line collapsed against PRO's own empty-estate premise. The tort analogy failed on the no-injury point. The sibling-fairness argument was rebutted because funded care, not collection from the brother, is what actually relieves the nearby daughter. Both sides used the A1/A2 format properly. CON's answers were more direct, explaining exactly how a tax-funded benefit pays the care provider and why taxpayers are not exempt. PRO's closing largely restated "family before strangers" without engaging the asymmetry. PRO's opening claim that children "created" the cost was also left unsupported.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A2.1-A2.3 followed by A4.3-A4.5. CON showed that once the estate is empty, "heirs" inherit nothing, and that the bill differs between equally raised daughters only because of the parents' illness. CON then traced the brother's payment to show it buys the sister no extra night of rest. PRO's B4.3-B4.4 response, that she is owed nothing and her labor is credited, did not answer what enforcement adds beyond cost-shifting.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by holding and sharpening a single decisive comparison across all five speeches: the differential burden tracks whose mother fell ill, not what any child received or did. A's "identical sacrifices, only one daughter billed" argument in A2.3 and A4.3 was never dissolved by Side B's reciprocity claim—B could only assert that the relationship, not the misfortune, is the basis, but the trigger of the duty plainly keys on illness, and B's closing restated rather than resolved this. A also defused B's best live route (the free-riding distant sibling) by showing that once public funding buys replacement care, the brother is not riding free on the sister's labor, and once her in-kind share is credited, his payment merely shifts who paid for the same night—giving her no additional relief. A's answers to B's pressure questions (public fund pays the provider directly; children pay into the pool via taxes) were direct and clean, and the closing crystallized the clash with the ex-ante insurance frame ("let need determine who receives care and means determine who pays"). B executed the format well and kept a coherent normative case (family before strangers), and B's pressure questions were pointed, but B's rebuttals often reasserted rather than answered—B4.2's tort comparison was muddled, and B4.3's "she is not owed wages" sidestepped A's actual question about what her relief consists in. No fake sourcing on either side; A's approximate, mechanism-based grounding was disciplined throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange around the nearby daughter: B pressed that only enforceable contribution stops the conscientious sibling from carrying the whole burden while distant siblings keep their money (B2.5, B4.3–4). A's A4.5–6 and A5.1 countered that once public funds buy the night of replacement care and her labor is fully credited, the brother's payment to the system changes who paid, not what care the mother receives or respite the daughter gets—neutralizing B's strongest route to victory.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B wins by making the decisive clash about who should bear unpredictable care risk. It gives a clearer public-care mechanism, answers the pressure questions directly, and shows that collecting from a sibling after public care mainly changes who pays rather than relieving the caregiving daughter. A’s family-first rhetoric is strong, but it never fully neutralizes B’s point that the duty imposes a second bill keyed to illness rather than to actual benefit or fault.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s reply on the nearby daughter and brother: public funds can buy replacement care and give her rest, while enforcing the brother’s payment only shifts costs, exposing the practical weakness in A’s fairness argument.
