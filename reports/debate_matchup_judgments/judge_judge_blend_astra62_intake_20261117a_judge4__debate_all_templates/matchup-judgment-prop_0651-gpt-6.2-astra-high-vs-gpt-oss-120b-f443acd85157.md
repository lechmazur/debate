### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): PRO won by drawing a sharp distinction between English as a shared language and English as a dominant gatekeeper of careers and incentives. That framing let PRO concede the benefits CON raised, such as outbreak alerts and international collaboration, while still locating a harm CON never touched: research questions abandoned before anyone writes them up, and field corrections that never reach the literature. CON mostly restated the benefits of a lingua franca, which PRO had already granted. CON also leaned on unverifiable or overstated claims, including "emerging royalty-sharing schemes," "empirical audits of grant programmes show," and English as "the only mechanism" for resource flows. CON's answers in the questions round sidestepped the actual pressure. Its A1 assumed that excluded projects find other funding, and its A2 quietly assumed that all operators already understand English. CON's closing brought in new elements such as venture capital without addressing PRO's incentive argument. PRO's speeches were concise, adapted to each new point, and ended with a clear reason the harm outweighs the benefit: lost inquiry cannot be recovered, while slower circulation can.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was the hundred-operators manual. PRO pointed out that operators who share a language need only one translation, and that teaching them English costs a hundred workers' time rather than one instructor's. CON's reply shifted to "when all operators understand English," which conceded the premise rather than answering it. PRO also asked how health workers' corrections are "built into" English peer review, and CON never answered.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A (PRO) built a coherent, original causal case: English dominance distorts incentives before dissemination, so translation after the fact cannot recover unasked questions. A's rebuttals were surgical — the "neutral vessel" point about who does unpaid translating, the visible-vs-screened-out grant counterfactual, and the shared-language translation point. A answered pressure questions honestly and directly with required A1/A2 labels, including a candid concession. Side B (CON) opened competently with the standard lingua franca benefits, but degraded over rounds: it invented unsupported mechanisms (royalty-sharing schemes, "empirical audits of grant programmes show a sizeable share"), misdescribed A's positions in its pressure questions and second rebuttal (A never argued for a hundred separate translations), and never engaged A's core counterfactual about work screened out by English requirements. B's closing doubled down on the funding-conduit claim with the overclaim that local initiatives "would collapse" without it. A made the debate easy to decide; B left its strongest burdens unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2: the direct concession in A1 ("I don't claim that... Listing the grants awarded tells us nothing about the value of the work screened out") dismantled B's survivorship inference, and the challenge "How, then, are those workers' corrections 'built into' peer review?" landed on a claim B never repaired. B's parallel answer leaned on an invented empirical audit, widening its credibility gap.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A won by making the decisive clash about incentive structures and lost knowledge clearer: English can circulate findings, but dominance can shape what gets researched, recorded, and credited locally. A’s pressure questions exposed survivorship bias and translation-cost assumptions, and A answered B’s questions directly. Side B offered plausible efficiency and funding-access benefits, but relied too much on optimistic downstream claims and an unsupported “empirical audits” assertion, leaving A’s core harm route largely intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 cleanly answered the pressure questions and turned B’s funding-point back on B: awarded grants reveal visible successes, not the local projects screened out by English requirements or the unpaid work of making findings usable at home.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B (CON) won by fixing the comparison the motion requires: English dominance versus a realistic counterfactual, not versus a cost-free multilingual ideal. B conceded the strongest PRO point, the unequal price of entry, and then showed that a higher cost relative to native speakers is not the same as a net loss. B also separated discoverability from erasure ("indexing is not editing"). Its distinctive "ability to answer back" frame tied its case to agency, not mere convenience. Side A (PRO) mostly repeated structural-barrier assertions. It overclaimed that infrastructure is "only" in English. It leaned on unverifiable specifics: a Kenyan startup losing months to translation and an unnamed UNESCO report. A also never engaged B's counterfactual. When pressed for a realistic alternative, A admitted that no multilingual alternative exists at scale. That admission undercuts A's net-harm claim rather than supporting it. A's closing restated its opening, and its final call to "dismantle this monopoly" was asserted rather than defended.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q1 asked A for a realistic alternative that would leave the Kenyan developer better off without English. A's answer conceded that such multilingual ecosystems "do not exist at scale," which effectively granted B's counterfactual framing. B's A1 then reinforced the point: without an English convention, the developer could face a different source language for each library. That exchange neutralized PRO's main economic harm argument.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by converting the debate into the right counterfactual comparison. A's case rested on asymmetry (non-Anglophones pay more than native speakers) and structural bias in indexing and prestige. B conceded the asymmetry honestly but showed it does not settle the motion: "A native speaker gets a better bargain; it does not follow that the Kenyan engineer takes a loss." B then supplied the missing comparison — the realistic alternative for speakers of small languages is not free multilingual infrastructure but dependence on intermediaries and translators — and framed English as giving the botanist the power to "answer back" and correct outsiders, a genuine gain in agency. A never squarely answered B's pressure question about the realistic alternative; A's Rebuttal 2 answer conceded localized ecosystems "do not exist at scale," which actually undermines A's own case that English is the harmful choice. A's closing leaned on overbroad claims ("searchable only in English") and a vague call to "dismantle the monopoly" without specifying what replaces it, which B exploited ("undoing the shared expectation buys neither the translation nor the guide"). B's epistemic discipline was cleaner: hypothetical framing, careful concessions, no invented statistics, versus A's vaguely sourced UNESCO reference, an oddly specific Kenyan startup anecdote presented as fact, and sweeping absolutes. B's closings crystallized the clash memorably while A's mostly repeated the opening.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 concession-and-weigh (B2.5): acknowledging the unequal price of entry as A's strongest point, then showing that paying more than a native speaker is not the same as being worse off, and reframing the metric as who gets to make and interpret knowledge — a move A never recovered from.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by making the decisive counterfactual and agency clash clear: English’s unequal cost is real, but A did not show non-anglophone users are worse off than in a realistic non-English alternative, while B showed how English can let scholars and developers answer back, correct outsiders, and avoid dependence on translators. A’s structural claims were forceful but often overbroad and less grounded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 answered A’s pressure by conceding documentation clustering imposes costs, then argued that abandoning English would expose users to fragmented source languages and remove the ability to query maintainers or correct misreadings directly.
