### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B wins a close debate by imposing a sharper test: corridors only deserve priority if they substitute for irregular migration, not merely respond to labor demand. A made a strong case that corridors build the identity, readmission, and employer-liability infrastructure for enforceable returns, but B answered that pressure directly, showing documented final orders can be executed through consular verification, reception assistance, and liability recovery without new recruitment quotas. B also exposed A's central evidentiary gap: legal take-up does not prove replacement of irregular hiring, especially when wage evasion is the real driver. A's closing reframed returns as the final step of a corridor system, but B's rebuttal before it showed that the final step still needs its own funding and that admissions cannot resolve accumulated final orders where legal entry already worked. The debate was competitive, but B gave the cleaner reason to prefer deportation capacity in the live bottleneck cases.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's handled pressure well by conceding that deporting workers does not cure a permit queue while showing the reverse: added permit slots do not cure wage evasion or execute outstanding returns. That exchange decisively turned A's demand-based argument into a burden to prove substitution, which A never fully closed.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides were disciplined and citation-free, but Side A controlled the decisive clash. A's core move — that the hardest parts of return (verified identity, origin-state readmission, employer repatriation liability) are byproducts of a negotiated corridor rather than things money can buy separately — survived the exchange. B's answer that returns-only deals and consular verification suffice was asserted, not mechanized, and B conceded jobs "can help" secure readmission without explaining why origin states would cooperate without the reciprocity A described. B's substitution test was genuinely sharp and well-pressed (especially the finished-project example and the take-up-vs-replacement distinction), and B's pressure Q2 forced A into a real diagnostic answer. But B's case increasingly assumed a world where legal channels already function and identities are already documented — begging A's central question — and B's "carrying out final orders at scale" framing quietly softened the motion's "mass deportations" without ever defending sweeps against A's rotation argument. A answered both pressure questions directly and in format, crystallized the comparison cleanly ("enforcement has nothing to stand on"), and won even on B's own ground by showing funded returns are the last step of a corridor-built system. B's closing was crisp but rested on the contested assumption that the last step is the underfunded bottleneck rather than the missing infrastructure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 answer to B's Q1: the reciprocity mechanism — origin states gain little from returns-only deals and absorb reintegration costs, so cooperation stalls, whereas corridors trade legal jobs and remittances for verified identity and advance readmission. B's B4.1-B4.2 response (fund consular verification; tomorrow's recruits need not cover today's orders) never neutralized the incentive problem, leaving A's executability argument as the live route to victory.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that mass deportations stall without the identity records, employer liability, and reciprocal readmission bargains that corridors create, so prioritizing corridors makes later returns executable rather than theatrical. Side B’s substitution test and bottleneck examples were sharp and well-grounded, but they never fully answered why origin states would reliably accept large returns without a jobs-and-remittances deal, or why enforcement alone stops vacancies from pulling replacements.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 answer on reciprocity: origin states gain little from returns-only deals, so corridors are what make documented, cooperative removal politically and practically available rather than a separable afterthought.
