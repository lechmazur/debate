### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): PRO built a coherent mechanism: the sending state controls a chokepoint, namely parentage recognition, lawful payment, and licence or bond access. PRO argued that operators who cannot be bound simply fail the "only when" condition. That turned CON's evasion examples into disqualification tests rather than proof of futility. CON's strongest line was the jurisdictional mismatch: harms occur on the ground, beyond the sending state's reach. CON paired it with a credible market-growth claim, that a legitimacy seal draws in risk-averse clients. Both points were live. PRO met them directly by arguing for continuing duties, reachable assets and bonds, and the claim that clients buy the legal file, which a broker forfeits by abusing the surrogate. CON never squarely answered PRO's Q2. Its A2 conceded that a ban does not address clandestine harms and gives an injured surrogate no forum, defendant, or record trail. CON fell back on a domestic altruistic alternative that PRO had already noted cannot meet all demand. CON's later speeches also largely repeated the "rebrand across the border" and "veneer" points without much progression. PRO adapted, narrowed the clash to leverage before pregnancy, and gave a crisper closing comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.4) answered CON's rebranding question directly: a new name cannot inherit a licence or parentage recognition. It then pressed the unanswered forum question, showing that a ban leaves an injured surrogate with no defendant, insurer, or record trail. CON's A2 partly conceded this point and could not overturn it.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A won the decisive weighing clash. B's core route—jurisdictional enforcement is impossible, and a state "seal" legitimizes and grows an exploitative market—was met head-on by A's choke-point argument: parentage recognition, lawful payment, bonds, and records are what agencies actually sell, so conditional legality attaches duties before pregnancy and gives law leverage a ban forfeits. A repeatedly exposed that B's ban leaves the injured surrogate with no forum, defendant, or payment trail, and B's answers (rebranding, local corruption, market-growth) were asserted as inevitable rather than established, sliding into the perfection test A flagged early. B's market-growth question was a genuinely strong pressure point, but A's reply—that risk-averse clients specifically purchase the enforceable legal file, so growth flows through compliant channels—was a credible answer B never dismantled; B's fallback that the gate "only controls homecoming" was answered by A's pre-pregnancy duty architecture. Both sides followed the A1/A2 format and argued the motion as written. A's case degraded more gracefully under pressure and made the final choice ("protection vs. invisibility as the price of doing business") easier to decide, earning a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.2), answering B's market-growth question: demonstrating that "approval" is valuable to clients only when duties are real, so scaling demand increases the value of compliance rather than evasion—this blunted B's most original argument and was never effectively reversed in B's closing.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the proposition’s conditional structure decisive. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): PRO had a real route to victory: bans do not eliminate demand, so prohibition may push surrogacy into gray markets with no accountability. It pressed that point consistently, and its Q1 on remedies for injured surrogates landed. B wins because it built and then exploited a sharper structural objection, namely that the decisive guarantees on parentage and long-term care depend on sovereign recognition and surviving institutions, not on anything agencies or clinics can supply. When PRO answered B's pressure questions, it raised the stakes with pre-transfer judicial and consular sign-off and permanent cross-border bonds held by a neutral body. B turned these answers into a clean dilemma. Either they are mere paperwork, and the limbo remains, or they amount to a hypothetical treaty-level regime that offshore markets exist to evade. PRO's closing never answered this dilemma and mostly repeated its gray-market framing. B answered PRO's questions directly: a ban offers an exit, criminal and medical law still apply, and the state refuses to build an enforcement pipeline. That answer is less emotionally satisfying but coherent. Both sides asserted unproven empirical claims about whether bans shrink markets. PRO's "well-documented pattern" and "routinely" leaned slightly more on unsupported authority. B also adapted more across rounds and narrowed the clash, while PRO widened its regime without defending it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 and closing exploited PRO's A1/A2 answers. B argued that formal consular and judicial sign-off and a neutral cross-border bond fund are not agency or clinic standards but treaty-level institutions. That leaves PRO defending either empty paperwork or a hypothetical harmonized regime. PRO never responded, and this neutralized its core parentage and long-term care claims.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Both sides executed cleanly, followed format, and answered pressure questions directly. But Side B built and sustained the decisive clash: the motion as written conditions legality on standards "agencies and clinics" can meet, while Side A's safety case progressively escalated to treaty-level commitments (pre-emptive judicial/consular sign-off in both jurisdictions, permanent cross-border bonds under a jointly accountable neutral body). B's dilemma — if these guarantees are absent, "enforceable" is paperwork; if present, PRO is defending a harmonized interstate regime, not clinic licensing — was never squarely answered. Side A's closing reasserted the gray-market comparison but did not resolve why its guarantees count as agency-and-clinic standards. Side A's strongest live route (bans leave injured surrogates with no remedy and children stateless) was met by B's honest, philosophically coherent answer: a ban denies contractual legitimacy while ordinary criminal, medical, and restitution duties persist, and recognition is sovereign either way. B also turned A's escalation of safeguards into evidence for its own thesis, a genuine argumentative reversal. A's gray-market pressure remains partially live, keeping this competitive rather than dominant, but B made the debate easier to decide on the motion's own text.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5-B4.7: Side B's second rebuttal dismantled Side A's A1/A2 answers by showing that pre-transfer judicial/consular sign-off and permanent cross-border bonds are treaty-level institutions, not licensing conditions, then flipped A's "bans need perfect enforcement" charge by showing A's own regime assumes perfect front-loaded enforcement.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by making the decisive clash about the motion’s limits: PRO’s strongest guarantees on parentage and long-term care ultimately depended on sovereign recognition, cross-border institutions, or treaty-like enforcement, not merely agency and clinic standards. Side B’s pragmatic harm-reduction case was strong, but its closing never fully answered A’s dilemma that these fixes were either beyond the named institutions or still vulnerable to sovereign refusal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered the ban-remedy pressure by distinguishing exit from contractual wage enforcement, then turned PRO’s parentage-sign-off and bond proposals into evidence that the safety case relied on powers beyond agencies and clinics.
