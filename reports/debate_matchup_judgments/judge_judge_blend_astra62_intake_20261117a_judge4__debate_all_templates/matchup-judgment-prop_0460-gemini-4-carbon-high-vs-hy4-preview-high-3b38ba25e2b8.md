### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO wins clearly. CON opened strongly: local vetoes are only one bottleneck, preemption may shift fights into courts, and centralized routing can create sacrifice zones. But the debate turned on CON's own alternative. In A2.3, CON added arbitration and a fallback that overrides a refusing community. PRO's Q1 forced CON to admit that the fallback overrides a holdout. That is a reduction in local veto power for the sake of buildout, which is what the motion endorses. CON tried to separate an upfront "default" from a last-resort "backstop." The motion does not require upfront preemption, though, so that line reads as a narrow reframe rather than a rebuttal. PRO also answered the upstream-bottleneck point well. Its argument was that siting certainty is what unlocks financing and transformer orders, so bottlenecks interact rather than sit independently. It also pointed out that eminent-domain disputes exist under CON's model too. PRO lost some credit for citing nonexistent reference IDs in B4.3 and B4.4. CON lost some for the late 2026/2029 illustration in its closing. Neither flaw changed the outcome.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's Q1 and its follow-up in B4.5 and B5.1, which pinned CON's "fallback if a community rejects a good-faith offer." CON's A1 conceded that one holdout cannot kill a corridor. PRO then framed the remaining dispute as only "upfront rule vs. hidden obstacle course," which collapsed CON's opposition to the motion.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): B better defended the proposition as written. A’s bounded fallback accepts reduced local veto power, while its claim that the motion requires unconditional upfront preemption is unsupported. More importantly, B explained why other bottlenecks do not make veto reform unnecessary and paired consolidated authority with binding community protections. A repeatedly asserted that upstream delays and local protections remained unaddressed despite those responses. B’s nonexistent reference IDs warrant a deduction, but its clearer comparative argument still earns a solid win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s holdout question forced A to acknowledge that its fallback overrides refusal. B then sharpened the comparison: both models retain a central backstop, but A adds disputes over whether offers qualify as good faith. B also directly answered the litigation objection by distinguishing county consent from individual landowner condemnation disputes.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO won the decisive clash over what happens after negotiation fails. CON admitted its fallback can override a refusing county, weakening its litigation and sacrifice-zone objections, since it ultimately relies on the same backstop. PRO also answered the upstream-delay charge by pairing siting certainty with federal deadlines and explaining how certainty unlocks financing and equipment orders, while CON largely repeated that upstream bottlenecks remain. CON's default-versus-fallback distinction was real, but PRO showed it mainly adds sequential disputes before the same override.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's pressure question on CON's fallback, developed in Rebuttal 2, was most decisive: it forced CON to concede that a holdout cannot ultimately kill a corridor, narrowing the debate to timing rather than whether veto power should yield.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON wins narrowly. Both sides were competent, and PRO had the cleaner master frame: concentrated burdens can be compensated, while delay imposes unpriced costs on millions. CON, however, did more damaging work inside the exchange. Its strongest move turned PRO's own admission that "the default route will proceed" against PRO's promise of binding consultation. If the route proceeds anyway, the protections look like paper, and communities lose their bargaining leverage. CON also supplied a concrete causal story: regulated utilities earn returns on capital spending, so they favor expensive greenfield lines. It then answered PRO's best pressure question, about what mechanism disciplines utilities, with "differential friction." That answer was plausible, even if PRO's closing partly reframed it as admitting the veto works through delay. CON's closing question about what forces a profit-driven utility to mitigate went largely unanswered. PRO did land real hits. It flagged CON's tension between calling review "paper" and warning of long injunctions. It also reasonably argued that wires can serve willing generation hosts. Even so, it never fully rebuilt why mitigation would be generous without leverage. Both sides misused a few reference IDs, a minor and roughly equal cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.1/A4.3 exploited PRO's concession that deadlines work "because the default route will proceed" and that binding review only requires showing regulators "weighed" alternatives. That recast PRO's "veto, not voice" distinction as consultation without leverage. PRO's closing counter, that differential friction admits the veto works by imposing delay, was strong but came late.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): B wins narrowly on the unresolved collective-action problem: indispensable regional lines can remain blocked despite benefits to people outside the vetoing locality. A effectively explained differential friction and exposed the gap between considering mitigation and requiring it. But A did not adequately answer why regional surplus would reliably secure timely consent rather than invite serial holdouts. Its claims that existing corridors prevent grid paralysis and that preemption triggers disabling generation bans were also too categorical. B therefore retained the stronger comparative case for bounded regional authority despite imperfect safeguards.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s complements-not-substitutes response, reinforced by its closing holdout argument, challenged whether A’s alternative could deliver necessary new connections. A successfully explained how veto friction encourages upgrades, but that explains developer incentives—not how indispensable new routes overcome persistent refusal.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won by turning A's speed claim against itself: binding safeguards either invite judicial halts or shrink to a record that regulators merely “weighed” alternatives, while differential friction gives veto power a concrete role in steering utilities toward existing and public corridors or genuine compensation. A defended compensated regional approval well, but never fully resolved that enforceability-versus-speed dilemma or B's backlash-to-generation argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: its differential-friction answer explained how keeping vetoes only over new private corridors disciplines utilities, and it exposed A's “binding” review as largely a paper record utilities can clear before condemnation.
