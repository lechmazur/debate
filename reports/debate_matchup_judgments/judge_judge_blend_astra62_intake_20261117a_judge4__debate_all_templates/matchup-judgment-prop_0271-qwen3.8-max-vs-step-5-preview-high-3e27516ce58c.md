### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): PRO wins clearly because it set a tight scope early: a parental veto over the engagement layer, with safety and accessibility features left intact. It then defended that scope consistently. PRO's coercion point, that a parent's only real option is to ban the whole app, survived CON's "buy a locked-down device" reply. PRO turned that reply into a concession that parents must pay to exit. CON's main route claimed recommendation engines are an indivisible "delivery spine" for safety, so turning them off would degrade protection. PRO's unbundling answer in A1, where the platform provides the floor and the parent sets a stricter ceiling, squarely met that claim. CON then partly conceded it by calling for regulation that mandates a "non-profiling safety floor." That proposal undercuts CON's own claim that profiling is necessary for safety. CON's strongest material was the longitudinal grooming-detection argument. PRO reframed it effectively as proof that protection has been made hostage to persuasion. CON also drifted toward attacking a "decisive veto over architecture," which is a stronger demand than the motion's call for "more power." CON admitted parents should have more influence and leaned on existing supervised accounts without showing those accounts already provide this power. CON's questions also contained a miscited reference ("P2.4"). Both sides had reasonable grounding. PRO made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B4.1–B4.2 answering CON's Q1. PRO argued "separation, not deletion": parents would veto behavioral ranking, autoplay and "for you" feeds, while age gates, filters and text-to-speech stay mandatory. PRO added that if an app claims safety requires profiling, it has admitted fusing protection with persuasion. This neutralized CON's delivery-spine argument. CON's later fallback, that regulation should mandate a non-profiling safety floor, effectively conceded that the two layers can be separated.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate. A defined the proposition narrowly and defensibly: a parental veto over the engagement layer (behavioral ranking, autoplay, infinite scroll) while mandatory safety floors stay intact. This framing absorbed B's best attack—the claim that recommendation engines are an indivisible "delivery spine"—by answering both pressure questions directly with the "separation, not deletion" model and the "platform provides the floor, parent provides the stricter ceiling" comparison. Critically, B's own remedy (regulation mandating a non-profiling safety floor) implicitly concedes that safety without behavioral profiling is achievable, weakening B's core technical-fusion claim; A exposed this by noting that a platform insisting safety requires profiling has fused protection with persuasion by choice. B's secondary arguments were weaker: the "buy another device / withhold hardware" exit framing conceded the coercion problem, the surveillance aside was thin, and B never explained why platform duties and a parental veto are mutually exclusive rather than complementary. B's strongest surviving line—loss of longitudinal signal for detecting cross-child harms like grooming—was met with a plausible answer (platforms patch within the boundary; the veto targets amplification, not safety calibration) that A maintained through closing. Both sides followed format, used A1/A2 structure, and avoided fake precision. A did more comparative weighing and left the decisive question—"may app makers make safety the price of engagement profiling?"—answered in A's favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B's pressure question demanding a technical mechanism for preserving safety after parents disable profiling, met by A4.1–A4.3 ("separation, not deletion": veto the persuasion layer, keep age gates, filters, and accessibility mandatory) and then B4.1's counter that only engineers can unbundle code—a counter undermined by B's own B4.2 call for regulation mandating a non-profiling safety floor, which A crystallized in closing as proof that the fusion is a design choice, not a technical necessity.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A built the stronger path to victory by keeping the motion focused on a binding parental veto over engagement-driven recommendation features, not parental control of all safety engineering. Its platform-floor/parental-ceiling framework accommodated regulation and expert patching while answering coercion and conflicting incentives. Side B raised a serious technical objection about intertwined safety and ranking systems, but too often treated every block or delay as total profiling withdrawal and repeatedly asserted, rather than established, that protective functions could not survive without personalized recommendations.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 was decisive: “separation, not deletion” directly answered both pressure questions by distinguishing mandatory safety and accessibility from behavioral ranking, then explaining how platforms could continue patching harms within a parent’s stricter boundary. B’s response identified a real implementation burden but largely repeated the indivisibility assumption instead of neutralizing that distinction.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a close debate. The motion sets a modest bar: parents should have more power than app makers currently allow. PRO stayed close to that bar and framed its proposal as a reversible "lever, not a lock." Its strongest support was the incentive argument: engagement-funded companies should not be the final referee of a child's stopping cue. That point stayed live throughout, and CON only answered it with "imperfect defaults constrained by rules." CON had the sharpest single move, a dilemma. If PRO wants only a bedtime pause, existing curfews already cover it. If PRO wants more, it becomes the kill-switch that removes curated paths. That dilemma did real damage. However, CON's case rested on two speculative claims: - that recommendations are the "safest path"; - that blocked children migrate to weaker apps. PRO contested both credibly. CON also drifted toward conceding the motion. It said parents "deserve transparent controls," endorsed autoplay restrictions as the answer, and admitted parents should control "when and whether a child uses a feed." PRO seized on that last line in its closing. PRO also distinguished clock-based curfews from recommendation momentum inside allowed time. PRO did have weaknesses. It repeated the "10 p.m. dashboard" refrain, and it never fully answered the concern that an indefinite delay could function as a kill-switch. Even so, its route to the ballot was cleaner and better matched to the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was CON's dilemma (A4.2/A5.1) against PRO's reply (B5.1–B5.2). CON argued that session limits and curfews already cover PRO's harms. PRO answered that curfews regulate the clock, not the recommendation layer within allowed time. PRO also turned CON's own concession that parents should control "when and whether" a child uses a feed. That reply blunted CON's best line and showed CON partially conceding the motion's core.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side A won the decisive clash. B's core route was the dilemma that a parental veto is either redundant (curfews and session limits already handle bedtime/autoplay) or a crude kill-switch that pushes children to manual search and weaker apps. A neutralized both prongs: the "lever not lock" framing with one-tap reversibility undercut the kill-switch characterization, and A's closing directly answered the redundancy prong by noting that curfews answer the clock and session limits answer duration, but neither addresses a recommender that adapts within allowed time. A also executed the format cleanly (direct A1/A2 answers) and landed a genuine reversal on the inequality point—arguing standardized overrides equalize what only tech-savvy families can currently approximate—which B answered only partially with the "overwhelmed parent won't fine-tune" claim. B stayed competitive with the "vote for outcomes, not control symbolism" frame and the pipeline-audit point, but the migration harm remained speculative and A's reframing of the comparison (today's no-exit feed vs. hypothetical risky search) blunted it. Both sides used plausible background reasoning without fake precision; A's imagery (steering wheel, dashboard vs. parent at 10 p.m.) made the stakes more graspable without substituting for argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.4) answering B's pressure questions: the lever-vs-lock response to the indefinite-delay/kill-switch question and the reframing of the migration worry around "today's reality: a child pulled through a personalized stream with no parental exit" was the exchange that broke B's strongest route.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side A gave a compelling account of engagement incentives and parental authority, with effective replies on delay, reversibility, and unequal technical capacity. Side B, however, built the stronger welfare comparison: recommendation-specific vetoes can sacrifice useful curation, while A’s clearest harms are addressed more directly by curfews, session limits, and autoplay restrictions. A never squarely showed that migration to search or weaker apps would be safer, often treating parental choice as itself proof of safety. B’s closing necessity dilemma therefore survived more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: if A means only a brief bedtime pause, existing targeted controls make the motion unnecessary; if A means sustained blocking or delay, B’s concerns about lost curation and blunt, unevenly managed vetoes return. A’s subsequent answers largely restated parental authority rather than defeating that dilemma.
