### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): B wins the distinction between making resilience desirable and making it a prerequisite for solvency. A's pre-agreed emergency clause genuinely answers the claim that automatic rules cannot accommodate disasters. But A never resolves why relief tied to equivalent investment milestones will be sufficient or timely when debt exceeds a government's investment capacity. B sharpens this mismatch and offers relief alongside adaptation finance, which A repeatedly mischaracterizes as merely hoping adaptation happens. B's clipped rebuttals and overstatement about unavoidable discretion are weaknesses, but its central challenge survives A's closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's closing correctly identifies that A's statement that debt service shrinks as verified investment rises confirms, rather than rebuts, the investment-capacity cap. This turns A's implementation clarification into a decisive question: why should the amount of necessary debt relief depend on how quickly resilience projects can be completed?
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling every iteration of Side A's conditional mechanism while defending a cleaner policy alternative (unconditional solvency relief paired with separate adaptation finance). When Side A initially required verified resilience spending before releasing relief, Side B exposed the liquidity trap of asking broke governments to spend money they do not have. When Side A pivoted to creditors funding seawalls directly, Side B showed that leaves old debt service draining the treasury; when Side A finally specified dollar-for-dollar debt cancellation as milestones are met, Side B proved this caps debt relief at physical construction capacity and leaves deeply insolvent countries in distress. Side B also broke Side A's automaticity advantage by showing that slow-onset disasters and project certification still rely on creditor judgment, and effectively turned Side A's dismissal of "imports and consumption" (fuel, grain, wages, clinics) and "portfolio insurance" framing into liabilities. Even factoring in a modest execution cost for having the final words of both rebuttals clipped, Side B decisively controlled the central clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B4.7]-[B4.8] and [B5.3]-[B5.4], Side B seized on Side A's clarification in [A4.3] that verified resilience spending cancels "an equivalent amount of debt service" as milestones are met, demonstrating that this rations relief by how much concrete a broke state can pour rather than how deep its insolvency runs—and then caught Side A's closing response [A5.3] literally restating that cap instead of refuting it.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won the central clash: PRO's condition adds a gate in front of relief rather than seawalls, since adaptation can be funded alongside unconditional relief for unsustainable debt. CON also sustained the liquidity and rationing critique—relief tracking verified spending first leaves deeply insolvent states paying—and used PRO's emergency suspension to show the rule binds mainly when least needed. PRO's credit-risk and repeat-default case remained plausible but never proved why contingency beats parallel adaptation finance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it turned PRO's emergency clause and 'equivalent amount' cancellation into concessions that the test is suspended in crises and relief is capped by a broke government's construction capacity.
