### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B wins because A's bright line was materially undermined. A argued an absolute ban on orbital strikes was safer and more enforceable, but B showed A had to permit suborbital kinetic tests to preserve its testing-cost answer, and those near-orbital suborbital strikes could still eject fragments into orbit. A's fallback that the line could be tightened conceded that safely requires looking beyond orbital status to physical consequences, which is closer to B's precautionary standard. B also supplied a plausible low-energy co-orbital design that could qualify under its hazard-based standard, keeping the overbreadth objection live. A's irreversibility and verification points were strong, but B converted them into consistency and burden problems rather than decisive grounds for banning every kinetic test.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive exchange was the turn on A's suborbital exemption: A relied on uncertain ejecta to reject all orbital tests but assumed suborbital target fragments would not reach orbit. B's point that a sufficiently energetic near-orbital suborbital strike could change fragment trajectories exposed A's bright line as a target-based rule rather than a true hazard-based safeguard.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A won on the clearest decision rule of the debate: asymmetric error-weighing. A established that an overbroad ban costs states a marginal data point recoverable via suborbital impacts, while a failed permit decision under B's regime produces permanent, shared, unrecoverable damage — crystallized by A's closing seize on B's own concession, "Nothing recalls debris after a mistaken approval." A's pressure questions forced B to defend its regime with a purely hypothetical qualifying test, which A exploited effectively. B's best live route — that A's suborbital exemption is inconsistent with A's own ejecta-uncertainty argument, and that suborbital impact tests arguably still count as "all kinetic ASAT weapons tests" — was a genuine and original hit, and B pressed it consistently through B2, B4, and the closing. But A's answers held: the physics answer (a suborbital target's fragments inherit suborbital velocity and face a far steeper climb to orbit) is plausible and directly dissolves the symmetry claim, and the purpose-based reading of "all" is a defensible interpretive move. B's precautionary default-refusal standard was coherent and well-grounded, but B never fully neutralized the gaming/incentive problem with pre-impact forecasts built on classified interceptor data. A's rhetorical execution ("loophole with paperwork," "Was the object in orbit? Then don't shoot it") made the clash easier to decide. A's repeated clipped turns (opening, both rebuttals) are a real execution cost and keep this from being a solid win, but the visible substance still carried the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's Rebuttal 2 / Closing against B's suborbital-symmetry attack: B argued A cannot invoke uncertain ejecta against orbital tests while assuming fragments fall back from suborbital ones, but A's closing answered with concrete physics — fragments from a target lacking orbital velocity face a far steeper climb to stay aloft — converting B's strongest consistency attack into a reason to tighten rather than license.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side B better showed that an observable ban on strikes against orbiting objects more reliably prevents irreversible shared debris than a predictive permit system. Side A landed real definitional and suborbital-exemption pressure, but never made the permit regime practically workable or showed that its remaining “clean” tests were more than a marginal hypothetical. Clipped speeches modestly hurt B’s execution without erasing that comparative edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure that nothing recalls debris after a bad forecast, combined with the demand for one real test meeting A’s own standard, forced only a low-energy hypothetical and left the permit door looking more dangerous than useful.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by narrowing PRO's case to a small residual risk: tests forecast to be clean that unexpectedly create long-lived debris. PRO's "authorized gamble" argument was real, but B showed that a debris-focused rule with conservative thresholds and strict liability addresses the same harm without requiring the broader category ban that entangles missile defense and deters key testing powers from joining. PRO admitted that without relevant powers the treaty cannot stop their tests and that neither rule prevents all cheating. B converted those concessions into a decisive participation problem: a treaty that the main testers reject mainly binds states that would not conduct the tests anyway. PRO's final appeal to reciprocal obligations was plausible but speculative against B's sharper feasibility and targeting comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive exchange was turning PRO's own admission that the treaty "cannot stop their tests" without the relevant powers into the participation dilemma, then pairing it with the dual-use point that observable conduct lets the same interceptor be proven suborbital. That reduced PRO's margin to uncertain clean tests while making the broader treaty look both harder to adopt and narrowly protective.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): B won by progressively narrowing the dispute to a clean comparison: PRO's treaty only adds value over a debris-based rule in the narrow case of a test forecast clean but turning dirty, and B answered that with conservative thresholds plus strict liability. B also exposed PRO's concession that a treaty without the actual testing powers "cannot stop their tests," and PRO's only reply — that refusal is "a prediction, not an established barrier" — never neutralized the participation problem. PRO's best live line (irreversibility: ex post liability can't undo debris) was met by B's closing observation that a banned test that happens anyway leaves the same debris, so both rules ultimately shape pre-launch choices. B's dual-use dilemma on "all" and the conduct-rule counteranalysis were original and damaging. B's execution cost: three clipped turns, but the clipped tails rarely cut off needed argument. A was consistent and honest but kept conceding ground each round without regaining the comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 [B2.1-B2.5]: catching PRO in the line-drawing contradiction — PRO banned "all" tests to escape definitional disputes, then introduced targets/trajectories/intent judgments — and reframing the debris rule as intent-free and objectively verifiable ("fragments are the one thing outsiders can track and count"), which set the terms for the rest of the debate and forced PRO's key concessions in A4.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A better showed that banning all kinetic ASAT tests creates dual-use and participation problems that keep the relevant testers unbound, while a debris-focused rule covers the cascade harm the motion names at lower political cost. Side B's irreversibility and "authorized gamble" points were forceful but did not outweigh those defects or establish that this specific treaty would actually prevent the harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 used Side B's own concessions—that debris-free tests add no cascade risk and that substantial protection requires the testing powers—to shrink B's remaining advantage to a narrow forecast-uncertainty margin not worth the adoption barrier.
