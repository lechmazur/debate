### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON wins on the strength of a timing dilemma it maintained from Rebuttal 1 onward. The disclosure is either late enough to be accurate, in which case the A-over-B comparison injury PRO named has already happened, or early enough to shape comparison, in which case conditional charges become estimates or padded figures. PRO's attempts to escape were thinner. The "middle zone" and "whole session" comparison answer was met by the point that rebuilding a cart is not cross-seller comparison. The "compounding learning" claim was answered by noting that an itemized final screen already informs the next purchase. PRO's best move was turning the "fees buried in base prices" point into a win. CON partly neutralized that by distinguishing the motion, which requires early disclosure of service charges, from an all-in pricing mandate, and by naming conditional-order buyers as the harmed party. PRO's closing restated its reading of the stakes rather than defeating the dilemma; it leaned on CON "admissions" that CON had already contextualized. PRO also had three turns clipped for length, a real execution cost: its opening and both rebuttals lost their final thoughts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1–A2.2 was decisive. CON seized on PRO's own clarification that disclosure happens only after the inputs are resolved, and showed that this "saves accuracy by surrendering the injury." This framed the too-late/too-early dilemma, which PRO never fully escaped. CON's Q1 and its A1 answer in A4.1, which conceded the number is exact but insisted the dilemma survives, reinforced the point.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by proving a fatal mismatch between Side A's claimed injury and its proposed remedy. Once Side A answered conditional-fee concerns by placing disclosure at an order-review screen after basket, address, party size, and seats are fixed, Side B showed that the fee appears too late to prevent the initial cross-seller distortion or sunk browsing time—reducing the rule to splitting checkout into two panes. Side A's escapes either claimed the benefits of an upfront all-in pricing mandate that the motion does not require (and that pads prices for conditional orders) or relied on repeat-purchase learning that pre-assent itemization at payment already supplies. Side B also covered all three named industries and maintained clean execution, whereas Side A left exclusive ticketing largely unanswered and suffered three clipped turns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal [B2.1-B2.2] turned Side A's feasibility defense [A2.3-A2.4] directly against its core harm [A2.1-A2.2]: arguing that fees are exact at order review once address, basket, party size, and seats are entered "saves accuracy by surrendering the injury," since the customer has already chosen between sellers and spent the browsing time, leaving Side A caught between late irrelevance and early guesswork.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A built the cleaner decisive clash: early disclosure is either accurate but after the A-over-B choice and sunk effort, or early enough to guide choice but speculative for conditional charges. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins because its central claim survived every round: buyers choose among offers before they reach checkout, so a fee revealed only at payment distorts that choice. A truthful checkout total cannot restore a comparison that has already been lost. PRO also neutralized CON's opening premise early. It showed that "variable" does not mean "unknowable": the inputs that set the fee are known before payment, so the charge, or the rule that sets it, can be shown earlier. CON's strongest lines were real but did not overcome this: - **Estimates that expire.** CON argued that an early figure which later changes is its own form of bait. - **The amendment charge.** CON argued that PRO's answer quietly rewrote the motion. PRO answered both credibly. Showing the current amount and updating it is never less accurate than silence. Disclosing a percentage or trigger rule is still disclosing the charge. Many such fees are simple fixed percentages or flat amounts anyway. CON's "self-liquidating" argument effectively conceded the lost-rival harm, then waved it away with "open a second tab." That shifts the seller's disclosure duty onto shoppers, which PRO named as the injury itself. CON also carried several costs: - Its second rebuttal was clipped for exceeding the word limit. - It overclaimed that "every consumer protection framework" places price disclosure at checkout. - It leaned on a supposed concession that PRO accurately rebutted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO's A2 answer in [B4.3]. CON had asked for one harm that existing deceptive-pricing law does not already cure. PRO offered two $60 tickets: one becomes $78 at checkout, while a rival at $68 all-in looked dearer during seat selection and lost the sale. Because the $78 itemization is truthful, a law aimed at lies does not reach it. This showed a concrete harm that existing law misses, and it forced CON to retreat to the "self-liquidating" and "second tab" responses, which PRO then turned in its closing.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won solidly by establishing and defending the decisive comparative principle: legal assent at payment does not cure market distortion at the comparison stage. Side A showed across all three sectors that withholding mandatory fees lets drip-priced offers ($60 plus a hidden $18 fee) displace honest rivals ($68 all-in) before checkout, and makes restaurant surcharges unavoidable after a meal is eaten—a point Side B largely abandoned after an unconvincing claim that diners should ask first. Side B cleverly argued that dynamic fees turn early disclosure into gameable fee schedules or expiring quotes, and that Side A quietly amended the motion. However, Side A neutralized this by explaining that menu percentages and seat-tier fees are already deterministic, and where delivery fees shift with live inputs, displaying the current computed charge and trigger before final checkout is strictly more accurate than showing an implicit zero until payment. Side B also lost minor ground for mischaracterizing A2.6 in its pressure questions and having its second rebuttal clipped at the word limit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's "expiring quote" and "amendment" critique across A2.1, A4.1, and A5.3—culminating in the observation that "silence is a guaranteed zero until commitment"—cleanly turned Side B's best argument on variability by showing that displaying and updating a live computed fee is far more honest than hiding mandatory charges during comparison.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): PRO kept a live comparison-stage harm: mandatory fees hidden until payment let a shaved price beat an honest all-in rival. CON's variability objection was answered by disclosing the fixed fee, percentage, current amount, or trigger once inputs are known before checkout; silence was never shown to be more accurate. CON's estimate and fee-schedule points created real pressure, but the self-liquidating walk-away answer ignored captured attention and the lost rival sale, and its clipped rebuttal weakened execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 A2 was decisive: the truthful $78 checkout versus ignored $68 all-in ticket showed a harm existing lie-focused law does not cure, directly answering CON's existing-law and cheap walk-away claims.
