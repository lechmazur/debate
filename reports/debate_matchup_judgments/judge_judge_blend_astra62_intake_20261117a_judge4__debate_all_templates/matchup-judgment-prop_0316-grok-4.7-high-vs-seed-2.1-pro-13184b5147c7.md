### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins because it held the motion to its actual scope: a right to know that AI ranks profiles and shapes exposure. It then showed that CON's case mostly attacks a different standard, one where notice must explain every burial, sort the whole market, and come with guardrails attached. PRO's "silence turns commercial allocation into a private verdict" framing gave a clear, modest benefit that CON never fully neutralized. PRO also answered the self-blame and comparison pressure directly with A1/A2. CON's strongest point was that generic notice gives little actionable information, and it had some bite. However, its two harm claims were weaker. The gaming harm sat in tension with its own premise that ranking is universal and already targeted. The claim that notice pre-empts stronger disclosure was asserted rather than shown. CON's SEO-history analogy was a late, somewhat brittle add-on. CON's closing largely restated earlier claims without resolving the contradictions PRO exposed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure questions did the decisive work. Q1 asked how notice could "confirm" a target to scammers when CON said ranking is already universal. Q2 asked why CON opposes the right to know when it insists users are owed more specific facts about boosts and filters. CON's answers, a suspicion-versus-confirmation distinction and an unproven pre-emption claim, did not repair the inconsistency. That left PRO's narrow right standing.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A won solidly by defending a tightly scoped right while proving that even baseline disclosure does real psychological and commercial work. A persuasively showed that naming the algorithmic sorter interrupts the certainty of personal inadequacy ("Silence turns a commercial sort into a private verdict"), reframes paid boosts as rented reach rather than organic chemistry, and stops platforms from presenting curated exposure as neutral chance—just as skimmed ad labels still enforce commercial honesty. Side B raised credible pragmatic concerns about checkbox fatigue, uniform notices, and platforms using generic banners as a regulatory fig leaf, but became repetitive late and lost its main offensive harm when A exposed a sharp contradiction in B's scammer argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's [A2.7] and Q1 ([A3.1]) decisively dismantled Side B's scammer argument by catching a core internal contradiction: B argued in [B1.2] that algorithmic ranking is already universal and obvious across major apps, yet claimed a generic user banner containing zero model weights would somehow turn an "unconfirmed rumor" into a new target for swipe-farm scammers ("It informs the person being scored, not the person hunting a manual").
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): PRO wins by defending the proposition as written: a narrow right to know a sorter exists. CON demanded that notice explain every burial, differentiate every model, and deliver guardrails, which PRO persuasively exposed as an inflated test. Knowing allocation is involved plausibly shifts self-blame to inquiry and makes paid boosts, bias, and engagement targets contestable. CON's gaming and preemption claims remained speculative, especially since ranking is already widely known and scammers already optimize, while PRO's answers to both pressure questions directly preserved comparison and attribution benefits without claiming an audit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A's Rebuttal 2 on self-blame: disclosure need not name the exact cause to stop an empty inbox reading as a personal verdict, undercutting B's central uselessness test while keeping the right narrow.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): CON (A) wins because it kept pressing the decisive question of what a bare AI notice actually tells a user, and PRO never answered it convincingly. CON's core argument held through every round: the notice names a technique, not an objective. Adaptive models can implement stated preferences, and fixed rules can ignore them, so the label proves no broken promise and exposes none of the harms PRO itself cited (disability demotion, beauty penalties, pay pressure). PRO's best idea was that notice is a precondition for other remedies. CON answered it directly: discrimination law and misrepresentation law turn on who was harmed and what was promised, not on whether the scorer was a neural net or a formula. PRO's A1 asserted that users could "newly prove" misleading marketing. CON then showed why the label does not contradict a promise that preferences matter. PRO's A2 admitted that hybrids trigger the duty and that nearly every app uses them. CON turned that admission into the claim that the notice distinguishes no competitor, which also undercut PRO's appeal to users switching services. PRO's closing relied on "floor of honest dealing" rhetoric and the "correcting a misimpression" framing. That framing had some intuitive force, but it largely restated the opening rather than engaging CON's specific rebuttals. CON's offered alternative, requiring disclosure of material factors, also blunted the charge that it simply favored opacity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's A1 decided the debate. CON argued that adaptive models can implement mutual preferences while fixed rules can ignore them, so an AI label cannot show that "mutual matching" marketing is false. Paired with turning PRO's admission that nearly every app triggers the duty into "the notice separates no honest competitor," this dismantled PRO's claims that notice enables enforcement and informed switching.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B won decisively by exposing a fatal mismatch between Side A's substantive harms and the motion's narrow mechanism. Side A justified the right through harms like disability or single-parent demotion, beauty penalties, and paywalled visibility, while insisting the rule only requires a one-sentence notice that AI is used without revealing proprietary weights or specific factors. Side B dismantled this link by showing that "AI" names a computational technique rather than an optimization objective: adaptive models can honor stated preferences while fixed non-AI rules can violate them, so an AI label neither proves deceptive marketing nor helps users spot bias or pay-to-play throttling. Once Side A conceded in [A4.3] that standard hybrid filters trigger the duty across nearly every major app, Side B cleanly established that the notice collapses into universal boilerplate rather than distinguishing honest competitors the way verifiable privacy and pricing disclosures do.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal ([B4.1]-[B4.5]), which turned both of Side A's pressure-question answers against PRO: Side B showed that an AI banner cannot prove "mutual preference" marketing is misleading because adaptive models can implement stated preferences while fixed rules can ignore them, and used Side A's concession that hybrid distance filters trigger disclosure ([A4.3]) to prove the banner would fire on nearly every app and distinguish no competitor.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing the proposed notice names a technique while withholding the material facts—objectives, pay pressure, or biased demotion—that users could act on. B also undercut A's enforcement claim: adaptive AI can implement mutual preferences and fixed rules can violate them, so the label proves no deception, while existing misrepresentation and discrimination law already target the actual harms. A's high-stakes autonomy case remained compelling but never answered how a universal, generic banner creates meaningful choice.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 decisively answered A1 by arguing an AI banner cannot prove broken mutual-matching promises because adaptive models may honor preferences and fixed rules may ignore them, exposing the core gap in A's precondition argument.
