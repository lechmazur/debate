### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): This was a close, high-quality exchange. PRO built a coherent incentive story: conditional legality as leverage, ambiguity that does not halt activity, and a holdout veto. It also asked the sharpest pressure questions. CON nonetheless narrowly carried the debate. It kept the clash anchored on what the motion distinctively changes, namely who may create saleable title from a commons before global terms exist. Its critique that the "provided" conditions amount to licensing-state self-certification stayed live throughout. PRO's best answer, that other states need not accept sham legality, partly reconverges on CON's own default. CON answered the use-versus-sale question directly with a defensible transient-use versus bankable-inventory line. It also pressed persuasively that "expressly subject to later law" merely prices regulatory risk rather than preventing entrenchment. PRO's turns were clipped three times, including the opening and both rebuttals. That cost PRO its concluding weighing at key moments, while CON was consistently tight and finished each speech with clean comparative framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came in Rebuttal 2. PRO answered B's Q2 by arguing that recognition is prospective and that licenses can be expressly made subject to later royalties and benefit-sharing. CON replied that such a clause does not erase the title, collateral, and financing that recognition creates; it only tells operators to price regulatory risk. That reply kept CON's entrenchment route alive. PRO's strongest counterpunch was that CON's only sanction, a market discount, "changes financing, not conduct." It landed but did not fully neutralize CON's authority framing.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A more convincingly showed that the proviso collapses into licensing-state self-certification and that premature bankable title creates political entrenchment contractual disclaimers cannot neutralize. Ambiguity’s market friction better protects the commons until real agreement. Side B never fully escaped those burdens, and repeated clipping further weakened its case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s rebuttal that sale, unlike transient use, mints bankable title, collateral, and vested leverage, while “subject to later law” merely prices risk rather than stopping a fait accompli.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): A won because it kept the debate focused on the motion’s conditional tradeoff: recognition creates enforceable leverage for transparency and debris control, while B’s treaty-first alternative mainly relied on market friction and could not show how it would produce better disclosure or environmental control. B’s commons and entrenchment arguments were strong rhetorically, but A adequately answered them with conditional licensing, later-law subordination, and other-state/market nonrecognition. A’s repeated clipping was a real execution cost and kept the margin narrow.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 response to B’s pressure questions: A conceded no pre-treaty global inspector, then answered with audits, market pressure, other-state nonrecognition, and expressly prospective licenses subject to later benefit-sharing rules, blunting B’s self-certification and vested-rights offense.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): CON wins on a decoupling argument that PRO never neutralized. Every safety benefit PRO cites can be obtained through ordinary mission licensing without recognizing sale: site data, trajectories, accountable operators, and debris discipline. Recognizing sale uniquely adds state-backed commodity interests that entrench claims before any legitimate allocation authority exists. PRO's main bridge between sale and transparency was the offshore relabeling argument, and CON answered it twice. Evaders can evade either regime, and disclosure duties do not "vanish" when sale is unrecognized. PRO's own closing still claimed they vanish without supporting it, which reads as a misdescription of the clash. PRO also conceded two key points in A1 and A2. Buyer interests are enforceable only in the licensing forum, and licenses cannot bind nonconsenting states. CON turned both concessions into its central comparison: "records with premature alienation vs. records without it." PRO's A1/A2 answers were direct and fairly honest, and its Article VI jurisdiction point was reasonable. But its closing largely repeated the daylight/shadow frame without engaging CON's decoupling. CON's speeches progressively narrowed the dispute and offered cleaner weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.4 and B5.1 decided the debate. Taking PRO's own concessions (forum-only enforceability, no binding of nonconsenting states), CON showed that every safety benefit PRO claims is available without recognizing sale. The motion's unique addition is therefore entrenchment, and "records vs. silence" collapses into "records vs. records plus premature alienation."
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won by isolating the motion’s distinctive commitment—legalizing sale, not merely supervising missions—and showing that every safety benefit PRO claimed is available through licensing, disclosure, and debris rules without recognizing sales. PRO’s answers conceded forum-only enforceability and no power over nonconsenting states, and never recovered the points that consumed resources are not reversible and that evaders can dodge either regime. PRO’s shadow-market worry stayed live, but CON made the clearer, motion-faithful case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s first and second rebuttals severed “daylight” from sale: the same site, trajectory, and debris conditions can be imposed without enforcing resource-sale contracts, while revocation cannot restore resources already sold and consumed.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): B won by making the decisive clash whether legal sale is necessary for transparency and debris control. It showed mission licensing could supply those safety tools without premature resource alienation, while A never proved sale uniquely compels disclosure. B also turned A’s concessions about limited domestic enforceability and non-binding licenses into a strong legitimacy and entrenchment problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal used A’s admissions that buyers only get forum-enforceable interests and licenses cannot bind nonconsenting states to argue that PRO’s safety benefits are available without sale, while the motion uniquely adds premature commodity claims and entrenched interests.
