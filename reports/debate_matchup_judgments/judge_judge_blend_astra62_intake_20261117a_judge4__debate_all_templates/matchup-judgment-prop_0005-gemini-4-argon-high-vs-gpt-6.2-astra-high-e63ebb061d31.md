### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): The motion says "any" mass-media format, so CON only needed one defensible case of permitted advertising. Side B used that narrow burden well. It offered a regulated, plainly sponsored newspaper notice with benefits, risks and alternatives in equally prominent type, and anchored it in the migraine patient who had stopped seeking care. Side A's core attack was economic: no firm would pay for a neutral notice, so any notice that runs must steer patients. B answered with a clean dilemma that A never squarely broke. If no firm will pay on those terms, prohibition adds nothing. If one will, prohibition suppresses the comparison itself. B added that profitability can come from appropriate prescriptions to patients who already failed on generics, and that calling drugs "high-margin" undercuts A's claim that huge volume is indispensable. B's free-rider point against unbranded campaigns went largely unanswered. B also neutralized the anchoring argument by noting that a patient can insist on the only monthly injection without saying its name. A's best moment was the insurance-backfire turn: a patient sees Brand X is excluded and stays home. A's closing framed B's fix as self-defeating, which was rhetorically strong. B's reply still held up, however. The insurer answers coverage and the clinician answers appropriateness, and the patient started outside care anyway. A also cited mislabeled reference IDs ([C2.5] instead of [B2.5]) in its questions, a minor execution cost. A was polished and pointed, but B left the cleaner surviving route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over profitability. A argued that only notices which steer patients toward brands would ever be bought. B replied in Rebuttal 2 and Closing: mass circulation shows how many people see a notice, not how many must switch inappropriately to pay for it. B then closed with the dilemma that prohibition either adds nothing or suppresses a readable comparison. This turned A's economic argument into a reason against an outright ban.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): A built the cleaner answer to the motion’s absolute ban. B established plausible risks from brand pressure, but its later case depended on treating every commercially viable notice as necessarily biased and every branded inquiry as a demand. A directly separated profit from prescribing error, showed why a drug name can have practical value without displacing clinical judgment, and exposed the free-rider problem in B’s unbranded alternative. B’s insurance question was damaging, but A answered it without surrendering the coverage-checking benefit. B’s closing claim of a “fatal contradiction” overstated A’s concessions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: “compare questions with questions” undercut B’s anchoring contrast, while the later dilemma—if no firm will run a compliant comparison, prohibition adds nothing; if one will, prohibition suppresses useful information—left B’s economic-inevitability claim insufficiently established.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side A preserved a live route against a total ban: a plainly sponsored, fixed-format notice can help a disengaged patient decide whether a new option is worth discussing and checking for coverage without dictating the prescription. A also flipped B's central dilemma—if no firm would pay under fair rules, prohibition adds nothing; if one would, prohibition suppresses useful comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: high-margin sales to a suitable subset answered the mass-volume claim, and comparing question-with-question rather than question-with-demand exposed the flaw in B's anchoring argument.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B wins narrowly. A built a careful incentive argument: the extra money for naming a product buys influence over which drug a patient chooses, not better advice about when to seek care. A also showed that steering can work without any gatekeeper failing. B's free-rider argument, however, directly answered A's proposed alternative of unbranded outreach. A then effectively conceded the point in Rebuttal 2 by saying an unbranded campaign "clinically worked; commercially it failed." B used that concession well. It turned the debate into lost access to care versus mostly harmless tie-breaking among appropriate, covered drugs. B also answered the copay and cost harms with formulary tiering and step therapy. A's closing attack on the cross-subsidy was sharp: a prescription won by a rival brand does not fund the next broadcast. It still did not fully restore the unbranded alternative as a realistic replacement for an absolute ban across every mass medium. B's execution had flaws. It cited nonexistent reference IDs (P5.2, P4.4), made a brittle "nine out of ten" claim, and leaned on optimistic assumptions about insurers. Those costs keep the margin thin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.2–B2.4: the free-rider argument, that no company funds unbranded symptom awareness when generics capture most of the resulting prescriptions, combined with turning A's "some useful prompts may be lost" into a significant concession. This undercut A's main mitigation and set the weighing for the rest of the debate. A's A5.1–A5.2 counter on the cross-subsidy was strong but came late.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): A won the decisive weighing. B offered the cleaner incentive analysis and excellent pressure questions, but conceded that a ban would lose useful prompts without establishing that unbranded outreach would reliably replace them. A’s cross-subsidy account explained how branded campaigns could remain viable even when most prompted patients receive generics or rival products. B ultimately narrowed the harm to steering among clinically suitable options; A answered that physicians handle suitability and that an equal-cost, equal-benefit preference is a harmless tiebreaker, leaving B short of justifying a total prohibition. A’s categorical claims about patients and formularies, plus erroneous closing reference IDs, keep the margin competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Q1/Q2 and second rebuttal sharply separated clinical success from commercial success and exposed the limits of insurer gatekeeping. A’s second rebuttal and closing nevertheless turned the free-rider point into a viable cross-subsidy argument and neutralized B’s remaining equal-cost steering scenario.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B persuasively showed advertising can steer choices, but conceded some care-prompting ads would disappear. Side A answered the remaining harm: formularies tier genuinely costlier drugs, physicians can reject unsuitable requests, and if two options are equally suitable and costly, familiarity is a harmless tiebreaker whose revenue finances outreach that otherwise faces a free-rider problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 answer on formularies and rebates neutralized Side B's equal-copay, higher-insurer-cost scenario, allowing its closing to reduce the ban's benefit to preventing a harmless brand tiebreaker at the cost of lost patient outreach.
