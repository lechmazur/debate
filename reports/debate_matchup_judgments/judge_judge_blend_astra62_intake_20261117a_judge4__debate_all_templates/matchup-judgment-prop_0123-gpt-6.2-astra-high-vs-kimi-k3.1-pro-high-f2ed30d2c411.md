### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): A (CON) won a close, high-quality debate. A set a clear standard early — a prominent warning must be truthful and proportionate, and the UPF category is a contested manufacturing proxy attached to nutritionally unlike products — and pressed it through every round. A's sharpest weapons were the substitution problem (labelled fortified yogurt swapped for a worse unlabelled product), the redundancy of a shared processing advisory in the two-yogurt comparison, and the burden-reversal in A2.6: if campaigns are needed to interpret warnings, warnings cannot displace campaigns as the main strategy. A also executed the weighing cleanly, conceding the targeted "high in sugar" disclosure while showing it cannot carry the broader mandate. B (PRO) was genuinely strong: the identification problem (even a perfectly educated shopper must decode ingredient lists one at a time at the shelf) stayed live throughout, and the closing division-of-labor frame — manufacturer knows the product, shopper knows the routine — was original and elegant. But B's key defensive move, reframing the label as a "prompt to consider your routine" rather than guidance, effectively concedes the warning carries no determinate health message, and B had to modify the design mid-debate (moving the frequency qualification into prominent text) under A's pressure. B never squarely showed the category signal is nutritionally accurate, only that it is administrable and thought-provoking. Both sides followed format perfectly, used no fake sourcing, and crystallized well; A simply left more of B's burdens unanswered than B left of A's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange ran from A2.6 through B4.5: A argued that if warnings require campaigns to interpret them, labels cannot be the main strategy; B answered that even perfectly educated shoppers face the identification problem at every purchase, so disclosure must be primary. A's A4.3 then landed the hardest blow of the debate — B's frozen-lunch answer concedes the late-shift worker and the weekly shopper receive identical signals despite radically different diets, so the "warning" is a prompt, not guidance — which A converted into the closing line that "identification is not understanding, and repetition is not proportionality."
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that the motion’s ultra-processed warning is an imprecise health judgment stamped on unlike products, creating substitution and fatigue risks that campaigns plus specific nutrient facts avoid. Side B’s point-of-sale and identification arguments were real, but its own concession that the label cannot judge frequency or diet let A treat the warning as a prompt rather than accurate guidance, so labels could not displace campaigns as the main strategy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s reply that “silence is not endorsement” does not stop a shopper from swapping a labelled fortified food for a worse unlabelled one, which kept the relative-choice harm alive against B’s category defense.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A won by separating the defensible specific sugar disclosure from the motion's broader ultra-processed mandate. A consistently showed that processing is an imprecise proxy, that a diet-pattern harm cannot be accurately judged from an identical product warning, and that undefined 'frequent' plus unlabelled substitutes risk worse choices. B's identification-at-the-shelf argument was strong, but B conceded the label cannot judge this purchase and still needs campaigns to supply that judgment, weakening labels as the main strategy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: a shared 'high in sugar' warning states the same verifiable product fact, while a shared processing warning only restates a contested category and cannot show whether a third option is healthier.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Both sides were excellent, but Side B (PRO) held the cleaner route to victory. B consistently reframed the motion as warnings-plus-education versus education-alone carrying the primary burden, and in Rebuttal 2 landed the decisive double-standard point: CON credits campaigns with teaching judgment across homes, schools, and restaurants, yet claims shoppers become helpless reading a warning beside a fibre panel. That weighing survived A's reply. A's strongest line — the wholegrain loaf whose warning conflicts with fibre advice, sharpened by the "prominence vs. neutrality" dilemma and the incentive point that emulsifier removal gets the visible reward — was genuinely damaging and original, and A's closing vignette was the best rhetorical moment of the debate. But B's answers held: the warning conveys one relevant fact, criteria can be reviewed, complementary disclosures can handle other goals, and campaigns suffer the same forgettability and misinterpretation A attributes only to labels. Both sides answered pressure questions directly with proper A1/A2 structure. A never fully broke B's core comparison, while B's asymmetry argument directly undercut A's live route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5-B4.6, where B exposed that CON demands labels resolve every substitution while treating every campaign limitation as manageable, converting A's bread example from a decisive objection into a design problem — closely matched by A4.4-A4.6, which sharpened the conflict framing and the misaligned reformulation incentive.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A won by making the live conflict concrete: a prominent UPF warning can pull a shopper away from the very choice sound advice encourages, and prominence rewards emulsifier removal more strongly than clearer nutritional gains. Side B’s complementarity and “not a full ranking” replies never resolved which signal should govern when both are understood, or why the less certain change should get the louder incentive. Campaigns’ memory problem was real, but A’s routine-plus-second-chance framing kept warnings useful without making them primary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2: after answering both questions, it showed that learning a warning’s limits still leaves the fibre-versus-emulsifier tradeoff unresolved, and that repetition then magnifies conflict rather than correcting a mistake.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A built the cleaner comparative test: what happens when advice is understood and a warning is added. The wholegrain-loaf conflict and misaligned reformulation incentive survived B's 'not a ranking' defence, while B mainly proved warnings are a useful reminder, not that they should be primary across the category.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A's Rebuttal 2: even a shopper who knows a warning is not a ranking still lacks guidance on the fibre-versus-processing trade-off, and B's concession of 'no automatic health benefit' from removing an emulsifier left prominence rewarding the weaker change.
