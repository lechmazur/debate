### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a competitive debate. It set up a clear causal story early: payroll taxes fall on labor while capital is subsidized, so some layoffs are driven by tax treatment rather than real productivity. It then kept that frame tied to every exchange. CON's (Side A) strongest attacks were real: - **Definition and gaming:** firms could avoid the trigger through attrition, contractors, or timing. - **Self-defeating revenue:** the fund shrinks if the tax succeeds in deterring automation. - **Neutral tax treatment:** fix the labor/capital tilt directly rather than taxing displacement. PRO answered the first two directly in its A1/A2. The key move was arguing that deterred layoffs leave workers employed and needing no aid, so revenue tracks need rather than collapsing. CON's follow-up, that attrition and task changes still create reskilling needs, was thinner. PRO's closing also neatly blunted it: retirees are not displaced workers. CON's neutral-tax alternative was its best counter, and it was never fully defeated. PRO's reply was only partial: ending capital subsidies would hit non-displacing investment, and CON's alternative relies on general revenue that CON itself admitted faces budget battles. PRO did overclaim at points. "One-to-one" and "exact proportion" are brittle, and its multi-year audit of labor hours and vendors partly conceded CON's compliance-burden point. On balance, PRO made the decisive clash easier to resolve and showed that every behavioral response to the tax beats the status quo.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over CON's "fund starves when the tax works" dilemma, pressed in A2.3, A3.2, and A5.2. PRO answered in B4.3: every deterred marginal layoff leaves a worker employed and needing no retraining aid, while every deployment that goes ahead pays in proportion to the displacement it causes. That reframed the supposed procyclicality as revenue matching need. CON's counter about attrition and task reskilling did not restore the dilemma's force.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): B built the cleaner path to victory by directly answering both pressure questions and linking deterrence, revenue, and adjustment need: avoided layoffs require less aid, while proceeded deployments fund support. Its treatment of attrition as non-displacement also blunted A’s strongest gaming objection. A offered serious unresolved concerns about compliance, mobile manufacturing, and the superiority of neutral labor-capital taxation, but too often repeated the shrinking-revenue dilemma after B had answered it and did not fully overcome B’s fairness comparison with general taxation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: its A1/A2 answers distinguished voluntary attrition from sudden displacement and reframed the alleged funding paradox as revenue and need moving together, while A’s later reskilling and administrative objections narrowed but did not erase that advantage.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the definitional and design burden central: attrition, outsourcing, timing, and contractor routes turned A's claimed objective trigger into a sieve, while A's expanded multi-year audit of labor hours and vendors confirmed the compliance morass. B's neutral-tax-treatment alternative also undercut the necessity of taxing replacement itself, even though A landed a strong proportional-revenue answer on the funding paradox.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was B's gaming critique in Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2: A's attempt to patch outsourcing and timing with broader audits strengthened B's claim that the levy traps blunt firms while sophisticated firms route around it.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins because it set the terms of the clash early and kept PRO on the defensive throughout. Its opening identified two structural problems in PRO's per-displaced-worker mechanism. The first was the greenfield-entrant loophole. The second was offshoring pressure under global competition. Each PRO patch then created a new vulnerability that CON exploited: - **Taxing all robotic capital** catches assistive tools. - **Benchmarking against a "comparable conventional plant"** taxes hypothetical never-hired workers and stretches the motion. - **Border charges built on sector proxies** are hard to administer and still leave domestic exporters carrying the tax abroad. PRO's answer on exporters, that domestic demand protects them, was weak, and CON caught it cleanly. CON also exposed a tension PRO never resolved. The levy was meant to discourage labor-shedding, yet it was set "below the productivity gain" so automation proceeds anyway. CON added that uneven gains mean a uniform charge would fall hardest on marginal firms. PRO's strongest live route was timing: who pays in the year of displacement. CON answered it adequately with general revenue pooling and budget smoothing. CON also turned PRO's thin-margin point into an argument against billing restructuring plants up front. PRO's closing mostly asserted that its patches worked, and it framed CON's thin-margin argument as a "concession," which overstated it. CON's closing crystallized the workability contrast more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1–A2.4 and A4.3–A4.4 were decisive. CON showed that PRO's fix for the startup loophole either abandons the proposition's focus on displacement or relies on imaginary baselines. It also showed that the border adjustment cannot be measured at the border and does nothing for exporters selling abroad. PRO's own B2.1 had conceded that without border adjustments capital flight "hollows out the tax base entirely," so this exchange undercut a pillar PRO itself had said its case depended on.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A built the stronger path to victory by exposing the levy’s design dilemma: taxing actual displacement disadvantages legacy employers, while taxing all automation requires counterfactual staffing estimates and sweeps beyond the proposition. A also showed that border proxies could burden manual imports without protecting exporters. B’s pressure questions about immediate retraining revenue were sharp, but A answered directly with pooled general revenues and budget smoothing. B’s core externality and timing arguments remained relevant, yet its increasingly elaborate patches never overcame A’s administrability and competitiveness objections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange followed A’s pressure questions: B answered directly but relied on hypothetical “comparable conventional plant” staffing and sector-level import proxies. A4 then dismantled both patches and identified the unresolved exporter disadvantage, leaving B’s case dependent on mechanisms it had not made workable.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B exposed A's shifting definition—from per displaced worker to taxing hypothetical never-hired workers and blunt border proxies—while answering the timing attack with pooled profits and budget smoothing. A never solved exporter disadvantage, assistive-vs-substitution measurement, or varying productivity gains, leaving its patches unworkable versus B's auditable alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: A's comparable-plant fix rewrites the proposition and requires arbitrary phantom staffing counts, while sector border charges punish manual imports and still burden domestic exporters abroad.
