### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins because its central idea survived the exchange. The risk of becoming identifiably expensive is itself something worth insuring before anyone falls ill, and only a common legal rule can secure that guarantee. CON's own opening conceded that healthy buyers leave a pool that cannot price risk unless participation is compelled. That concession undercut CON's later claim that buyers could simply "contract for that stability" privately. PRO also handled CON's subsidy alternative well. It asked who owes the balance when means-tested aid falls short, and showed that under unrestricted pricing the sick person still carries a penalty for her illness. CON argued cleanly and consistently. Its best moves were: - distinguishing a shared chance from a transfer once a diagnosis is known; - catching that PRO's treasury-pays proposal "funds an accurate price rather than limiting one"; - pressing the scope and line-creep problem. These points did not fully land. PRO's narrowing to diagnosis and medical history is still a legal limit on actuarial pricing, so it satisfies the motion. PRO's no-recourse-to-the-patient rule answered the treasury catch. CON's "an ex ante wish is not consent" line struggled against PRO's point that the protection cannot be bought once it is needed. Both sides kept good epistemic discipline and used no fake precision. Both used the required A1:/A2: answer labels properly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over the shortfall. CON argued in A4.6 and A4.7 that once the treasury pays the calculated difference, the actuarial price is funded rather than limited, so the healthy buyer becomes a trapped residual payer. PRO answered in B4.2 and B5.2 that a guarantee is only real if the law bars recourse to the patient when public funds fall short, which makes it a genuine limit on what she can be charged. PRO added that reclassification protection cannot be purchased after diagnosis. Together these left CON's "fund it openly instead" alternative unable to deliver the same promise.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) gets a razor-thin but real edge. The decisive move is the forecast/bill distinction: actuarial knowledge need not determine who is charged, and a renewal surcharge after diagnosis breaks the ex ante insurance promise both sisters purchased. B makes the market-failure point (voluntary community rating unravels through adverse selection) robustly and then turns A's subsidy alternative against itself: subsidies still leave the sick sister facing shortfalls, means-testing, or a residual bill. A's scope critique and "trap vs solidarity" framing are sharp and prevent this from being a decisive win, but A's "ex ante wish is not consent" line does not fully disarm the core insurance-theoretic point that commitment before risk resolves is the essence of the product. Overall, B gave the cleaner reason to vote for the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's closing reframe [B5.1] was the most decisive exchange: a forecast says what the pool needs; a renewal bill says whose money the pool may collect. That answer directly neutralizes A's visibility argument by showing actuarial calculation can remain transparent without sending the bill to the sick sister. A responded coherently that the public can fund the difference, but B had already shown why that route still makes the sick sister a residual claimant when aid falls short.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued at a high level with clean structure and no fake sourcing. The decisive clash was the sisters' renewal problem. A also handled the treasury-shortfall dilemma best: any "fund it openly" scheme either allows recourse to the patient (recreating the threat to coverage) or legally bars it — which is precisely the limit the proposition demands. B's strongest surviving points were the mandate/exit critique and the observation that A's statutory cabin (diagnosis and history only) is narrower than the motion's "statistical risk," but the slippery-slope extrapolation was speculative and A's statutory-list answer blunted it. B's answers to pressure were competent but slightly less damaging; A's Q2 on visibility and B's A2 response left B leaning on "incidence and exit," which A had already reframed via the cost-shifting discount argument. A made the debate marginally easier to decide and closed by weaponizing B's own line ("the bill names that owner").
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A4.2/A4.3 and closing: answering B's subsidy question by showing that with a surcharge plus means-tested aid the sick sister still bears the penalty, and that a full treasury guarantee either permits recourse to the patient or itself constitutes the legal limit A advocates — turning B's "fund it openly" counterproposal into a concession of A's core claim.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Both sides were sharp and disciplined, with no fake precision on either side. Con's core move was to separate the price of a risk from a household's ability to pay. Con argued that solidarity is best expressed through an income-based guarantee paid at enrollment, which made it a credible rival account of solidarity rather than a denial of it. Pro's strongest line was definitional. It argued that Con's model leaves actuarial pricing as the legal rule, so it is not the limit the motion demands. Pro's Q2 forced Con to admit that a nurse who can afford a tripled premium for genetic disease pays it. Con owned that answer directly, added the "no cliff" clarification, and then turned the "unchosen luck" principle with the cashier parallel: low wages are unchosen too. Several pieces of Con's pressure were never squarely answered by Pro: - **Risk adjustment:** Con argued that Pro's own risk adjustment concedes diagnosis-linked dollars are not the solidarity violation. - **Two millionaires:** Two equally wealthy owners pay the same progressive tax, yet only the floodplain owner gets a discount. - **Common premium:** A common premium still needs the income assessment Pro dismissed as "paperwork." Pro's closing largely reasserted that solidarity means refusing to price unchosen luck. It did not explain what forbidding the risk charge adds once affordable access is guaranteed. Pro answered Con's questions well, giving a clear factor-based principle and "tax the millionaire." However, Con's rebuttal showed that taxing the rich can fund the discount without justifying it. Overall, Con left the cleaner comparative test, so Con wins a clear but competitive decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over Pro's answer to Con's millionaire question. Pro's "Tax the millionaire, don't subsidize the mansion" (B4.2) met Con's reply in A4.5: two millionaires of identical means pay the same progressive tax, yet only the floodplain owner gets the exposure discount, so taxation "can fund that discount, not justify it." Pro never answered this, and together with the cashier "unchosen wages" turn it undercut Pro's claim that solidarity requires banning risk pricing rather than guaranteeing affordability.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): B wins because A ultimately conceded the key live question: under A's proposal, insurers could still charge more for cancer, age, or floodplain, and those who could pay would pay. B turned that concession into a decisive proposition-level problem: A's income-based guarantee is not a legal limit on statistical risk, but a means-tested subsidy layered on top of it. A's two-millionaires point was a sharp cost for rate bands, but B answered with progressive financing and kept the clash focused on whether unchosen luck may be priced at all for essential coverage. That more directly matched the motion as written and gave the cleaner reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's use of A4.1 was the decisive exchange: once A admitted that risk surcharges could remain and that those who could afford them would pay, B could show the proposal left actuarial fairness as the legal price, not a legal limit.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): This was an unusually clean, high-level clash. Side A's best route was extracting B's concession (A3.1 → B4.1) that insurers may still charge more for cancer, age, or floodplain, framing B's means-tested credit as "poverty relief" that leaves actuarial fairness as the legal price. But B consistently defended that concession as its actual framework: solidarity governs what leaves the household's bank account, not what the plan receives, and B4.7/B5.4 gave a genuine weighing argument — once access is guaranteed, forbidding the risk charge buys nothing for those who need help. B's risk-adjustment symmetry point (B2.3) further undercut A's claim that the diagnosis-dollar link itself violates solidarity. Meanwhile, A never answered B's refined two-millionaires example (B4.5): two equally wealthy owners on different streets — the floodplain one still gets the subsidy under a common premium, and "tax the millionaire" funds the discount without justifying it. A's closing mostly reprised the B4.1 concession rather than resolving that open pressure. Both sides answered pressure questions directly with proper A1/A2 structure; both used cautious, well-grounded reasoning with no fake sourcing. B ended the debate with its live pressures answered and A ended with one unresolved, so B takes a narrow win on a framework both sides executed excellently.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive sequence was B4.5: after A answered the millionaire subsidy pressure with "tax the millionaire" (A4.2), B refined the example to two millionaires of identical means on different streets — both pay the same progressive tax, yet only the floodplain owner receives the effective discount under a rate band. A never responded to this equal-means version, leaving A's community-rating mechanism carrying an unexplained regressive subsidy through the end of the debate.
