### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side B wins a clear but competitive debate by successfully turning Side A's own concessions into a decisive reframing. Side A's strongest move was to borrow only the Antarctic principle of setting claims aside while keeping Arctic states and Indigenous peoples in control, and it answered pressure questions with concrete screening criteria. However, Side B repeatedly and effectively exposed that this stripped-down proposal no longer resembles the Antarctic Treaty System, which is the motion's explicit anchor. Side B also offered a live alternative—targeted Arctic Council accords with binding harm thresholds and regional arbitration—that preserved the cross-border protections Side A wanted without importing the Antarctic model's diffuse accountability. Side A's closing crystallized the shared-risk principle well, but it did not neutralize Side B's core charge that the motion's "similar to the Antarctic Treaty System" label carries institutional logic that paper guardrails cannot contain. Side B's rebuttals were sharper and more comparative, and its pressure questions forced Side A into a defensive posture of disavowing Antarctic features. The debate was engaging, with a clear clash over whether the Antarctic bargain can be borrowed without its membership and governance structure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's most decisive rebuttal was the repeated observation that Side A kept stripping away every defining feature of the Antarctic Treaty System—non-Arctic voting, blanket resource freezes, broad review—until the proposal no longer resembled the motion. This turned Side A's reasonable narrowing into an admission that the copy-paste model is unworkable for the lived-in Arctic, and it reframed the debate around the actual institutional logic of the Antarctic model rather than Side A's idealized version.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): Side A built a cohesive, concrete case showing why national licensing and post-hoc response accords fail across shared ecosystems and disputed seabeds, while cleanly adapting the Antarctic Treaty's claim-standstill and ex-ante review bargain to an inhabited Arctic. Side B pressed a credible critique that Side A stripped away major features of the actual Antarctic Treaty System, such as non-regional voting membership and blanket extraction bans. However, Side A neutralized Side B's regulatory-delay objection with specific screening criteria and a fixed deadline, and trapped Side B in Pressure Questions. Forced in Rebuttal 2 to propose Arctic Council side accords featuring disputed seabed standstills, cross-border harm thresholds, Indigenous seats, and a regional panel empowered to halt national projects, Side B contradicted its own Rebuttal 1 claims that clean cross-border thresholds do not exist and supranational vetoes diffuse accountability, allowing Side A's closing to show that piecemeal accords either leave dangerous gaps or concede treaty governance under another label.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 dismantling of Side B's "locally rooted" accountability argument ([A2.1]-[A2.2]—showing that hunters across a strait are also an Arctic community yet cannot vote out a neighboring licensing minister because "the rig, rather than the risk, stays on its side of the line"), followed up by Q2 ([A3.2]), which forced Side B in [B4.3] to endorse a supranational panel able to halt national drilling in direct tension with Side B's earlier objections.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): B made the clearer comparative case: shared risks require pre-licensing rules that follow the harm rather than the licensing border, and “similar to” the Antarctic system can reasonably mean borrowing its claims standstill without copying its membership. B directly answered both pressure questions with workable review limits. A offered a serious accountability objection, but relied too heavily on an expansive, contestable characterization of the Antarctic model and ultimately proposed binding arbitration with Indigenous participation and halt powers, substantially converging with B’s functional case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s alternative was decisive: once A accepted a regional panel capable of halting licensed projects under binding cross-border standards, B showed that the accountability dispute had largely collapsed, leaving piecemeal accord coverage as A’s main remaining distinction.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): CON wins by making the debate turn on whether a claims freeze creates an actual accountable regulator. PRO's Antarctic analogy and race-to-the-bottom case were strong in the abstract, but CON showed Arctic states already have responsive authority in real spills, that narrow no-prejudice bargains can work without resolving borders, and that bundling issues can give holdouts more leverage. PRO's binding arbitration panel answered the pressure only by assuming the very consent in dispute, and CON's reply that naming arbitrators does not secure jurisdiction left that live route substantially weakened.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to the proposed arbitration panel was decisive: if states accept a limited emergency order, a claims freeze adds nothing; if they reject it, naming arbitrators does not secure consent. Refusing the package does not cost a holdout Arctic access but lets it charge exemptions as the price of accepting the treaty.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won every major clash with superior institutional accuracy, sharper causal analysis, and unanswered comparative weighing. Side B showed that freezing sovereignty claims neither creates an operational regulator nor resolves cost-allocation disputes, that without-prejudice emergency rules can address disputed transit zones directly, and that bundling all Arctic issues into one treaty increases holdout leverage rather than eliminating loopholes. Side A remained organized and readable but repeatedly conflated seabed claims with surface shipping, retreated after misstating the status of heavy fuel oil rules, and never answered Side B's demonstration that treaty holdouts cannot be locked out of coastal or high-seas access.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [B4.4]-[B4.7], Side B dismantled Side A's arbitration-panel and commons-access mechanisms by showing that states willing to accept emergency port orders without prejudice do not need a regional claims freeze, while unwilling holdouts already possess coastal rights and high-seas access and could simply charge a higher price—such as fuel exemptions—for joining a bundled treaty.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): Side A built the stronger comparative case by separating title disputes from the practical questions of spill response, liability, and regulatory consent. Its without-prejudice tanker example showed that binding cooperation need not await a claims freeze, while its holdout analysis exposed how comprehensive bundling could increase rather than reduce obstruction. Side B offered a clear environmental and sovereignty-rivalry case, but repeatedly asserted rather than demonstrated that territorial competition causes exemptions and that a treaty panel or access condition could secure consent. Its closing restated that causal claim without answering A’s decisive objections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions, then showed that a limited emergency-jurisdiction agreement works without a freeze if accepted, while naming arbitrators cannot compel acceptance if rejected. Its demonstration that a holdout retains existing coastal and high-seas access badly damaged B’s proposed enforcement incentive.
