### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built the more coherent and durable case. The core clash was the scope of "style or likeness": A's fictional-successor overbreadth example was genuinely strong, but B answered it directly (A1 in B4.1) by setting the trigger at deliberate recognizability — prompting by name, tuning to recordings, marketing a "return" — while leaving genre influence free, and then defended that reading in closing as giving "of" its ordinary work. B's timing argument (permission asks whether the performance should exist; disclosure only governs an already-made asset) and the burden placement (uncertainty falls on the party with the prompt, model, and profit) were never squarely neutralized; A's counter that "association is not inherently wrongful" remained an asserted principle without comparable weighing. B's "squeeze" observation (B4.5) — that CON inflates style for overbreadth while shrinking the injury to confusion for remedy — cleanly captured A's strategic tension. A performed well on disclosure-alternative and the exclusivity concern, but suffered execution costs from two clipped turns (opening and first rebuttal), losing the end of its distributive and disclosure arguments. Both sides followed the Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 formats correctly and avoided fake sourcing; B was more disciplined, more responsive, and rhetorically sharper ("silence is not yes," "the dead artist is inventory").
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5's "squeeze" plus B4.3's answer to A3.2: B showed that A inflated "style" to any lineage when attacking breadth but collapsed the injury to audience confusion when attacking the remedy, and demonstrated that a disclosed fake ballad still carries the artist's recognizable self into uses they never chose — damage A's closing ("recognizability is also the basis of false-endorsement rules") only partially absorbed.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): A more convincingly distinguished shared artistic influence from commercially reconstructing an identifiable artist, and explained why disclosure does not resolve the latter’s consent problem. B raised legitimate concerns about stylistic boundaries and uncertain authorization, but repeatedly treated deception rules as sufficient without adequately answering nondeceptive identity appropriation. Those implementation concerns keep the result competitive, while A’s clearer allocation of authority and risk supplies the stronger reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first pressure question exposed that B’s fictional-vocalist example described generic tradition, not an individual artist’s style or likeness. B explicitly conceded this, weakening its central overbreadth example; A then persuasively argued that excluding generic influence interprets the motion rather than abandons it.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): B made the clearer distinction between shared artistic influence and deliberately recognizable synthetic performance, then explained why disclosure leaves unconsented commercial use of identity intact and why permission should precede generation. A raised genuine overbreadth and clearance concerns, but its generic-era example did not establish a performance “of” a particular dead artist, and its alternative primarily addressed deception rather than B’s residual identity harm. A’s two clipped turns also weakened execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions and exposed the squeeze in A’s case: A inflated “style” into any artistic lineage when alleging overbreadth, then shrank the injury to audience confusion when comparing remedies. B’s contrast between generic influence and commercial “ventriloquism” survived that exchange.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a closely fought but real victory. A's central pressure—demanding the concrete right that prior permission protects once disclosure removes deception and no recording, composition, or mark is copied—was posed in Pressure Questions and never decisively answered. B's reply ("control over commercial use of identifiable persona" and "substitution for an authorized release") was met with A's sharpest rejoinder: that is a right not to face a disclosed competitor, i.e., the motion's conclusion smuggled in as premise. B's best counter—the soundalike distinction based on cheap AI replication and no bargaining performer—was absorbed by A's "production method, not a different entitlement" response, and B never explained why the estate rather than the living performer benefits from that distinction. A also consistently pressed the motion-breadth problem ("style or likeness" versus B's narrowed "artist-specific manner"), and while B offered a plausible two-route reading, the administrability burden (courts resolving "recognizable constellations") stayed unresolved. Execution costs compound B's problems: both B rebuttals were clipped for exceeding the word limit, so their full force was never seen. A's A1/A2 answers were direct, labeled, and cited opponent claims accurately; A's closing crystallized the decisive clash cleanly. B's case was coherent and moderate, with an intuitively appealing anti-appropriation frame, but ended the exchange on the defensive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer to B3.2 ("none: no concrete right is injured"), followed by the reframing of B's substitution harm as "a right not to face a disclosed competitor" and the dismantling of the soundalike distinction as a production-method difference rather than a different entitlement. This neutralized B's strongest remaining route to victory and made A's anti-deception alternative the cleaner decision rule.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B wins the policy comparison. A identifies a plausible identity harm beyond deception, but inadequately explains why disclosed AI resemblance warrants prior permission while living soundalikes remain permissible. B also exposes the unresolved breadth of “style” and the practical veto inherent in requiring approval. A’s reassurance that other studios could seek licenses never answers an estate’s ability to refuse them. B’s questioning and subsequent rebuttal sharpen these weaknesses; A’s questions largely invite B to reinforce its existing case. A’s twice-clipped rebuttals add an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B answers A’s soundalike distinction by separating production method from entitlement: cheap replication and the absence of a bargaining performer do not themselves establish why estates should control disclosed imitation. A’s closing repeats those distinctions without resolving that challenge or outweighing the gatekeeping costs.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): A built the cleaner path to victory by holding B to the motion’s broad “style or likeness” wording and exposing the missing entitlement behind estate licensing. B offered a coherent persona-appropriation concern, but repeatedly narrowed the rule toward artist-specific impersonation without resolving boundary uncertainty or explaining why disclosed competition itself justifies prior permission. A’s soundalike and tribute comparisons were damaging, and its closing crystallized the remaining dispute as resemblance and lost sales rather than deception or a taken asset. B’s two clipped rebuttals further weakened its execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 directly answered both pressure questions: no objective resemblance threshold exists, and “substitution for an authorized release” describes competition rather than an independently established right. Its response that cheap AI replication changes scale, not entitlement, effectively neutralized B’s soundalike distinction.
