### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built a coherent case on control, fairness, and results, then decisively exploited Side A's own concessions. A's opening argued producer fees were regressive and hidden; A's rebuttal 1 then flipped to call embedded fees "visible" and "targeted" and the municipal tax a "hidden subsidy from low-use households to high-use ones" — adopting B's fairness frame outright — only to revert to the regressivity claim in later speeches. That internal inconsistency, plus A's concession that no taxpayer curbside system matches deposit-return recovery rates, left B's core route intact and unanswered. B's pressure questions sharply surfaced these contradictions, and B answered A's questions directly with the A1/A2 format and honest concessions ("I don't need a guarantee"). B's closing crystallized the clash memorably ("the same trucks run the same routes; the only thing that changes is who gets the invoice"). A's remaining material — risk-smoothing via municipal borrowing and universal service — was real but partially mishandled, since B showed it means residents absorb the risk through debt. B had more clipped turns (format cost), but A also clipped once, and the argumentative edge clearly favors B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.5: B answered A's "redesign vs. price pass-through" question by showing both outcomes beat the status quo under a recyclability-scaled fee, then compiled A's own concessions (visible targeted cost, hidden tax subsidy, high deposit-return rates) to leave almost nothing of CON's case standing.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built a cohesive, grounded case on design incentives, proportional fairness, and real-world recovery rates while cleanly dismantling Side A's (CON) objections. Side A suffered a severe self-contradiction in [A2.3]-[A2.4] by arguing PRO's exact position—that price-embedded levies are visible and drive recyclable design whereas municipal taxes are an invisible subsidy—and never resolved that contradiction under pressure, even reverting in closing to calling shelf-price pass-throughs hidden. Side B also neutralized the waste-deserts claim by separating public route operations from who pays the invoice, refuted the claim that recyclability-scaled fees reward exotic polymers, and turned municipal bond-smoothing into taxpayer debt for corporate packaging choices. Side B's repeated minor word-limit clips at the end of four turns are the main execution flaw keeping the margin shy of 3.0.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side A inadvertently argued in [A2.3]-[A2.4] that producer levies are visible and push the market toward recyclable designs while municipal taxes create a hidden subsidy, Side B pinned that concession in [B3.1] and paired it in [B4.1]-[B4.2] with a decisive answer to Side A's Q1: whether firms redesign packaging to cut fees or pass fees into prices where cleaner-packaged rivals can undercut them, both paths beat taxpayer funding's zero redesign incentive, while difficulty-scaled fees penalize rather than reward exotic polymers.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A won by keeping the motion on who pays, not who drives the truck. It showed that producer fees either spur redesign or shift cost onto consumption, while taxes hide a cross-subsidy and leave designers unbilled. Side B never repaired its own concession that an embedded fee is visible and market-forcing while a municipal tax is a hidden low-use-to-high-use subsidy, and its volatility and universal-service points did not explain why the same collection system could not simply invoice producers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's capture of B2.3–B2.4, followed by the answer that both redesign and price pass-through beat taxpayer funding, neutralized Con's redesign-guarantee attack and left little reason to keep the cost in the tax channel.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): Side A (CON) built a coherent three-pronged case (incidence falls on shoppers, the weight-based incentive points toward unrecyclable lightweighting, and producer-controlled funds create incumbent capture) and sharpened it every round. Side B (PRO) repeatedly undermined itself: it conceded prices rise "a few cents," conceded the lightweighting threat is real, then in [B4.3] defended the fee as a household "consumption-based contribution," which abandons the motion's core claim that producers rather than taxpayers pay. CON's demolition of the "progressive because smaller in absolute terms" argument (the bread-tax analogy) was decisive and unanswered. PRO also leaned on brittle fake precision (Swedish 25% pouch reduction, 80% PET rates, exact margin and fee percentages) that CON correctly flagged as unverifiable, and PRO's closing was a recap of [B4.1]/[B4.2] that dodged the live concessions. CON's pressure questions were sharper and its rebuttals did the comparative weighing work; PRO's answers, while properly formatted, opened new vulnerabilities rather than closing them.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.5–A4.6: CON trapped PRO between its own concessions—PRO's [B4.3] defense of a household "consumption-based contribution" proved shoppers, not producers, pay, and CON's point that measuring progressivity by absolute rather than proportional burden would make a bread tax "progressive" destroyed PRO's equity fallback and was never answered.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): Side A (CON) won decisively by dismantling Side B's case on both fiscal incidence and packaging incentives while maintaining far superior consistency and epistemic discipline. Side A established early that industry-wide packaging fees across thin-margin staples pass through to shelf prices, then relentlessly tracked Side B's contradictions—moving from corporate profit absorption, to "a few cents" per item (which across annual purchases funds the whole bill), to defending a household consumption levy in Turn 7 as "progressive" based on absolute spending rather than share of income. On design incentives, Side A locked in Side B's concession that weight-based fees encourage unrecyclable multilayer pouches unless corrected by regulatory rules, then caught Side B staking its closing on per-gram weight savings anyway. Side B's solid point on ring-fenced funding stability could not offset its backwards Q1 (which attributed its own claims to Side A), brittle Swedish statistics, and repetitive closing, though both sides paid a minor execution cost for tail-end clipping.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Turn 8 refutation ([A4.1], [A4.5]-[A4.6]) of Side B's equity pivot: Side A showed that Side B had abandoned corporate payment to defend a checkout levy on shoppers, and punctured Side B's confusion between absolute spending and tax incidence ("Progressivity is measured by share of income. By PRO's test a bread tax would be progressive").
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON)): Side B wins because it converted PRO's concessions on price pass-through and regulator-dependent redesign into a decisive attack on the motion as written. PRO's equity and incentive case relied on unverified specifics and later collapsed into a household consumption charge, while CON showed deposits and recyclability rules could do the useful work without a regressive checkout levy. PRO's misaimed questions and closing that restated the gram-saving incentive left the core clash unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal and closing: once PRO admitted shelf prices rise and that only a regulator schedule stops weight fees from favoring unrecyclable pouches, the producer-pay claim reduced to a new name on a shopper-funded cheque.
