### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B built the stronger analytical case by separating the proposition’s aggregate net-harm premise from the unresolved comparative question of whether arrival caps outperform targeted rules. A made a vivid case about simultaneous crowds, finite space, pollution, and enforcement, but too often asserted that net harm meant capacity was exhausted or that caps were the only complete remedy. B answered with timed access, emissions conditions, and housing protections while avoiding double-counted benefits. A’s closing effectively exposed B’s concessions that district controls could become restrictive and berth caps could sometimes be easier to enforce, but B had already framed those as context-dependent comparisons rather than surrendering its central burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal decisively identified A’s leap from total damage under current rules exceeding revenue to the claim that the particular arrivals excluded by a cap would remain net-harmful after targeted reforms. Its warning against crediting a cap with relief already delivered by those reforms remained unanswered.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): A won by exposing the missing step from aggregate harm to a marginal arrival cap. Timed district access, routing, emissions conditions, and conversion rules could provide the same relief at lower cost, while B mainly asserted that volume and convergence made caps necessary. B's enforcement critique of pedestrian passes narrowed the gap but did not prove streets are full throughout the usable day or that targeted pollution rules remain insufficient.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's total-versus-marginal rebuttal in A2.5 was decisive: B repeatedly treated today's net damage as proof that the next arrival excluded after better rules would cause net harm, without answering the double-counting and comparison problem.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): A won by turning B’s district-pass concession into a de facto cap and arguing that historic cores have fixed daytime bottlenecks that targeted rules cannot fully cure. B’s total-versus-marginal harm distinction was sharp, but it relied too heavily on contingent enforcement assumptions and conceded that berth caps may be preferable when streets stay full.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 and closing answered B’s pressure by treating the whole street network as the bottleneck, making B’s district passes look like a harder-to-enforce version of the same cap.

### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): A made the clearer conditional case: pricing, transfers, and cleaner vehicles may remove the need for a cap, but they do not answer a persistently overloaded place when every operator pays. A also answered both pressure questions directly and accommodated substitution risks without abandoning the motion. B offered strong efficiency and housing-substitution objections, but relied too heavily on speculative market shifts and repeatedly mischaracterized binding arrival slots as a retreat from capping.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 exposed the central flaw in B’s pricing alternative: either the tariff suppresses some trips, sacrificing business just as a cap does, or everyone pays and the crowd remains; either way, operators’ willingness to pay does not establish that revenue exceeds residents’ losses.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): B won by defending the motion's conditional test: count revenue, substitution, and housing effects, but refuse the next arrival when resident costs still exceed gains. B showed pricing either filters arrivals itself or accepts the crowd, while willingness to pay does not prove residents are compensated. A's substitution and irreversibility warnings were serious, but B absorbed them as reasons to review and loosen caps rather than proof that a binding slot limit is never justified, and A overstated slot caps as outright banishment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B's response that refusing further morning bookings is what makes scheduling binding, coupled with the pricing dilemma: raise the tariff until transit clears and some trips are excluded anyway, or collect every fee while crowding persists.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): A won narrowly because it framed the motion as a marginal-cap question and kept resident welfare central. It conceded substitution and made caps self-correcting, blunting B’s backfire, while showing B’s pricing and transfer alternatives could clear a bus lane or raise revenue without proving the square was not over capacity or residents were compensated. B was sharp on substitution and planning risk, but its claim that efficient group modes should never be capped remained too absolute against the proposition’s conditional trigger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 and closing turned B’s festival surge-pricing point against it: a high bid measures operator willingness to pay, not resident harm, and a clear bus lane beside a packed square is too narrow a success test.
