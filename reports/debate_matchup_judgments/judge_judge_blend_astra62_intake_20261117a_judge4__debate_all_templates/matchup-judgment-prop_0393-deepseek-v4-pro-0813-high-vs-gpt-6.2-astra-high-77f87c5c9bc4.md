### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): B, arguing PRO, opened strongly. It argued that construction is the cheapest time to add access, that a retrofit is a poor substitute, and that universal design benefits nearly everyone. A, arguing CON, then steadily narrowed the clash to one question. Does a uniform baseline produce the most usable homes for households under fixed budgets? A's tools were the nonprofit case, the household-level "choice" test, and a concrete enforcement mechanism. That mechanism means delivering accessible units by size and location through permits and subsidy conditions. Together these gave A a coherent alternative rather than plain obstruction. A answered both pressure questions directly, with clean A1:/A2: labels. A's answer to Q2 neutralized B's best challenge, which was what guarantees the targeted units actually get built. B gave a good line in its A1 answer, distinguishing features that gate access from features that can be adapted later. However, B mishandled A's Q2 dilemma. It treated the dropped family apartment as inaccessible when A's hypothetical had specified it was accessible. A exposed this error cleanly. B's second rebuttal was also clipped, which counts as an execution cost. B's closing largely repeated "fund the baseline" and the fire-exit analogy. It never engaged A's point that funding the baseline is sometimes not an option the builder actually has. A did the better comparative weighing, so A takes the debate. The margin is not larger because B's retrofit-cost and long-horizon framing remained a live, reasonable route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.4–A4.5 decided the exchange. B had answered A's Q2 by claiming the dropped two-bedroom was "an inaccessible two-bedroom" that is "not a real choice." A pointed out that the two-bedroom in the hypothetical was already accessible, so enforcing the studio clearance would strike the one home that fit the family. A added that calling this a "financing failure" only "identifies whom to ask for more money; it does not put the home back." This turned B's own access-as-precondition logic against an inflexible baseline.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B made the decisive trade-off clearer: an inaccessible studio and an unbuilt accessible two-bedroom can both represent lost housing choices. B answered A’s delivery-mechanism challenge with enforceable permit and subsidy requirements, while A’s insistence on funding the baseline never resolved what happens when money and layout genuinely cannot preserve both. A presented a strong lock-in and retrofit case, but its clipped second rebuttal and repeated fire-exit analogy left the central opportunity cost insufficiently answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it corrected A’s misdescription of the already-accessible two-bedroom, showed that calling the dilemma a financing failure does not restore the lost home, and proposed proof of an unavoidable conflict rather than mere hardship pleading.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by reframing choice per household rather than per unit and giving it concrete force: under a fixed budget, universal studio clearances could eliminate an already-accessible two-bedroom. Side A answered the trade-off mainly by demanding more subsidy and, when pressed, said it would enforce the standard even then, leaving B's central loss unrepaired. B also answered A's mechanism pressure with permit and subsidy conditions tied to documented shortages by size, rent, and location, while distinguishing structural features from adaptable ones.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it accepted that inaccessible units are not choices for wheelchair users, applied the same test to a studio for a two-bedroom family, and showed A's enforcement position sacrifices one form of access for another rather than expanding overall choice.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built the cleaner causal story. Layout decisions are cheap at design stage and impossible to recover later, so targeted units and retrofit grants cannot substitute when a household's needs change. PRO also defined a concrete standard and a defensible feature-level exemption, which kept its case inside "modest premium." CON's supply and regressivity concerns were real but mostly asserted, never quantified, and it conceded in A1 that no retrofit fund can recover misallocated space. That left relocation as CON's only remedy, which PRO effectively dismantled. CON's main late attack, that PRO retreated from the motion, overreached. Basic-capacity requirements are still stronger universal-design standards, and an exemption where costs are not modest fits the motion's own condition. It does not concede that costs are high on common sites. CON's closing turn, that a mandate makes accessible vacancies scarcer, was clever but came late and stayed thin. CON's first rebuttal was also clipped, a real execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B's Q1 and B4.5 against CON's walker critique. CON had argued that new construction rules cannot help current tenants. PRO replied that tomorrow's new building becomes existing housing when its tenant later needs a wheelchair. That pushed CON into conceding in A1 that retrofit cannot recover the turning space, which undercut CON's targeted-retrofit alternative.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): A made the clearer comparative case: paying a modest upfront premium can preserve a home’s usability, while targeted retrofits and relocation cannot always recover space or guarantee a suitable affordable vacancy. B raised genuine supply, affordability, and exemption-enforcement concerns, but too often asserted unit losses and overstated A’s consistent feature list and feature-specific exemption as abandonment of the motion. A answered both pressure questions directly and weighed lost units against lost use of existing homes; B’s clipped first rebuttal also weakened execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B honestly conceded that retrofit funding cannot reclaim bathroom space allocated to a neighboring unit, then offered relocation assistance. A turned that concession into the decisive comparison: assistance with leaving is not the ability to stay, and relocation still depends on finding an affordable accessible vacancy. A’s direct, concrete answers on exemptions and required features further blunted B’s narrowing attack.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by establishing that basic layout capacity cannot be retrofitted once space is allocated elsewhere, while Side B ultimately concedes this and substitutes relocation assistance. A also answers the existing-tenant objection by noting tomorrow's new apartments become future existing homes, and its cost-ceiling exemption and defined “basic capacity” were consistent with its opening rather than a retreat. B's supply and regressivity concerns remain serious, but its targeted alternative depends on finding an affordable accessible vacancy, which A effectively frames as losing a home rather than preserving one.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A4.5 turning B's “new standards don't help current tenants” point into a future-tenant argument, followed by B4.1 conceding a retrofit fund cannot recover bathroom turning space and offering relocation instead.
