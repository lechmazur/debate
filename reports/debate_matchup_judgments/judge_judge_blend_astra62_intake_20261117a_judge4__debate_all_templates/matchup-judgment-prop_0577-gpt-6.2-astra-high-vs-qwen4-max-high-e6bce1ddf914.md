### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B wins a clear but competitive decision. B’s central test—whether a permit prevents an irregular entry or stay rather than merely legalizing a hire—did more work. A’s strongest move was the conversion/guaranteed floor and the overstay point, but B showed a manifest only proves legal entry, not a switched journey, and payroll records do not secure departure. B also pressed the employer-cost objection: unless legal routes change hiring incentives, larger quotas may leave the cash contractor in place. A’s perverse-effects argument about enforcement was vivid but overclaimed its net effect; B properly distinguished first-trip deterrence from post-crossing debt. A had memorable framing, but B gave the cleaner route to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttal of A’s “guaranteed floor”: a legal worker picking berries proves legal status, not that anyone abandoned an irregular journey or displaced an irregular hire. That directly removed A’s no-counterfactual conversion claim.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate through superior analytical precision and decisive rebuttals on the core causal chain. Side A had stronger empirical grounding—effectively using Germany's Western Balkans route, Spain's Huelva contracts, historical border lock-in, and border patrols' blindness to visa overstayers—which kept the round close. However, Side B established the governing metric (irregular entries and stays actually prevented) and exposed two critical flaws in Side A's case. First, via [B3.1] and [B4.3], Side B showed that where contractors profit by evading minimum wages and payroll costs, expanding employer-linked permits does not induce those employers to hire legally, whereas border enforcement can still price or delay prospective crossers out of the trip regardless of employer preferences. Second, when Side A conceded in [A5.1] that direct substitution is symmetric and staked the debate on conversion providing a counterfactual-free floor, Side B dismantled that claim by showing that putting a harvest hire on a manifest does not reduce irregular migration unless it prevents an irregular crossing or ends an existing irregular stay.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's takedown of Side A's conversion floor and harvest-reallocation claims across [B4.3] and [B5.1]-[B5.3] (building on [B2.5]). When Side A argued that legal manifests guarantee counterfactual-free conversion while deterred crossings merely shift berry-picking to interior cash workers, Side B showed this conflates formalizing farm labor with reducing irregular migration: deterring a family's crossing still eliminates one irregular entry and subsequent stay even if an already-present worker picks the crop, whereas hiring a legal recruit abroad neither proves a switched journey nor causes an interior irregular worker to leave.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B won a close, substantive debate by keeping the comparison fixed on net irregular entries and stays prevented. A offered vivid mechanisms and a strong critique of enforcement-induced settlement, but never showed that this effect outweighs deterred first journeys. A’s later “conversion” and “guaranteed floor” claims depended on the very counterfactual A said they avoided. B answered both pressure questions directly, conceded enforcement’s limitations honestly, and consistently exposed the targeting and employer-demand assumptions in A’s case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer in Rebuttal 2 and closing reversal of the harvest example were decisive: a legal manifest does not prove an irregular journey was displaced, while hiring someone already inside after a deterred crossing can leave the harvest unchanged yet eliminate an irregular entry and stay.
