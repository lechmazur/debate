### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Both sides were exceptionally strong and disciplined, with clean pressure questions, direct A1/A2 answers, and real progression. A's best live route was burden allocation: an individual cannot detect cohort-level screening, so the duty to test must sit with the institution. B's disclosure-plus-targeted-challenge alternative only partially answers this, since it still depends on someone initiating a claim. However, B's counter-route — that a disparity trigger taxes the very individualized, age-correlated evidence (pension deposits, tenure, app behavior) that helps older applicants, pushing firms toward cruder, more defensible screens — survived the whole exchange. A's validation-protocol answer (A4.3–A4.4) was the strongest repair, but B's reply that "feasible," "comparable," and "reasonable" are settled only in the very proceeding a firm's exposure depends on, and that ignoring deposits shrinks the alternative set ex ante, was never fully neutralized; A's closing largely restated the ideal-audit outcome rather than resolving the ex ante deterrence. B also conceded honestly under pressure (A2 on pension deposits) and crystallized the choice more memorably: "condemn exclusionary rules, not the instruments that show an older person qualifies." Narrow but real edge to CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.2, where B conceded that pension deposits "should win" in an ideal audit but showed that the firm buys the model before any audit, and that ignoring deposits both keeps observed disparity below the trigger and shrinks the set of "reasonably available alternatives" — directly turning A's validation answer into proof of the incentive problem. A's strongest counter was A4.1–A4.2, showing that disclosure of a single decision cannot reveal cohort-level weighting effects, which is why the trigger must fall on the institution.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side A built the cleaner, more consistent path to victory by proving that individual rejected applicants lack the pool-wide visibility to challenge opaque screens, while turning Side B's flagship "retired borrower" example to PRO's advantage. Pressed by A's Q1 on how hidden cutoffs get challenged, Side B retreated from defending current law to proposing mandatory disclosure of material factors and validation logic—implicitly conceding the need for stronger protection while still failing to show how one applicant given a facially neutral reason (like a collateral cutoff or weighted resume gap) could detect systemic exclusion without pool-level testing. Side B kept a credible concern alive regarding ex-ante uncertainty when legitimate predictors correlate with age, but Side A mitigated it with a concrete out-of-sample pre-deployment benchmark and exposed the logical flaw in B's claim that dropping helpful income data would reduce disparity exposure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's retired-borrower argument across [A2.1], [A3.2], and [A5.3]: when B claimed in [B4.2] that ignoring pension deposits helps a lender stay below a disparity trigger and shrink "reasonably available alternatives," A cleanly noted that ignoring verified income that approves more older borrowers at comparable risk neither reduces age disparity nor makes existing account data "unavailable," flipping B's chilling-effect example into an incentive to retain the individualized model.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): Side A built the stronger path to victory by showing why disclosure and complaint-driven challenges cannot reliably expose cumulative, hidden exclusion, while institutions can test whole decision pools before deployment. B’s legal-uncertainty and perverse-incentive critique was serious and memorably framed, but A answered it with concrete validation steps, comparable-risk comparisons, and a contemporaneous reasonableness benchmark. B never fully explained how its narrower alternative would systematically detect harms that produce no informed complaint, or why firms would abandon demonstrably more accurate models that A’s test allows them to defend.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B’s pressure on ex ante compliance and disparity-versus-validity, followed by A’s Rebuttal 2: disclosure explains one rejection but cannot reveal pool-wide exclusion, while pre-deployment validation and documented alternatives give firms a workable defense without treating disparity itself as wrongdoing.
