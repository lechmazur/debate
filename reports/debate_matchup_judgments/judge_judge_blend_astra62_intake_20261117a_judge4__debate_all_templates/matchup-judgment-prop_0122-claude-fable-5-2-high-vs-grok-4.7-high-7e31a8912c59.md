### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): A won by turning PRO's own concession into the decisive clash. Once B admitted that a thirteen-year-old could still be tried on proof, found to have committed the act, and securely confined, A showed this was responsibility under a gentler name. A's strongest move was the guilt/danger distinction: confinement for a proven act has a proportionality ceiling, while confinement for predicted danger can be renewed without one. B's fallback was that conviction and protective placement are legally different, but A undermined this by showing the practical coercion remains and weak states could abuse the welfare route even if the criminal conviction is barred. B had a principled capacity argument, but A's comparative realism and final weighing made the blanket floor look like a re-labeling with worse safeguards. Both sides had clipped rebuttals, but A's surviving line was cleaner and more damaging.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's exploitation of B's secure-placement admission: the state still locks the child up, but now for "danger" rather than guilt, removing proportionality and making the floor a locked door under a gentler name.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.2 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): A ran the strongest available CON strategy: a dilemma forcing B to either gut its alternative of coercive power or concede it is juvenile justice renamed, plus a sharp "guilt is the limit, danger has no ceiling" reframe. But B answered the dilemma honestly and directly (A1/A2 format, conceding compulsory placement on proof beyond reasonable doubt with determinate terms while insisting the conviction, record, and offender pathway are the distinct harms the motion targets), and B's "a floor is a ban, not a hope of competence" institutional answer to weak-state realism outperformed A's symmetry counter. A's own concessions — that seven is too low and that the record could simply be sealed — narrowed its ground to a label-and-proportionality argument that B's closing ("the difference is the finding of criminal guilt"; "Above it, mitigate. Below it, protect") crystallized more cleanly. Both sides followed format and used no fake sourcing; A's clipped second rebuttal cost it its wrap-up while B's clipped first rebuttal lost less substance, which slightly tilts a near-even exchange toward B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The A2.1–B4.1–A4.4 sequence: A's dilemma about compulsory welfare tribunals forced B to concede the state may confine a thirteen-year-old on proof beyond reasonable doubt, and A pounced that "a state that confines a child for a fixed term because of what he did is holding him responsible." B's recovery — that protective placement answers danger without declaring guilt, and that only the conviction/record/offender pathway is what "fully punishable" means — preserved B's route to victory and set up the winning closing.
