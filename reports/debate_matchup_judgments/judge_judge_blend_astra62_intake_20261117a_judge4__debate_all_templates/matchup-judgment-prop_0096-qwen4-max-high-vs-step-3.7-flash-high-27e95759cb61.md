PRO adapted across rounds and closed by crystallizing the concession rather than adding new material.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins clearly. It confined the motion to dubbing and sorted PRO's worst cases, fake endorsements and unwanted spinoffs, into problems that publicity law and purpose limits already handle. It then seized on PRO's concession that a single project-wide opt-in satisfies the rule. That turned PRO's remedy into "boilerplate with a heading," signed at the same table with the same leverage and still no set price. CON also pressed repeatedly that the motion sets no rate, so PRO's promised "additional compensation" was never part of what it was defending. When PRO pushed on non-union performers, CON answered directly: a statute capping voice grants binds non-union contracts just as universally as opt-in does. PRO never engaged that answer. PRO's closing still described CON's position as collective bargaining, which by then misdescribed it. PRO also leaned on an unsupported "80%" figure in three speeches. Its unconscionability answer was effectively rebutted as a costly, slow lawsuit route that leaves performers no better off. CON had a minor tension of its own: it said the motion forbids consent inside the contract, yet also treated a signing-time box as compliant. PRO never exploited this. Across rounds CON narrowed the clash, while PRO mostly repeated its framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1–A2.2 decided the debate. CON took PRO's own line that "Studios can negotiate a single opt-in for all dubbing" and set up a dilemma: either opt-in is boilerplate with a highlighted box, or it is a per-use veto that creates holdouts. PRO never escaped this. CON reinforced it in A4.1 and the closing with the line "The motion changes the font, not the bargain."
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): A won the surviving comparison. B successfully rebutted per-language holdouts, but never established that opt-in supplies the compensation or bargaining protection it repeatedly promised. A exposed B's unsupported legal presumption and explained how statutory scope limits could protect non-union performers; B's closing instead repeated the union-only objection. B also relied on unsupported categorical legal claims and an ungrounded statistic. However, A understated the value of informed agreement and belatedly strengthened its alternative, keeping this competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's zero-pay question forced B to invoke unconscionability. A then explained that an existing legal remedy does not establish a new payment entitlement from opt-in, directly undermining B's promised compensation protection.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 4 Max (high) (CON)): Side B won by isolating dubbing from endorsements and unrelated sequels, then pressing a dilemma Side A never escaped: a single project-wide opt-in is merely highlighted boilerplate signed under the same leverage, while per-use consent creates holdout and substitution problems. B also showed the motion sets no pay floor, so the promised compensation and presumption against free extraction were additions, not the proposition. Side A's closing mischaracterized B's universal statutory scope-and-price alternative as union-only collective bargaining.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal was decisive: if one project-wide opt-in satisfies the rule, consent becomes paperwork shielding the studio; if consent must be renewed, the holdout objection applies, and a floor of zero is not a floor.
