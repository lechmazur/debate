- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B wins a competitive, high-level debate by exposing the boundary dilemma in store-only structural separation and pairing it with concrete, unanswered conduct remedies. ### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): A razor-thin win for PRO. The debate narrowed to two decisive clashes: (1) whether the incentive survives separation via "total ecosystem value," and (2) whether favoritism becomes more or less detectable after a split. On (1), B's claim that the flagship remains the ecosystem's lock-in driver was strong, but A's rejoinder—that this confirms the danger and that separation removes the direct bottom-line tie while forcing preference into externally visible cross-entity acts—held. On (2), B's inversion (internal paper trail vs. opaque "most favored customer" contracts) was the most original move of the debate, but A's counter that internal discretion is invisible to developers while bilateral deals are auditable, challengeable artifacts was at least as plausible, and A's closing crystallized it cleanly. Critically, B never answered A's empirical point that conduct remedies are "the experiment we are already living—slow, case-by-case, one API change behind"; B asserted that conduct rules attach to the levers but did not address the track-record critique, leaving a live route for A unneutralized. Both sides followed format perfectly, answered pressure questions directly with A1/A2 labels, and avoided fake sourcing. A's consistent conversion of B's best lines ("the gate remains" becomes the premise, not the defense) plus the unanswered conduct-remedy-failure point gives A the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Round 4's detectability clash: B2.3 argued separation trades visible internal bias for opaque bilateral dealing, and A4.1/A5.3 answered that an internal budget line is self-policed and invisible while a cross-entity contract is an external, auditable act subject to an identical-terms test. A's conversion of B's strongest original argument into PRO's visibility win was the debate's decisive exchange.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A won a competitive, well-argued debate by prevailing on the decisive clash over incentives and enforceability. Side B mounted a clever offensive, arguing that because the gatekeeper keeps the platform levers, separation merely shifts favoritism from traceable internal decisions into "most favored customer" contracts while introducing friction into integrated security and user features. However, Side A convincingly demonstrated that severing ownership removes the direct profit motive to throttle rivals and gives neutrality rules an external corporate boundary to audit, whereas integrated firms hide preference inside informal product discretion and shared roadmaps. Side A also smartly turned Side B's attack on defining "comparably situated" developers back against Side B's own conduct-remedy counterplan under vertical integration. Side B kept the margin competitive with credible points on integration friction and split accountability that Side A left quiet in the final turns, alongside a brief phrasing stumble by Side A in A4.3/A5.1, but Side A built the cleaner and more persuasive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's "contract drawer" turn across [A3.1], [A4.1]-[A4.2], and [A5.3]-[A5.4]: Side A showed that structural separation does not replace conduct rules but "gives them an external object to bite on," converting invisible internal management discretion into auditable cross-entity terms, and then showed that Side B's critique of "comparably situated" tests condemns a conflicted owner-gatekeeper far more than a separated one.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by keeping the burden on whether separation actually prevents self-preferencing. Its ecosystem-incentive and contractual-favoritism arguments survived A’s claim that ownership removal makes neutrality enforceable, while B also consistently defended direct conduct rules and integration costs. A’s identical-terms test and external-audit response were strong, but did not fully show why separation outperforms those rules when the operator retains every gatekeeping lever.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first and second rebuttals were decisive: favoritism could persist through ecosystem lock-in and facially lawful contracts rather than disappear with ownership. A answered with auditability and comparably situated treatment, but never completely closed the gap between making favoritism challengeable and preventing it.
