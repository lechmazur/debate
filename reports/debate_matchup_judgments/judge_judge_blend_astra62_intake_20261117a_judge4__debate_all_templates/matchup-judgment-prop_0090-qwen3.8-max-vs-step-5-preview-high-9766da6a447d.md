### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins with a coherent through-line: core safety and visibility functions need a zero-glance, tactile override that still works under degraded conditions such as glare, gloves, vibration, lag or a reboot. CON's best idea was that safety depends on implementation rather than medium, and it was a real one. But CON's case leaned heavily on steering-wheel shortcuts, haptics and automation. PRO exploited this effectively: a steering-wheel button is itself a physical control, so CON's safest path concedes PRO's principle. CON's replies did not neutralize that dilemma: - Calling it a "semantic trick" and "redundant modalities of a software system" was not persuasive. - In closing, CON misattributed those shortcut examples to PRO's own evidence. CON also repeatedly inflated the motion into a "guarantee of safety" or a "blanket mandate" that bans screens. PRO correctly rejected this burden early, and CON never recovered from that correction. On the layout-variation point, PRO's answer was that bad buttons are fixable implementation issues, while a screen hub adds common-mode failure. That reasonably answered CON's strongest pressure. Neither side offered measured data. PRO's structural reasoning about glance burden was more convincing than CON's appeals to updatability and standardization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange is PRO's Rebuttal 2 (B4.2/B4.5). PRO argued that if CON's fastest zero-glance path is a steering-wheel shortcut, that shortcut is a physical control. CON is therefore left choosing between conceding the need for hardware and trusting a flat tile that fails under degradation. CON's reply (A4.1/A4.4) relabeled the shortcut as a "software modality" but did not answer the substance of this dilemma.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the debate focused on the decisive weakness in Side A's position: PRO argued that core functions need a zero-glance manual override under degradation, while CON repeatedly located safety in physical shortcuts, haptics, or automation. Those alternatives either depend on hardware inputs, thereby conceding PRO's principle, or fail to provide the same fallback when a display lags, reboots, or washes out. CON's strongest reframe—that safety depends on implementation, not material—was real and well argued, but it did not neutralize PRO's structural point that a dedicated physical switch can be found and confirmed by feel while a touchscreen tile still requires visual targeting. PRO also absorbed the bad-layout objection effectively by arguing that poor physical controls are fixable design failures, whereas routing core functions through a screen adds common failure modes. The exchange left Side B with the cleaner path to the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was PRO's pressure and rebuttal that CON's safest examples—steering-wheel shortcuts—are physical controls, so CON's own case shows the safe path is not mainly the touchscreen. CON's answer that these are merely software-triggered modalities was less persuasive and left the display-failure path still dependent on visual or non-tactile interaction.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO wins on the central mechanism. A physical control can be located by contour and muscle memory without looking, while even a fixed screen icon requires a glance, and the conditions that make defrost and hazards urgent (fog, glare, gloves) are the ones that degrade screens. CON's best answer was A1, and it conceded that the goal is "not perfect blind operation," which admits the eyes-off-road gap PRO built its case on. CON's escape routes also helped PRO: its fallback to steering-wheel shortcuts and isolated hazard circuits reads as a request for tactile access. PRO's A2 answer kept temperature, fan, direction, and defrost physical, which drew a defensible line on what counts as "mainly." CON scored real points. Its backup-versus-primary distinction is sound engineering logic, and the "buttons hide state" point went largely unanswered. Still, CON's case leans on idealized screen design, while PRO's mechanism stays more intuitive and was never squarely neutralized. PRO had minor overclaims, such as "a button cannot glitch" and "exact seconds," but they were not decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came in Rebuttal 2. PRO's A1 argued that learning an icon's location is not the same as blind operation, because flat glass offers no edge or detent and haptics confirm only after the press. That forced CON to concede that "the safety goal is not perfect blind operation." The concession turned the debate into a glance-versus-no-glance comparison, which favors PRO under degraded visibility.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the clash on no-look, no-software operation under degraded driving conditions. Side A's well-designed touchscreen response was plausible but more conditional: it still required a glance, visible state, or fallback input. B effectively turned A's concession that core functions need independent backup into a reason to make tactile controls primary for the exact functions drivers need under stress. A defended the screen-first model ably, but B gave the cleaner, more intuitive path to victory on safety and reliability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive move was pressing the fallback concession: if hazard and defrost need independent backup when a display fails, the control required in the failure mode should be primary. B then showed that fixed icons can be learned but still cannot be found by contour in fog, glare, or gloves, narrowing A's screen-design answer.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO wins because it preserves a live tactile distinction CON never neutralizes: fixed icons still need a visible glance, while shaped controls can be found by feel in fog, glare, or gloves. CON's reliance on steering-wheel shortcuts, voice, and independent backups repeatedly concedes that non-screen tactile access is necessary for the urgent functions the motion names.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was Side B's Rebuttal 2 A1 answer: learning an icon's location is not blind operation because glass gives no edge or detent and haptics only confirm after contact, directly answering CON's fixed-dock defense.
