CON kept that comparison sharper through the close; PRO’s broken closing citations further weakened crystallization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s electoral-lock-in exchange was decisive: a binding flawed reform can skew the very parliament meant to reverse it, and A’s reply treated that as a design quibble rather than neutralizing the threat to its reversibility claim.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON)): CON wins the central accountability clash. CON also more persuasively shows transient, unstaffed members remain vulnerable to untracked informational lobbying, whereas PRO mainly lists safeguards without showing they create real sanction or monitoring.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 answer on self-undermining correction: parliamentary inaction preserves electoral accountability, but a bad binding electoral rule can tilt that very correction mechanism before voters can respond.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by making the central clash about accountability clearer: advisory power diffuses responsibility and allows politicians to ignore or bury citizen recommendations, while binding power creates a visible, contestable decision that can still be corrected through elections, courts, sunsets, and later review. CON’s lock-in and self-corruption worries were serious, but PRO successfully framed them as design problems rather than fatal objections, and CON never fully solved the incumbent-conflict problem under advisory assemblies.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by keeping the debate on legitimacy for coercive power and forcing a live contradiction in Side A’s own terms. A made a plausible case that elections fail when harms are diffuse and donor rewards concentrated, and correctly noted that ordinary statutes are also later revisable. But A never discharged the accountability burden: members vanish, long-term costs fall on others, and the offered checks—testimony, written reasons, civilian return, and later legislative revision—are weaker than an electoral sanction. B’s “cannot have it both ways” line, pressed from the first rebuttal through the close, used A’s own concession that assembly decisions are no more permanent than ordinary statutes. A’s answers to agenda capture and minority overreach stayed soft and partly circular. B therefore left the cleaner reason not to transfer binding authority.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal, especially the claim that A cannot call decisions binding while also treating free later legislative revision as the safety valve, was the decisive exchange and remained unanswered in kind through the close.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side B made the central clash clearer: binding law needs electoral accountability, not just demographic resemblance. A effectively attacked hollow legislative accountability, but never resolved B's dilemma or distinguished binding effect from advisory advice merely revisable later. B's flawed-versus-absent check also answered the minority-rights comparison more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 dilemma was decisive: if legislatures can freely refine assembly decisions it is advisory, if they cannot there is no democratic correction. A repeated the revisable-statute concession through Rebuttal 2 and the closing instead of explaining the changed default.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.1 Pro (PRO)): B wins narrowly because it kept the debate focused on the affirmative problem: advisory assemblies can be ignored when elected officials face concentrated lobby pressure, while binding status removes the silent-shelving escape hatch. B also gave a workable account of binding power as legally operative but revisable like ordinary statutes, which blunted A's finality dilemma. A's accountability and minority-rights pressure was sharper rhetorically, but it leaned too heavily on treating binding as irrevocable and did not fully restore confidence in electoral accountability as a superior check.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's "either it truly binds or it is advisory" dilemma, met by B's ordinary-statute analogy and A's reply that later legislatures must answer for repeal, defined and narrowed the decisive clash.
