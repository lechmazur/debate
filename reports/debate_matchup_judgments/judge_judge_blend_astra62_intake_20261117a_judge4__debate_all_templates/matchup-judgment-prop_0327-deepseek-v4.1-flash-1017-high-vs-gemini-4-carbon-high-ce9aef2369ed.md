### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): CON wins a competitive debate because it kept two live routes intact while PRO's core harm story weakened over the exchange. First, CON's fork argument forced PRO into a costly concession. In B2.4, PRO said platforms need not carry gore or hate merchandise, yet also promised "content-neutral rules." CON's Q1 exposed that tension directly. PRO's A1 was honest: it admitted no content-neutral rule excludes those categories and conceded that porn is just a "test case." That honesty also committed PRO to forcing mature tiers for gore and offensive merchandise. CON exploited this well in A4.3 and A5.4. Second, CON's specialist-rails point undercut PRO's "livelihood veto" and "payment death sentence" stakes. PRO tried to turn this into a concession in B2.5 and the "necessity" closing, which was a clever reframe. CON answered it by arguing that separation across firms is exactly what preserves curated trust. CON also showed that creators are not left without payment options. PRO's best remaining route was the limiting principle. Its line that "market demand is simply the stigma measured" is sharp. However, CON offered a plausible stop line: explicit sexual commerce is a product category, not a viewpoint, and bookstores and broadcasters already draw that line daily. That leaves the limiting-principle clash roughly contested rather than a PRO win. On weighing, PRO's tiering solution never fully answered CON's institution-level brand-trust argument about parents, schools, and sponsoring banks. PRO's reply that the platform can "pass compliance costs" was also effectively mocked as unable to replace defecting partners.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q1 and the follow-up in A4.3 were decisive. CON pinned PRO's "content-neutral rules" claim against PRO's own carve-out for gore and hate merchandise. That forced PRO to admit porn is merely a "test case." CON then turned this into a broader burden: under PRO's rule, every family storefront would need mature tiers for porn, gore, and offensive merchandise alike. That made PRO's position look far more sweeping than its narrow opening framing.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO)): A wins by distinguishing separation across competing firms from mandatory adult tiers within each platform. B established that adult commerce can be governed safely, but not that age-gating preserves institution-wide brand promises or prevents customer and banking-partner exit. A also offered specialist processors and web distribution as alternatives whose inadequacy B did not establish. B's objection to unbounded reputational exclusion remained live, but A more convincingly weighed creator access against commercial autonomy and concrete relationship losses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A directly exposed the weakness in B's cost-recovery answer: passing along compliance costs cannot replace departing customers or sponsoring banks. B's closing returned to technical governability without resolving the distinction between safe operation and unwanted institution-wide association.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): CON more convincingly defended private brand curation as legitimate, not mere stigma, while showing specialist rails already supply access and that age-gates do not preserve a zero-association promise. PRO’s later admission that it would not defend brand exclusions of gore or offensive merchandise undercut its earlier distinctions and let CON show the principle forces mature tiers far beyond the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s exploitation of A’s retreat from content-neutrality in A4.1, turning the “test case” concession into a requirement that every platform host opt-in wings for porn, gore, and offensive merchandise.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a clear but competitive debate. PRO's case rested on three pillars: - **Bottleneck power.** Visa, Mastercard, Apple and Google are core infrastructure, not boutiques. - **A separable residue of stigma.** Once objective safeguards are met (fraud controls, chargeback caps, age-and-consent verification, geofencing), what remains is reputational fear alone. - **Perverse harms.** Exclusion drives lawful creators toward predatory shadow processors and harder-to-police markets. CON's case had real strengths. Its claim that "reputational risk is commercial risk" and its challenge to whether "solely" can be administered were the most serious pressure in the debate. However, PRO's pressure questions forced costly concessions: - **On abandonment.** CON admitted that few banks, merchants or consumers would actually abandon cards or phones. That shrank its earlier "rails buckle under backlash" story to quieter worries, such as a partner reducing co-branding. - **On gambling.** CON had argued gambling is carried only through tightly licensed, age-gated, jurisdiction-specific arrangements. PRO turned this into a concession that such category handling is administrable. - **On specialized channels.** PRO answered that niche processors still run on Visa and Mastercard rails, and mobile users still live on iOS and Android, so specialized processors are no real alternative. CON never squarely engaged the perverse-harms argument about shadow markets. Its main reply was the claim that a mandate would provoke blunter bans or exit. That claim was undercut by its own concession that partners would not flee these bottleneck networks. CON's equation of reputational risk with commercial risk also edges toward defining the motion away rather than answering it. PRO lost grounding credit for citing nonexistent reference IDs ([C1.3], [C1.7], [P1.4], [P2.6], [C4.2] and others). Even so, it made the decisive clash clearer and better answered the live pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange began with PRO's Q2, which asked which banks, merchants or consumers would realistically abandon cards or smartphones under an industry-wide norm. In A2, CON conceded that few would. PRO's second rebuttal and closing then leveraged that admission, together with CON's own gambling description, to argue that the remaining risk is "only stigma." This reduced CON's commercial-risk case to speculative, low-level brand discomfort.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): B wins on comparative harm and access. It explained why bottleneck exclusion threatens livelihoods and why specialized processors cannot bypass dominant payment rails. A persuasively showed that reputational losses need not involve mass abandonment, but did not establish why those costs outweigh exclusion or adequately defend its alternative channels. B's categorical claims that access creates no brand injury, along with repeated nonexistent reference IDs, narrow the victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response that specialized processors still depend on Visa and Mastercard directly undermined A's proposed alternative and remained unanswered. A's strongest counter—that partners can reduce co-branding without abandoning networks—successfully limited B's claim that bottleneck power eliminates reputational costs, but did not resolve the comparative stakes.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): Side A won by tying the motion's "solely reputational" test to platforms' existing age-gated, jurisdiction-specific handling of other controversial lawful categories, then weighing residual brand discomfort against bottleneck exile and less-safe shadow markets. Side B's brand-equals-commercial-risk and administrability points were the strongest counter and kept this competitive, but they mostly restated associational preference rather than identifying a non-reputational risk that equally verified adult creators uniquely pose. A's nonexistent reference IDs were a real execution cost and lowered grounding, not enough to erase the clearer comparative path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Q1 and second rebuttal were decisive: once B conceded jurisdiction-specific gating for gambling, exclusion of creators meeting the same fraud, consent, and geofencing controls collapsed into stigma, and B's later brand-association reply did not restore a distinct operational justification.
