### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): A won a clear but competitive debate. A built the stronger systemic mechanism: political pressure deters through unpredictable punishment, turns ambiguous rules into rational self-censorship, erases inquiry without a paper trail, and drives out skilled teachers. B's permanence point was strong, but A answered it by showing visible bad content can be challenged or corrected while invisible erasure destroys the analytical habits that make correction possible. B's reversibility response relying on "next unit" or "next hire" was too easy and did not adequately answer attrition or the chilling effect of public accusations.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A4.1/A4.2 was decisive: it met B's toughest pressure by explaining why deterrence scales with unpredictable punishment and why an unrecorded omission can be harder to correct than visible slant, since no alarming handout goes home to trigger scrutiny.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Both sides were competent, format-compliant, and free of fake sourcing, but Side B built the cleaner comparative path to victory. B's central weighing rule — misinstruction installs a "counterfeit of knowledge" that must be unlearned, while an omitted lesson is a fillable gap — was established early, defended consistently, and crystallized memorably in the closing. B also landed two genuine consistency attacks: the visibility double standard (A calls content episodic because it is visible, yet the chill systemic though invisible) in B2.5, and the resilience/permanence contradiction (A treats students as resistant to bad texts but missed inquiry as permanent) in B4.6. A's strongest route — elastic rules make retreat rational, and the accusation itself is the punishment — was pressed well via pressure questions, but B answered directly (the syllabus record answers complaints; zero risk was never the job; teachers already navigate elastic age-appropriateness standards), and A's systemic-magnitude claim remained largely mechanism-asserted against B's repeated challenge that an unrecorded absence cannot be weighed as the larger harm. A's attrition point was met with B's "loads the result / next hire restores permission" response. Side A argued plausibly and sharply, but B exposed more weaknesses in A's framework than A exposed in B's, and made the final choice easier to make.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's B4.6, catching Side A's double standard — treating students as resilient against graded dogma while treating missed inquiry and teacher exits as permanent — was the sharpest single exchange, and it dovetailed with B2.5's earlier visibility double-standard attack to materially damage A's core systemic-chill route.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): B builds the stronger comparative case: political complaints can deter sound instruction regardless of careful framing, with spillovers into inquiry and teacher retention. A usefully challenges B's unsupported prevalence claims and easy assumptions about correcting misinformation. However, A repeatedly substitutes ideal curriculum boundaries for actual political incentives and dismisses departing teachers rather than answering the loss of expertise. Both sides overstate scale and reversibility, limiting the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's argument that accusations and investigations themselves impose punishment directly undermines A's reliance on professional precision and a defensible syllabus. A explains how teachers might ultimately justify their lessons, but not why that protects them from the ordeal that motivates self-censorship.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B wins a competitive debate because it turned A's own admission that quiet orthodoxy survives into a direct attack on the claim that controversial content is reliably visible and correctable. A's later distinction between nameable curricular targets and unrecorded slant was useful, but B's closed-room point remained decisive: captive minors graded by an adult face authoritative miseducation as active corruption of inquiry, while self-censorship leaves gaps that still permit accurate baseline learning. A's clipped first rebuttal was a minor execution cost, but the deeper issue was that B gave the cleaner harm comparison after the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1 to B4.1: B used A's "quiet orthodoxy, spoken and never written down" concession to break the "specific, visible, correctable" anchor, then answered A's repair by arguing that evasion makes indoctrination harder to catch, not less dangerous.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): This was a close, high-quality debate fought on a shared "which harm disables correction" test. Side A built an original mechanism case (covering a unit is not teaching it; pressure polices the documentable layer while sheltering unrecorded slant) and a sharp closing dilemma. But Side B landed the most damaging single blow by exposing A's contradiction between "excesses are visible and correctable" and "quiet orthodoxy survives," and then gave the more concrete recovery: critical habits are built across non-flashpoint material (ratification debates, classics, labs), baseline accuracy under worst-case caution still leaves tools intact, and captive minors cannot audit the authority grading them — which directly rebuts A's claim that dogma "can still be challenged." A's counter that students never learn tools they were never shown was asserted more than supported. Side A also bore execution costs: a clipped rebuttal 1 and a Q2 that mischaracterized B's worst-case framing as a grant. Both sides argued the motion as written with clean grounding and no fake sourcing; B simply made the decisive weighing slightly easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 catch that A's concession "quiet orthodoxy, spoken and never written down, is what survives" shattered A's "specific, visible, and correctable" framing — the exchange that defined the rest of the debate, including both Pressure Questions rounds.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): A won the comparative clash. B convincingly explained why mandatory coverage does not preserve instructional depth, but never adequately defended treating controversial instruction as readily identifiable and correctable. A distinguished ineffective policing from the seriousness of its target and explained how students could retain critical-thinking practice outside chilled discussions. B’s closing largely repeated the disputed correctability claim rather than answering those challenges. Both sides asserted their harms’ prevalence more than established it, keeping the result competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest rebuttal was that hidden classroom orthodoxy undermines B’s visibility-and-correction advantage: pressure failing to catch harmful instruction does not establish that the instruction is the lesser threat. B’s attempted distinction between visible materials and oral slant narrowed the motion’s comparator without adequately justifying that exclusion.
