### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by turning A’s central feasibility response into a liability. A’s moral case that safety should not depend on income was strong, but B showed that truly free best features create costly attacker probing, rationing, and hidden behavioral tiering. A’s risk-triggered checks and severity triage never fully answered who pays at scale, how users get protection before harm, or why that hidden system is fairer than transparent paid depth. B also handled the fairness pressure better by conceding emergencies can override tiers while defending paid capacity as a contribution, not an entitlement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive rebuttal was that A’s risk-triggered verification and severity-based triage were “tiering by another name,” but less transparent and often after warning signs had already exposed victims. A did not fully neutralize this reframe.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by forcing the motion’s “best safety features” wording to do real work. A basic free block, report, and phone check changes little; document checks, cross-account blocking, and deep scam response reintroduce cost, attacker probing, and scarcity. Side A absorbed the equity pressure by conceding severity overrides for true emergencies, then showed Side B’s risk-triggered model is less transparent tiering without a surviving revenue source or an answer to ranking-based coercion. Side B’s principle—that payment poorly tracks vulnerability and that fraud controls have network benefits—remained forceful but did not close those gaps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s fork: if free-by-default means only tools already commonly free, the motion is empty; if it means the best checks and investigations, cost and probing return. The later charge that risk-triggers and severity queues are opaque tiering, not universal best-in-class safety, was the exchange Side B never neutralized.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): A narrowly wins because it defined the motion plausibly as free core and advanced safety tools rather than unlimited mandatory review, answered the “basic-only mootness” pressure with risk-triggered verification and severity-based triage, and kept the decisive normative focus on allocating safety by risk and harm rather than ability to pay. B’s funding, attacker-probing, and capacity arguments were concrete and strong, but they leaned too heavily on a worst-case reading of “free by default” and never fully neutralized A’s network-benefit and anti-paywall principle.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive: it answered B’s scope and funding pressure with a workable model—risk-triggered verification, free blocking using platform signals, and severity-based scam triage—while contrasting that with payment-based access.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): B wins on the decisive burden. PRO convincingly showed wealth is a poor safety screen and risk-based allocation is better, but failed to prove that making the best safety features free by default would actually improve safety. B kept alive a clear alternative: a substantial free safety floor plus paid premiums to fund costly manual review, deeper device analysis, and dedicated investigation. PRO's own triage and automation answer conceded that capacity must still be rationed, which B turned into the unresolved question of whether price must be prohibited. A's two-tier harm was blunted by B's broader free-floor description and risk-conditioned benefits. Despite the clipped second rebuttal, B answered the pressure questions directly and crystallized the comparison more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 reframing of PRO's triage/automation remedy as a concession: once PRO admits universal best-in-class access cannot be unlimited, the live issue becomes whether price may still be prohibited, not merely whether risk should guide allocation.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the motion's choice sharp: reserving the effective safety tools for payers rations protection by wealth, creates a farmable tier, and is unnecessary once capacity is allocated by risk, automation, and operating revenue. Side A usefully distinguished basic protections from higher-touch work and pressed costs, but its price-does-not-screen concession, inconsistent claim that paid upgrades need not confer priority, bad reference IDs, and clipped second rebuttal left that route less cleanly neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal and closing, which used Side A's concession that price poorly screens criminals to show that paid priority still inverts protection, while triage and one-time binding can ration capacity without a wealth gate.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by making the central clash about whether price is a legitimate way to ration effective safety tools. A answered capacity concerns with risk-based triage, automation, and one-time verification, then used B's concession that price does not screen scammers to argue that paywalls give predators purchasable credibility while leaving vulnerable users with slower help. B's free-floor versus premium-upgrade distinction was coherent, but B never fully showed why paid best-tier features remain necessary once risk-based allocation was accepted, and the clipped second rebuttal hurt execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal was decisive because it conceded that free does not mean unlimited manual review, but argued that capacity can be allocated by risk and need rather than subscription price, blunting B's strongest capacity objection.
