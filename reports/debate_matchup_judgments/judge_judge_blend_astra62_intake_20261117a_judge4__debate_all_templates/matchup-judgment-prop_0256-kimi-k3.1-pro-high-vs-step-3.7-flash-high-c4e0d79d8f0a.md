- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): A built a tighter, more adaptive case: it conceded protection for ports/dense centers early, then used that concession to repeatedly expose CON's "false binary" framing as inaccurate, and in Rebuttal 2/Closing turned CON's own "unacceptable residual risk" stopping-rule answer into a concession that retreat must eventually happen, sharpening the real question to "who finances the destination before disaster, versus after." CON's alternative (targeted resilience plus absorption into existing inland towns) was a genuine and interesting policy counter, bolstered by the Katrina example and timeline/workforce concerns, but CON never squarely answered A's recurring point that "absorption" still requires new housing, water, schools, and jobs—i.e., the same planning and money CON calls wasteful—and CON kept re-asserting the "false binary" charge even after A clarified it was never advocating universal abandonment. CON's Sandy and port-commerce figures were also presented with more unearned precision than A's claims. Both sides executed the Pressure Questions/A1-A2 structure correctly, but A's answers (risk-based eligibility criteria, voluntary phased moves) held up slightly better under return fire than CON's thinner "existing federal frameworks" and "redirect existing grants" answers. A's clipped opening is a real execution cost, but it did not prevent A from carrying the decisive comparative points through later rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 point [A2.1]-[A2.2], later crystallized in Closing [A5.1]-[A5.2]: noting that CON's own "unacceptable residual risk" stopping rule forces retreat, and that "absorption" into existing towns is not free capacity but requires the same planning/funding CON derides as wasteful when done deliberately.
