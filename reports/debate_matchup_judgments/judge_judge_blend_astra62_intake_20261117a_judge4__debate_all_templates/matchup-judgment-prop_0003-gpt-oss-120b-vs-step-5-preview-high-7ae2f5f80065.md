### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): CON wins clearly. PRO built its case on brittle and likely invented specifics: a Portland "Housing First" demolition that cut homelessness by "12%," and a Boston "Historic District Overlay Flex" framework. Neither earns credit. PRO's unit arithmetic was also internally inconsistent ("ten to fifteen times" versus "nine-to-ten units"). Three PRO speeches were clipped for exceeding the limit, and the closing cited a nonexistent reference ID (P4.1). Substantively, PRO's strongest route was the supply multiplier. CON neutralized it by pointing out that PRO measured gains only on the demolition site and ignored the district's wider buildable inventory. PRO then dismissed that inventory as "vanishingly small" without support. CON consistently pressed the irreversibility asymmetry. It also showed that PRO's climate offset depends on uncertain behavior, such as residents giving up cars and towers omitting parking. CON's framing of the motion as a blanket "demolition trigger" was somewhat uncharitable, since the motion applies only when protections actually block needed density. PRO never exploited this weakness, so CON's cleaner, better-grounded case carried the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's closing turned PRO's A1 answer against it. If inclusionary mandates, rent control, and escrow can be legally bound to a density permit, they can be bound to adaptive-reuse and nonhistoric-parcel permits too. On that logic, demolition adds nothing but irreversibility, which undercut PRO's main safeguard argument.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): Side A won decisively through cleaner comparative framing, sharper rebuttals, and far stronger epistemic and format discipline. While Side B had a real point on parcel-level floor-area ratios and transit-oriented density, Side A reframed supply at the district level (noncontributing parcels, surface parking, vacant offices) and established a clear asymmetry between permanent landmark loss plus upfront demolition carbon versus contingent affordability and car-reduction promises. Side A also caught Side B reading unstated inclusionary mandates into the motion and dismantled Side B's contradictory preservation safeguards. Meanwhile, Side B relied on brittle, unsupported specificity (such as an exact 12% Portland pilot statistic and a named Boston flex program), cited a nonexistent reference ID, and got clipped mid-sentence in three separate turns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A2.3]-[A2.5] and [A3.2], Side A dismantled Side B's claim that salvaging beams and prohibiting "mere shell-keeping" avoids façadism ("You cannot recycle the essence of a landmark"), forcing Side B in [B4.2]-[B4.3] to concede that design review cannot save whole structures and falls back on façade retention.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): Side A won the central comparison: PRO's supply arithmetic counted only demolished parcels while dismissing district-wide noncontributing sites without inventory, and its affordability and design safeguards, if enforceable, would work on non-demolition permits too. A thus showed demolition adds irreversibility without adding a unique benefit, while PRO relied on brittle examples and suffered repeated clipping.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing was decisive: if inclusionary mandates and strict review can be bound to a demolition permit, they can equally be bound to adaptive-reuse and nonhistoric-parcel development, leaving demolition with no upside except permanent loss.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): PRO wins clearly. It built a coherent causal case: blanket protections on transit-rich land push density outward, which drives exclusion and car-dependent sprawl. It openly accepted the motion's cost of losing some significant buildings, and it narrowed its policy to mapped, criteria-based relaxation with adaptive reuse tried first. CON opened with plausible values: heritage, tourism, and embodied carbon. But its execution was weak. - It leaned on unsupported magnitude claims ("millions of visitors," "billions"). - It cited vague "studies consistently show" and an invented case citation (*City v. Preservation Board*). - Its Rebuttal 1 was clipped before the judge saw all of it. - Its own pressure question, and then its A2 answer, conceded that current preservation rules block the very conversions CON offered as its alternative. - It misattributed "densify elsewhere" to PRO, and its closing mislabeled the bright-line demand as CON's own. CON's slippery-slope argument stayed partly live. However, PRO's A1 gave a concrete administrative answer, and CON's reply relied on fabricated legal authority. CON also never squarely answered PRO's spatial-sorting and sprawl-carbon point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 and closing turned CON's own answer [B4.3] against it. CON admitted that underused upper floors and courtyards stay empty because preservation rules block interior alterations and code upgrades. PRO used that admission to show that CON's "humane alternative" is itself vetoed by the regime CON defends, which reframed preservation as exclusion "with a preservation label."
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won dominantly through disciplined burden-owning, sharp comparative weighing, and surgical pressure questioning, while Side A (CON) unraveled in the second half. Preservation Board) in Rebuttal 2, and fatally conceding in A4.3 that current preservation rules block feasible interior conversions before attacking a strawman in Closing.

Side A walked straight into the trap in A4.3, admitting that existing preservation ordinances restrict interior and code upgrades so severely that "the preservation regime itself is the barrier," which Side B cleanly leveraged in B4.3 and B5.1 to collapse CON's status-quo alternative.
