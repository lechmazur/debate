### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): CON wins a competitive debate on the strength of its rebuttal work. PRO built a reasonable case: voluntary testing fails because of race dynamics, a standardized floor fixes that incentive, and publication enables outside scrutiny. CON then narrowed the clash to publication and standardization and kept it there. Its central device was a dilemma. A published report is either too thin to verify or detailed enough to become a gameable map. PRO answered that outsiders can spot omitted test conditions, but that answer conceded that "complete" means covering the required categories rather than publishing the underlying evidence. CON exploited this, calling the result "publish the index." CON's gaming argument also landed. Held-out prompts, adversarial red-teams and updates erode the "same clear hurdle" PRO had promised. PRO's response, that these would be "pre-announced methods," did not fully dissolve the tension. PRO's best pressure was Q1: which of CON's alternatives stops a first launch before harm? It forced CON to specify a confidential permit gate. That answer shifted CON somewhat from its opening, which leaned on post-release tools. However, it directly rebutted PRO's "binding floor versus voluntary self-assessment" framing. PRO never exploited the tension that such a permit regime arguably worsens CON's own entrenchment and legal-defensiveness concerns. PRO's closing introduced regulator capture fairly late. CON answered it adequately: publishing only what the standard made visible does not cure capture. Overall, CON made the decision easier: it shrank the debate to the marginal value of publication and standardization, and it argued more persuasively that this margin is weak.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange runs from B3.1 through A4.1 to B4.3/B4.4. CON pressed what outsiders can actually verify. PRO answered that "complete" covers required categories and conditions, not raw logs. CON turned this into the thin-brochure versus gameable-map dilemma, and PRO's closing reasserted its position rather than resolving that dilemma.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a high-quality exchange. A's core dilemma—publication is either too thin to verify or detailed enough to teach the test—was pressed consistently and B's answer (publish hazard class, version, threshold, failure rate) never fully resolved it, since A showed outsiders could only "note boxes," not verify prompt strength or version fidelity. A's strongest move was A4.1/A4.5: answering B's pressure question with a concrete confidential regulator gate (full access, rerun tests, refuse or stage launch, fraud penalties), which broke B's "binding floor vs voluntary self-assessment" dichotomy and showed a binding pre-launch regime without publication's defects. B landed real points—regulator capture, watchdog pressure, penalties attaching to noncompliance not ugly findings—and answered pressure questions cleanly with proper A1/A2 format, but B's reinterpretation of "complete and publish" as "publish the index" and the tension between a "same clear hurdle" and an evolving anti-gaming apparatus were never fully squared. Both sides used no fake sourcing and progressed the clash well; A made the debate easier to decide with sharper comparative weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1 and A4.5: A answered B's sharpest pressure ("which alternative stops a first launch?") with a binding confidential pre-deployment permit gate, then dismantled B's framing that the only choice was a public floor versus voluntary self-assessment—showing the real comparison is an adaptive pre-launch veto versus a public snapshot that goes stale and invites gaming.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by turning PRO’s transparency case into a practical dilemma: published standardized reports are either too thin to verify or detailed enough to invite gaming, while A’s confidential pre-launch gate answered PRO’s central pre-deployment pressure more directly. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides were sharp, but PRO built the cleaner path to victory. CON's strongest weapon was the dilemma that a published evaluation is either meaningless or an attack map. PRO met it directly. It specified a concrete public dossier: version IDs, coverage matrix, coarse severity categories, and limitations, with exploit chains kept confidential. It also turned the targeting point by noting that coarse weakness data helps defenders and downstream deployers as much as attackers. CON's own A2 answer then proposed mandatory confidential pre-deployment filings with version IDs, scope declarations, module triggers, rejection power, and penalties. That narrowed the dispute to publication alone and undercut CON's earlier claims that standardization is premature and that penalties chill candor. PRO exploited this cleanly in its closing. CON's closing called the confidential regime a PRO concession when it was CON's own proposal, which is a misdescription. CON's chill argument was largely neutralized by PRO's distinction between penalizing omission and penalizing bad scores. CON's remaining claim that outsiders can't detect gaming had some force but was partly answered by the advertised-context-versus-missing-module mismatch test. Minor reference-ID slips occurred on both sides.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came in Rebuttal 2. PRO's coverage-matrix answer (A4.3–A4.5) included the line that "if a common regulator format can catch weak scope, that is the standard to publish; if it cannot, secrecy is trust with better stationery." This converted CON's confidential-audit alternative into evidence for a standardized regime. CON's reply about narrowly defined contexts did not restore its claim that the motion's core mechanism is unworkable.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Both sides ran disciplined, well-matched cases built around one live clash: whether publication specifically (as opposed to confidential regulator filings) adds verification value or just creates an attack map and chilling. Side A's dilemma was sharp and persistent, but A leaned on repetition — the lawsuits/headlines and "outsiders can't detect gaming" points were restated across three speeches without much deepening, and A's answer to B's Q2 conceded that its alternative has no public check at all. Side B progressed more: the pressure answers supplied concrete mechanics (public dossier contents, coverage matrix with mandatory module triggers and negative disclosures), the "where is not how / warning that a lock is weak is not a copy of the key" distinction blunted the attack-map dilemma, and the closing flip — that naming weak categories helps defenders and downstream deployers as much as attackers — was real weighing, not evasion. B's turn of A's own meta-argument (A's concession of regulator power, version IDs, and scope declarations "is a standardized evaluation") plus "secrecy is trust with better stationery" crystallized the verifiability stakes more memorably than A's closing crystallization. Minor symmetric execution costs: both sides used stray mis-prefixed reference IDs ([P2.3] on A, [C2.1] on B). B answered pressure more constructively and made the debate easier to decide, earning a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B's Rebuttal 2 / Closing versus A's attack-map dilemma. A argued publication must either expose targeting data or become vacuous narration; B answered with the where/how distinction, the lock-and-key analogy, the coverage matrix making scope omissions legible, and finally the closing flip that disclosed weaknesses save defenders the same search costs they save attackers — while exposing that A's confidential-audit alternative concedes the entire standardized-evaluation machinery, reducing the dispute to publication alone, where B's verifiability framing landed cleaner.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): PRO built the clearer winning path by giving a concrete account of what publication would disclose, how raw exploit details could remain confidential, and how penalties would target concealment rather than bad results. CON’s attack-map and false-assurance pressure were strong, but PRO’s public-dossier and coverage-matrix answer preserved a meaningful external check while blunting the dilemma. PRO also framed the final choice more decisively: standardized evaluations plus publication provide pre-release verifiability that confidential regulator-only review does not.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s second rebuttal directly answered the key pressure questions by specifying minimum public fields, confidential appendices, mandatory coverage triggers, and penalties for scope mismatch, which weakened CON’s claim that publication must be either meaningless or an attack map.
