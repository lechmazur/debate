### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won by narrowing the debate to one decisive question: if evidence of a free choice can sometimes be compelling, why must the law refuse it in every case? Its examples showed concretely how safeguards would operate. The caregiver daughter, and the private questioning about whether the patient feels free to accept help, both turned CON's coercion concerns into reasons to refuse doubtful cases rather than reasons for a blanket ban. CON (Side A) had a real route to victory through hidden coercion and irreversibility. However, it asserted rather than supported its key weighing claim that wrongful approvals would vastly outnumber wrongful refusals, and PRO exposed that gap directly in its closing. CON also contradicted itself on the slippery slope. In A2 it defended the terminal/degenerative criterion as principled and morally weighty, but in its closing it called the same exclusion "arbitrary discrimination." That undercut its own expansion argument. Its grounding was also weakened by overclaims such as "every jurisdiction" and the somewhat overstated Canada description. CON's claim that the harm of refusal is "finite and correctable" was effectively turned by PRO: for a dying patient, that harm is finite only because the time to choose runs out. PRO answered both pressure questions directly. It gave a clean principled answer on the mental illness question, grounding the distinction in what delay costs rather than in whose suffering counts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing (B5.1–B5.3) was most decisive. It exposed CON's conflation of the number of people exposed to pressure with the number who would actually be wrongly approved despite safeguards. It then turned CON's "finite" framing: irreversibility justifies a demanding burden of proof, not a law under which no patient can ever meet it. This, combined with CON's self-contradiction between A4.2 and A5.2 on the terminal criterion, left CON's main weighing unsupported.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B built a far more coherent, responsive, and epistemically disciplined case. Rather than treating safeguards as infallible, Side B showed how requiring affirmative verification of dependable support—and denying approval whenever voluntariness is in doubt—addresses systemic neglect without writing a blanket refusal into law for supported, dying patients. Side B cleanly answered the mental-illness expansion pressure by distinguishing what delay costs in terminal or degenerative decline from treatment resistance ([B4.3]), exposed Side A's leap from background social pressure to wrongful approvals ([B5.1]), and turned Side A's "finite harm" framing on its head ([B5.3]). By contrast, Side A fatally contradicted its own slippery-slope argument under pressure in [A4.2]—explicitly arguing that the terminal or degenerative criterion has real moral weight and is not an arbitrary barrier before calling that same exclusion porous and "arbitrary discrimination" in [A4.3] and [A5.2]—and overreached empirically by claiming expansion occurred in "every jurisdiction" with similar laws.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Two linked exchanges stood out, led by Side B's Q2/A2 sequence ([B3.2], [B4.3]): Side B neutralized the equal-protection slippery slope by explaining that terminal or degenerative trajectory tracks "what delay costs, not whose suffering counts," while B's Q2 trapped Side A into conceding in [A4.2] that the terminal/degenerative criterion is morally weighty rather than arbitrary. Side B then sealed the debate in [B5.1]-[B5.3] by exposing [A4.1]'s conflation of people experiencing social pressure with patients approved despite safeguards, and noting that refusal is only "finite" for dying patients because their time runs out.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO maintained a live, concrete route: withhold approval whenever voluntariness is in doubt, but do not equate exposure to pressure with wrongful approval or treat compelling, supported choices identically to coerced ones. PRO also gave a principled terminal/degenerative limit based on the irreversible cost of delay. CON relied on repeated assertions about inevitable expansion and a far larger hidden coerced population, with overbroad Canada claims, and never justified refusing the admittedly free, well-supported patient whose lost time cannot be restored.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 and closing exposed CON's central weighing gap: hidden coercion proves wrongful approvals are hard to count, not that they vastly outnumber the certain wrongful refusals imposed by a blanket ban.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins on the decisive clash. Its central claim is that safeguards check whether a request is competent and stable, but cannot tell suffering that relief cannot touch from suffering whose relief is simply unavailable. That point is concrete, carefully built, and was never squarely answered. PRO (B) relied heavily on two moves: - **The refusal-of-treatment analogy.** CON answered it cleanly: refusal withdraws consent to bodily intrusion, while assistance requires separate justification. CON added that pure autonomy cannot explain why PRO limits the right to terminal or degenerative cases. - **Assertions that tested safeguards "eliminate" the risks.** These stayed general and unsupported, along with claims like "thousands" suffering. PRO's A1 answer conceded that assessors must defer care-contingent requests. CON then pressed the dilemma of a waiting list that closes entirely, and PRO's closing simply restated "accessible care" without engaging it. PRO also misdescribed CON's patient as facing "unrelenting agony," which CON caught. PRO's A2 distinction based on future decline was rebutted by noting that stable-injury patients may already lack the capacity to act. CON conceded the real cost to patients with truly unrelievable suffering. That concession made its case more credible rather than weaker. PRO's later speeches mostly repeated its opening.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.4–A4.5 exchange carried the debate. PRO had said the framework defers requests that depend on accessible care. CON asked what happens if the waiting list closes tomorrow. Either the patient's claim grows stronger as his community serves him worse, or assessors must detect a wish to live with help that two consistent requests cannot reveal. PRO never answered this dilemma.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won decisively by outmaneuvering Side B on both core clashes: the analogy to treatment refusal and the sufficiency of strict safeguards. Rather than denying the tragedy of intractable pain, Side A honestly conceded that cost and showed that standard clinical safeguards—even working perfectly—verify competence and diagnosis without distinguishing irreducible biological agony from suffering caused by unmet caregiving needs. Side B staked its defense in Rebuttal 2 on claiming assessors would defer requests tied to accessible care, which both contradicted its earlier status-quo critique and walked into a fatal dilemma in Side A's final two speeches that Side B left completely unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A4.3]-[A4.5], after Side B answered Q1 ([B4.1]) by saying assessors must defer a patient waiting a year for "accessible" night care, Side A noted this abandoned PRO's earlier claim that delay justifies immediate relief and posed a lethal fork: if closing the waitlist unlocks eligibility, worse community neglect strengthens the clinical case for death; if assessors must still defer, two consistent requests cannot detect a rational patient who has stopped asking for unavailable help.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A convincingly distinguished refusing treatment from authorizing clinicians to cause death and showed that repeated, screened requests prove stability under deprivation, not what a patient would choose with adequate care. Its accessibility dilemma dismantled Side B's deferral safeguard, while conceding and weighing the strongest case of truly irrelievable suffering.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: if closing the care waiting list strengthens eligibility, worse public provision creates a stronger claim to death; if it does not, assessors cannot infer an enduring wish from requests made when needed care is unobtainable.
