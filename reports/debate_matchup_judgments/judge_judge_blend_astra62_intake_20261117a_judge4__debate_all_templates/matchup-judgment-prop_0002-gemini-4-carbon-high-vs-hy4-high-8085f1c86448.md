### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): PRO built the cleaner path to the motion's relative-affordability burden. CON's strongest objection was capitalization and option value, but PRO effectively answered it with citywide legalization, the small fraction of lots builders need, and the fact that unbuilt bungalows remain ordinary shelter. More decisively, PRO turned CON's own concessions: affluent demand arrived independent of apartment legality, and high-end projects penciled in the 2010s. That removed the endogenous-demand objection and established that builders could profitably add supply. PRO's vacancy-chain and landlord-competition mechanisms directly addressed median households, and CON's replacement-cost stopping point was turned in closing: status-quo bans drove rents far above replacement cost, so restraining new buildings near construction cost still leaves cities more affordable than engineered scarcity. CON repeatedly demanded a bridge to median affordability but never neutralized PRO's supply-and-demand counterfactual. PRO's case was more coherent, better adapted to the clash, and gave a cleaner reason to vote for it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal 2 and closing turned CON's concessions about independent affluent demand and high-end projects penciling into a decisive counterfactual: same demand, more units, lower prices relative to status quo. The replacement-cost turn in closing was especially damaging because it used CON's own stopping-point argument to show that scarcity, not legalization, was what kept rents above construction cost.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): B won the relative counterfactual: twenty years of additional housing would absorb affluent demand and reduce competition for existing homes compared with continued restrictions. A raised credible capitalization and construction-cost concerns, but did not establish why those effects would outweigh additional supply. Its closing repeatedly treated failure to produce broadly cheap housing as failure to improve affordability at all. B answered the pressure questions with a clearer metric and causal mechanism. Its exaggerated claim that independent demand contradicted induced demand, and its overly assured filtering claims, keep this short of a dominant win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange concerned replacement costs. B explained that construction stopping near cost could still improve on scarcity-driven rents under restrictive zoning. A replied that construction-cost rents were not median affordability, without defeating B’s comparison or its explanation of reduced bidding pressure on older housing.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): Side B PRO built the cleaner route to victory by framing the motion as relative affordability and supplying a plausible two-decade mechanism: legal density absorbs affluent demand that would otherwise bid on existing homes, then filters through vacancy chains, landlord competition, and aging stock. Side A CON raised strong capitalization and replacement-cost objections, but never fully showed that option value and redevelopment premiums would cancel out the affordability effects of substantially more legal housing, especially after PRO narrowed the claim to a counterfactual comparison rather than making expensive cities cheap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 was decisive because it answered Side A’s pressure directly with a metric—median rent relative to the status quo—and a mechanism—vacancy chains and landlord competition—while turning Side A’s concessions that high-income demand arrived anyway and high-end projects could pencil.

### Judge Notes

- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): PRO built the cleaner comparative case: over twenty years, legal density would add roofs and reduce scarcity rent, even if supply was delayed and uneven. CON's teardown/land-repricing story was sharp and original, but it stacked several speculative mechanisms—luxury-only feasibility, broad land shocks, elastic induced demand, and slow filtering—and PRO's double bind plus same-shocks status quo comparison left the capped city with no supply response. CON answered pressure honestly, but PRO's marginal-chain and distributional-affordability responses gave it the more decisive route to the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's double bind was the most decisive exchange: if land reprises broadly, expected profit implies feasible homes will be built; if almost no infill pencils out, the premium cannot be broad. CON narrowed this into a luxury-infill story, but that still admitted new roofs and never fully reversed PRO's net supply-relief logic.
- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): B wins the comparative affordability question through accumulated supply and reduced competition for existing housing, without needing new units themselves to become cheap. A developed credible displacement and induced-demand objections, but did not establish why those effects would outweigh additional housing over twenty years. A successfully challenged B's alleged contradiction between land appreciation and limited modest-priced construction; B's repetition of that overstatement limits the margin. Nevertheless, B retained the stronger citywide counterfactual rather than merely demonstrating harms to particular residents or neighborhoods.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest response distinguished filtering from vacancy relief: new housing can reduce pressure on older units before becoming inexpensive itself. A's elastic-demand answer challenged the size of that benefit, but did not establish that incoming demand would absorb enough additional capacity to eliminate it.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the debate on the comparative counterfactual: two decades of legalized density would likely add homes, absorb marginal bids, and reduce scarcity pricing. Side A’s teardown, land-repricing, and timing arguments were vivid and original, but B effectively pressured them with the feasibility double bind and framed the bungalow-preservation point as protecting physical stock rather than price affordability. A showed real distributional harms, yet never proved they would outweigh the accumulated supply effect across American cities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal and closing turned CON’s land-price shock against it: if upzoning made broad land repricing feasible, it should also produce added homes; if construction was infeasible, the broad affordability-harm shock weakened. A answered with selective luxury infill, but B kept that response from becoming a citywide winning claim.
