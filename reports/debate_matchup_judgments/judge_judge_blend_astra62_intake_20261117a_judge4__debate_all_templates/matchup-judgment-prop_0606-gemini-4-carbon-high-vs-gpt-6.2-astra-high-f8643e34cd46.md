### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won by making licensing depend on a remedy that actually works. It argued that crossing a price line shows an access problem but does not show a licence would help. The two-ministry example made that gap concrete, and PRO never fully closed it. PRO's strongest moves were its first rebuttal and its closing: - **Chicken-and-egg:** suppliers will not bid without legal standing. - **Stalking horses:** rivals will stop bidding if their offers only help the incumbent bargain. - **Closing reframe:** CON's shortfall test, divided by the number of patients, is itself a threshold. CON answered each pressure point concretely and in the required format: - Conditional licence-plus-contract awards remove the need for suppliers to commit capital before standing exists. - Pooled regional tenders answer the small-market problem. - Simultaneous final offers with "no last look" let rivals compete for a real order rather than serve as stalking horses. CON's pressure question also drew a damaging concession: under PRO's model, patients rationed just below the ceiling must wait because the ceiling was "miscalculated." The growing-population follow-up made that concession costly. PRO's closing reframe was clever but was blunted by CON's closing. A per-patient budget figure tells the minister why a discount matters. It does not show that anyone else can supply. CON also had already shown that its process could look ahead before patients go untreated. PRO repeatedly described CON's model as resting on a "secret" or "hidden" trigger, which CON explicitly rebutted. PRO also leaned on assertions that a published threshold would deter trade pressure, and CON reasonably countered that the threshold's assumptions are exactly what a seller would contest.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over Q2 and A2, on bids and last looks. PRO argued that rivals would become unpaid stalking horses whose bids merely tell the incumbent what to beat. CON replied with simultaneous final offers, no last look, and a pre-committed award with a licence attached. That turned PRO's best incentive argument into support for CON's view that protection comes from the award rule, not the ceiling. CON then pressed PRO's "miscalculated ceiling" concession as proof that PRO's model must do the access-shortfall work anyway.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B wins by making the decisive distinction between an affordability failure and a workable licensing remedy. A's strongest chicken-and-egg and stalking-horse objections were substantially neutralized by B's conditional-tender model with final offers, no last look, and a licence attached to the winning bid. A argued that B's shortfall test implies an affordability threshold, but B reasonably conceded that affordability data matters while showing it is not sufficient grounds for overriding a patent when no supplier can deliver better access. B's closing crystallized this: a budget-derived price tells the ministry why a discount matters, not why refusing would cost the incumbent the order.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response to the stalking-horse problem was the most decisive exchange: give the incumbent no last look, require final offers by the same deadline, and commit in advance to awarding the contract with a licence attached. This directly answered A's pressure question and turned the supply-bid objection into a structured competition design rather than a reason to prefer threshold-based grounds.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): B made the clearer case that affordability alone does not establish a workable patent remedy: a licence matters only when credible supply can improve access. Its conditional tenders, pooled procurement, and simultaneous final offers answered A’s entry and “stalking horse” objections without waiting for actual rationing. A offered a strong proactive-pricing rationale, but relied too heavily on calling B’s criteria secret or delayed, and its claim that any below-ceiling rationing meant the ceiling was miscalculated left the threshold looking reactive rather than independently decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions: a published coverage shortfall prevents licensing merely for cheaper copies, while simultaneous final offers with no incumbent last look give rivals a genuine chance to win. It then used population growth to expose the weakness in A’s “miscalculated ceiling” answer.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON (A) wins a competitive debate by keeping more live routes standing at the end. PRO's case was elegant. Its distinction between grounds to license and a duty to license, and its claim that a published threshold changes the seller's offer in advance, were its best lines. The closing used CON's concession about "flat refusal despite genuine hardship" to argue that the threshold merely defines hardship beforehand. CON, however, sustained three independent harms that PRO only partially answered: - **Incentive distortion.** The threshold formula caps prices tightest where clinical need is largest. PRO conceded that "some investment may shift" and that profit, not revenue, is the right measure. - **Deterred tech transfer.** Routine licensing in middle-income markets would discourage voluntary know-how sharing. PRO answered this only for producers who need no know-how. - **Fiscal free-riding.** PRO admitted that publicity alone cannot stop free-riding. Its patch of letting sellers challenge fiscal-capacity calculations met a clean dilemma from CON: either litigation recreates delay, or elastic "competing needs" let wealthier buyers cap prices. CON's answer to PRO's first question split the cases sharply. Where rivals can supply, continued exclusive supply reveals acceptance of the ceiling; where they cannot, the threshold gives no leverage. PRO's closing reply to this ("what another country can afford") was thin. CON's late concession that targeted licensing already applies in hardship cases narrowed the gap and was well exploited by PRO. Still, the cumulative weight of the unrebutted dynamic harms gives CON the clearer path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A1 answer in Rebuttal 2 turned PRO's Q1 into a dilemma. For complex biologics without rival capacity, the threshold yields no leverage. For simple tablets, any ministry would license rather than overpay, so continued exclusive supply reveals the price floor. Paired with the dilemma over PRO's fiscal-challenge patch (litigation delay or elastic loopholes), this left PRO's closing to re-assert benefits rather than neutralize CON's harms.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B wins a clear but competitive debate. A built a genuinely strong access and bargaining case, especially by distinguishing grounds from duty and showing that no rival means no forced license. But B more effectively exposed the structural costs: pre-published ceilings risk reference-pricing spillovers, deter voluntary tech transfer, and punish high-burden diseases because the cap tightens as need grows. A conceded that some investment may shift, and its fiscal-capacity fix was persuasively shown to recreate delay or invite free-riding. B also turned A's strongest pressure question back by explaining that continued exclusive supply is uninformative only where the threshold is impotent, while revealing where generic rivals exist. Overall, B gave the cleaner comparative reason to reject the rule as a standing trigger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1 was the most decisive exchange. It directly answered A's pressure question by distinguishing no-rival cases, where the threshold gives no leverage, from rival-can-supply cases, where rational ministries would license rather than overpay, so continued exclusive supply would reveal the published ceiling and undermine confidential tiered pricing.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Carbon (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by accepting targeted licensing in A’s strongest hard case, then isolating the additional costs of A’s universal, pre-published trigger. A effectively exposed gaps in B’s confidentiality argument and defended immediate access, but its revenue-based innovation response was damaged, its tech-transfer answer remained incomplete, and its fiscal safeguard left B’s delay-versus-free-riding dilemma alive. B’s concessions and comparative weighing made the policy choice clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal decisively corrected A’s flat-revenue argument by distinguishing revenue from net profit as treatment volume and production costs rise. B’s second rebuttal then sharpened the clash by separating cases with and without a viable generic supplier and answering A’s last-resort challenge directly.
