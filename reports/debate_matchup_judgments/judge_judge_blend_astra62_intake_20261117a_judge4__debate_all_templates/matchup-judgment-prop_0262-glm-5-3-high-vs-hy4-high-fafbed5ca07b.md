### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): PRO built a coherent case: decline caused partly by policy, a positive-externality market failure, and modest tools. Its strongest move was narrowing the dispute to scale once CON accepted libraries, parks, and zoning reform. However, PRO's opening and second rebuttal were both clipped, a real execution cost. CON was also more disciplined in the rebuttal rounds. CON's charge of a definition switch, from cafés and pubs to the "nontransactional" good, damaged PRO's decline metric. CON's dilemma held through the closing: neutral infrastructure is already accepted and adds nothing new, while selective grants and zoning preferences lack proof of additionality over legalization and upkeep. PRO answered with a threshold and the "differs in degree, not kind" argument, which were reasonable but not decisive. CON pointed out that the threshold lacked a baseline and a stopping rule, and that the feedback loop measures use rather than net gain. The result is competitive, but CON made the decision rule clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.3/B4.7 dilemma decided the debate. If "revival" means barrier removal and library upkeep, CON already accepts it and the motion adds no new obligation. If it means selective subsidies, PRO owes proof of additionality and never supplied it. PRO's best counter, that a library budget is itself a selective transfer (A5.2), blurred the line, but it did not supply the missing net-gain evidence.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): B built the more complete affirmative path: policy-driven decline, underprovided social benefits, and concrete funding and zoning tools that address financing barriers legalization cannot fix. A’s churn, definition-switch, and additionality objections were sharp and kept the debate competitive, but its upkeep-versus-curation distinction did not neutralize expanded library provision or explain why selective siting becomes illegitimate merely because a venue is commercial. B answered both pressure questions directly and narrowed the dispute effectively, though its two clipped turns and occasionally asserted decline evidence prevent a larger margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to A’s library question was decisive: ordinary public facilities already involve chosen locations, budgets, and hours, while legalization alone cannot overcome rent and financing gaps. A’s additionality response was strong but showed a need for program discipline rather than defeating active revival altogether.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won the central dilemma: if revival means libraries, parks, and legalization, it adds no new duty; if it means selective grants and zoning preferences, PRO never proved additional, freely chosen attachment over barrier removal. A's exposure of PRO's shift from cafés and pubs to nontransactional free rooms, plus its additionality test, left PRO's market-failure and rent-gap claims without a measurable threshold.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it answered both pressure questions directly, distinguished neutral general infrastructure from chosen venues, and showed PRO's threshold and “empty rooms lose grants” test measure attendance, not net new belonging or displacement.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): PRO wins a competitive debate. Its strongest asset was a coherent framework: a third place is defined by low-threshold access, and decline means that unconditional access has thinned, not that social life has vanished. Within that framework, PRO paired a public-goods mechanism with a remedy that combined active zoning and targeted funding. CON landed real blows. It argued that PRO's definition was gerrymandered, that libraries already exist and are already funded, that the case risked being unfalsifiable, and that PRO never produced direct evidence of decline. PRO answered the pressure questions directly and concretely. It named a recurring operating fund for evening and weekend hours as the new commitment. It also offered an observable stop rule based on repeat drop-in use, which blunted the unfalsifiability charge. CON's replies were weaker on several fronts: - **Stop rule:** CON said the rule only works slowly. That narrows the objection rather than defeating it. - **Zoning:** CON's reliance on repealing restrictive zoning partly concedes the "zone for" half of the motion. - **Library evidence:** CON's claim that busy libraries prove the free layer thrives was effectively reframed by PRO as pressure on the rare free door. CON also paid an execution cost when its opening was clipped. Its strongest remaining point, the lack of demonstrated scarcity, stayed live but was not decisive against PRO's structural argument that a revenue-based demand test cannot see excluded users.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came over falsifiability. CON's [B3.2] charged that PRO's case could not fail by design. PRO's [A4.2]–[A4.3] answered with a concrete stop rule and argued that "demonstrated demand" only registers demand that has already been converted into revenue. CON's reply that failure would be "slow" ([B4.5]) conceded that the case could in fact fail, which left PRO's accountability framing largely intact.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): B edged a close debate by answering the practicality and falsifiability pressure directly: recurring operating support was clearly distinguished from repeal-only zoning, and repeat-use trials supplied a plausible stop rule. B also exposed the weakness in treating commercial participation, empty rooms, or busy libraries as conclusive measures of latent demand. A’s motion-specific objection—that actual decline was asserted rather than demonstrated—and its critique of B’s narrowing definition remained forceful, preventing a larger margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s A1/A2 answers were decisive: they named a spending commitment repeal could not achieve and gave observable failure conditions, substantially neutralizing A’s “faith with a budget line” attack. B’s later toll-booth rejoinder then sharpened why revealed commercial demand was an inadequate baseline.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): PRO won the central comparison: cafés, gyms, coworking and run clubs may show migration, but they remain conditional on spending, membership, transport or belonging, while repeal alone still lets rent allocate space to higher-paying uses. PRO specifically explained what funding adds—keeping free civic rooms open at useful hours—and answered pressure with a concrete evening/weekend operating fund and a repeat-use stop rule. CON's burden and falsifiability attacks were strong, and its clipped opening hurt completeness, but it never squarely refuted why legalization alone would preserve a free, low-threshold layer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it directly answered both pressure questions, distinguished a bounded accountable trial from unfalsifiable faith, and exposed CON's demonstrated-demand test as counting only demand already converted into revenue.
