  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was B's rebuttal that majority approval does not establish a particular patient's consent, while a family conversation framed as presumed agreement can shift rather than remove distress; PRO answered with majority-error arithmetic but never supplied the missing patient- and family-level showing.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-6 Astra (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): A wins because B offers a plausible mechanism for transplant gains but never adequately establishes the separate no-added-harm conditions. A directly distinguishes missed donation opportunities from unwanted procurement and exposes how B's procedural inquiry leaves disputed family testimony and distress unresolved. A overstates that family vetoes necessarily eliminate all gains, but B's repeated appeals to proper safeguards do not close the central gap between legal authorization and informed non-objection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer to B's numerical comparison was decisive: missed opt-outs need not outnumber missed opt-ins for ethical violations to increase. B subsequently acknowledged greater risk to unwilling patients, but did not explain how its notice and inquiry safeguards would reliably prevent that risk from producing additional violations.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B's dilemma survived: presumed consent must either override families, accept their objections as a de facto veto, or subject them to credibility inquiries. B also won the ethical asymmetry—missed opt-ins cause no bodily violation, while missed opt-outs create non-zero unauthorized removals. A's safeguards depend on verifying meaningful notice from a blank record, which B showed is indistinguishable from missed notice, undermining both the transplant gain and the no-violations claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal decisively turned A's procedural hold and meaningful-notice safeguard against it: after the hold, clinicians must still cut or cancel, and an empty file cannot distinguish informed silence from a missed letter.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = MiniMax M3.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by turning B’s safeguards into an unresolved implementation dilemma: a blank record cannot distinguish informed silence from missed notice, and the proposed inquiry either creates distressing bedside credibility tests or collapses into de facto veto that erodes transplant gains. B’s default mechanism was plausible, but its procedural-hold answer never fully answered the organ-viability binary or the ethical asymmetry of moving from zero unauthorized removals to any non-zero risk.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 took B’s procedural hold and showed it still ends in a cut-or-cancel choice under time pressure, while pairing that with the strong moral asymmetry between missed opt-ins and missed opt-outs.
