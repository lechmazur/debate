### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): PRO wins because its central route survived the exchange: competitive pressure strips out voluntary controls, and liability acts only after the harm, so a non-optional ex ante floor does necessary work. CON's most effective weapon was the "you've conceded my case" trap. It argued that once PRO specified a staged, authenticated, scoped interrupt, the mandate collapsed into CON's own graceful-degradation toolkit. That framing was rhetorically sharp, but PRO answered it cleanly in the closing: showing a mechanism is feasible does not show it is unnecessary. The motion asks whether the control should be optional, and CON never squarely defended allowing swarms with no human override. CON also damaged its own position in several ways. Its closing admitted that the non-revocable stop is "the one thing engineers would not choose," which implicitly confirms that the mandate binds. Its claim that PRO had relied on insurers and procurers as the enforcers slightly misread PRO, who invoked them as enforcing attestation under a mandate. And its answer to PRO's Q1, that nothing stops the no-override competitor under the mandate either, was weak. CON's secure-versus-fast dilemma and its vagueness objection were real pressure. PRO softened both by pointing to automatic safe-holds with human authority over restart, by allowing redundant local interrupts, and by noting that liability faces the same definitional problems. Those answers carried some tension with the word "human" in the motion, which keeps the margin moderate.

PRO argued that this proves feasibility rather than necessity, and that law's function is to make the human stop non-optional when competitive bidding would otherwise remove it. That reply blunted CON's best line and reasserted the floor-versus-optional framing that PRO had held since the opening.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): This was a close, high-quality exchange, and PRO edges it. PRO built a clear frame: harms from compounding swarm failures can be irreversible, ex post liability cannot stop them, and a designed interrupt beats an improvised infrastructure yank. It then forced the decisive pressure point with Q1, asking CON to name a mechanism that stops a cascade mid-flight. CON answered honestly with sector rules such as order cancellation for trading swarms and e-stops for physical fleets. PRO then turned that answer: CON had itself stressed that general-purpose systems' defining feature is unforeseeable use, so a patchwork of sector rules cannot anticipate where the cascade happens. PRO also clarified that "hard" means non-waivable rather than crude, and grounded certification in functional-property regimes, which blunted CON's charge that graceful interruption means abandoning the motion. CON's best route was a sharp dilemma: a mandate specific enough to define a safe stop cannot cover general-purpose systems, while one general enough to cover them cannot specify a safe stop. Its point that "halt on ambiguity" either halts constantly or invites override was genuinely damaging. However, CON never fully answered PRO's claim that the absence of a designed interrupt leaves only a worse, undesigned halt. CON also lost some ground on execution. It cited nonexistent IDs [P4.1-P4.2], and its closing attributed the trading and factory examples to PRO's closing when they were CON's own. On balance, PRO's case that CON's alternative fails on CON's own premises survived better than CON's dilemma.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange ran from PRO's Q1 through CON's A1 and into PRO's closing (A4.5, A5.1). CON answered "name a mid-flight stop" with sector-specific halts. PRO showed that this answer contradicts CON's own premise that general-purpose swarms move into unforeseen domains, leaving CON without a real-time stop for the very systems the motion covers. CON's specificity/generality dilemma (B4.7) was a strong counter but did not fully neutralize this turn.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): CON wins because it converted the safety intuition into a legal-design dilemma PRO never closed. Targeted stops can halt defined cascades; a general-purpose mandate still cannot specify, certify, or trust a safe quiescent state for unanticipated uses without constant halts, overrides, or a destructive yank. PRO’s late “hard means non-waivable” reframe and ex-post critique were real, but the closing restated sector-rule lag rather than answering that specification trap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: after accepting bounded operational halts, it showed PRO’s certification and “halt on ambiguity” answers force a choice between a mandate too specific to cover general-purpose swarms and one too vague to be safe.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (high) (CON)): A won by making the central clash about irreversible, compounding harm and by clarifying that “hard” means a non-waivable, certified stop guarantee rather than a crude yank. Its pressure exposed that CON’s targeted-rule alternative only covers foreseeable bounded harms, while CON’s safe-state objection, though strong, remained a difficulty argument rather than a full refutation of a default-halt functional mandate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Q1 forced B to supply a live stop mechanism; B answered with bounded sector halts, but A used B’s own unforeseeability premise to show that patchwork rules cannot cover novel general-purpose cascades.
