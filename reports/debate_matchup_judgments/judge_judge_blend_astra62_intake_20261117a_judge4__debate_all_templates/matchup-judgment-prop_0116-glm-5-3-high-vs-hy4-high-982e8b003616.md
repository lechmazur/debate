### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): PRO opened with a clear skin-in-the-game mechanism. Its "universities control the denominator" reframe was its best move. CON's attribution and risk-selection objections stayed live throughout, however, and PRO's answers progressively narrowed its own policy. By Rebuttal 2, PRO had limited liability to institutional charges, excluded living costs and interest, invoked "pattern, not person," and added an access floor. CON exploited this well: each patch moves the policy toward prior price regulation and away from making universities bear default costs, so the benefit can be had without the liability. PRO's Q1 pressure was good, and CON answered it directly with a concrete pre-borrowing eligibility-and-remediation mechanism rather than relying on more disclosure. PRO's closing gradient argument (a scaled cost versus a binary threat) was a fair point but came late. It did not answer the core charge that defaults are a noisy trigger. PRO also overstated a CON "concession" on incentives. CON's own case had flaws, including a stray fragment in its opening, and its remedy sharpened later than ideal. Still, PRO was clipped twice for exceeding the word limit, which is a real execution cost. Overall, CON made the decisive clash, direction of the incentive versus intensity, clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 ([B4.4]–[B4.6]) turned PRO's A1/A2 safeguards into concessions. It argued that the access floor exists only because the levy makes low-cushion students costly. It also argued that once PRO restricts liability to institutional charges and monitors intake, the useful policy is prior tuition regulation rather than default liability. PRO never fully recovered from this exchange.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): B wins because CON more convincingly established that default-coinsurance aims the incentive at the wrong moment and variable: repayment after graduation is shaped by borrower circumstances and labor-market risks outside university control. PRO’s later narrowing to institutional charges and an access floor was responsive, but it effectively conceded that the policy needed prior price/outcome regulation to work. B turned that into a cleaner reason to reject the proposition as written rather than patch a default liability scheme.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s reframe in rebuttal 2 was the decisive exchange: PRO’s safeguards—limiting liability to institutional charges and adding an access floor—showed the real work is prior tuition and outcome regulation, not making universities co-insure student-loan defaults.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): In a high-level exchange, Side A (CON) won by proving that charging universities for student-loan defaults prices noisy post-graduation repayment failures—heavily shaped by family wealth cushions, living-cost debt, and regional wages—rather than institutional misconduct alone, and that multi-cohort averaging merely repeats this attribution error when low-income students sort into specific schools and public-service fields. Side B mounted a strong case around controlling the tuition denominator and contrasting a continuous financial gradient with binary eligibility cutoffs, but Side A decisively turned Side B's Rebuttal 2 patches (limiting debt to institutional charges and adding a Pell access floor) into concessions that default levies make low-cushion students financial liabilities, while showing that ex-ante conditional loan eligibility addresses overpricing before borrowing occurs. Side A also maintained cleaner execution, avoiding Side B's two word-limit clips.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side B argued in [B2.1]-[B2.2] that schools facing default charges could only cut tuition or illegally discriminate—and later bolted on a Pell-enrollment access floor in [B4.3]—Side A dismantled both defenses in [A2.1] and [A4.4]-[A4.6] by detailing lawful, subtle risk-selection tactics (shrinking program seats, cutting need-based aid, dropping evening sections, tightening prerequisites) and showing that needing an inspection apparatus to police avoidance proves the default levy points incentives at the wrong variable.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): This was a close, well-matched debate, and CON edges it on the decisive incentive clash. PRO built a clean core case around the "one-way bet": the school controls price and enrollment and holds the outcome data, yet externalizes default losses. PRO's scaling answer (repetition, program level, a partial charge tied to debt) partly blunted CON's indiscriminacy objection. PRO also landed one point CON never answered: loan caps and disclosure can themselves ration access or scare students away from poor-region programs. The debate turned on cream-skimming, however. CON framed exclusion as the cheap compliance path, and PRO's answer in A2 was weak. Saying that risk-sharing "gives no discount" for cream-skimming is simply wrong, because excluding risky students lowers future defaults and therefore lowers the charge. PRO's "forfeits enrollment revenue" assumes the seats cannot be refilled with safer students. CON exploited this well in B4.4: a school can wait out the transition and buy permanent immunity. CON also turned PRO's admission that the low-tuition public "should feel some cost" into a concession of indiscriminacy. PRO's closing reframe, that "no charge to escape is the defect," was clever but did not neutralize the bounty point. CON's clipped opening is a real execution cost, but its core arguments were visible. CON's rebuttals were sharper overall, so it narrowly carries the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3–B4.5 answering PRO's A2. CON argued that its alternatives create no charge to escape, so they create no exclusion incentive. It then showed that PRO's "embedded defaults" defense of cream-skimming is only temporary, after which exclusion permanently removes the liability. That left PRO's main incentive mechanism exposed going into the closings.
- **DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): B argued more convincingly that risk-sharing would become a bounty on excluding risky students. PRO's accountability frame was strong, but B's rebuttals exposed a live unresolved problem: even if existing defaults persist temporarily, a school can cream future cohorts and eventually eliminate liability. PRO's admission that low-tuition regional publics and predatory for-profits should both feel some cost also strengthened B's indiscriminacy charge. B's loan-caps/disclosure alternative survived well enough against PRO's attacks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 was the decisive turn: it answered PRO's exclusion defense by showing the school can wait out existing defaults and then achieve permanent immunity by excluding risky cohorts, while also seizing on PRO's concession that both predatory and low-tuition programs should feel cost.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a competitive, high-level debate by winning the central clash over institutional compliance incentives and proxy accuracy, even after accounting for a minor execution cost from clipping the final word of its opening. Side B built a coherent, disciplined case around the "one-way bet," debt-scaled partial exposure, and how loan caps can shift credit shortages onto applicants rather than forcing institutional price discipline. However, Side A consistently turned Side B's answers into concessions: it showed that cutting tuition or adding grants costs budget officers immediately whereas screening out higher-risk first-generation, poor, and adult borrowers eliminates future default charges at far lower cost; that Side B's answer on low-wage mission fields relied on outside subsidies the policy itself does not provide; and that penalizing both predatory for-profits and low-tuition regional publics in weak labor markets punishes accessible institutions for regional poverty.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After Side A's Q2 [A3.2] asked what policy feature makes price cuts or grants cheaper for a budget officer than excluding risky students, Side B answered in [B4.3] that exclusion does not erase defaults already embedded in current cohorts while admitting a dean might still cream-skim. Side A decisively dismantled that defense in [A4.4]-[A4.5] by showing that embedded cohorts graduate within a few years, meaning a budget officer only has to wait out a brief transition period to buy permanent relief from serving disadvantaged borrowers.
