### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a close, high-quality technical exchange. B built the only live causal route to a verdict: potent short-lived gas plus cheap, fast, verifiable repairs versus infrastructure-heavy CCS, and B honestly absorbed every accounting correction A demanded (additionality, full costs, recurring failures, symmetric targeting) while showing the combined advantage survives that discipline. Side A (CON) fought skillfully on epistemics—its additionality and "benefit exists ≠ spending advantage" pressure was the sharpest argumentation in the debate—but A abandoned its own opening claim that CCS is the stronger proposition and offered only "unproven," never contesting B's underlying economics or giving a reader any reason CCS spending does better. B's closing question—why exceptional CCS sites should overturn the general combination of leverage and simplicity—went effectively unanswered beyond burden-shifting. A's rigor kept this competitive, but B made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's additionality attack (A2.1–A2.2: tightening earns only the avoided detection interval, not the whole leak volume) was the sharpest single move, but Side B's response in B4.1–B4.4—conceding the interval-only credit, noting recurring failures create recurring opportunities, and insisting both portfolios be judged prospectively as additional programs—neutralized it and preserved B's comparative edge.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B built the stronger substantive case on the near-term comparison by linking methane's potent short-term warming leverage, immediate deployment on existing equipment, fuel conservation, and simple mechanical maintenance (including tighter repair deadlines on continuously emerging failures) against CCS's upfront development delays and full capture, energy, transport, and storage chain. Side A mounted a surgical marginalist critique—forcing Side B to credit only the leak interval shortened beyond baseline rules while charging recurring search and verification costs—which kept the clash close. However, once Side B neutralized Side A's permanence argument and used Pressure Questions to expose Side A's asymmetric comparison with already-operating CCS and assumed spare infrastructure, Side A retreated to pure agnosticism. Dismissing massive physical, timing, and infrastructure advantages as saying nothing about per-dollar superiority left Side B with the far more convincing reason to vote in a closed-book policy comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A2.4]-[A2.5], Side A landed a sharp contrast between tighter LDAR buying uncertain searching and "operating CCS" buying measured capture on concentrated streams. Side B's Pressure Questions ([B3.1]-[B3.2]) and [B4.4] exposed the double standard of comparing marginal new LDAR spending against already-built, successful CCS while assuming ready transport and storage capacity. Forced to answer directly in [A4.1]-[A4.4], Side A conceded full incremental capital, delay, energy, and disposal costs for CCS and disclaimed any portfolio-wide advantage for favourable CCS sites, forfeiting its affirmative route to victory.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A won by imposing a symmetrical marginal test: tighter LDAR earns credit only for leakage avoided earlier than the existing regime, net of full recurring costs. Side A answered both pressure questions directly and showed potency and simplicity do not establish comparative cost-effectiveness.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal was decisive: recurring leaks may create value, but tightening captures only the avoided interval beyond baseline detection, so PRO's mechanism did not prove a spending advantage over additional CCS.

### Judge Notes

- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a technically even matchup on the strength of its positive case and one decisive concession. Both sides argued cleanly with plausible mechanisms and no fake sourcing. Side A's framework—benefit per dollar, additionality, counterfactual duration—was analytically sharp and repeatedly forced B to defend marginal yield rather than potency per tonne. But A's own positive case rested on a narrow CCS niche (concentrated streams near operating storage), and in Rebuttal 2 A explicitly conceded it had not established spare CCS capacity to absorb equivalent spending. B exploited that opening: LDAR applies across all operating oil and gas sites using standard equipment, while ready-CCS depends on stacked favorable conditions. B also won the timing clash (repairs begin with the next inspection; even favorable CCS waits on connection and storage transactions) and turned A's additionality demand back on CCS ("a known stream is known to exist, not known to be additional"). A's closing "tightening" distinction was its best late move, but B had already answered the diminishing-returns claim with the asymmetric super-emitter payoff and the point that uncertainty is a reason to measure, not to leave leaks running. A's rigorous skepticism narrowed the gap but never supplied the comparative scale its own framework demanded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A3.1/A4.1 exchange: B's pressure question on CCS niche capacity forced A to concede it had no capacity estimate, which B then leveraged in closing ("CON expressly conceded that capacity was unestablished") to convert A's own evidentiary standard into the reason A's case fails.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a disciplined, high-level debate by building a broader causal case—combining methane's roughly eightyfold twenty-year warming potency, immediate atmospheric relief, modern remote/sensor screening, and simple component repairs across already-operating facilities—while proving that Side A's (CON's) near-term CCS defense relied on defining away CCS's core infrastructure bottlenecks. Side A executed a sharp marginalist critique focused on recurring search costs, intermittent leak persistence, and the burden of "tightening" existing rules, which kept the race genuinely competitive. However, once Side B forced Side A to concede that spare capacity in ready CCS storage was unestablished and to narrow its claim to settings where preventable leaks are sparse and storage is accessible, Side B held the much stronger comparative case for equal near-term spending.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Pressure Questions ([B3.1]-[B3.2]) and synthesis in [B4.3] and [B5.1] forced Side A to concede in [A4.1] and [A4.3] that its concentrated-stream CCS niche had neither established spare capacity nor automatic additionality, effectively trapping CON inside the narrow caveat of [A4.6].
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by keeping the burden on marginal benefit per dollar from tightening rules. B showed major leaks are potent and promptly repairable, but never established that additional inspections would find enough preventable emissions, after failed surveys and counterfactual duration, to beat targeted CCS. A also answered the additionality pressure symmetrically and showed that starting sooner does not prove a larger cumulative two-decade benefit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A's distinction between methane potency per tonne and benefit per dollar, reinforced by the point that repeated detection explains how money is spent, not how much additional warming it prevents.
