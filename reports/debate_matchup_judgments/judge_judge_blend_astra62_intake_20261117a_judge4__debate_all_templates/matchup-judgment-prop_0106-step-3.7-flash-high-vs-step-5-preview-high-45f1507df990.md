### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON)): CON wins a competitive debate on precision and responsiveness. PRO had a strong route: untreated puberty also causes irreversible change, and adolescent cohorts differ from the older desistance data. Its best moment was the A2.4 distinction between childhood and adolescent cohorts. But PRO undercut itself by overclaiming. It said the safeguard "eliminates any risk," called treatment "fully reversible," and leaned repeatedly on an exact "4x" suicide figure and a "<1%" regret figure as if they settled the matter. It also kept restating these points instead of answering CON's specific objections about evidence quality and consent. CON stayed disciplined throughout. Its core claim was that endorsement is not evidence, that hormones are not reversible, and that minors cannot meaningfully weigh permanent infertility. CON answered both pressure questions directly and refused PRO's demand for a guarantee as burden-shifting. It also neutralized the chemotherapy analogy and the precocious-puberty data by noting they come from different contexts. CON's closing caught PRO applying "fully reversible" to the whole package of treatment, which was a clean, damaging point. CON's weakest spot was the symmetry argument. It never fully engaged that natal puberty also forecloses options, and its claim that "the harm of waiting can be treated" was asserted rather than shown. That gap keeps the margin moderate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and closing did the most damage. They showed that PRO's evidence on blocker reversibility (decades of precocious-puberty data) does not carry over to cross-sex hormones. They also pointed out that PRO's closing claim of "fully reversible" ignores permanent sterility. And they separated a minor understanding the word "infertility" from adult capacity to accept that loss. Together these exposed PRO's central overclaim and reframed the asymmetry of harms in CON's favor.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by keeping the decision on irreversible effects, minor capacity, and low-certainty benefit evidence, then damaging Side B’s defenses.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it rejected the demand for a suicide-risk guarantee, separated comprehension from adult consent, and showed why chemotherapy and precocious-puberty analogies do not justify permanent hormone effects.
- **Qwen 3.8 Max 0902** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): A won by making the irreversibility and consent clash decisive. - **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO wins a competitive debate by holding the cleaner frame. Under the motion, treatment proceeds only when diagnosis, parental consent and clinician agreement are all present. Within that frame, PRO cast blockers as a reversible staged step and denial as a non-neutral harm. PRO also neutralized CON's comorbidity point as an argument for better assessment, not prohibition. CON's strongest live routes were real. Hormones are irreversible, and the motion's safeguards are not spelled out. CON pressed both points effectively in its pressure questions and second rebuttal. However, CON leaned on a repeated "60–90%" desistance figure and applied it to all diagnosed minors, including adolescents. PRO's answer that the figure comes largely from pre-pubertal referrals went essentially unrebutted beyond reassertion. CON also overstated the European restrictions as outright pauses. In its closing, CON conceded that care "could just as easily exist with the structural guardrails European regulators already demanded." That concession undercuts CON's case for a categorical ban. CON's opening was also clipped. PRO left some vulnerabilities. It overstated how fully reversible blockers are and never squarely answered the bone-density point. Still, PRO answered both pressure questions directly and weighed more clearly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2 was decisive. It argued that desisters exit at the reversible blocker stage under staged clinician agreement. It also argued that the 60–90% figure is drawn from pre-pubertal referrals rather than the diagnosed adolescent cohort. Together, these points blunted CON's central irreversibility-plus-desistance argument, which CON then only restated.
