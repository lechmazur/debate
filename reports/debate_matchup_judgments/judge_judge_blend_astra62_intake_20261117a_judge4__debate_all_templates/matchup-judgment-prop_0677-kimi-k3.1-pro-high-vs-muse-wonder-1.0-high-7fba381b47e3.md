### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but CON (A) built the more durable structural case: that a bare disclosure duty, as the motion actually defines it, grants no right to explanation, records, preservation, or review, so an employer can comply fully while refusing every follow-up. CON's sharpest and most decisive move was the boilerplate dilemma pressed in Rebuttal 2 and the pressure-question exchange: if "meaningful influence" is read broadly (covering ranking, ordering, filtering), employers will issue universal boilerplate that destroys the very pattern-detection signal PRO relies on; if read narrowly, it recreates exactly the rubber-stamping and classification disputes PRO waves away. PRO's "door, not the whole remedy" framing and asymmetry argument (employer knows, worker doesn't) remained rhetorically powerful through the closing, but never squarely resolved this dilemma, and PRO's claim that notice lets a worker "identify the tool and timeframe before evidence disappears" was shown by CON (A4.3) to overstate what the motion's text actually requires (no vendor identification, no preservation duty). CON also effectively reframed the complaint/audit mechanism as working off suspicious treatment rather than off individual knowledge of automation, undercutting PRO's "thousand applicants" pattern-detection story. CON's opening was clipped, a real execution cost, but the substantive content survived and was echoed in later speeches, limiting the damage. On balance, CON's rebuttals landed more damaging and unanswered blows on the mechanism PRO needed to carry the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3-A4.4: CON's demonstration that PRO's disclosure mandate doesn't actually require vendor identification or record preservation, combined with the "comprehensive coverage vs. discriminating trigger" dilemma, directly undercut PRO's central claim that disclosure meaningfully enables pattern detection and evidence preservation.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully framing disclosure as a necessary prerequisite for all other remedies, effectively co-opting Side B's main argument. While Side B proved that notice alone does not compel records or correct bias, Side A persuasively argued that workers and regulators cannot target a hidden automated process, meaning existing accountability mechanisms remain functionally useless without the mandate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal cleanly neutralized Side B's core attack by conceding that disclosure is not a full remedy, but reframing it as the indispensable "door" to make those remedies reachable.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3.1 Pro (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by decisively dismantling Side B's (PRO) core argument that disclosure is a necessary prerequisite for legal remedies. Side A effectively demonstrated that existing enforcement mechanisms (discovery, audits, discrimination claims) do not require prior individual notice to function, and that the mandated disclosure alone provides "awareness without leverage" since it grants no right to records, explanations, or appeals. Side A also trapped Side B in a dilemma regarding the "meaningfully influenced" trigger: a broad interpretation leads to useless boilerplate, while a narrow one invites evasion via nominal human approval. Side B's rhetoric was strong and memorable, but Side A's logical precision and sharp rebuttals secured the win, despite a clipped opening.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which dismantled the "boilerplate vs. signal" dilemma and proved that the mandate's "unlocked" remedies actually rely on external enforcement powers that do not require prior individual notice to function.
