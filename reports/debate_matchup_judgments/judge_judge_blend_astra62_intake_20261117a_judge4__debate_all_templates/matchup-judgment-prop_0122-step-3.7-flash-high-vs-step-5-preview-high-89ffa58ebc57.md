### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON wins a clear but competitive debate. PRO's strongest live route was the bias argument: capacity assessments are administered by system actors and fall hardest on poor and racialized children, so a fixed age line protects them better. PRO pressed this consistently. CON's answer, independent experts and putting the burden on the prosecution, is somewhat aspirational. Even so, CON adds a fair point that bias relocates rather than disappears. PRO's empirical pillar was badly damaged: - The US-versus-Norway comparison conflates trying youths as adults with the age of criminal responsibility. - It relies on brittle, unsourced figures (60% versus under 20%, "3 to 5 times"). - In A1, PRO conceded that age 14 is not the standalone causal variable. CON exploited this effectively in rebuttal and closing. PRO also had execution problems: - Its opening was clipped for exceeding the word limit. - It overclaimed a "consistent neurotypical threshold" at 14. - Its later speeches largely repeated the bias and recidivism points without adapting. CON had flaws of its own: - It referenced a nonexistent ID (P1.1). - It slightly misdescribed PRO as removing under-14s from all legal jurisdiction. - Its "raise the age if a legislature wishes" line softened its opposition to the motion. Still, CON framed the clash more crisply as mechanism versus label. It turned PRO's concession into the decisive point, and its vacuum argument about countries that lack welfare infrastructure stayed live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.4 and A4.3 attack on the Norway comparison was decisive. CON argued that Norway's outcomes stem from restorative infrastructure rather than the number 14, which drew PRO's A1 concession that the age is not a standalone cause. CON then used that concession to argue that the motion imports a label without the mechanism, which undercut PRO's main safety and recidivism claim.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by turning the motion into a choice between a mechanism and a birthday. Side A showed that Side B’s Norway comparison does not isolate statutory age, then used Side B’s own concession that fourteen is not a standalone cause. Side A also answered the bias and vacuum questions by keeping compulsory assessment, diversion, and secure care inside legal process, while the motion only moves the line and hopes institutions follow. Side B kept a real administrability and bias critique, but leaned on brittle exact recidivism and confession figures, had a clipped opening, and never explained what capacity changes at that birthday or why a low age with a rebuttable incapacity presumption could not stop full adult-style punishment. Side A slightly overstated Side B as deleting all jurisdiction, yet still left the cleaner reason to reject the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Norway attack in Rebuttal 1, completed after Side B conceded age is not itself causal: the motion then demands a label rather than the restorative mechanism both sides say actually reduces harm.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Step 5 Preview (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by turning PRO's Norway evidence into a concession that age 14 is not itself the protective mechanism, then showing capacity, diversion and secure intervention deliver that mechanism without an arbitrary birthday. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins clearly. It built a coherent case on developmental capacity, the harms of criminal processing, and a defensible bright line. Its decisive move was separating "consequence" from "conviction": everything CON demanded (restitution, mandated treatment, secure placement) can be imposed without a criminal label. CON (A) never adapted to that distinction. It kept asserting that a fourteen floor "eliminates all accountability" and offers only "vague welfare referrals," even after PRO listed specific enforceable non-criminal measures. CON's own answers damaged its case: - Its A1 conceded that the only safeguard in low-age systems is unbound prosecutorial discretion, which is routinely overridden by political pressure. That undercuts its claim that existing flexible systems work. - Its A2 named restitution and secure therapeutic placement as the real stakes, the same tools PRO places outside criminal liability. CON's later speeches were largely repetitive. It also misread the motion's binary as PRO's invention, while PRO kept the debate anchored to the motion's actual question. One caveat on PRO: its claim that an "overwhelming majority" of states set the age at fourteen or higher is somewhat overconfident.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2. It answered CON's demand for an "enforceable consequence" with concrete civil and welfare tools backed by contempt powers and secure care. It then reconciled the stigma and recidivism claims by locating the harm in criminal processing itself. This collapsed CON's central victim-justice and accountability route, and CON never meaningfully responded.
- **Grok 4.7 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by keeping the motion’s actual choice in view: raise the age of criminal responsibility rather than abolish every response to harm. B separated enforceable consequence from criminal conviction and met the sexual-assault hard case with specific non-criminal tools—secure care, supervision, restitution, and restorative confrontation—while A never showed why those tools require a criminal label and kept calling them “vague welfare” through the close. A’s admission that discretion collapses under tough-on-crime pressure strengthened B’s bright-line case more than A’s “just reform” alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered the demand for a gravity-matching consequence with concrete enforceable civil and welfare measures, then tied stigma and failed deterrence to criminal processing itself—a distinction A’s closing never met.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Step 5 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO separated criminal conviction from enforceable consequences, showing secure care, treatment, restitution and supervision can protect victims without stigma or false adult culpability. CON's victim and deterrence case depended on equating welfare with no response, and CON conceded existing discretion is unbound and uneven, undermining its flexible-system alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 answers on enforceable non-criminal sanctions and criminal-processing stigma directly collapsed CON's claim that raising the age means unaccountability.
