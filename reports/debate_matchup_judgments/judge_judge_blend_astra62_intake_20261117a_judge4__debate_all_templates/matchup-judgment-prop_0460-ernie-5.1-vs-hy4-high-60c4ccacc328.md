### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON (A) wins clearly. A set up the decisive distinction early: a faster permit is not the same as a finished, energized, accepted line. It then forced PRO to prove that removing the veto buys completed steel. PRO never met that burden. PRO relied on a single example, Champlain Hudson. A neutralized it by pointing out that contested corridors drawing litigation does not show that the objectors were wrong, or that imposing the route would have delivered power sooner. PRO repeatedly claimed that A had "conceded" cost allocation, queues, and equipment delays were investment-solvable. That misdescribes A, who had criticized PRO for waving those factors away. PRO also leaned on a burden shift ("name a single corridor") rather than offering affirmative evidence. A answered both pressure questions directly with clear A1:/A2: labels. The answer on deadlines and reasoned decisions was concrete: written reasons, mapped harms, and review of unsupported denials. A then exposed a genuine contradiction in PRO's safeguards. PRO's case was largely repetitive across speeches. Its closing even conceded that reform is designed to stop a community from forcing a burial-site reroute, which made A's legitimacy framing land harder.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's judicial-review dilemma in A4.5–A4.6 and A5.2 was decisive. A argued that if review can halt a line until a route objection is fixed, the local veto survives and the promised speed disappears. If review only enforces compensation and mitigation, local power is gone. PRO cannot use B4.1 to claim community protections survive while using B4.5 to claim the blocking power is eliminated.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A won by dismantling Side B's claim that reform can strip local veto power while still protecting communities from irreversible route harms, and by cleanly separating faster permit approval from faster energized steel. However, Side B misread [A2.4] as a concession that non-siting bottlenecks like cost allocation and queues are easily solved with money, and never resolved the contradiction between letting construction proceed while grievances are heard and promising enforceable route adjustments. Side A's institutional case that binding route leverage drives local settlement whereas preemption breeds construction injunctions and multi-corridor backlash carried the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In [A4.5]-[A4.6] and [A5.2], Side A trapped Side B's judicial-review defense [B4.1] in an unanswered fork: if courts can halt construction until a farm, wetland, or burial-site objection is fixed, binding local blocking power and delay remain; if builders proceed and courts only compel compensation or mitigation checklists, protection over irreversible harms is gone—a tension Side B worsened in [B5.2] by arguing reform is designed to stop burial-site objections from forcing reroutes.
- **Muse Wonder 1.0 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (CON)): CON wins by sustaining the approval-versus-energized-line distinction and showing PRO never proves veto removal delivers finished lines faster. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built a clear decision rule: corridor-wide local veto amounts to serial unanimity, and safeguards such as notice, route comparison, standards, mitigation, compensation, and reviewable reasons can replace it. Under pressure, however, A4.1 conceded that a neutral arbiter issues a binding decision without unanimous consent. A4.2 then called veto "the cause" of courtroom delay. CON's closing mostly restated durability without resolving its own concession.

CON answered in A4.1 that a binding arbiter decision ends it. PRO's closing in B5.1–B5.3 turned that answer into a concession of the proposition: evidence, conditions, and reasons bind, while refusal does not.
