### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) built a precise counter-model. It would protect the act of preservation, meaning capture, backups, migration and consultation when no usable edition exists. It would also shield good-faith mistakes from punitive damages. But it would keep an ordinary injunction against remote lending while a licensed digital edition is available. That move directly answered PRO's paralysis and bankruptcy concerns. Side B (PRO) leaned heavily on recasting CON's carve-outs as conceding the motion. This works only if "traditional enforcement" means strict-liability statutory damages. CON rebutted that cleanly in A1: an unlicensed copy is not automatically infringement, and an injunction is itself traditional enforcement. PRO also conceded that a court can enforce the one-copy limit. CON turned this into an admission that judicial limits on circulation need not destroy libraries. PRO's best line was the student whose library cannot afford a license. CON answered it honestly: the need today is real, but it is a question of who pays for access, not of preservation. That separates the motion's "preservation" rationale from a general access subsidy. PRO also repeatedly cited nonexistent reference IDs ([C1.1], [C1.4], [C2.3], [C2.1], [C2.6]). It overstated copyright as strict liability for every copy. Its A1 justifications, that use reveals corrupted files and sustains public support, were thin, and CON dispatched them. CON's progression narrowed the debate cleanly to whether lending a substitute for an available edition is "preservation," and CON won that clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive rebuttal was CON's A4.1–A4.3. CON denied that its carve-outs concede the motion, since an injunction against future loans is an ordinary copyright remedy that leaves every page intact. It then used PRO's own concession, that a judge may stop the second simultaneous loan, to show that judicial limits on lending do not destroy libraries. That left PRO's "veto over human memory" framing without support.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won a close but clear debate. A's opening framework—judge libraries "by what they do, not whether they earn a profit"—was a principled line that held through every round: protect capture, migration, and consultation of unobtainable works, but preserve the author's injunction remedy against lending a substitute for an available edition. B's main route was the concession trap ("your own model abandons traditional enforcement"), and A answered it directly in A4.1 by distinguishing act-specific exceptions from institutional exemption and noting an injunction is an ordinary remedy that leaves every page intact. A's pressure Q2 (fifty simultaneous loans) forced B to concede tailored judicial enforcement of a one-copy limit, which A skillfully turned in A4.3: if a court can stop the second loan, why must the first be beyond the author's reach? B's best material—dark-server imagery, the student who must "miss out today," perpetual rental licenses—was rhetorically vivid and honestly forced A's costliest concession, but it never broke A's core distinction between controlling a lending service and controlling the surviving text. B also repeatedly cited nonexistent reference IDs ([C1.4], [C2.3], etc.) across four speeches, a recurring grounding sloppiness, though the intended claims were identifiable. A's closing crystallized the clash cleanly; B's closing mostly restated the concession loop. Rhetorically B was more memorable, but A did the sharper comparative and responsive work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A4.3 turn: after B conceded in B4.2 that courts could enforce the one-copy lending limit, A pointed out that a judicial limit on circulation need not destroy an independent library, and that owning one book explains the number one but not why the first digital loan must be beyond the author's reach—directly undermining B's claim that any enforcement is ruinous.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B drew and defended the decisive distinction between preserving a work and distributing it while a commercial edition remains available. Its capture-first, access-sensitive alternative secured the archive’s independent copy while retaining narrow injunctive remedies for authors, directly answering PRO’s institutional-collapse argument. Side A made a forceful case that inaccessible preservation and revocable licensing impoverish public access, but repeatedly treated any act-specific protection as wholesale adoption of its institutional exemption and relied on nonexistent reference IDs. B answered both pressure questions candidly, acknowledged the student’s present access loss, and explained why that loss concerns circulation and payment rather than whether the text survives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it explained why exceptions for capture and reasonable mistakes do not imply blanket immunity, used A’s concession that courts may enforce a one-copy limit to separate judicial oversight from institutional destruction, and showed that an injunction against current remote loans leaves every archived page—and future access if the edition disappears—intact.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Opus 5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a competitive debate because its narrow model holds up better after the exchange. It proposed a standing, function-based exemption for preservation copying and controlled consultation, while leaving enforcement in place against market-substituting distribution. It defended that model consistently through the paywalled-newspaper example and the Monday/Tuesday framing. CON's strongest route was motion fidelity. PRO conceded that the Internet Archive's in-print lending should not be exempt, and CON argued that what remained was simply fair use, which amounts to traditional enforcement. That argument had real force and was framed memorably. It still falls short in three ways: - **Surviving exemption:** A functional preservation exemption for libraries "such as the Internet Archive" remains a genuine exemption, even if partial. - **The decisive gap:** CON never squarely answered PRO's distinction between ex-post, work-by-work fair-use defense and authorization granted from the outset. - **Weak answer to pressure:** CON's reply to Q1 relied on a prediction that "no publisher sues." That is not an existing rule, and PRO turned it effectively in closing by asking what the writer gains from leaving the librarian uncertain. CON's moral-hazard argument also weakened once PRO kept damages available for distribution. Both sides stayed grounded, and CON's description of the Internet Archive's practices was plausible. Overall, PRO made the cleaner comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange came after Pressure Questions. CON argued that the Monday-backup exemption is trivial because "rights holders do not drag libraries into court over silent backups." PRO replied that a prediction about lawsuits is not permission, and that withholding authority gives writers nothing that enforcement against distribution cannot already supply. That reply neutralized CON's claim that fair use already covers preservation.
- **GLM-5.3 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by relentlessly prosecuting a single decisive clash: B's own case narrowed the exemption to preservation copying plus tightly controlled consultation, while conceding that public distribution, the Internet Archive's signature practice, should remain fully subject to traditional enforcement. A's refrain that this "is traditional copyright enforcement" with a thin carve-out landed repeatedly, and A correctly weighed B's supervised-consultation regime (verifying subscriptions, blocking database substitutes) as recreating the enforcement machinery the motion strips away. B's best surviving route was the genuine ex ante/ex post distinction: fair use is a defense after suit, not standing authority a librarian can act on before a paywalled story silently changes, crystallized memorably as "a prediction about lawsuits is not permission" and "only today can we save the first." That route was real but never fully answered A's charge that the exemption as defended covers little beyond what fair use and licensing already permit, and that the motion names the very institution whose flagship practice B surrendered. Both sides followed format, answered pressure questions directly with A1/A2 labels, and argued without fabricated sourcing. A made the debate easier to decide on the motion as written, which was the decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 [A2.1]-[A2.2] was the decisive exchange: it caught B's concession that courts must police which use occurred and stop market-substituting downloads, arguing this abandons the blanket exemption and merely describes existing fair-use analysis. B's Rebuttal 2 response (exemption authorizes preservation copying from the outset, damages for "Tuesday" distribution don't require liability for "Monday's" save) was B's strongest moment, but A's Rebuttal 2 [A4.4]-[A4.5] turned B's own Monday/Tuesday framing against it, showing the exemption shrinks to the moment nobody reads the copy.
- **Kimi K3.1 Pro (high)** (A = GPT-6.2 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): A built the more durable case for a function-specific exemption: preservation copying and controlled archival consultation can be authorized in advance while substitutive public distribution remains fully enforceable. B’s exposure of A’s Internet Archive concession was sharp, but B too often treated any surviving copyright enforcement as proof that no exemption existed, and answered A’s ex ante legal-uncertainty argument mainly with assertions about fair use and the unlikelihood of lawsuits. A’s closing directly neutralized that response: predictions about litigation are not permission to preserve ephemeral material.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 most damagingly highlighted A’s concession that the Internet Archive’s signature public-lending model would remain infringing. A’s direct A1/A2 response nevertheless preserved the core distinction between a protected Monday preservation copy and unlawful Tuesday distribution, leaving B’s blanket-immunity critique unable to eliminate A’s narrower preservation rationale.
