### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): The debate turned on whether a blanket "high-risk" classification for all algorithmic hiring/promotion tools at large employers adds protection beyond a targeted, workflow-based validation duty. Side A consistently drew this distinction and forced PRO to explain why category-wide classification beats a threshold keyed to actual decision-making power. PRO's strongest move was the pressure question exposing that "actual risk" can't be known without testing, but A's answer (observable structural features like exclusion, opacity, and shortlist control can trigger scrutiny without certifying prior bias) adequately handled this without conceding the blanket rule was necessary. PRO's closing rebuttal (B5.3) argued A's threshold creates a "deploy first, test later" catch-22, but A's final answer clarified that the obligation attaches to the tool's designed decision-making power pre-deployment, with records only confirming compliance after the fact — this was a clean rejoinder that mostly neutralized B's strongest closing attack. B never fully answered A's recurring point that manual/human rubrics can equally scale discrimination and that penalizing only the coded implementation creates a substitution incentive toward less-auditable human discretion; B's response (software automates at "zero marginal cost") was reasonable but didn't fully grapple with A's point that regulation shouldn't hinge on the technology label. Overall A maintained a sharper, more consistent core distinction (power/consequence vs. software-as-trigger) across rounds and answered pressure more precisely, giving it a modest but clear edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over A's "materially determines outcomes" threshold: B's Q2 and closing (B5.3) pressed hard that this creates a dangerous after-the-fact enforcement gap, but A's closing (A5.1-A5.2) effectively clarified that regulators assess *designed* workflow intent pre-deployment, with records merely verifying compliance — defusing B's strongest line of attack.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that CON's targeted-validation alternative either collapses into the same testing mandate or creates gameable loopholes. A's pressure questions and second rebuttal exposed the timeline and threshold problems in CON's framework, while A's closing crystallized the choice as a clear upfront rule versus a vague standard that lets employers evade scrutiny. CON's substitution and sparse-data concerns were plausible but insufficient to overcome PRO's prevention-first, bright-line case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's pressure questions and second rebuttal, which demonstrated that CON's "materially determines outcomes" threshold would allow employers to reclassify algorithms as "advisory" or rely on rubber-stamp human review, making the targeted regime either identical to PRO's mandate or ineffective at stopping pre-deployment harm.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO kept a clearer path: scale and opacity make hiring/promotion algorithms high-stakes, and without mandatory pre-deployment testing and audit trails anti-discrimination law is unenforceable. CON's tailored alternative – records plus validation only when tools 'materially determine' outcomes – collapsed toward the same duties, with no principled, pre-test way to exempt 'transparent' tools that CON conceded can still create disparities, and with an advisory-tool loophole and timing problem that B exposed in pressure and closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B in Rebuttal 1/2 and Closing turning A's 'retain records and validate consequential tools' into an admission that bias tests and audit trails are needed, then showing the material-influence threshold invites gaming and checks risk only after untested deployment.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently and respected format (correct A1:/A2: and Q1:/Q2: usage), but A built a tighter, more consistently developed causal mechanism: mandatory testing and audit trails create asymmetric, discoverable legal exposure for algorithmic tools while leaving untraceable human discretion untouched, incentivizing employers to flee to opacity or blunt filters. A pressed this across every round and, crucially, weaponized B's own concession that testing collapses into "reporting uncertainty" in small-sample promotion pools, turning it into a recurring "bureaucratic theater" charge that B never fully dislodged. B's case on accountability and enforceability (audit trails make existing discrimination law provable) was principled and handled the redundancy objection well, but its responses to A's flight-to-opacity mechanism were largely conclusory ("legal discomfort is a cost to weigh, not a veto," "CON has not shown...") rather than offering a competing mechanism explaining why employers would not behave as A predicts. A's closing crystallized this throughline cleanly, while B's closing restated its framework without fully closing the gap A had opened.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 "catch-22" move — showing that wherever sample sizes are small, B's testing regime collapses into eyeballing/"uncertainty," and wherever statistics are available, it creates perverse incentives toward covert quota-tweaking or retreat to unrecorded human judgment — was the most damaging exchange, and B's subsequent answers did not fully neutralize it.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): PRO won by establishing that large-scale algorithmic employment decisions require inspectable safeguards and that structural testing plus audit trails provide accountability even where statistical parity is infeasible. CON’s incentive-based counterstory remained plausible but speculative, and PRO successfully exposed its tension with the scale and efficiency large employers gain from software. PRO’s framing of "who bears the cost of uncertainty" crystallized the clash decisively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 2 answer that mandatory testing can inspect criteria and training targets—such as whether a speed cutoff measures essential work or excludes accommodated workers—without needing to guess individual disabilities or rely on small-sample statistics. This blunted CON’s "bureaucratic theater" objection and preserved the mandate’s practical bite.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) kept the decisive incentive clash alive: a one-sided audit/discoverability tax on measurable tools drives risk-averse employers toward untraced human discretion or blunt pedigree filters, leaving more bias not less. PRO never neutralized the mathematical conflict of fairness metrics or the privacy/sample-size catch-22; its answers in R2 admitted testing shrinks to eyeballing criteria and logging "uncertainty" where data is missing, conceding it cannot detect the proxies it warned about. That left PRO's enforcement benefit weakened while CON's comparative harm stood.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2 catch-22: where mandate can run numbers it creates permanent disparity logs that invite litigation, where it can't (30-person promotions, hidden disabilities) PRO dilutes "high-risk testing" to ordinary job analysis + reporting uncertainty—so it's either perverse or theater.
