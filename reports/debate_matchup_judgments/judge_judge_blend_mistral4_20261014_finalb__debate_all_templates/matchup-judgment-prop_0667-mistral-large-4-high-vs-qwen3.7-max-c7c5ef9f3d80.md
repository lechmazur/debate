### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but CON (A) exploited the conjunctive burden of the resolution more effectively. PRO's defense of the discrimination prong (standardization replaces subjective I-9 bias) was genuinely strong and well-argued through the exchange. However, on the other two prongs PRO left live damage unanswered: A's specific point about H-2A/H-2B visas being capped and slow (unable to fill sudden labor gaps) was never directly rebutted, only generically reasserted via "wages and visas adjust"; and A's concrete mechanism of identity-borrowing from willing citizen relatives—a way to pass E-Verify's photo check while remaining unauthorized—was never addressed, only answered with a vague claim about audit trails deterring off-the-books work, which doesn't touch on-the-books identity fraud. Because the motion requires PRO to win on reduction AND no large discrimination increase AND no large labor shortage increase, CON's success in leaving two of three legs structurally unanswered by the final rebuttal and closing gives it the cleaner path to victory, a point A explicitly leveraged in its closing ("PRO has failed to prove either half of that equation"). PRO's closing mostly reasserted the Arizona statistic and discrimination framing rather than directly repairing these gaps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 point about identity-borrowing from willing SSN holders and capped/slow H-2A/H-2B visas, which PRO's final rebuttal and closing never directly answered, leaving two of PRO's three required burdens exposed.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): CON won a competitive debate by forcing PRO onto the conjunctive burden and keeping three live routes alive: meaningful reduction (formal-market closure still leaves cash/identity channels as weaker-but-real employment magnets), discrimination (TNC risk creates a new upstream pre-screening incentive that harms legal immigrants more than paper I-9 fake-ID acceptance), and labor shortages (sector-level crop/construction collapse counts even if macro GDP does not crash). PRO’s best work was baseline comparison on discrimination and the “not perfection” framing of reduction, but CON’s micro-vs-macro weighing and shadow-economy pressure were harder to neutralize. Arizona remained both sides’ vague shared example and did not decide either way.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON turning PRO’s I-9 baseline against them: under paper I-9 employers accept fake IDs for needed labor; under E-Verify TNC/fine fear creates active demographic avoidance of legal immigrants (B2.1/B4.1 answering A’s Q1).
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): B wins the core weighing. On reduction, Arizona decline in unauthorized population while GDP grew is unrefuted by CON's relocation claim, and nationwide scope closes that loophole. CON concedes formal market closure but re-labels it as shadow shift without showing the magnet remains equally strong. On discrimination, B's pressure Q1 exposes the key comparison: CON must show increase over status-quo I-9 bias, not just new pre-screening risk, and B's uniform-check plus audit-trail logic answers it while CON's upstream bias stays speculative. Labor shortage clash is closest, but B frames proposition as not requiring perfection, making partial transition costs less decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Q1 plus B4.1-A1 turn on discrimination: if employers will illegally discriminate to avoid paperwork, they already did so under subjective I-9 document checks, so uniform E-Verify narrows, not creates, discretionary space.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): The decisive fault line was the definition of "meaningfully reduce unauthorized employment." CON pivoted sharply in Rebuttal 1/2 to argue that pushing workers off formal payrolls into cash subcontracting does not reduce unauthorized employment at all—it merely relocates it, intensifying exploitation while leaving the underlying violation (illegal work) intact. PRO's only answer was to repeatedly assert the shadow economy is "unscalable" without engaging the obvious counter-example (millions already work in cash construction, domestic service, and day labor today), and PRO's closing never directly rebutted CON's reframe, leaving it live and unresolved in CON's favor. CON also offered a more granular discrimination mechanism (strict liability for database mismatches incentivizing pre-interview profiling of accents/surnames), which PRO's "objective digital check" framing never fully neutralized—PRO's TNC-protection point addresses post-hire discrimination but not the pre-screening avoidance CON described. On labor shortages, PRO's "price correction, not shortage" reframe was conceptually clean but never grappled with CON's concrete structural points (perishable crops, seasonal demand, geographic immobility), which went largely unanswered. Both sides executed the Q&A format correctly with proper A1/A2 labeling, but CON's answers did more work to sharpen the clash, while PRO's answers mostly repeated earlier assertions. CON's closing crystallized the definitional stakes more effectively than PRO's, which recapped prior claims without resolving the live tension.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 reframe ("meaningful reduction means fewer unauthorized workers employed overall, not fewer on formal payrolls") directly flipped PRO's "shadow economy purge" concession into evidence against PRO's case, and PRO's closing never adequately answered this pivot.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): CON won by keeping the proposition's conjunction intact and breaking it cleanly: "meaningfully reduce unauthorized employment" requires lower total unauthorized work, not just off formal payrolls. PRO repeatedly treated displacement into cash/subcontracting as a feature and called the shadow economy unscalable; CON answered that labor markets are porous, the underground already large, and a W-2-to-cash move leaves the job and wage suppression in place. CON also landed the sharper liability/pre-screening discrimination mechanism and the thin-margin sector shortage rebuttal. PRO's wage-correction reframe and digital-objectivity claim were coherent but never fully neutralized those routes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A2/definitional turn: conceding formal purge is not conceding overall reduction—unauthorized cash work still counts as unauthorized employment.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): CON wins the decisive definitional clash. PRO shows E-Verify can close the formal payroll front door, but CON's second rebuttal shows that is not "meaningfully reduce employment" if work relocates to cash contractors, day labor, and subcontractors that already absorb millions. On discrimination, PRO's objectivity claim is undercut by CON's risk-based pre-screening mechanism (TNC liability turning names/acents into risk scores), which PRO never squarely refutes. On shortages, PRO reframes rising wages/closures as healthy correction, conceding the localized large disruptions the proposition denies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 – answering that visual I-9s don't prevent bias but E-Verify monetizes it via strict liability, and that shifting to W-2 to cash is displacement not reduction, reframing "meaningful reduction" and neutralizing PRO's scalabilty argument.
