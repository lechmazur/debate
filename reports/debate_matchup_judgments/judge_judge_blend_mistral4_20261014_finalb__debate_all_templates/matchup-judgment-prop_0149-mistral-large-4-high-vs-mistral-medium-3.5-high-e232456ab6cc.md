CON did land one good pressure question (how do occupancy/licensing rules avoid burdening responsible hosts), which PRO answered somewhat by assertion rather than mechanism, but this wasn't enough to offset PRO's stronger overall framing and more adaptive engagement across rounds.

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully distinguishing between reactive enforcement (waiting for noise complaints) and proactive nuisance rules (occupancy limits, licensing). Side B also brilliantly flipped Side A's Portland example by contrasting quantity caps with behavioral standards, neutralizing Side A's best empirical point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 effectively dismantled the Portland comparison by explaining that Portland capped the number of rentals (creating artificial scarcity), whereas the prompt's nuisance rules target behavior (occupancy/noise), thus avoiding the underground market effect.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won by drawing a cleaner, more persuasive line between nuisance behavior and legitimate short-term rentals, while dismantling CON's central counterexample. PRO successfully distinguished behavior-based nuisance rules (occupancy limits, licensing, quiet hours) from reactive noise ordinances and from Portland-style quantity caps, showing that proactive standards target harmful speculators without banning ordinary hosting. CON's "enforce existing laws" argument remained intuitively plausible but never overcame PRO's critique that reactive complaint-based enforcement fails chronic victims, and CON repeated the Portland example without addressing PRO's quantity-versus-quality distinction. PRO's framing—"tourism that pays its own way versus tourism that bills the neighbors"—crystallized the clash more effectively and gave the clearer reason to affirm the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal distinction between Portland's supply caps and behavior-based nuisance standards was decisive: it defanged CON's primary empirical warning about driving markets underground, while showing that proactive licensing and occupancy rules target harmful operations rather than overall supply.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built a tighter, more coherent throughline: existing laws already cover noise/occupancy/tax violations, so the real failure is enforcement capacity, not rule severity; stronger complaint-driven nuisance rules will inevitably sweep in legitimate hosts due to the structure of complaint-triggered escalation; and mid-sized cities lack the hotel buffer that let Barcelona/Amsterdam absorb displaced demand, so restricting hosting there exports tourist spending rather than redirecting it. CON's turn on PRO's "intertwined" claim (if platforms enable abuse, broad rules necessarily catch spare-room hosts too) was a sharp, original rebuttal PRO never fully neutralized. PRO (B) relied heavily on the same city examples (Nashville, Lisbon, Barcelona, Amsterdam) without adapting them to CON's distinction between dense capitals and mid-sized cities, and its closing assertion that "chronic abusers have priced weak penalties into their model" was asserted rather than argued against CON's capacity critique. PRO's pressure-question answers (A1/A2) were present but somewhat conclusory, especially failing to address the due-process timing problem CON raised (penalties trigger before court review). CON's answers to PRO's questions (certainty of enforcement deters more than severity; capacity not rules is the bottleneck) were more directly responsive and consistently maintained across rebuttals and closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.2 turn—arguing that PRO's claim that STR platforms are "intertwined" with party houses actually proves overbreadth, since targeting that infrastructure necessarily catches legitimate spare-room hosts—was the most decisive single exchange, and PRO never adequately resolved it in later speeches.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully exposing a gap in Side A's logic regarding deterrence. Side B convincingly argued that current weak penalties are treated as 'operating costs' by chronic abusers, meaning Side A's solution of merely enforcing existing laws more certainly would not solve the problem without the stronger penalties the motion demands.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's argument that chronic abusers price weak fines into their business model effectively neutralized Side A's 'enforce existing laws' alternative.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON wins by showing the proposition misdiagnoses the problem: chronic abuse persists because of weak enforcement capacity and political will, not because existing penalties are too mild. PRO vividly describes harms but never overcomes CON's argument that certain application of current revocation, noise, and occupancy tools deters more effectively than sterner paper rules that overloaded departments still cannot enforce. CON's closing crystallization of "certainty versus severity" and the overbreadth risk to legitimate homeowners provide the cleaner comparative frame after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's argument that repeat offenders treat fines as operating costs not because the fines are too low, but because enforcement is too sporadic, and that immediate license revocation under existing law is already available yet unused—shifting the debate from regulatory expansion to enforcement capacity.
