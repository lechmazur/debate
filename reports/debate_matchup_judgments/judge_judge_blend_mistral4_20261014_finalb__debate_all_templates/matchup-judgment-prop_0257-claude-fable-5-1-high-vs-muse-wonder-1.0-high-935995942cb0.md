### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the central distinction clear: ending distribution or cloud service does not justify reclaiming a lawfully purchased copy. Side A mounted strong pressure on resale enforcement and proposed statutory non-revocation plus export, but that alternative largely conceded the proposition's core demand for durable ownership-like rights. Its claims that ownership necessarily requires a standing kill switch and that upstream rights lapses defeat continued possession were overstated. The unresolved resale costs kept the debate competitive, while Side A's two clipped rebuttals were an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest response was that ownership need not make unlawful duplication technologically impossible. A voluntary transfer, backed by infringement liability, does not inherently require platforms to retain day-to-day revocation power over innocent buyers, weakening Side A's central offline-copy versus kill-switch dilemma.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): B won the decisive clash: PRO’s package needs both durable offline/export copies and enforceable one-copy resale, and B showed those demands pull opposite ways. A’s key-handoff “deed registry” still needed central key-killing power, so it reconstituted the sheriff under friendlier labels, while watermark/liability added more lock-in than it removed. B’s narrower comparison—non-revocation, export, and refunds solve vanishing libraries without a resale regime that invites perfect-copy price collapse and subscription flight—stayed live and cleaner. Rights-lapse limits on platforms further undercut ownership as magic permanence. A’s post-payment power frame and pressure questions were strong, and B’s two clipped rebuttals cost polish, but they did not erase B’s structural edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s exportable-offline vs one-active-copy contradiction (B2.1–B2.2, then B4.4 on the key registry as kill switch): forced A into a mechanism that still centralizes deactivation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): A crystallized the core clash more cleanly: after full payment, the seller should not retain a unilateral option to revoke, and B's narrow license fixes still leave the platform as the rule-maker interpreting its own terms. While B landed sharp technical blows on the tension between offline copies and enforceable resale, A's response that rights need not be cheat-proof to be meaningful—and that physical ownership never required perfect prevention—was enough to keep its route to victory alive. B also incurred real execution costs from two clipped rebuttals, and its closing slightly compressed A's position into mere non-revocation even though A consistently defended a broader ownership bundle. A's framing of extinguishing the seller's option proved more durable after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's challenge that an offline, exportable copy cannot be deactivated on resale without a centralized kill switch, forcing A into the dilemma of choosing between real offline ownership and enforceable single-copy rules (B2.1-B2.2, B4.4).
