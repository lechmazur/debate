### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but CON's core structural critique—that linking "full internalization" to tax-incentive status is both over- and under-inclusive (an unsubsidized or below-cap project can externalize identical costs; a cost-internalizing project still gets an unlimited giveaway)—was never squarely resolved by PRO. CON also won the key definitional exchange: when PRO tried to use CON's own listed tools (connection fees, take-or-pay contracts) to prove "full internalization" was workable, CON cleanly exposed the equivocation between "incremental/triggered" costs and "full" shared-cost allocation, and distinguished financing timing from ultimate cost incidence in B4.1. PRO's rebuttals were rhetorically sharp (the "blank checks in the dark" and "extraordinary privilege" framing) and successfully turned CON's pioneer-developer-agreement concession into a closing point about feasibility, but it never fully answered why tying the remedy specifically to tax status—rather than universal utility cost-causation rules CON proposed—is the correct policy fit. CON's closing crystallized this necessary/sufficient gap cleanly, leaving the more decisive, unresolved logical objection on its side.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1/B4.1 exposing PRO's equivocation between "incremental/triggered" costs and "full internalization," reinforced by the financing-vs-incidence distinction, was the most damaging and least rebutted exchange of the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON successfully showed the motion mismatches remedy to harm: utility cost socialization should be fixed through universal cost-causation rules, not an arbitrary tax-incentive cap that misses unsubsidized loads and allows unlimited giveaways to projects that internalize costs. While PRO landed effective points about families paying twice, CON's persistent structural critique—that pioneer-developer agreements solve the externality without distorting tax policy—proved the cleaner path to victory after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's turning of the pioneer-developer agreement in Rebuttal 2 and Closing: after PRO cited it to prove full internalization is workable, CON showed this actually demonstrates direct utility contracts work independently of tax status, undermining the need for the proposed linkage.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): CON won the targeting clash. PRO’s double-subsidy is vivid, but CON showed the remedy mismatches the harm: "fully internalized" for shared, decades-long upgrades is indeterminate, pioneer-developer agreements can protect ratepayers for all loads without a tax trigger, and a cap is arbitrary/underinclusive (below-cap projects can externalize, above-cap projects may still be overcharged). B never resolved A’s Q1 about the cap threshold and conceded the utility-contract solution, leaving PRO’s linkage unjustified.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1/A4.3 turned PRO’s feasibility point into a concession: direct cost-causation tariffs and pioneer agreements can finance shared upgrades and reconcile later users, proving universal utility regulation works and exposing PRO’s tax-status linkage as neither necessary nor sufficient.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): A built a tighter mechanism (attributable-cost internalization, not 100% of joint costs) and used it to defuse B's "poison pill" and "false dilemma" framings, most decisively in A4.5 where A turned B's own proposed minimum-take contracts into proof that internalization is already compatible with normal practice — a point B never directly re-engaged. A's pressure answers (A4.1-A4.3) were clean, labeled, and responsive. B's strongest and most persistent line — that the rule lets rich hubs offer uncapped incentives while locking poorer, upgrade-needing towns out of competition — was pressed well and never fully neutralized, only partially answered via a risk-allocation/"no moral entitlement" argument, so B kept a live route to the end and landed the final, complete, unanswered closing blow. However, A's rebuttal work was more precise overall and successfully reframed B's central institutional-contradiction challenge, giving A the stronger throughline despite format penalties from two clipped speeches (notably the incomplete closing), which cost A some final crystallization and let B have the last clean word.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.5's judo move — showing that B's own proposed minimum-take utility contracts are themselves a form of the internalization the proposition demands, not an alternative to it — was the most damaging unanswered exchange, undercutting B's central "false dilemma" framing for the rest of the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that cost internalization targets only attributable project costs, not 100% of shared networks, and that CON's fiscal-surplus and minimum-take-contract alternatives either collapsed into that standard or relied on shifting risk to captive ratepayers. While CON's regional-inequality objection created real pressure, PRO successfully reframed the cap as a protection against exploitative bidding wars rather than a barrier to poor towns, and showed that CON's win-win scenario depended on optimistic cross-ledger transfers that are neither automatic nor evenly distributed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's turn in Rebuttal 2 that minimum-take utility contracts assigning demand risk to the developer are a form of internalization, not an alternative to it, which undermined CON's claim that existing utility mechanisms made the tax cap unnecessary.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) kept the stronger live route: attributable-cost internalization, not 100% of shared assets, with public share + reimbursement credits, so joint-cost indivisibility does not kill the rule. CON's best pressure - regulatory contradiction and poor-town inequality - was answered by distinguishing generic tariff approval from project-specific allocation, and by showing minimum-take contracts are internalization, not an alternative. If school surplus truly dwarfs grid costs, the project survives the cap; if not, capping protects the smallest ratepayer bases from a double giveaway. That weighing survived clashes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R2 A1-A2: tax authority verifies a regulator-determined attributable share, public funds genuine spare capacity with credits, and distinguishes generic tariff legality from internalization - neutralizing both the contradiction charge and the wealthy-hub vs poor-town claim.
