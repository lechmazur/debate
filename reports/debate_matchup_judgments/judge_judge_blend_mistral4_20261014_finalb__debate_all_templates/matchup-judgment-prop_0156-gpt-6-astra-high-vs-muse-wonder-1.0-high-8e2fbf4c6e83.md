### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully separating institutional survival from political solidarity. Side A effectively argued that while the financial machinery survived, it did so through coercion and blame, which hollowed out the political consent required for true solidarity.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A1 and A2 in Rebuttal 2 perfectly distinguished between institutional cooperation (driven by fear of collapse) and true political solidarity (publics willing to back future help), effectively turning Side B's claim that the survival of commitments proved solidarity.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): B won a close, high-quality clash by tying the motion’s comparison to citizen political learning: asymmetrical conditionality taught humiliation in the south and resentment in the north, so institutional survival hollowed rather than preserved solidarity. B answered pressure more decisively, kept the distrust-versus-consent frame live, and made the vote clearer on the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s A1/A2 (Rebuttal 2): institutional cooperation under bank/export fear is weak political solidarity; lasting two-way distrust and overridden democratic choice outweigh repayable, conditional mechanisms.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a clearer comparative path: the response conditioned assistance on externally imposed austerity and democratic override, generating a durable "double lesson" of humiliation in debtor states and resentment in creditor states that reshaped party systems and displaced elected governments. Side B defended institutional risk-sharing and rescued commitments as solidarity, but Side A successfully rebutted that this cooperation was transactional self-preservation driven by fear of collapse rather than genuine political solidarity. Side A’s answers to pressure questions were sharper, defining solidarity as requiring mutual trust and electoral consent that the response systematically eroded, while Side B never specified what magnitude of distrust would outweigh its institutional claims. The motion’s focus on political distrust versus European solidarity favors Side A’s interpretation that preserving the machinery while exhausting the trust that legitimizes it left distrust as the larger legacy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s first rebuttal argument that German voters could effectively dictate Greek pensions and taxes while Greek voters could neither reciprocate nor change those policies by voting, converting Side B’s democracy defense into evidence of hierarchical asymmetry that corroded trust on both sides.
