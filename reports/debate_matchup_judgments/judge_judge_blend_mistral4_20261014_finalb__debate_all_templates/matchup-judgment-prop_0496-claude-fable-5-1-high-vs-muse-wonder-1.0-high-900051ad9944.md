### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by separating democratic consent from personal willingness to serve. A compellingly showed that contractors can obscure costs and dilute responsibility, but did not prove those failures are inherent or that regulation cannot address them. B also showed why a blanket ban is overinclusive when legislators can expressly authorize, fund, disclose, and police temporary contracted force. A's clipped second rebuttal and closing reliance on an already-answered draft argument weakened its finish.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal decisively answered A's pressure: refusing conscription rejects a method of manning, not necessarily the mission, while speed, specialized skill, and reversibility provide legitimate reasons to contract. This undercut A's claim that contractor use itself proves absent public consent.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a very tight debate by proving that PMCs structurally evade democratic accountability by severing the chain of command and hiding the true cost of war. While Side A brilliantly argued that 'friction is not consent' and that legislatures can theoretically regulate contracts, Side B successfully demonstrated that the market value of PMCs is precisely their ability to bypass those very frictions and public testing of consent.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 identification of the severed chain of command (where accountability stops at a rebranded firm rather than an elected minister) perfectly answered Side A's hypothetical about regulated PMCs.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the decisive clash locked on structural design: combat contractors exist to strip the political costs (troop ceilings, visible dead, votes) that make democratic restraint bite, and B’s concession that a ban “will not magically produce a public willing to draft tens of thousands” supplied A’s cleanest route. B reframed accountability as authorization-plus-means and answered the pressure questions with speed/reversibility and ends/means distinctions, but never neutralized why the instrument is systematically preferred when it damps those costs, nor why two decades of “could regulate” left the gaps intact. A’s second rebuttal was clipped (real execution cost), yet the overall path—openings mechanisms, friction-as-test weighing, and closing crystallization of B2.5—made the proposition easier to decide for PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 1 / Pressure pivot: contractors are the tool that makes legislative evasion painless, crystallized later by treating B’s “no magic draft” line as proof force at that scale lacked citizen consent.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): B won by showing that ex ante counting, funding, notification, and scope rules can preserve contractor capacity while directly addressing accountability. A never adequately answered why an enforceable ban would outperform enforceable transparency rules, and its closing incorrectly treated B's proposed checks as merely retrospective. B also sustained the comparative risk that banning documented contractors can shift force toward less auditable proxies. B's repeated clipping narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response that the same observable conduct can enforce either a ban or a counting rule was decisive: enforcement of regulation preserves contracts, information, and legal targets, while enforcement of a ban may only displace capacity. This directly weakened A's bright-line distinction.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's distinction between regulation and prohibition, proving that the exact same enforcement mechanisms apply to both. Side A successfully weaponized Side B's claim that embassy guarding is "overt" to neutralize Side B's core "off the books" harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 expertly turned Side B's answer about overt proxy missions, pointing out that if embassy defense cannot be secret, then the contractor doing it isn't "off the books" either.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): CON won a close, high-quality clash by turning PRO’s own enforceability and overt-protection concessions against the ban: the same on-the-ground evidence that would police a ban also polices counting/reporting, and a ban leaves no invoice, scope, or defendant while a regulated contractor leaves all three. PRO’s three-links accountability frame and consent-before-shooting rhetoric were sharp, and the bright-line policing answer was honest, but never fully neutralized the substitution/paper-trail comparison or the charge that the real goal was fewer missions rather than better control. Clipped CON turns cost some polish, but the decisive comparative weighing still favored keeping the documented actor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 / Closing capture of PRO’s A1 concession that embassy/convoy protection is overt and location-specific, dissolving the “off the books” harm for the exact combat roles at issue, paired with the same-evidence enforceability flip.
