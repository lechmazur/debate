### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by exposing the ban's product-based underinclusiveness and offering a plausible less restrictive alternative. A persuasively identified scarcity, entrenchment, and asymmetric risk, but never adequately explained why the same aquifer withdrawal becomes uniquely unmanageable when sold as plain water rather than soda or another product. B's emergency claims and broad reading of “aquifers” were overstated, and its closing used erroneous reference labels, but its central regulatory comparison remained stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was the soda-plant comparison: if automatic cutoffs can govern farms and beverage factories, A needed a stronger reason they cannot govern bottlers; if corporate pressure defeats enforcement, A's preferred regulation of those larger users faces the same problem.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): A (CON) won by turning PRO’s bright-line case into a consistency trap: either automatic drought cutoffs and bonds can control extractors—so a ban is unnecessary—or they cannot, in which case PRO’s farming/industry carve-out fails by the same logic. A kept the soda-versus-pure-water arbitrariness and emergency-network clashes live and sharper through the rounds; B’s answers narrowed “municipal” and product distinctions but never fully neutralized those routes. B framed stakes memorably, yet A made the easier decisive comparison after full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s regulation dilemma plus soda/water arbitrariness (especially A2.3–A2.5 and A5.1–A5.2) against B’s claim that only pure-water bottlers create irreversible corporate stakes.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by exposing the fatal arbitrariness and practical loopholes of the proposition. Side A convincingly argued that banning pure water bottling while allowing soda, beer, and agriculture to pump the exact same aquifers does not save water, but merely shifts extraction to other corporate actors with identical sunk-cost incentives. Side A's closing dilemma—either the government can enforce smart regulations (making the ban unnecessary) or it cannot (making the ban ineffective against other industries)—crystallized the logical failure of Side B's case. Side B relied on a strong moral frame but could not adequately defend the practical inconsistencies of the ban.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's persistent attack on the "soda vs. water" distinction, culminating in the closing argument that a beverage conglomerate could simply add sugar to bypass the ban, proving the prohibition fails to protect the aquifer while crippling emergency supply chains.
