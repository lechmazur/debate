### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): A ran a sophisticated, narrowing strategy: concede that orthodoxy-enforcing blasphemy laws must go, but carve out a tightly bounded "sacred respect" exception (deliberate desecration of a designated object within a reserved ceremony) to defeat the categorical "abolish" claim. This was clever and forced B to do real work. But under pressure questioning, B exposed the fatal flaw: once trespass/consent law is conceded to cover uninvited outsiders, the only surviving case is an invited participant — almost definitionally an internal reformer or dissenter — whose "desecrating purpose" can only be adjudicated by deciding whose theology is authentic. That reproduces exactly the orthodoxy-policing harm A had already disclaimed. A's final answers (uncertainty "defeats liability," courts just identify "that congregation's practice") never explain how a court avoids taking sides between a reform faction and an orthodox faction who disagree about what counts as contempt versus devotion. B's closing crystallized this collapse cleanly, while A's closing reasserted the framework without repairing the entanglement problem. B's broader case (heckler's veto, minority vulnerability, hate-speech/incitement line) also stood largely unrebutted as a matter of general principle. The debate was close and intellectually serious on both sides, but B's exploitation of A's narrowed position in Rebuttal 2 and Closing gives it the cleaner, more complete path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.3–B4.4), responding to the pressure-question answers, showing that A's sole surviving scenario is an invited internal dissenter whose prosecution would resurrect exactly the orthodoxy-enforcement A had already conceded must end — a point A never fully neutralized in Rebuttal 2 or Closing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): A won by progressively cornering B’s “narrow sacred-respect” defense into a single synthetic case—an invited participant’s quiet non-obstructive destruction of their own text—then showing that case is already handled by content-neutral consent/trespass rules or else becomes criminal enforcement of internal orthodoxy, which B had already conceded must end. That collapse, plus cleaner weighing (zero added protection for peaceful worship vs. high majoritarian-abuse residual), made the proposition easier to decide. B kept a live philosophical interest in ceremonial character and honest burden framing, but never restored a durable residual that justified keeping the full legal category against mandatory abolition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 (A4): answering B’s invited-participant hypo by equating it to ordinary consent/trespass and reclassifying the actor as an internal reformer, so a religious-insult add-on resurrects the orthodoxy enforcement B abandoned.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A progressively collapsed Side B's case into a single edge-case scenario and showed that even this was either covered by content-neutral property and consent rules or constituted internal religious dissent that B agreed should not be criminalized. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, and the pressure-question round genuinely sharpened the clash. A's constructed hypothetical (the non-threatening public desecration) was a clever test case that forced B to bite real bullets, and A's closing used vivid framing to make the costs of abolition feel concrete. But B handled the pressure more honestly and effectively: it answered A1/A2 directly, then used the ex-believer self-desecration example to expose that A's "manner not message" distinction is not actually content-neutral, since the restricted "method" is itself the most forceful form of the protester's message — a point A's closing never squarely rebutted beyond reasserting that the act "contains zero argumentative content." B also kept returning to the strongest, best-grounded empirical point (blasphemy laws are typically wielded against minorities and dissenters rather than protecting them), which A's hypothetical about majority-on-minority desecration campaigns never fully displaced. A's rebuttal 2 treated B's consistent bite-the-bullet answers as new "concessions" that "decide the debate," but those consequences were already implicit in B's opening framework, so the rhetorical leverage there is somewhat overstated. Overall B gave the cleaner, more internally consistent path to the motion and more directly neutralized A's central distinction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal 2 (B4.4-B4.6), which used the ex-believer's self-desecration-as-protest example to show A's "manner vs. message" line collapses, since the prohibited method can itself be the protest's substance — a point A's closing did not successfully answer.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): A kept a clearer live path: equal citizenship cannot mean a legal duty of sacred deference, and any “narrow” insult law still turns on contested sacred status. A answered B’s pressure honestly, then used those answers to show B’s substance/manner escape collapses on the ex-believer protest and that order-based justifications create a heckler’s veto while pure-degradation justifications simply revive blasphemy enforcement. B made the remainder cases and minority-degradation costs vivid and exploited A’s concessions well, but never neutralized the neutrality problem at the core of those laws. On the proposition as written, A’s comparative reason to abolish remained cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s A1/A2 plus A4.4–A4.6: accepting the tinderbox remainder, then showing B’s method restriction still criminalizes symbolic repudiation because the object is sacred.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side A wins by proving that blasphemy laws cannot be neutrally administered: criminalizing desecration necessarily empowers majority officials to decide which religious rejections are permissible, enforcing deference rather than protecting citizens against coercion. Side B’s vivid hypotheticals and forceful use of A’s concessions kept the debate competitive, but B never overcame the “sacred to whom” problem or showed how to objectively separate legitimate protest from punishable insult without viewpoint discrimination. A’s closing crystallized that trade-off cleanly: governments can protect worshippers and act against violence without claiming ownership of conscience.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s sustained pressure on the ex-believer hypothetical, showing that CON’s “method not message” distinction collapses because the method is only criminal when the object is sacred to someone else, revealing the law as viewpoint-based enforcement of religious deference.
