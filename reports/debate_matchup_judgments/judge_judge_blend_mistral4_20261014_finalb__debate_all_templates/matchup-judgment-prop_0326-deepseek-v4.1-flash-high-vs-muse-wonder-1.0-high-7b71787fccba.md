### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases, but A kept tighter control of the narrative. A's core move — forcing B to admit "None, and none should" when asked what stops an app from detecting grief and selling affection back — became the decisive concession that A exploited cleanly in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, framing B's autonomy argument as endorsing unlimited engineered dependency. A also answered the pressure questions directly with a workable design-based test (payment/lapse-triggered affection vs. opt-in roleplay), addressing the surveillance objection concretely. B's strongest counter — that premium tiers themselves "intensify affection based on payment," collapsing A's test — was a genuinely damaging point, but it arrived at the end of a rebuttal that was cut off before B could fully land the resulting dilemma, and A's closing left the specific feature-gating vs. affection-withdrawal distinction under-defended rather than fully refuted. The clipped Rebuttal 2 is a real execution cost for B, and overall A's case progressed more cleanly from opening through closing without losing its central thread.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 answer (A1/A2) distinguishing opt-in roleplay from payment/lapse-triggered affection, directly countering B's surveillance and workability pressure questions, set up the framing that B's own "None, and none should" answer could be read as conceding the core harm — though B's unfinished premium-tier rebuttal in Rebuttal 2/Closing was the closest thing to a counter-knockout, left incomplete by the format clip.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by identifying a meaningful regulatory gap: ordinary fraud and cancellation rules do not address systems that adaptively cultivate attachment and then use withdrawal, churn risk, or payment triggers to monetize it. Side A raised strong autonomy, surveillance, and line-drawing objections, especially that premium memory or intimacy could be mistaken for manipulation. However, Side B narrowed its proposal to design-based evidence, opt-in roleplay, and audits of payment and retention mechanics rather than policing fictional content. Side A’s admission that nothing should stop grief-responsive monetization made B’s core harm especially vivid, while A’s clipped second rebuttal imposed a modest execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s answer distinguishing requested clingy roleplay from affection altered by payment, lapse, streak, or churn risk was decisive. It substantially answered Side A’s strongest claim that regulators would necessarily police personality and plot, while preserving the argument that ordinary consumer law misses engineered emotional leverage.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that AI companions exploit adaptive, personalized reciprocity beyond ordinary entertainment, and by narrowing its proposal to auditable design features—payment-linked affection, streaks, and default manipulations—rather than policing plot or personality. Side B’s autonomy framework was strong, but its concession that existing law should not block grief-targeted monetization [B4.1] confirmed the core harm Side A identified. Side A also defused B’s surveillance objection by demonstrating duty could be discharged through product audits, easy deletion, and opt-in crisis tools rather than reading private transcripts, leaving B’s workability critique weakened after a clipped rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 [A4.1–A4.4], which distinguished user-requested roleplay from algorithmic retention levers and showed duty-of-care could be enforced through design and pricing audits rather than transcript surveillance, directly neutralizing Side B’s strongest objections.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and the vagueness objection (B's strongest weapon) was real, but A's test in A4.1—combination of market targeting, personalized initiation of simulated hurt, and monetized relief—gave a workable, function-based line that B never fully dismantled. B's A1 answer in [B4.1] conceding that guilt-based paywalling is "permissible" so long as disclosed was a costly concession that A exploited effectively in closing: it exposed that CON's framework treats disclosed billing as sufficient even when the leverage itself (manufactured emotional debt) is never disclosed or limited. B's rebuttal that this concession "decides the debate" their way was asserted rather than demonstrated — they reasserted the vagueness point without re-engaging why disclosed price should settle the manipulation question. A's closing crystallized this gap sharply, while B's closing mostly restated the vagueness critique without landing a fresh blow on the conceded point. A also handled the crisis-detection pressure question with a workable safe-harbor answer (explicit signals only, automated pause-and-refer) that B critiqued as "hindsight liability" but without showing why the bright-line (explicit statements only) collapses in the way ordinary ambiguous roleplay would. On balance A built a narrower, more defensible version of its rule across rounds and converted a key admission into the clinching closing argument, giving it a clear but not overwhelming edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to Q1 in Rebuttal 2 ([B4.1]), conceding that disclosed guilt-based paywalling is permissible, which A then leveraged in closing to argue that disclosure of price never discloses or limits the manipulative leverage itself.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by identifying conduct that ordinary fraud and billing rules do not reach: a personalized companion simulating pain over the user’s absence and monetizing relief from that pain. B answered the vagueness objection with a functional, multi-part test tied to targeting, initiation, personalization, and payment, while preserving adult access to companionship. A raised credible line-drawing and chilling-effect concerns, but its admission that transparent guilt-based paywalling should remain permissible made B’s case for distinct rules substantially easier to accept.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s most decisive move was turning A’s direct acceptance of guilt-based paywalling into proof that existing consumer law leaves the central exploitation untouched. A’s fiction analogy remained relevant, but B persuasively distinguished generalized narrative sadness from personalized, initiated, and monetized simulated distress.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won because CON conceded under pressure that the core harm—guilt-based paywalling of affection—is permissible under existing law, so long as billing is disclosed [B4.1]. PRO seized this admission to show that ordinary consumer law cannot reach the engineered leverage at the heart of the business model [A5.1-A5.5]. PRO also offered a workable functional test (personalized initiation of simulated distress + monetization of relief) that distinguished AI companions from passive entertainment, answering CON’s overbreadth charges [A4.1]. CON’s vagueness objections were plausible but never fully neutralized this route to victory, and their closing largely restated earlier claims rather than recovering from the concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s pressure question Q1 in Turn 5, which forced CON to admit that a companion may say “it hurt when you left” and sell back warmer affection transparently, exposing that existing law leaves the central harm untouched.
