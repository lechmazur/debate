### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won by showing that broad-access skilled professions and increasingly complex work were central to mass expansion, while expanded access also dismantled class and gender barriers. A persuasively explained how wage premiums can coexist with screening, but repeatedly asserted rather than established that generic graduates vastly outnumbered new skilled opportunities. B's invalid P-reference labels and some unsupported aggregate claims narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was its answer to A's attempt to quarantine nursing, teaching, accounting, and allied fields as a capped licensed core. By tying those broad professions to regional mass universities and pressing A's concessions that they both grew and required real training, B exposed the missing comparative support for A's claim that inflation was larger.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins because it successfully undermined Side B's empirical and theoretical core. B argued that inflation governed the middle and margin of mass expansion, but A demonstrated with concrete, plausible examples (nursing, teaching, accounting, logistics) that the middle of mass expansion genuinely required higher skills and absorbed millions of graduates into expanded professional roles. A landed a decisive rebuttal in its second rebuttal by exposing that B could not simultaneously concede graduates are more trainable, that broad professions require real training and grew, and still maintain that firms are trapped paying decades-long premiums for unused curricula in unchanged jobs. This contradiction, combined with A's strong point that pure selection cannot explain a persistent wage premium when universities dipped far deeper into the population, left B relying on an overly narrow emblem (the sales supervisor) that could not outweigh the structural transformation of the knowledge economy and the permanent opening of professional life to women and working-class students. B kept the debate competitive with crisp framing, but its route to victory was materially damaged by A's sharper comparative weighing and internal critique.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2, where it exposed that PRO's concessions on graduate trainability and the genuine expansion of broad-access professions (nursing, accounting, teaching) create a fatal tension with PRO's claim that inflation dominates the middle—forcing PRO into the implausible position that firms have been trapped for decades paying premiums for unused curricula in unchanged roles.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): CON wins on the decisive margin comparison. PRO's ticket-vs-seat framing was clear and its displacement explanation for a persistent premium was plausible, but CON's profit-motive pressure — firms would not pay higher wages for decades for unused paper, plus mass dipping would collapse quality if only selection — was never squarely neutralized. CON successfully reframes PRO's "narrow" gains: nursing, teaching, accounting and allied operations are not a capped elite but the core of mass expansion that grew by millions and requires real advanced training that outgrew short routes, and breaking class/gender monopoly is permanent opportunity, not bounded. Tickets rose in some office jobs, but seats multiplied more.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's profit-motive + depth-of-pool response in B2.1 and B4.1 — if degrees were just filtering unchanged jobs, premiums would have been bid down as enrollment multiplied, and trainability can't be both valuable and unused.
