### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-mechanized cases and answered pressure questions directly, making this a close contest. But the decisive exchange came in the pressure-question/rebuttal-2 pairing on feasibility: A asked why an owner would sell under heavy replacement mandates, and B candidly answered "that is the point," conceding that its conditional model effectively freezes existing residential parcels and shifts new supply onto vacant lots and underused sites. A's closing exploited this concession sharply, arguing that B's alternative guarantees chronic scarcity on the very parcels most valuable near transit, reproducing the "invisible displacement by bidding" dynamic A had warned about from the opening. B's closing pivoted to an "order of operations" framing (entitlement before guarantee) rather than directly rebutting the scale/sufficiency problem with vacant-lot-only development, leaving that critique live and unresolved in A's favor. B did land a real hit of its own — exposing that A's tax-funded compensation is post-hoc and non-binding — but A partially patched this with upfront voucher/statutory-payout language, and it was less structurally fatal than B's "that is the point" admission. On balance, A's case tracked the motion's explicit trade-off more cleanly and closed with the more damaging, unanswered point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure question on why an owner would sell under uncompensated replacement mandates, and B's candid "that is the point" answer in Rebuttal 2, which A then weaponized in closing to argue B's conditional model effectively freezes densification on the very parcels near transit that matter most.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won a competitive debate by keeping the decisive comparison live: transit demand still arrives without new units, so blocking by-right densification turns displacement corridor-wide, silent, and uncompensated, while by-right makes it localized and mitigable. CON pressed timing and binding-guarantee pressure effectively, but never fully escaped the scarcity trap or the mobility/lock-out costs of freezing residential parcels; B converted A's "and that is the point" concession into the cleaner ballot reason.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 / closing conversion of A's owner-will-not-sell stance into deliberate footprint freeze that manufactures the vacancy scarcity CON later cites against vouchers.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that the CON alternative of conditional, mandate-heavy densification effectively freezes existing residential parcels near transit, manufacturing the scarcity that locks out future working-class residents and fuels corridor-wide displacement. While Side B landed a sharp "order of operations" frame and exposed tensions in claiming binding mitigation under by-right approval, Side A's core mechanism—that affluent newcomers do not vanish when a mid-rise is blocked, but instead bid up older stock—remained largely intact. Side B's counter-reliance on vacant land and demand dispersal was less substantiated, and Side A's closing crystallized the trade-off compellingly: visible, compensable transition risk on a few lots is preferable to invisible, permanent exclusion across the corridor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4) was decisive: it directly answered Side B's pressure questions, then weaponized B4.2's concession that replacement mandates mean owners of older rentals simply will not sell for redevelopment—"and that is the point"—to prove the CON model sacrifices residential supply and entrenches scarcity.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases: A's vacancy-chain/shortage-drives-displacement logic versus B's land-value-windfall/speculative-teardown mechanism. The debate turned on A's answer to the pressure questions, where A proposed a self-funding interim-housing/right-to-return mandate baked into by-right rules. B exploited the cracks in that answer sharply and specifically: it requires finding "comparable interim housing" inside the very shortage-stricken market A says doesn't have vacancies, and it creates a pre-permit incentive for speculators to buy out tenants before any replacement obligation attaches — crystallized in B's memorable "permanent expulsion with a receipt" line. B also forced a concession-style admission from A (that unprofitable teardowns simply shift development to parking lots/commercial sites) and reframed it as proof that targeted, conditional zoning is the superior path — a rhetorical move A's closing never squarely answered. A's closing largely restated its standing framework rather than directly rebutting the "concedes the debate" framing or the pre-permit buyout loophole, leaving B's sharpest attacks unanswered at the end. A's macro case about shortage-driven displacement remains strong and under-addressed by B's "just build on parking lots" proposal (which doesn't fully grapple with capacity or the NIMBY dynamics of discretionary review in wealthy areas), but B's tighter, more pointed rebuttal work in the final two rounds gave it the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 response to A's interim-housing mechanism (B4.3–B4.4), which highlighted the "payout elsewhere" loophole and reframed A's parking-lot/commercial-site fallback as a concession that conditional, targeted zoning is actually the better tool — a point A's closing did not directly rebut.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a competitive debate by converting B's own mitigation package against it: interim housing plus deep replacement rents either will not pencil or creates pre-permit buyout incentives, while B's fallback that unprofitable teardowns simply leave older rentals alone and build on parking/commercial sites effectively concedes A's conditional targeting path without blanket by-right entitlements on vulnerable stock. B kept a strong scarcity-and-compensation frame and clean answers on temporary bridges, but never showed why municipalities must accept short-term demolition risk on occupied low-rent buildings once densification can be steered elsewhere. A's R2 and closing made the decisive choice clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2: used B's interim-housing/right-to-return answers and "if it doesn't pencil, build on parking lots" fallback to argue B had dismantled its own case for blanket by-right and conceded conditional steering.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing that Side A's own concessions undermined the case for blanket by-right upzoning. B showed that A's proposed tenant protections either created a fatal loophole (incentivizing pre-permit buyouts and "payouts elsewhere"), required impossible conditions (finding interim housing in the very shortage A described), or were strict enough to make teardowns unprofitable—in which case developers would simply build on parking lots and commercial sites. B successfully turned these answers into support for conditional planning that steers density to non-residential parcels and affluent areas while keeping vulnerable rentals standing. A never recovered from this double-bind or resolved why blanket by-right over occupied low-rent buildings was necessary if the goal was to avoid demolishing them.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 8), where B used Side A's own answers about interim housing costs, tenant payouts, and the fact that strict replacement mandates would push developers to parking lots and commercial sites, to prove that blanket by-right over vulnerable buildings was either dangerous or redundant compared to conditional planning.
