- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B won a competitive debate by forcing the proposition’s real burden: not whether scores can be useful, but why they must be compulsory. A answered pressure directly and kept the “all signals are unequal” comparison alive, yet never fully justified compulsion over availability plus guidance. Edge to B on decisive clash and weighing.

- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A built a stronger comparative case, effectively demonstrating that test-optional policies create a strategic ambiguity that disproportionately harms under-counseled applicants, while providing concrete, auditable safeguards against the misuse of test scores. ### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively exposing the gap between 'tests are sometimes useful' and 'tests must be universally mandated.' A's capitalization on B's concession regarding access-oriented campuses, combined with a sharp critique of B's inconsistent trust in admissions officers, proved decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's argument that B applies inconsistent faith in policy: trusting officers to flawlessly contextualize a socially patterned test score, while insisting they are incapable of neutrally evaluating a missing score under optionality.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): B won by holding the proposition’s real burden—compulsory reinstatement across all public campuses, not mere permission to consider scores—and never letting A close the gap from “scores can help” to “every applicant must supply one.” A’s strategic-disclosure attack and common-scale framing were sharp and memorable, but B consistently showed optionality already captures celebrated high scores, that no-adverse-inference guidance plus school-context review answers the blank-score problem better than a universal barrier, and that A’s own light-weighting concession for regional/access campuses defeats a blanket mandate. A’s clipped first rebuttal also cost some comparative force. Net: clearer live route to rejecting the motion for B after full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5/B5.3 turning A’s “weight lightly at access campuses” answer into a concession that a universal testing requirement is overbroad relative to mission diversity.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side A built a more compelling comparative case by exposing the practical flaws of test-optional policies. The argument that test-optional creates a "strategy game" where well-advised, affluent applicants selectively disclose scores while first-generation students inadvertently hide them was highly persuasive. Side A effectively dismantled Side B's defense that admissions officers can simply "promise not to infer weakness" from a missing score, pointing out that comparing a complete file to an incomplete one inherently advantages the former. While Side B made strong points about the unequal burdens of testing and the overbreadth of a universal mandate, Side A's framing of the test as a necessary "anchor" for contextual review and its sharp closing on "curating absence" secured the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in Rebuttal 2 to Side B's claim that officers can adopt an "explicit evidentiary rule" not to penalize missing scores. Side A sharply noted that "a policy statement cannot erase the practical fact that officers see strong scores from well-advised applicants and blanks from everyone else, and must still choose between them," effectively neutralizing Side B's primary defense of optionality.
