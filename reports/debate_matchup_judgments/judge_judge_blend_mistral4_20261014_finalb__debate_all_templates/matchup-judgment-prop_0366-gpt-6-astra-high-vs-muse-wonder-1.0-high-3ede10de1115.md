### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but CON more effectively tracked the actual burden set by the motion ("default for almost every big title"). CON's sharpest move was B2.1/B3.1, pressing PRO to admit that a "title-by-title" or "justify exclusivity" standard either leaves theatrical-first functionally unchanged (if studios can still invoke risk) or forces excluded viewers to bear the same cost whenever a title is chosen for exclusivity. PRO's A1 answer in Rebuttal 2 never cleanly resolved this: it redefined "default change" as a planning/justification requirement without showing this differs materially from existing case-by-case practice, effectively drifting toward CON's own "targeted simultaneous releases under theatrical-first" proposal. CON's closing then weaponized PRO's own concessions (universal access, substitution risk, exhibitors/studios absorbing losses) to argue PRO had conceded the cannibalization mechanism while failing to establish it was outweighed across "almost every" major title. PRO's accessibility case was emotionally and causally strong and never adequately rebutted on its own terms, but PRO's economic rebuttal stayed abstract ("that is precisely why differentiated strategy is stronger") rather than engaging CON's slate-wide fixed-cost and habit-shift mechanism. CON's structural argument, pressure questions, and closing synthesis gave the clearer, more decisive path to resolving the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q1 in Pressure Questions (B3.1), forcing PRO to clarify how a title-by-title justification standard actually reverses the default rather than replicating existing selective practice, which PRO's subsequent answer (A4.1) never fully resolved and which CON exploited in its final closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively linking the economic realities of the film industry to the accessibility goals Side B championed. Side A used the pressure questions to force Side B into conceding that their model would result in a cheaper substitute for universal audiences and that studios/exhibitors would have to absorb the losses. Side A decisively capitalized on this, arguing that you cannot determine systemic, habit-shifting harms on a title-by-title basis before the damage to theaters' fixed costs is already done.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal brilliantly crystallized Side B's concessions from the pressure round, pointing out that the parties Side B expects to absorb financial losses are the exact same entities funding the films and physical accessibility infrastructure in the first place.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing that the concrete, ongoing exclusion of disabled and medically vulnerable viewers from the cultural moment outweighs the speculative systemic harm of broadening simultaneous releases. While Side B effectively raised economic risks and cannibalization concerns, Side A successfully framed these as uncertain risks to be assessed title-by-title, not as automatic justifications for near-universal restriction. Side A also cleanly answered pressure questions, neutralized B's claim that theaters are always the more accessible venue, and crystallized the clash around burden of justification in the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal exposing the tension between Side A's call for a reversed default and its retreat to title-by-title differentiation, and Side A's sharp response that partial inclusion is still a meaningful improvement over routine exclusion and that studios can assess cumulative risk before release rather than assuming it.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A built a strong moral and burden-shifting case for day-and-date release, anchored in vivid accessibility examples and a "exclusion must justify itself" frame. But Side B's economic rebuttal—distinguishing studio revenue from exhibitor revenue, stressing the irreversibility of cinema closures versus a reversible delay, and refuting the sports-broadcast analogy by noting that broadcast money actually funds the sporting enterprise while a rental does not fund the theater—went largely unanswered in Rebuttal 2. B also executed a sharp late turn, showing that A's own hedge ("adjust the slate... where evidence shows") effectively conceded that economic viability is a prerequisite rather than an afterthought, undercutting A's "default should be inclusion" framing. A's pressure-question answers were honest but vague on thresholds, while B's parallel honesty about "no universal attendance percentage" was paired with a clearer causal mechanism (margin coverage, distributional mismatch). Overall B did more of the comparative work the later rounds were supposed to prioritize, leaving the decisive economic clash less rebutted by A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 point (B2.4) distinguishing broadcast revenue (which funds the sports enterprise) from home-rental revenue (which does not fund the theater losing customers) directly gutted A's recurring sports analogy, and A never returned to repair that analogy in Rebuttal 2, leaving a live hole in PRO's case.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively weaponizing Side B's concessions about cinema viability. Side A successfully proved that because exhibitor revenue sharing is merely a conditional promise, dismantling the theatrical window as the default risks permanently closing theaters, which outweighs the temporary 4-week cultural delay for home viewers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal decisively neutralized Side B's sports analogy and revenue-sharing proposals by pointing out that structural sustainability is a prerequisite to changing the default, not an afterthought.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A won by framing the clash as certain, repeated exclusion versus speculative economic harm, and by showing that delaying an already-ready accessible edition protects exclusivity rather than inclusion. While Side B raised a real distributional problem about cinema revenue, Side A answered it with concrete mitigations—exhibitor sharing, tailored exceptions, and additive audiences—while B could offer no threshold proving that expanding simultaneous release for more big titles would collapse venues. A's reversal of the burden of proof in the closing crystallized the decisive choice: participation should be the default, exclusion the exception.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal that unbundling accessibility from timing still forces disabled viewers to miss the opening-weekend cultural moment, undermining CON's claim that accessibility can be separated from simultaneous release.
