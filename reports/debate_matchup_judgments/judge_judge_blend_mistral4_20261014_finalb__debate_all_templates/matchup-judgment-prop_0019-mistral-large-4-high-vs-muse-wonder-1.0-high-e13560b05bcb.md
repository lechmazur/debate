### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won by isolating the missing link between use of a common tool and an agreement among competitors. Side A repeatedly assumed that shared algorithms expose deviations, enforce adherence, and pool confidential strategies, but those features are not inherent in every shared rent-setting tool. Once A limited its test to confidential pooling plus adherence discipline, B persuasively argued that A was requiring evidence of coordination itself rather than defending cartel-level scrutiny based on shared use. B also better weighed the costs of reversing the investigative burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange was its response that visibility is not agreement and ordinary competitive loss is not cartel retaliation, followed by the charge that A cannot invoke a cartel presumption to discover agreement and then use that agreement to justify the presumption.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's 'hub-and-spoke' analogy and successfully arguing that Side B abandoned the proposition's actual text. Side A demonstrated that Side B's definition of 'discipline' (rivals seeing a lower price and undercutting it) is just normal market competition, not cartel enforcement. Furthermore, Side A trapped Side B into admitting they would shift the burden of proof onto landlords just for using software, which Side A effectively weighed as an unacceptable cost for small businesses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Turn 8 (Side A Rebuttal 2) decisively won the debate by pointing out that Side B's own test (confidential pooling plus adherence discipline) conceded the core clash, shifting from 'scrutinize shared tools' to 'scrutinize tools that already prove coordination.'
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): CON successfully showed that PRO's functional-equivalence argument collapsed into requiring proof of the very elements of direct coordination (confidential pooling, commitment, punishment), which makes the categorical proposition either redundant or overbroad. By distinguishing vertical software purchases from horizontal agreements and exposing that PRO's narrowing test in Rebuttal 2 was effectively CON's own evidence-first standard, CON maintained a cleaner path to victory on the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4) turned PRO's attempt to narrow the debate into a decisive concession, demonstrating that once PRO required proof of confidential data pooling and adherence discipline, the proposition's category-based scrutiny became unnecessary because those facts already establish direct coordination without treating all shared-tool users as cartelists.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A won by consistently distinguishing scrutiny from automatic liability and showing why a shared hub using rivals’ private data can function like a common pricing agent. Side B’s scope objection was meaningful, but it repeatedly treated the proposition as categorical condemnation rather than an investigative trigger and overstated the claim that retained discretion necessarily defeats concerted action.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest answer was that requiring proof of a separate back-room agreement before scrutinizing the platform creates a software loophole: the relevant coordination may occur through pooled data, common recommendations, and the hub itself. This directly weakened Side B’s central agreement-requirement defense.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully distinguishing between 'scrutiny' and 'liability', pointing out that Side A's demand for proof of agreement before scrutiny is circular since the evidence lies within the software itself. B also decisively countered A's 'profitability' argument by explaining that holding units vacant is only profitable when the algorithm assures landlords that their rivals will do the same.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.4 effectively neutralizing A2.7 by pointing out that the independent profitability of leaving units empty relies entirely on the coordinated assurance that rivals won't undercut.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A established a strong functional equivalence between pooled-data algorithmic pricing and hub-and-spoke coordination, effectively neutralizing B's formalistic insistence on a "meeting of the minds" by showing the platform itself mediates the collective action. B spent too much time defending public-data tools that A's opening explicitly excluded, and failed to answer A's core economic argument that algorithmic assurance of rival behavior converts vacancies from competitive pressure into coordinated profit. A's pressure questions and direct A1/A2 responses sharpened the clash, while B's rebuttals grew repetitive and never fully engaged why the consultant analogy shouldn't apply to software.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal, where it answered B's pressure questions by explaining that the "as if" rule closes a software loophole rather than presuming guilt, and that retained discretion to cheat is also present in direct cartels—thus exposing the circularity of B's proof-before-scrutiny standard.
