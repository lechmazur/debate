### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by establishing that scarce curb space creates external costs and that dynamic prices change marginal behavior through consolidation, off-peak use, pooling, walking, transit, and remote staging. Side A raised meaningful equity, displacement, and implementation concerns, but often treated successful demand reduction as mere rationing and overstated the revenue tradeoff as a fatal contradiction. Its managed-free alternative still required substantial enforcement and offered a weaker account of why flat rules would respond to changing scarcity. The clipped first rebuttal was also a modest execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered the revenue “paradox” by making revenue secondary to clearing scarcity, then explained how ride-hail pricing can reduce curb footprints even without consolidating destinations. Side A’s later relabeling of those substitutions as rationing did not neutralize their congestion benefits.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A won by successfully turning Side B's own claims into vulnerabilities, especially regarding the shift of congestion to residential side streets and the 'revenue paradox.' Side A also provided a plausible mechanism for managed access that neutralized Side B's pressure on enforcement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 systematically dismantled Side B's answers from the pressure round and capitalized on key concessions to secure the win.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): CON (B) won the central weighing. PRO (A) established tragedy-of-the-commons and ticket-churning, but CON turned PRO's own concessions: calling a shift to side streets a "success metric" [A2.4] exports hazards to residential neighborhoods, and admitting revenue falls if pricing works [A4.1] undercuts the transit-funding promise [A1.4]. B's pressure questions (revenue paradox and ride-hail elasticity) exposed that tension, while B's alternative of targeted enforcement of bus/bike lanes plus flat/waivable permits directly answered A's surveillance-equivalence challenge. A never overcame the ability-to-pay vs need distinction. Clipping of B2.1 was an execution cost, but B's live route — managed free access preserves broad public access without auctioning the curb — remained less damaged.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's attack on A2.4/A4.1 in Rebuttal 2 and Closing — framing the "shift to side streets is success" as exporting externalities to neighborhoods with children/seniors and using A's revenue-falls admission to collapse the funding justification.
