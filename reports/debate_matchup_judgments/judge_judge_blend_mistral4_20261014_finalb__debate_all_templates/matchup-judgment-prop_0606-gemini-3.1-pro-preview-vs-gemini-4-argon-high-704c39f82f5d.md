### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won a competitive debate by showing that an automatic threshold may undermine confidential tiered pricing, fail to produce complex biologics, and weaken investment in high-risk therapies. Side B established the moral urgency and bargaining value of compulsory licensing, but relied too heavily on assertions that tiered global sales would cover development costs and that patent threats would induce voluntary transfer of proprietary know-how.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A most decisively answered the claim that licensing threats force cooperation: a patent override does not transfer cell lines, trade secrets, or manufacturing expertise, so the threat may be too weak to compel originators to create an effective competitor.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): A won by converting B’s abstract social-contract case into three live practical failures: transparent thresholds wreck confidential tiered pricing via reference-pricing spillover, patent-only CLs do not deliver biologic know-how, and B’s A1 concession (“the affordability cap stays”) openly accepts pipeline collapse when capped cumulative sales cannot fund high-risk R&D. B kept moral force and threat-as-leverage rhetoric, but never neutralized the empty-shelf comparison or the math clash A pressed through rebuttals and closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2/closing use of B’s “affordability cap stays” answer, tying it to abandonment of specialized R&D and empty shelves versus confidential access.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B wins decisively by exposing the mechanical and economic flaws in Side A's case. Side B effectively demonstrates that an automatic, transparent affordability threshold would destroy the confidential tiered pricing systems that currently facilitate global access, inevitably triggering international reference pricing and causing firms to delay or withhold launches. Furthermore, Side B highlights the practical limitations of compulsory licensing for complex biologics, which require unpatented trade secrets and tacit manufacturing know-how that a patent override does not provide. The debate is sealed in the later rounds when Side B forces Side A to concede that the affordability cap would remain even if it mathematically fails to cover the sunk R&D costs for specialized therapies with small patient populations. By accepting that the private model would "fail" under these conditions, Side A effectively concedes that the policy would collapse the innovation pipeline for the most critical modern medicines. Side A relied heavily on moral rhetoric and idealized market behavior but failed to solve these substantive logistical hurdles.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, which brilliantly capitalizes on Side A's Pressure Question answers. By highlighting Side A's explicit concession that "the affordability cap stays" even when cumulative global sales cannot cover R&D for specialized therapies, Side B proves that PRO's policy would intentionally collapse the research pipeline for complex cures. Side B also decisively refutes PRO's claim that the mere threat of a compulsory license would force originators to surrender proprietary biologic cell lines and trade secrets for modest royalties.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the cleaner economic comparison: prices above a genuine capacity-based threshold often yield exclusion and little revenue, whereas licensing preserves royalties, expands treatment, and pressures firms toward voluntary affordable pricing. Side A raised serious concerns about threshold manipulation, wealthy-country use, reference pricing, and R&amp;D incentives, but repeatedly escalated these into asserted inevitabilities such as total capital flight without establishing their magnitude. B also weighed present, certain access losses against the more speculative innovation harm and offered direct safeguards against abuse. Both sides incurred minor costs from incorrect reference IDs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered both pressure questions, explained why systemic metrics and review constrain arbitrary thresholds, and argued that wealthy countries’ higher capacity preserves substantial innovation revenue. A’s high-threshold/low-threshold dilemma was sharp, but B’s closing clarified that affordability concerns system-wide access rather than merely a nominally low price.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO built the cleaner live route: an automatic affordability trigger converts zero public revenue and blocked access into volume royalties plus immediate use, forces voluntary pricing beneath a transparent ceiling, and indexes by capacity so peer IRP among rich systems need not collapse funding. CON pressed hard on kill-switch incentives, patent risk, and reference-price contagion, but never fully neutralized the “no money that systems cannot pay cannot fund R&D” math or the HIV/HCV generics counterexample. PRO answered both pressure questions directly and used the closing to crystallize the same clash; CON’s strongest counter (high thresholds fail access / low thresholds kill R&D) was blunted by the systemic full-access ceiling framing. Clear but competitive edge to PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s A1/A2 and follow-on weighing (B4/B5): systemic cost-effectiveness ceilings plus high rich-country thresholds answer abuse and R&D survival while locking in that priced-out markets yield neither access nor trial funding.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by grounding the debate in the concrete mechanics of health economics and pharmaceutical procurement. A's core insight—that companies cannot extract R&D revenue from health budgets that literally lack the funds, and that wealthy nations already use cost-effectiveness thresholds to deny coverage (yielding zero revenue)—effectively neutralized B's abstract fears of capital flight. A's responses to pressure questions were exceptional, clarifying that thresholds rely on systemic metrics rather than arbitrary political whims, and that manufacturers will simply price at the top of a wealthy nation's threshold to retain exclusivity. While B made strong points about International Reference Pricing and the tension between high and low thresholds, B failed to overcome A's decisive framing that the status quo for unaffordable drugs is zero revenue and zero access, whereas the proposition guarantees maximum feasible revenue and universal access.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which resolved B's "generous threshold" contradiction by explaining that a high threshold represents the maximum a system can pay for universal access, allowing manufacturers to earn billions while solving the access problem, thus dismantling B's claim that high thresholds leave access unsolved.
