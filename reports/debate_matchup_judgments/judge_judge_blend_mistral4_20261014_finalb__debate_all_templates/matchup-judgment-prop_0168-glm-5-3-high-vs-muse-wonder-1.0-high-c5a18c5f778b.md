### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases — B's structural "science per dollar" efficiency argument versus A's political-economy rebuttal that efficiency gains mean little if the shift shrinks the total budget. The decisive moment was A's pressure question about historical crewed-program cuts: B answered honestly (A1: "the money largely left NASA") rather than dodging, but that honest answer handed A the ammunition for its strongest closing line — "divide your numerator by a shrinking denominator and the arithmetic stops mattering" — which synthesized the whole debate's stakes memorably. B's own pressure question (name a crew-dependent science task) exposed a real weakness in A's case, since A's answer leaned on a crewed Europa mission that is barely plausible, and B exploited this sharply in closing ("difficulty is not proof we need humans... they can attempt the hard places where no crew can follow at all"). That exchange was strong for B but did not fully neutralize A's budget-denominator argument, which remained the more structurally decisive and better-crystallized point by the end. A's rebuttal 2 was clipped near its final sentence, a real execution cost, but the substantive content had already landed before the cut. Overall, A's throughline from opening to closing was tighter and more clearly resolved the central clash, giving it a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure question about the post-Apollo/Shuttle "dividend" and B's honest A1 answer ("the money largely left NASA"), which A then converted in closing into the decisive "shrinking denominator" framing — the single clearest, most damaging exploitation of a live concession in the debate.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): A won by converting the live clash from pure science-per-dollar accounting into budget existence: B’s own answer that Apollo savings “largely left NASA” became A’s decisive weighing that the motion shrinks the denominator rather than multiplying discovery. B kept a strong structural efficiency case and a coherent lean-crew residual, but never neutralized that political-economy route; A’s drilling answer and closing crystallized why “most” as practiced risks enterprise decline rather than a reallocated fleet. Clipping cost A polish, not the comparative center.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2/Closing flip of B’s admission that post-Apollo money left NASA, turning science-per-dollar into fewer dollars for science.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won because it successfully undermined Side B's (PRO) solvency with a historical and structural argument about political budgets. When pressed, B conceded that past cuts to crewed programs caused money to leave NASA rather than shift to robotic missions. A turned this into a decisive "shrinking denominator" argument: the motion's science-per-dollar metric is meaningless if the total budget collapses, leaving agencies with less total science. A also effectively neutralized B's fallback of a "leaner crewed program" by showing it was the exact low-Earth-orbit holding pattern B had mocked. While B argued persuasively that robots are structurally cheaper per unit of science, A demonstrated that the proposed reallocation would not actually materialize in practice, making the motion self-defeating.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal and closing crystallized the debate by seizing B's concession that Apollo's savings "largely left NASA." A framed this as fatal to the motion: without crewed programs to anchor political support, exploration budgets wither, so shifting "most" funding to robots produces fewer total dollars for science rather than more science per dollar. This turn reframed the decisive question from cost efficiency to budget survival.
