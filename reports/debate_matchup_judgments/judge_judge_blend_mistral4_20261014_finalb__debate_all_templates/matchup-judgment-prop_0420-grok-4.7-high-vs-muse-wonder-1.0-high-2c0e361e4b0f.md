- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): A built a tighter, more coherent throughline: first-line contacts arrive unsorted, so the AI's gatekeeping decision is itself the risky work, and A pressed a genuine dilemma B never fully escaped—either the company trusts the AI's sorting (and misreads become silently "contained" losses) or it builds a parallel human/audit/override system to catch failures (which eats the claimed savings). B's safeguards (hard rules on "fraud language," non-refusable escape, audits, reopen tracking) were met head-on by A's point that customers don't use regulatory vocabulary and that audits catch failures only after trust is spent, and B's closing answer to this ("customers take the escape when the first answer fails") did not resolve the detection problem. B also suffered two format-noted clippings (opening and rebuttal 1), losing its weighing conclusion and part of its accountability rebuttal, a real execution cost. Both sides handled the pressure-question structure correctly, but A's questions (measuring true resolution vs. deflection; what triggers escalation besides the bot's own judgment) exposed the sharper vulnerability, and A's answers in Rebuttal 2 converted that into the decisive closing dilemma. B's case was substantive and well-argued but ultimately left the core trade-off (savings vs. a credible exception path) unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 point ([A4.3]-[A4.4]) that customers don't announce "fraud" or "regulated denial" in the words B's hard-rule safeguards rely on, paired with the dilemma that an easy, visible human escape undercuts the claimed deflection savings while a scarce one just hides failures until audit—this was never squarely neutralized by B's closing.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (A4), which effectively turned Side B's proposed safeguards (easy bypass, strict rules, audits) into a parallel, costly system that defeats the premise of the cost savings.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully framing first-line service as fundamentally a diagnostic and sorting task, rather than just answering pre-labeled routine queries. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently with real clash, but CON (A) built and sustained the more decisive structural argument: that the cost savings the motion requires depend on high containment (most contacts never reaching a human), while the trust/accountability protection PRO relies on depends on honest, well-staffed escalation — and these two requirements erode each other. A pressed this via a sharp pressure question (what containment rate is needed, and why would a firm paid to hit it keep escalation staffed?) that PRO never answered with more than "it need not approach total deflection" and "routine volume covers fixed costs," which A correctly noted substitutes "covering costs" for the proposition's actual claim. PRO's design-based defense of accountability (tool-calling bots can't freely promise, errors are bounded and fixable) was a reasonable rebuttal but didn't fully resolve the dilemma A posed about who absorbs a wrong promise before rollback. A also got the last word and used it to directly rebut PRO's closing counter (that clean routine resolution and honest escalation are compatible) by reiterating that escalation "scored to contain" will be thinned under the very economic logic PRO relies on. This made A's throughline sharper and left a cleaner, more memorable final reason to doubt the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure question "What containment rate must the bot hit for cost cuts to exceed losses... and why would a firm paid to hit that rate keep escalation staffed and visible?" was the pivotal exchange — PRO's answer (no single rate needed, routine volume "covers fixed costs") never squarely closed the gap, and A exploited that in Rebuttal 2 and the closing to argue the savings and the quality-protecting escalation cannot both be fully realized.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing human first-line support as a rigid, script-reading queue where agents lack the authority Side A attributes to them. Side A's strongest push—that savings intrinsically require understaffing the escalation tier—was effectively dismantled by Side B's simple arithmetic: automating the large volume of routine queries proportionately reduces staffing needs even if the escalation tier remains robust.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 effectively neutralized Side A's claim about human judgment by pointing out that front-line agents are timed, forbidden to grant exceptions, and punished for leaving scripts, making human deviation a source of inconsistency rather than a trust-building advantage.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by successfully dismantling CON's idealized view of first-line human support and defending the structural economics of AI replacement. PRO effectively neutralized CON's strongest point—the accountability dilemma—by explaining how API-constrained bots and bounded liability outperform off-script human errors. CON argued forcefully that cost savings inherently require underfunding the escalation tier, but PRO convincingly showed that the massive marginal cost savings of AI on routine volume more than fund a high-quality specialist tier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response to the accountability pressure question (B4.1), which cleanly resolved the dilemma by distinguishing between free-text hallucinations and API-constrained tool use, while pointing out that honoring a bounded bot error is still superior to disputing an unrecorded human promise.
