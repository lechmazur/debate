### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): The debate turned on whether a fixed 30% no-take acreage mandate is justified over tailored, enforceable conservation. CON consistently pressed the sharpest structural objection: acreage is not a biological metric, and PRO never established why 30% specifically (rather than well-placed, possibly smaller, permanently protected networks) is necessary or sufficient. PRO's strongest moves were the enforcement-simplicity argument and the "backstop against political capture" framing, but CON's rebuttals exposed real gaps: a clear boundary doesn't guarantee detection capacity, the 30% figure doesn't track where breeders or nurseries actually are, and the mosaic answer in Rebuttal 2 created tension with PRO's own earlier claim that small reserves leak fish via boundary-crossing. CON also kept returning to the livelihood asymmetry PRO never closed — PRO explicitly conceded in the pressure-question answers that the 30% obligation holds even without guaranteed transition funding, which CON repeatedly leveraged as the decisive cost PRO could not offset. PRO's closing restated the asymmetric-risk framing well rhetorically but didn't resolve the core demand: justify the specific percentage threshold rather than the general value of permanent refuges, which CON had already conceded could be valuable. CON's final speech crystallized this gap cleanly: "Protect breeding grounds because they need protection, not because a national map has yet to reach 30%." That is the single cleanest distillation of the central clash, and PRO never fully answered the demand for why 30% as a floor (versus well-designed smaller/targeted permanent protection) outperforms the alternative on a case-by-case basis. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions round competently with proper A1/A2 labeling, but CON's answers more directly exposed PRO's reliance on assumed-perfect boundary enforcement versus assumed-failing conventional management — an asymmetry CON named explicitly and PRO did not fully rebut.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.5) pointing out PRO's dilemma: PRO's mosaic defense (small coastal zones preserving access) contradicts PRO's earlier dismissal of smaller reserves as leaky and ineffective — this internal tension was never resolved by PRO's closing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won by holding the decisive burden: PRO had to justify a universal 30% no-take floor over tailored protection that already includes permanent refuges, not merely prove overfishing is dangerous. A kept that comparison live—percentage is not habitat quality, spillover and access are not guaranteed, management remains necessary in the remaining 70%, and acceptance without transition support is a real livelihood gamble. PRO’s collapse narrative and fail-safe framing were forceful, but never closed why 30% specifically is necessary across fisheries rather than asserted as a political and biological minimum.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 mosaic/additional-benefit turn: small coastal mosaics undercut the scale guarantee while large fixed acreage can still strip reachable productive grounds, so PRO’s extra cost is unearned.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): CON wins by exposing that PRO never bridges the gap between "no-take reserves help" and "at least 30% of every nation's EEZ is necessary." CON's sustained pressure on this specific threshold—showing that acreage does not guarantee placement, habitat protection, or enforcement—remains largely unanswered. PRO's best arguments (fail-safe against political capture, spillover, simpler enforcement) are blunted by CON's sharp rebuttal that closing 30% still leaves 70% to manage, that political capture also affects boundary-setting, and that CON's alternative already includes targeted permanent refuges without an arbitrary national floor. PRO's shifting stance on transition funding (from bridgeable subsidies to acceptance even without them) and its unresolved mosaic dilemma—where small reserves leak but 30% mosaics somehow protect village access—further weaken its livelihood defense. CON successfully reframes the debate as effective protection versus a rigid percentage, and this framing endures through the final exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question and subsequent rebuttal exposing the mosaic dilemma: PRO cannot simultaneously claim small reserves fail because fish cross boundaries and assert that a 30% mosaic preserves village access, while CON additionally highlights that productive, reachable grounds are not interchangeable hectares, devastating PRO's "70% open means 70% income preserved" assumption.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built a tighter, more specific case by hammering the exact fiscal capacity problem the motion itself stipulates: nations where communities "depend heavily" on fishing are precisely those least able to fund the compensation PRO's model requires. When pressed, PRO conceded that unfunded closures should be "postponed," which A correctly exploited in Rebuttal 2 and Closing as a dilemma — either the 30% mandate is symbolic and unenforced in exactly the hardest cases, or it is forced through and devastates families, contradicting PRO's own safeguards. A also landed a sharp internal-contradiction hit (promising "nearby access" while simultaneously demanding effort reductions in the remaining 70%), which PRO never fully resolved, only clarifying that some fishers "will fish less." PRO's case stayed coherent and well-structured (30% as a floor, not uniform exclusion; insurance against estimation error), but it relied on abstract reassurances ("funded phase-in," "prioritize small-scale access") that A's pressure question exposed as unworkable under the motion's own premise. A's rhetoric (“fiscal fantasy,” “wrecking ball vs. scalpel”) was also more vivid and made the stakes easier to grasp. PRO's closing restated its insurance framing without squarely closing the postponement loophole, leaving CON's decisive objection live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question (A3.1) asking what happens when a nation lacks funds for compensation, and PRO's answer that the closure must simply be "postponed" (B4.1) — which A then used in Rebuttal 2/Closing to show the mandate either fails to protect anything or forces impoverishment exactly where the motion says dependence is highest.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built the cleaner path to victory on the motion’s hardest clause—“even when coastal communities depend heavily.” It forced PRO to admit permanent exits, lasting assistance, and postpone-when-unfunded phase-ins, then showed that this either freezes the 30% mandate in the developing coastal states the proposition most concerns or manufactures unfunded job loss. PRO’s insurance/refuge case remained live and respectable, but it never neutralized the fiscal-livelihood trap or showed why a rigid no-take floor beats targeted effort/gear/size tools once both sides accept effort cuts. CON’s pressure answers and closings crystallized that comparison more decisively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 / Closing conversion of B’s “postpone unfunded closures / lasting assistance / external help” answers into a collapse of the mandate under heavy dependence—either indefinite delay or impoverishment—while noting B’s remaining-access fixes are CON-style management without the lockout.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won by showing that PRO's 30% no-take mandate fails precisely under the motion's own condition of heavy coastal dependence. CON exploited PRO's concession that unfunded closures must be postponed, demonstrating that in the developing nations where dependence is greatest, the policy either freezes into indefinite inaction or forces impoverishment. CON also revealed that PRO's effort-reduction defense collapses into CON's own targeted-management approach, making the 30% lockout a superfluous, fiscally impossible wrecking ball. PRO's insurance argument remained theoretically live but was materially undermined by CON's demonstration that the "premium" is unaffordable and the policy evades its own mandate where it matters most.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal turning PRO's admission that unfunded closures should be postponed into proof that the 30% mandate is either hollow or catastrophic for the very dependent communities the motion centrally addresses.
