### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built the tighter case: the structural-asymmetry opening was clear, and critically, PRO's pressure question ("if a sponsor still prefers silence, what consequence remains—and how is that not market access conditioned on disclosure?") exposed a real tension in CON's alternative, since CON had earlier spoken of "publication requirements tied to approval" and only later retreated to "fines, not access denial." CON's rebuttal answered the format correctly but the retreat looked like a concession under pressure, and PRO's closing exploited it by arguing that fines are a survivable "toll" that won't deter hiding the most damaging results. PRO also directly answered CON's strongest question (why does disclosure matter if the regulator already has the file) by distinguishing the regulator's private review from what prescribers and guideline-writers actually see — a clean, undamaged mechanism CON never fully rebutted (CON's antibiotic example assumed naive payer reaction to a bare missed endpoint, ignoring PRO's earlier point that disclosure requires contextual reporting, not failure-labeling). CON's case on jurisdiction-shifting and definitional ambiguity was reasonable but PRO's answer (agency-adjudicated completeness, outcomes reported as estimates not verdicts) neutralized much of it. PRO's Rebuttal 1 being clipped for length is a real execution cost, but it did not prevent PRO from recovering and sharpening its case in the following rounds, while CON's position suffered a more damaging substantive wobble on enforcement mechanics.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question Q1 ("what consequence remains—and how is that not market access conditioned on disclosure?") combined with CON's subsequent answer retreating from "tied to approval" to "fines, not access denial," which PRO then used in its closing to argue CON's alternative collapses into a toothless toll.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly without fake sourcing, but A built the more durable case. A's core move—that regulators already see the full dataset before approval, so the label and post-market tools already encode the "negative" findings—forced B to locate the real harm somewhere else (journal/citation bias), and A then correctly noted that an entry-gate disclosure requirement does nothing to fix journal selectivity or post-launch burial, which is where B itself admitted much of the distortion occurs. A also exploited the motion's central ambiguity: when pressed to define "negative" sharply, B's answer ("disclosure precedes approval, full stop") never supplied a workable line, letting A's delay/litigation argument stand largely unanswered. B's framing ("market access is a privilege, not a right," "price of admission") was rhetorically strong and the sovereignty point was never fully neutralized, but B did not adequately answer A's point that even a compliant sponsor can be delayed by rival objections or scope disputes under a vague standard, nor did B explain why fines/promotion bans targeting the actual bad actor (the under-reporter) are insufficient compared to withholding an already-vetted medicine from patients. A's closing crystallized this cleanly: a cleared drug being held hostage to an undefined disclosure dump is a cost the motion cannot justify given that the underlying problem (journal bias) survives the gate anyway.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's repeated pressure on "what does an unfiltered public post uniquely prevent if the label is already accurate" (A3.2, A4.6) combined with exposing that B never sharpened "negative" beyond "full stop" (A4.6) was the most damaging exchange, since it attacked both the mechanism and the definitional core of B's proposal without B ever supplying a concrete rebuttal.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing that confidential regulatory review does not correct the publicly distorted evidence base used by physicians, guideline writers, and researchers. Side B made the sharper rhetorical case about delay and patient leverage, but treated disclosure as an undefined data dump despite A identifying existing registry and endpoint frameworks. Its alternatives also did not match the pre-market timing or enforcement leverage of the proposed condition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest response was that an accurate label cannot substitute for public access to failed trials: prescribing, meta-analysis, off-label use, and future research depend on the broader evidence record. Side B’s claim that journals may remain selective did not show that accessible registry results would be useless.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): CON won by keeping focus on execution costs the PRO never cured: regulators already see the full file so the label is accurate, "negative" remains undefined beyond "disclosure precedes approval", and post-launch publication bias cannot be policed by a pre-market gate. A showed narrower alternatives (scaled fines, promotion bans) punish late reporters without withholding a vetted drug, turning B's leverage argument into patient-as-hostage. PRO's literature-distortion point stayed live but was not tied to a unique error the gate prevents.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's paired pressure and second rebuttal that B's answer "disclosure precedes approval, full stop" [B4.1] abandons defining "negative" and that calling state-created delay "voluntary" launders the design [A2.5 / A4.3-A4.5], leaving the definition and delay clashes unanswered.
