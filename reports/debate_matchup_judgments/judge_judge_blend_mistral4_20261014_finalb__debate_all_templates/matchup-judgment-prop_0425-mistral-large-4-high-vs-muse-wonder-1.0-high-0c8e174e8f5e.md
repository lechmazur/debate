### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) built a more rigorous and sustained equity-and-reach argument: commuters, night-shift workers, disabled and anxious students cannot be reached by physical-first spending no matter how fast it is built, while a digital platform can reach them asynchronously and reveal who is missing before physical infrastructure is designed. Crucially, A's point that "no design tweak lets a parent working nights be in two places at once" (A2.2) was never squarely answered by B, who kept retreating to "we can design lounges with quiet corners and evening hours," which doesn't solve simultaneous-presence impossibility. A also directly answered B's pressure questions (phones/asynchronous tools vs. need for private rooms) while B's answers to A's pressure questions mostly reasserted the "construction is fast" claim without resolving the structural-exclusion problem. B's economic appreciation/depreciation framing was repeated but never engaged with A's counter that physical spaces carry recurring maintenance/staffing costs and sit empty much of the time. A's closing crystallized the clash cleanly ("who gets counted before presence is funded"), giving the cleaner decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer in Rebuttal 2 (A4.2–A4.4) directly rebutting B's Q2 about digital access barriers by noting commuter lounges still require travel and childcare while phone-based asynchronous tools do not, combined with A2.2's unanswered "two places at once" point, which B never neutralized in later speeches.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): A won the decisive ordering clash. The motion is sequencing under scarcity, and A showed that physical-first systematically counts only students already able to appear, turning “prevention” into selection of the available. A’s bridge-by-design replies (matching, named hosts, check-ins, async phone use) answered B’s substitute charge more concretely than B answered irreducible non-presence (night parents, immunocompromised, first-entry anxiety). B’s destination and mission rhetoric stayed memorable but treated access as secondary and never neutralized the who-gets-counted burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1/A4.6: digital can route toward embodied contact and notice missed check-ins; a lounge cannot route itself to people who never enter and only “notices” after repeated attendance they cannot yet give.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing the selection bias in funding physical spaces first: they naturally serve students already able to show up, while digital platforms offer universal, asynchronous reach and generate data to inform inclusive physical investment later. B decisively reversed A's accessibility critique by showing that async, text-based digital support runs on phones students already use, undermining the claim that digital exclusion is comparable to physical barriers. A's "waiting room" frame was rhetorically powerful but could not overcome B's argument that a destination no one can reach is not a commons, and that building physical-first amounts to selection masquerading as prevention.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1 and B4.2 in Rebuttal 2, where B answered A's pressure questions by detailing concrete bridge mechanisms (interest matching, named hosts, check-ins) that convert digital contact to physical attendance, and dismantled the digital-exclusion argument by noting that asynchronous support runs on existing phones without requiring private quiet space, directly reversing A's accessibility frame.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built real cases, but B's mechanism was tighter and more directly responsive to the motion's actual "before" sequencing question: the opt-in problem (digital requires self-initiation, exactly where need is greatest), repetition-based belonging, and the scarcity/reversibility argument (lost physical culture is hard to rebuild; digital features can be "added by contract next term") were never fully dismantled. A's equity case (disabled, working, international, abused students excluded from physical spaces) was genuinely strong and persistently pressed, but A's key cost-efficiency rebuttal got clipped by the word limit (an execution cost), and A's answer to B's sharp pressure question about the anonymity-vs-monitoring contradiction ("multiple doors") was plausible but not fully convincing, leaving a live tension in A's case. B also successfully reframed "first ≠ only" to show physical prioritization doesn't abandon existing digital access, while A's "sequencing is arbitrary" rejoinder didn't fully neutralize B's reversibility logic, which B's closing reiterated as the uncontested last word on that specific clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question exposing the contradiction between A's claim of untracked, anonymous digital access and A's later claim that platforms can "see a student drift, reach out privately" — a tension A addressed but never fully resolved — combined with B's structural turn that digital features are cheap to add later while physical community, once lost, is not.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): B won by locking the motion as a next-pound sequencing question, not a total replacement of digital care. The belonging/isolation causal story, the “coordinator needs something real,” irreversibility of lost rooms/clubs, and contact-vs-care weighing stayed live end-to-end. A’s inclusion, anonymity, and scale case created real pressure, but too often collapsed expansion of engagement platforms into existing crisis counseling, undercut itself on privacy-vs-monitoring, and lost comparative force after the clipped first rebuttal and weaker conversion defense. B answered pressure more cleanly and made the decisive choice clearer: apps can be added later; hollowed community cannot.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.6/B2.1 coordinator trap plus B3.1 anonymity-vs-monitoring pressure: forced A to defend both untracked privacy and drift-detection outreach, while reframing digital as amplifier of an emptying campus.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A built the stronger path to victory by sustaining a clear comparative story: physical infrastructure is the scarce, hard-to-restore foundation of community, while the digital layer can be added by contract later. A’s reframing that universities already maintain crisis portals and counselling pages—so the question is the next marginal pound, not a lifeline switch-off—deflated B’s ethical urgency, and A derived a damaging turn from B’s own concession that digital tools coordinate rather than substitute for physical community. B’s inclusion narrative was powerful but struggled to resolve A’s pressure on the tension between anonymity and monitored engagement, and B’s clipped first rebuttal left key points under-addressed. Both sides were rhetorically sharp, but A crystallized the decisive clash more effectively: belonging grows from repeated, unpressured contact in place, and once that culture is hollowed out, digital tools only perfect a doorway into an empty room.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s first rebuttal (A2.1–A2.6) was the most decisive exchange: it narrowed the motion to the next discretionary pound by showing that basic digital services already exist, exposed the “contact versus care” flaw in B’s scale argument, and converted B’s coordination point into a reason to fund physical spaces first, since a coordinator needs an actual community to coordinate.
