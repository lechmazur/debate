### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases, but PRO (A) sustained a sharper comparative frame throughout: it repeatedly distinguished merger-specific efficiencies that require consolidation from efficiencies merely claimed, and in Rebuttal 2/Closing it offered a concrete evidentiary standard (operating plans, bottleneck analysis, weighing against competitive harm) that mirrors real antitrust practice without relying on fake citations. CON (B) landed good mechanisms (double marginalization, marginal-cost synergies, acquisition exits funding startups) but leaned on asserting efficiencies as "certain" and "textbook," which PRO exploited effectively. The pivotal exchange was A's Q1 on entry barriers: B's answer (barriers are unchanged regardless of the merger) never actually identified what constrains the merged firm's pricing post-merger, instead reframing the comparison — a partial dodge that PRO exploited cleanly in Closing by distinguishing "barriers to entry" from "loss of an existing competitive constraint." PRO's answers to B's pressure questions were more directly responsive, including an honest concession (alternatives like licensing "will not always suffice") that strengthened rather than weakened its credibility. Both closings crystallized the clash well, but PRO's framing of the asymmetry (hard-to-rebuild rivalry vs. speculative efficiencies) proved more resilient to the live objections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question on entry barriers (Q1) and B's comparative-but-non-direct answer in Rebuttal 2, which PRO then exploited in Closing by separating "unchanged entry barriers" from "loss of an existing competitive constraint" — this exchange most clearly exposed the weak link in CON's case.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by sustaining the asymmetric framing that a blocked merger preserves independent decision-making and actual competitive constraints, while an approved merger permanently removes a rival. Side A’s efficiency claims were plausible but treated too many savings as certain pass-throughs, and its "entry barriers exist either way" defense was squarely answered by B’s closing insight that an existing rival is an actual constraint that disappears under consolidation. B’s pressure questions exposed the vulnerability in A’s entry story, and B’s closing crystallized the decisive clash more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s closing reframing that "an existing rival is a constraint" directly undermined CON’s defense that entry barriers persist regardless, showing that lost rivalry is permanent while blocked mergers still permit investment and contractual cooperation.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A demonstrated superior economic reasoning, particularly in distinguishing between fixed and marginal cost savings and explaining how reduced competitive constraints lead to higher markups regardless of entry barriers. Side A effectively exploited Side B's weak dismissal of high entry barriers as a mere "regulatory problem," cementing a decisive advantage on the core pricing clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's "regulatory problem" defense regarding entry barriers, sharply pointing out that an existing rival provides an immediate competitive constraint that hypothetical future entrants cannot replace while consumers bear the brunt of higher markups.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by consistently forcing the debate back to the motion's actual comparative burden—whether the *additional* deals a stricter standard would block are net harmful—rather than letting PRO rest on vivid but generic examples (killer acquisitions, airline consolidation) that don't establish the marginal case. B introduced a genuine economic mechanism (double marginalization, hold-up/specialized-investment problems in vertical deals) that A never substantively rebutted beyond asserting it was "narrow" and "seldom materializes." Crucially, B's reversibility critique—that blocked deals aren't costlessly replaced by licensing/JVs because of renegotiation and specialized-investment risk ("missed investment can have lasting costs too")—directly undercut PRO's central asymmetry-of-error argument, and PRO's closing simply restated that asymmetry without answering the rebuttal. B also showed strong epistemic discipline by openly admitting it could not substantiate an industry-wide causal claim in the pressure-question exchange rather than overclaiming, which is exactly the kind of honest calibration the judging criteria reward. PRO's case was coherent and rhetorically forceful but grew repetitive across rounds and left the decisive late clash (reversibility of blocking errors) unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.4-B4.6), arguing that "losing an independent owner is observable; losing effective competitive pressure depends on what that firm could actually accomplish alone" and that blocking errors are not cheaply reversible via licensing/JVs, directly dismantled PRO's asymmetry-of-error framework, which PRO's closing failed to re-engage.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing PRO never established the critical marginal comparison: that blocking additional horizontal and vertical deals beyond current levels would produce net benefits. CON deployed concrete economic mechanisms—double marginalization, specialized investment holdup problems, and complementary capacity gains—that gave a plausible account of real costs from stricter blocking. It successfully undermined PRO's asymmetry-of-error framing by demonstrating that divestiture and joint ventures are imperfect substitutes for ownership, and that preserving a firm's independence does not guarantee it can finance production or constrain incumbents. PRO's reliance on past industry examples of consolidation coinciding with price increases did not establish that the next blocked deal would be harmful rather than efficiency-enhancing, and its "killer acquisition" point was narrow enough to support targeted review without justifying broader prohibition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (Turn 8), where it directly answered PRO's pressure questions by naming concrete vulnerable deal classes (manufacturers with complementary plants, vertical combos requiring specialized investment) and exposed that PRO treated uncertain futures asymmetrically—demanding ex ante proof of efficiencies while treating preserved independence as a guaranteed competitive benefit.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully dismantling Side A's core weighing mechanism—the asymmetry of error. While Side A argued that blocking beneficial mergers is easily remedied by contracts or joint ventures, Side B introduced the transaction cost and hold-up problem regarding specialized investments, showing that blocking a vertical deal could permanently kill an innovation. Side A failed to adequately address this specific economic mechanism in later speeches, relying instead on repeating the "killer acquisition" point, which Side B had already conceded should be blocked but argued didn't justify a broader prohibition. Side B's epistemic discipline and sharper economic reasoning secured the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they directly attacked Side A's claim that false positives are easily reversible via contracts, pointing out that contracts cannot solve the hold-up problem for specialized investments, meaning a blocked merger could permanently prevent an innovation from reaching consumers.
