### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): A won a competitive debate by defending the motion as a targeted, incremental intervention rather than a complete housing solution. B persuasively raised substitution, rental access, and better-targeted alternatives, but repeatedly treated failure to guarantee a financed buyer wins each house as failure of the policy. A more plausibly showed that removing a systematic, permanent, high-scale bidder can cumulatively improve local buyers’ odds even if some cash competitors remain. B’s claimed construction harm was also weakened by A’s explicit exemption for building new homes, while B’s two clipped turns added an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s answer to the landlord-substitution attack was decisive: flippers generally return homes to the ownership market, smaller landlords lack institutional scale, and the policy needs to improve aggregate odds rather than guarantee one buyer one house. B asserted that another cash bidder would simply replace the fund but never established that substitution would be complete.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): CON won the decisive mechanism clash: even after institutions exit, sellers still take the next clean cash offer, so the ban mostly reshuffles landlords or flippers rather than delivering existing homes to financed local buyers. PRO’s ownership/equity frame and “different in kind” permanent-hold story were strong and memorable, but PRO’s own concessions (remaining cash buyers; some homes stay rented; “marginal/cumulative” shift) left the motion failing its own crowd-out test. CON’s alternatives (first-look, down-payment/appraisal support) attacked the stated cash/speed handicap more cleanly and across all cash bidders. Clipped CON turns were an execution cost, but did not reverse the comparative outcome.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s sustained “next cash buyer still wins / landlord reshuffle” line, crystallized in A2 and A5 against PRO’s B2.6 and B4.3 concessions that flippers and small landlords remain and often keep the home rented.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by decisively proving that banning institutional investors merely swaps them for other cash buyers (flippers, wealthy individuals, small landlords), leaving the local financed buyer still crowded out. Side A also brilliantly resolves the tension in Side B's case by distinguishing between the existing home market (where institutions are marginal, so a ban doesn't lower prices much) and the build-to-rent exit market (where they are dominant, so a ban hurts new supply). Side A's policy alternatives (first-look windows, down-payment help) directly solve the cash-speed disadvantage without the collateral damage of Side B's ban. Side B's arguments were solid but failed to overcome the "next cash buyer" problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it answers the pressure questions by distinguishing the existing market from the build-to-rent exit market, and introduces first-look windows as a superior alternative that actually puts financed families ahead of all cash buyers.
