### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by distinguishing selective tunneling in genuinely constrained cities from the motion's broader demand for heavy national investment. A persuasively exposed hidden surface costs and explained why steady pipelines can reduce tunneling costs, but too often assumed that the same underground capacity was inevitably required. B supplied phased surface alternatives, emphasized opportunity cost and irreversibility, and showed that A's waiting argument only works after necessity has already been established.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's final response to A's central question was decisive: asking whether waiting makes a tunnel costlier presupposes that the tunnel must eventually be built. B preserved a live alternative of exhausting adaptable surface measures first, which A's claim that reallocated road space creates no new capacity did not adequately defeat.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO kept the decisive clash on the full ledger: capacity must be built somewhere, surface corridors carry land/demolition/litigation/forever-trenching costs, and waiting while land fills in raises the eventual tunnel bill. CON scored real hits on irreversibility, opportunity cost, and white elephants (Cincinnati/Rochester) and answered pressure cleanly, but its surface “alternatives” often reallocated already-scarce right-of-way rather than creating new capacity, and its “selective only when impossible” standard conceded the necessity path while merely inserting costly delay. PRO’s sustained comparison therefore resolved the motion more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s second-rebuttal/closing chain: CON’s priced surface answer (BRT/existing ROW) is reallocation of a full surface, not new capacity; Cincinnati’s empty tubes illustrate stop-start abandonment, not the failure of steady investment; waiting therefore makes the same hole dearer.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): PRO won by controlling the comparative frame. The core clash was whether tunnels are expensive in absolute terms or cheaper than the full cost of surface expansion in dense cities. PRO consistently forced CON to price the surface alternative, and CON never produced a priced corridor that beat the tunnel on a full-ledger comparison. CON's fiscal "tunnels versus hospitals" frame was rhetorically sharp but analytically hollow because capacity still had to be built somewhere, and PRO showed that waiting only raises land costs and makes future tunneling more expensive. CON's strongest counter—abandoned tunnels as white elephants—was turned by PRO in the closing: Cincinnati's stranded tubes were victims of stop-start funding, proving PRO's learning-curve argument rather than refuting it. CON's retreat to "selective tunneling" in the closing effectively conceded the necessity PRO defended, while failing to rebut PRO's claim that delay itself drives the high costs CON feared.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 turned CON's flooding examples against it by noting that New York's subway reopened within days of Sandy while overhead grids took far longer, and that Tokyo's storm tunnels are themselves a tunnel solution to flooding. PRO's Closing then neutralized CON's Cincinnati/Rochester examples by framing them as funding-collapse victims that vindicate PRO's stop-start thesis rather than proving tunnel obsolescence.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing that Side A's necessity test confines tunneling to exceptional saturated corridors, which does not adequately justify heavy country-level investment. B also supplied cheaper capacity alternatives and directly challenged the utility case. A established that tunnels can be valuable in dense cities, but did not prove that elevated rail, busways, reallocation, and shallow trenchless methods generally fail before costly boring becomes necessary. B's clipped rebuttal and erroneous reference labels reduced its margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's decisive response was that A's capacity-saturation threshold proves selective last-resort tunneling rather than the broad, increasingly necessary investment priority in the motion. Pairing that point with elevated rail and busway alternatives undercut A's claim that downward construction is the only remaining route to capacity.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): CON won the decisive clash on the motion as written. PRO’s best path was megacity capacity ceilings and finite-vs-forever disruption costs, but under pressure PRO redefined “heavily” and “increasingly necessary” into a last-resort saturation test that almost no corridor meets and that mostly restated CON’s selective-bore policy. CON consistently beat the capacity comparison with elevated rail/busway grade separation and cheaper trenchless utilities, held the opportunity-cost exchange rate, and crystallised that PRO had proved the exception while the proposition demanded the rule. A minor clip on A’s Rebuttal 2 and PRO’s still-plausible lifecycle framing keep it competitive rather than dominant.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 1 and later close: treating PRO’s “corridors where the surface has failed / absolute saturation” threshold as a concession that collapses “invest heavily” into rare selectivity.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): CON won by demonstrating that PRO's own test for tunneling necessity—capacity saturation so severe that no reallocation or elevated alternative is possible—applies only to rare exceptions, which contradicts the motion's claim that heavy investment is "increasingly necessary" as a national priority. CON sustained a devastating opportunity-cost frame, showing that cheaper grade-separated alternatives (elevated rail, busway) deliver capacity without the brutal boring premium, and that preferring tunnels because elevated options have noise or shadow costs is aesthetic preference, not geometric necessity. PRO never recovered from the selectivity trap, offered weak responses on resilience (the "obsolete standards" retreat crumbled against Zhengzhou), and failed to prove that deep utility tunneling beats cheap trenchless methods for retrofits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's turn in Rebuttal 1 and the Closing that leveraged PRO's "capacity saturation" threshold [A4.1] against the motion, crystallized as "PRO proved the exception; the motion requires the rule" [B5.2]. By showing that almost no corridor clears PRO's bar, CON transformed PRO's apparent concession into a decisive refutation of "increasingly necessary" heavy national investment.
