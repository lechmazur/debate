### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently and stayed disciplined about evidence, but B built a more concrete affirmative mechanism (zoning illegality plus rent-driven market failure against lingering) and used it to press a real tension in A's position: A supported funding/zoning for "existing" libraries and parks while resisting new civic-space creation, even though both require the same land reservation, budget allocation, and official judgment A claimed to fear. A's best answer—"known use" versus "predicted demand"—was a real distinction, but A then conceded in A4.2 that a new facility "might win case-by-case" in an underserved suburb, which functionally grants exactly the kind of targeted zoning/funding B was arguing for, undercutting A's claim to have rejected the "revival mandate" wholesale. B's closing correctly seized on this as a concession of the foundation of its case, and A's closing restated the case-by-case framing without fully re-establishing why it is principled rather than a semantic retreat. A's skepticism about causation and the "elastic category" favoritism worry were solid and never fully erased, keeping this competitive, but B's sharper pressure question, more vivid mechanism, and cleaner exploitation of A's inconsistency gave it the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure questions (B3.1–B3.2) exposing that A's support for funding "existing" libraries/parks logically required the same land/budget judgments A rejected for "reviving" new third places, followed by B4.3's direct callback when A's answer (A4.2) conceded new facilities "might win case-by-case."
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON won by keeping the decisive burden live: PRO had to show not only that some venues are valuable or that markets price out lingering, but that a broad third-place “revival” presumption beats barrier removal plus case-by-case public services. CON repeatedly separated deregulation/maintenance from mandated reservation and subsidy, treated causation as unresolved, and blocked PRO’s late attempt to rebrand ordinary library/park support as full concession. PRO’s market-failure and zoning points were forceful and forced real concessions on tools, but never cleanly proved systemic decline or planner superiority as a class.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1/B4.2: collapsing parks-and-permission into a revival mandate vs requiring comparative, case-by-case justification.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON successfully maintained that while specific venues may deserve support, PRO failed to prove that third places as a category have declined enough to justify a broad government-led revival mandate, and better crystallized the debate around whether policy should presume that designated spaces manufacture belonging.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's untangling of PRO's attempt to collapse maintenance of libraries and deregulation into a concession on active revival (B2.1, B4.2), exposing the coherent distinction between targeted intervention and blanket mandate.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON built a tighter, more falsifiable causal story: the historical parallel of free or cheap non-commercial third places (malls for low-spending teens, church basements, fraternal lodges) declining alongside commercial ones undercuts PRO's claim that market failure/zoning, rather than a demand-side cultural shift, is the primary driver. PRO's pressure answer (A2) conceded multiple causes and fell back on asserting "unmet need" despite revealed behavior, which is a weaker, less falsifiable move than CON's concrete analogy. On the housing-tradeoff clash, CON's rebuttal that density-bonus courtyards/rooftops are private residential amenities rather than genuine third places (A4.3) was a sharp, specific objection that PRO's closing never directly answered, leaving a live unrebutted point. CON also successfully forced PRO to narrow its "active funding and zoning" agenda down to largely uncontroversial housekeeping (maintaining libraries/parks, removing bad zoning), which CON then used effectively in the closing to frame the live disagreement as only the costly, riskier elements (mandates and subsidies) that CON had already damaged. PRO's case remained coherent and theoretically grounded (externalities, public goods) but relied more on assertion in the decisive exchanges and left key critiques unanswered in its closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure question (A3.2) asking why non-commercial spaces like lodges and churches also declined, and the follow-up in A4.1/A4.4 reframing this as a demand-side cultural shift rather than market failure, was the most damaging exchange; PRO's answer (B4.2) partially conceded multiple causes without fully recovering its original causal narrative.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): CON won by forcing the decisive distinction: cultural/digital substitution plus simultaneous decline of free venues undercut a pure shortage story, while “active” fund-and-zone demands beyond baseline maintenance and deregulation carry real housing and patronage costs that PRO never neutralized. PRO kept a live public-goods frame and flexible design answers, but those did not restore the original breadth of the motion once CON pinned the retreat to housekeeping.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2/closing trap on density-bonus courtyards still costing housing floor-area and on “pilot/sunset” subsidies as admission of guesswork while parks already go neglected.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side A wins because it successfully resisted Side B’s attempt to narrow the motion to exclude maintenance of existing public spaces. Restoring library hours, repairing parks, and removing zoning barriers are active funding and zoning for revival when decline has made them unavailable, and Side A showed this was always part of its plan rather than a retreat. Side B’s strongest objections—the housing tradeoff and fiscal scarcity—were plausibly mitigated by Side A’s emphasis on site-sensitive design, density bonuses, and pilot programs. While Side B’s cultural-shift argument created real pressure, Side A demonstrated that residual unmet need persists for teenagers, low-income residents, and local coordination in ways digital life does not replace, giving a cleaner affirmative path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s turning of Side B’s “under-maintained parks and libraries” point in Rebuttal 1 and the elaboration in Rebuttal 2 that restoring declining public facilities is itself active revival. This exposed Side B’s semantic narrowing of the motion and dissolved the fiscal-scarcity objection by showing that the proposition naturally covers funding the very spaces Side B admitted were struggling.
