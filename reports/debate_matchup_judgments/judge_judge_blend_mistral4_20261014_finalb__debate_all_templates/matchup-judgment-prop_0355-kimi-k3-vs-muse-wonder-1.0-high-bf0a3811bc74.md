### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but CON (A) landed the more decisive sustained attack. A's "control follows risk" and "panel capacity is a commons" frames were pressed consistently and sharpened across rounds, culminating in A4.3's capture of PRO's own concession (B4.1: "the next tenant waits, joins a neutral queue, or funds an approved upgrade") as proof that PRO's right collapses exactly when capacity binds, and that every proposed safeguard (load-sharing systems, insurance audits, queues, mediators) quietly converts the landlord into an unpaid operator and long-term risk-bearer rather than a mere permission-giver. PRO's "tenant pays" framing never fully recovered from this, since the machinery needed to make the right workable was conceded to run through landlord administration and liability. PRO's rebuttals were solid (capacity exceptions, inspection standards, deadline/queue mechanisms) and its closing crisply reframed the issue as "must a no be earned," but it never neutralized the core commons/liability-transfer critique, leaving CON with the cleaner, more damaging comparative story by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3's response to B4.1: pointing out that PRO's own fix for capacity exhaustion (queue/upgrade funding) proves the "right" evaporates precisely when it matters and turns the landlord into the program's administrator, undercutting the "tenant pays everything" premise.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by defending a bounded presumption rather than an unconditional entitlement: tenant payment, licensed installation, inspection, insurance, landlord-imposed conditions, and genuine safety or capacity exceptions. Side B compellingly identified shared-capacity and administrative burdens, but its claim that ownership should permit refusal indefinitely was too absolute and did not justify silence or unsupported rejection when the installation is demonstrably safe. A ultimately made the motion easier to decide: a no must rest on the building’s facts rather than title alone.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest response was that actual exhaustion of capacity defeats an installation, while hypothetical future use does not automatically defeat a safe present request. Its direct answers on neutral allocation, reserved essential capacity, objective electrical records, and specific written objections substantially answered B’s upgrade and tribunal pressure, although B retained a meaningful concern about ongoing administration.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively exposing the hidden administrative and structural burdens of Side B's proposal. By framing electrical panel capacity as a shared "commons" and demonstrating that Side B's solution turns the landlord into an unpaid operator managing queues, load-sharing software, and insurance audits, Side A dismantled the PRO premise that tenant-funded installation is a burden-free default. Side B's concessions in Rebuttal 2 regarding queues and capacity limits fatally weakened their case, allowing Side A to successfully reframe the "right to request" as an uncompensated transfer of operational control.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which capitalized on Side B's admission that the right evaporates when capacity binds and requires the landlord to administer a queue, effectively proving that the proposal transfers operational control rather than merely granting access.
