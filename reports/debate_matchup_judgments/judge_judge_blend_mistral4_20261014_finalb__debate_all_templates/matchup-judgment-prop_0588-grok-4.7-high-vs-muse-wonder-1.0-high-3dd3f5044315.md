### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-argued cases, but A's framing proved more durable across the exchange. A set the decisive test ("verified, sustained forcing avoided per dollar") that B explicitly accepted, then exploited a genuine dilemma it never fully escaped: cheap LDAR misses intermittent leaks, but detection tight enough to catch them (continuous monitors, shorter intervals, verified repairs) imports real costs that erode the "cheap fix" premise. A pressed this in Rebuttal 1, sharpened it with pointed pressure questions, and in Rebuttal 2/Closing showed B's answer (monitors go "where releases already spike") doesn't resolve the core tension of catching new, unpredictable releases. A's durability argument (CO2 injected stays removed; leak closures must be perpetually re-bought) was also never fully neutralized — B's best counter (early years of forcing can't be repaid later) was a good point but was met and partially blunted in A's closing. B's answers to A's pressure questions were competent but more qualitative/hand-wavy than A's responses to B's questions, which engaged more directly with the capture-timeline and targeting critique. Overall A made the debate easier to decide by maintaining a consistent, escalating pressure point that B addressed but did not dismantle.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 dilemma — "Make detection strong enough to catch intermittent releases, and the marginal dollar buys surveillance. Keep it cheap, and it keeps missing the releases being claimed" — created a persistent tension B never fully resolved in later rounds.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by focusing on marginal spending rather than methane potency alone. A established that methane repairs can act quickly, but never adequately resolved B's dilemma: cheap, intermittent surveys miss releases, while sufficiently comprehensive monitoring, verification, and repeat repair dilute the claimed cost advantage. B then offered a more auditable comparison through targeted, near-ready capture projects at concentrated streams. Both sides lacked firm per-dollar quantification, but B more effectively exposed that gap in A's affirmative burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response that A's proposed tighter intervals, continuous monitors, and verification “spend away” the cheap-seal advantage was decisive. A narrowed monitoring to high-risk sites, but B persuasively answered that intermittent failures may occur elsewhere, forcing a choice between missed emissions and expensive broad surveillance.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully imposing the burden of verified, sustained forcing avoided per dollar and exposing a dilemma in Side A's case: leak rules that are loose enough to be cheap miss intermittent leaks, while rules tight enough to catch them require expensive surveillance across thousands of dispersed sites, eroding the claimed cost advantage. Side B reinforced this with a targeting asymmetry—CCS spending can be directed to engineered, near-ready concentrated sources, whereas tightening rules must spread across the regulated population before leaks are known. Side A's responses were plausible but increasingly hand-wavy about verification costs and did not fully answer why intermittency would not force wide, costly coverage. Side B also effectively framed near-term benefit as cumulative forcing avoided across the whole window, making its year-three-or-four start credible rather than disqualifying.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 (B2) and Rebuttal 2 (B4) introduced and sharpened the dilemma that tightening leak rules forces a choice between cheap, miss-it-when-it-happens surveillance and expensive continuous monitoring, destroying Pro's opening claim of cheap, immediate fixes.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent frameworks, but B more consistently anchored the clash to the motion's explicit "near-term" window, repeatedly forcing A to concede that even "bolt-on" concentrated-stream CCS needs design, tie-ins, and commissioning before delivering a single verified ton, while leak crews act within months. B's information-asymmetry mechanism (operators don't capture full value of found gas, so profitable fixes still go unfound) was a strong, specific answer to A's "marginal dollar" critique, and B answered the pressure-question exchange cleanly with required A1/A2 labels. A's best move — reframing CCS away from a megaproject strawman toward metered, concentrated-stream capture — was clever and original, but A never substantiated the claim that continuous capture tons would outweigh methane's near-term potency; it mostly asserted "lead time is a start date, not a result" without grounding magnitude. A's Rebuttal 1 was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost that blunted its momentum right when it needed to consolidate its strongest counter-framing. On balance, B gave the cleaner, better-supported path to the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answer to A's pressure questions (A1/A2), explaining that tightening buys "the hunt" for leaks that are profitable to fix but not profitable to find or verify, directly rebutting A's marginal-dollar/diminishing-returns framing with a concrete market-failure mechanism.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A won by explaining what tighter requirements additionally purchase: the monitoring, assignment, deadlines, and verification needed to discover and truncate otherwise persistent leaks. Side B raised a strong marginal-spending challenge but repeatedly assumed that profitable or obvious repairs already occur, despite A's credible search-cost and split-incentive explanation. B also overstated recurrence as erasing past methane reductions while understating CCS lead times, downtime, and continuing operational costs. A therefore offered the stronger near-term causal comparison, while B's first rebuttal being clipped was an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest response was that a cheap repair may still never happen because hunting for the leak is not itself profitable. This directly answered B's claim that tightening merely buys diminishing-return inspections and established why monitoring can generate additional reductions rather than double-count existing repairs.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A won by maintaining that near-term climate benefits are front-loaded: methane repairs deploy in months and avoid a super-potent pollutant immediately, while CCS spends years in design and construction before delivering partial, energy-consuming reductions. Side B’s core critique—that methane spending hits diminishing returns because cheap fixes already exist—was squarely neutralized by Side A’s argument that leaks persist due to detection and enforcement gaps, not repair economics, so tightening buys the missing hunt that unlocks those tons. Side B never overcame the timing asymmetry or showed that stack-metered CCS tons would actually outweigh immediate methane cuts within the decade on a dollar-for-dollar basis; their case remained skeptical of methane’s magnitude rather than constructive about CCS’s near-term superiority. The decisive clash resolved in Side A’s favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to the “self-funding repairs” critique: showing that persistent leaks indicate detection and enforcement gaps, not that cheap fixes are already exhausted, directly saved PRO’s core cost-effectiveness claim and undermined CON’s marginality trap.
