### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): A built a tighter, more responsive case. Its central move—that Western "leverage" (software updates, training, audit rights) does not restrain abuse but makes repression more efficient—directly undercut B's core practical defense of continued engagement, and B never produced a mechanism showing why future safeguards would succeed where documented abuses show past ones failed; B's rebuttal 2 simply reasserted "safeguards must be stronger" without engagement. A also answered the China/Russia substitution threat with a coherent (if idealistic) complicity/coalition-building frame, while B's closing kept repeating the same substitution point without advancing it. A's pressure questions were sharper and exposed a real tension in B's position (if abuses persist under current safeguards, how does withdrawal of updates actually stop deployed systems?), and A's answers to B's own questions were more developed than B's shrinking, assertion-heavy rebuttals. B's opening and early rebuttals were competitive, but its argumentative energy visibly thinned across rounds (261 words, then only 174 words in Rebuttal 2), leaving key clash points under-defended at the moment it mattered most.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's "software updates do not create restraint, they create capacity; training does not teach human rights, it teaches targeting" (A2.2/A4.1) directly gutted B's leverage argument, and B's reply ("safeguards must be stronger") never actually answered why continued Western supply would change outcomes given documented ongoing abuse.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that institutional context makes these exports predictably repressive and that vendor support increases authoritarian capacity rather than reliably constraining it. Side B kept supplier substitution as a serious objection, but repeatedly asserted that audits and cutoff clauses provide meaningful restraint without explaining why existing abuse persists or how cutoff recalls deployed systems. A also supplied a plausible partial-benefit case through higher acquisition costs and loss of premium support, so the ban did not need to eliminate every alternative supplier to reduce harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange targeted the claimed leverage of continued sales: maintenance, updates, and training make surveillance more effective, while cutoff cannot remove already-deployed infrastructure. Side B answered that stronger safeguards and degraded updates retain pressure, but never established that these measures outweigh the added repressive capacity.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully dismantling Side A's core defense of "Western leverage." B convincingly reframed support contracts and software updates not as ethical guardrails, but as the maintenance of repressive efficiency. Furthermore, B handled the "legitimate policing" argument beautifully by emphasizing that institutional context (due process, free press) determines whether surveillance is policing or persecution, rendering A's comparisons to Western democracies largely moot.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 attack on A's "leverage" argument, brilliantly summarized as confusing "maintenance with morality" by pointing out that updates and training just make repression more efficient.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): A built a vivid, emotionally grounded case (Xinjiang, FinFisher, Pegasus/Khashoggi) establishing real harm, but the core causal claim—that a ban reduces harm rather than merely relocating it—was never fully defended against B's substitution argument. B's point that Huawei/Hikvision already dominate authoritarian surveillance infrastructure (and that Xinjiang's own camera network is largely Chinese-built) exposed a real tension in A's case that A answered only with assertion ("regimes prioritize Western tech," "inferior alternatives") rather than engagement. B also pressed harder on feasibility (dual-use software vs. trackable nuclear material), and A's answer ("capability-based thresholds," citing encryption controls) was thin and didn't resolve the blunt-instrument objection that a ban can't distinguish legitimate from repressive use. B's pressure questions landed and were answered candidly by A, but A's own answers to B's questions were vaguer. B's closing synthesized the decisive mechanism dispute (ban vs. relocation to unaccountable suppliers, with a concrete alternative of targeted sanctions/end-use monitoring) while A's closing mostly recapped opening examples without engaging that live alternative. A's opening was also clipped for length, a real execution cost. Overall B maintained a more coherent, harder-to-rebut throughline across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 point that Chinese/Russian systems are "already deployed at massive scale" and that the ban "cannot distinguish a police force tracking kidnappers from a secret police tracking dissidents" was the most damaging unanswered blow, since A's closing never returned to neutralize the substitution/feasibility combination.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by making policy effectiveness, rather than moral condemnation alone, decisive. Side A established severe abuses and showed that Western tools can provide valuable capabilities, but its answers on defining covered technology, applying the human-rights trigger, and preventing substitution remained largely conclusory. Side B persuasively argued that a blanket ban would redirect procurement to less accountable suppliers, sacrifice leverage, and obstruct legitimate policing in flawed states. Side A also failed to explain why regulated engagement and targeted sanctions could not outperform a blanket ban, while its opening was clipped.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its answer to Side A’s Pegasus question: it conceded Western tools can be uniquely effective while arguing that inelastic demand and mature Chinese and Russian alternatives make a ban redirect procurement rather than end surveillance. Side A showed some degradation benefit but never adequately weighed that benefit against lost oversight, legitimate uses, and supplier substitution.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by effectively grounding their arguments in concrete examples of abuse and using strong precedents (encryption, arms controls) to dismantle Side A's feasibility objections. Side A's substitution argument was neutralized when Side B forced the concession that Western tech is uniquely effective for high-stakes targeting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Q1 brilliantly exposed a contradiction in Side A's substitution argument, forcing Side A to concede that Western tools are uniquely effective for high-stakes targeting, proving the ban would meaningfully degrade authoritarian capabilities.
