### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built a cleaner, more sustained structural critique: either a state with genuine shortage is exempted (in which case the ban survives and the rule changes nothing) or it is not exempted (in which case a panel is forced to override incomplete, politically sensitive stock data, producing either hollow or illegitimate enforcement). This dilemma was introduced early and never dissolved. PRO (B) increasingly relied on infrastructure—regional reserves, emergency financing, a satellite/customs "observatory"—that is not actually created by "rules limiting export bans," and CON's sharpest line ("if funded reserves existed, bans would already be less necessary... enforcement merely reallocates scarcity") exposed that this support system is assumed rather than delivered by the motion. PRO's Mali 2022 example and beggar-thy-neighbor framing were vivid and grounded, and its pressure questions were sharp, but CON's answers (A1/A2) directly met them by distinguishing bans-on-traders from state-ordered exports and by reframing sovereignty around sequencing (domestic unrest arrives faster than diffuse refugee blowback). CON's closing crystallized the clash concisely; PRO's closing was clipped before finishing, a real execution cost under the judging rules. Overall CON gave the clearer, more resolved path to a decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1/A4.4-4.5 (Rebuttal 2): CON's reply to the verification/penalty mechanism — pointing out that export bans control private traders rather than state-directed exports, and that reserves/financing are assumed rather than produced by the rule — directly undercut PRO's core enforcement architecture and was never fully recovered from.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's enforcement mechanisms and successfully checking Side B's attempt to fiat a regional reserve system that the motion does not create. Side A convincingly argued that without those un-fiated reserves, the rule merely reallocates scarcity and forces leaders to choose between domestic riots and continental penalties.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal brilliantly dismantled B's penalties by pointing out that suspending non-staple export preferences cuts off the very foreign exchange a starving country needs to import food.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing the fatal flaw in Side A's technocratic approach: the "exemption dilemma." Side B convincingly argued that any rule limiting export bans must either exempt countries with genuine shortages (making the rule hollow and subject to evasion via incomplete data) or compel exports during famines (which no political leader will accept). Side B also effectively penalized Side A for smuggling in massive, unstated mechanisms like "regional strategic reserves" and "emergency financing" to make the rule work, correctly pointing out that the motion only concerns limiting bans, not building a continental welfare state. Side A's closing was also clipped, adding an execution cost. Side B's political economy reasoning was sharper, more grounded in reality, and decisively neutralized Side A's case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, specifically the dismantling of Side A's "observatory" and penalty mechanisms, proving that satellites cannot count household stores and that suspending non-staple exports harms the very foreign exchange needed to import food, thereby collapsing Side A's enforcement model.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built a tighter, more mechanistic case: it conceded the sovereignty worry up front by proposing a calibrated rule (short duration, notification, sunset, dispute review) rather than an absolute ban on bans, and it consistently used that distinction to defuse CON's "riot scenario" framing. In the pressure round and Rebuttal 2, B followed the required A1:/A2: format and used the answers to sharpen its case (ex ante deterrence via known sunsets, reciprocal incentives to build reserves, and the externality framing that domestic accountability protects insiders while exporting hunger to neighbours). CON (A) delivered vivid, intuitively forceful rhetoric ("legal fiction evaporates in a riot," "ex-post review is an autopsy") and scored a real hit by noting that PRO's mechanism also can't stop a ban within 72 hours, but it largely repeated the "law yields to necessity" assertion across rounds without directly engaging B's marginal-deterrence and reciprocity arguments, and its own Q2 answer (electorate as the real check) was turned against it in B's closing as the very externality problem the debate is about. B's closing crystallized the clash more cleanly ("whose judgment ends a ban") and left CON's live route (domestic political accountability as sufficient check) looking structurally unable to protect anyone outside the banning state's borders.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2 answers plus B4.4) turning CON's own pressure-question answers into proof that CON has no remedy for pre-emptive, early bans and no enforceable limit it would actually accept — directly exposing the gap between CON's rhetoric about riots and its actual policy content.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing the issue as a regional collective action problem where domestic politics actively incentivize externalizing starvation. Side A relied too heavily on an extreme 'riot' scenario, which Side B neutralized by noting the rule allows short-term emergency measures and that, even with imperfect compliance, setting a sunset clause deters preemptive cascades.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing correctly and decisively pointed out that Side A's proposed check ('domestic political survival') actually incentivizes governments to maintain export bans, because domestic voters will reward ministers for hoarding food at the expense of landlocked neighbors.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully distinguishing between absolute prohibitions and procedural limits (sunsets, notifications, and ex-post review), effectively arguing that the rule changes behavior at the margin where most bans actually occur (pre-emptive and prolonged closures). Side A also delivers a decisive blow in the closing by dismantling Side B's reliance on "domestic accountability," pointing out that domestic voters will reward a minister for keeping food home and externalizing the hunger to neighboring countries. Side B relies too heavily on extreme edge cases (riots and massacres) to dismiss the treaty, and fails to provide a compelling mechanism to stop the more common, politically convenient export bans that devastate regional trade.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing argument dismantling Side B's "domestic accountability" check. Side A brilliantly points out that an electorate facing high prices will reward a minister for imposing and renewing a ban, meaning domestic political survival inherently externalizes the crisis rather than solving it.
