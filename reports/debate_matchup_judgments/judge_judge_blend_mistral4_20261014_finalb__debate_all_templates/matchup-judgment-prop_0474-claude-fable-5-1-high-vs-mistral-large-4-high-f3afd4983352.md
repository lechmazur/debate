### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by keeping the debate centered on the decisive word, “comparable.” It persuasively distinguished imposing some cost from imposing aggressor-tier costs, showing how equalizing those tiers could remove escalation incentives and undermine cooperation. Side A effectively distinguished ordinary botnet recurrence from knowing state tolerance, but its late reliance on conditional, reversible sanctions and a hypothetical multilateral process weakened its defense of aggressor-comparable punishment. Its closing then dismissed the graduated approach it had effectively adopted. B had minor overstatements and miswritten reference labels, but these did not outweigh its clearer comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s closing contradiction press was decisive: A needed conditional, behavior-sensitive, reversible measures to avoid sanctioning cooperative states, yet also denounced graduated sanctions and insisted harboring should cost as much as attacking. B showed that either A retreats from “comparable” or inherits the escalation and cooperation harms.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's defense of "comparable" sanctions. B successfully demonstrated that equating toleration with aggression flattens the deterrence ladder, and cornered A into inadvertently arguing for B's own alternative (a conditional, graduated ladder) to avoid sanctioning allies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal expertly neutralized A's Article 51 defense and exposed how A's attempt to protect allies relied on adopting B's own graduated ladder and inventing a non-existent multilateral tribunal.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing an irresolvable tension in PRO's case: either the sanctions are truly comparable to aggressor-tier measures, in which case they erase the escalation ladder and create permanent hostile havens with no evidence of improved cooperation; or they are conditional, reversible, and multilateral, in which case they constitute a graduated ladder and fail the motion's own standard. PRO's damaging concession that no historical case exists of aggressor-tier sanctions improving botnet cooperation [B4.3], combined with the inability to explain why allies like Germany wouldn't be caught by the standard without resorting to precisely the kind of selective, accuser-driven discretion CON warned about, left PRO with no live route to victory. CON's closing crystallized this contradiction perfectly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal and closing that leveraged PRO's admission of no empirical case [B4.3] and PRO's retreat to conditional, reversible measures [B4.2, B4.5], demonstrating that PRO's actual policy was a graduated ladder—which CON had advocated for and which contradicted the motion's demand for comparability to aggressor-tier sanctions.
