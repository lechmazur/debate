### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): A built a cleaner, more defensible decision rule throughout: distinguish unsafe/coercive applications (which both sides agree can be restricted) from a blanket categorical ban on all non-medical genetic enhancement. A's pressure question forced B to admit it would prohibit even a hypothetically risk-free, non-competitive cosmetic edit, exposing that B's case ultimately rests on an unargued premise that aesthetic benefit deserves zero weight rather than on risk analysis alone. A exploited this concession decisively in Rebuttal 2 and Closing ("Safety therefore cannot carry your whole case"), and B's closing mostly restated the coercion/pleiotropy arguments without directly defending the zero-value premise or explaining why a demonstrably safe, non-competitive edit must still be banned. B's "regulatory paradox" point (testing requires unethical trials on healthy people) was its strongest unanswered thread, but A's targeted-restriction framework and the autonomy/proportionality framing gave the more coherent, pressure-tested path to a verdict.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question Q1 ("would you still prohibit a nonheritable cosmetic edit if independent review found its risks comparable to accepted cosmetic surgery") and B's answer "Yes, we still prohibit it" — which A then used in Rebuttal 2 to show B's position reduces to valuing aesthetic benefit at zero rather than resting on risk assessment, a charge B never fully rebutted.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by narrowing the clash to proportionality: a demanding evidentiary gate can already refuse unsafe edits without a permanent purpose-based ban. A’s regulatory-paradox and irreversibility points were strong, but after A admitted even a risk-free aesthetic edit would still be banned, B forced A to carry coercion and capacity claims as categorical justifications and showed those claims remain too sweeping to support forbidding every adequately evidenced, consensual nonmedical use. B answered pressure directly, kept safety and dual-use distinctions live, and crystallized that A defended refusing dangerous procedures rather than the extra lock the motion requires.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 (B4.5–B4.8): after A’s A1 yes-even-if-risk-free answer, B made safety unable to carry the whole PRO case and demanded independent proof that coercion/resources justify banning every otherwise acceptable use.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B successfully showed that strict prohibition is overbroad. By exposing that Side A would ban even a credibly safe, consensual aesthetic edit, B demonstrated that A treats non-medical purpose as automatically dispositive rather than regulating demonstrated harms. B's framework of demanding evidence, restricting competitive spillovers directly, and permitting safe uses proved more proportional and intellectually coherent. A's regulatory paradox and arms-race arguments had force, but B defused them by showing that difficulty establishing safety justifies refusal, not categorical prohibition, and that classifying interventions by actual effects rather than stated motive resolves the boundary problem A alleged.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal, where B noted that A would prohibit even a "risk-free" edit, proving safety could not carry A's argument and revealing that A assigns aesthetic benefit zero weight independent of risk.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but CON (Side A) landed the more decisive blow in the Pressure Questions exchange. A's Q1 forced PRO to concede in B4.1 that even a genetic edit "safer than surgical anesthesia" would still be banned, stripping away the biological-risk justification and exposing the prohibition as resting purely on a market-normalization/paternalism rationale. A exploited this concession effectively in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, repeatedly framing PRO's position as criminalizing safe bodily autonomy merely to freeze competitive benchmarks. PRO's reframe (protecting "the freedom to remain unmodified") was conceptually sound but increasingly abstract, and PRO never fully neutralized A's concrete counters: that prohibition cannot stop offshore access for the wealthy, cannot resolve pre-existing natural genetic inequality, and funnels unavoidable demand into unregulated black markets with worse safety outcomes. PRO's workplace-pressure argument (employers rewarding enhanced output without explicit coercion) was a strong standalone point that CON only partially answered, but it wasn't enough to offset the damage from the safety concession and the inequality/black-market rebuttals. CON's case was more concrete and harder to escape; PRO's case, while principled, became narrower and more theoretical as the debate progressed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question Q1 ("why doesn't that standard require banning cosmetic surgery... would you permit a localized edit proven safer than surgical anesthesia?") and PRO's forced concession in B4.1 that such an edit would still be prohibited—this exchange reframed the entire debate around paternalism rather than safety, and CON capitalized on it through the remaining rounds.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won by forcing A’s concession that even a localized edit safer than surgical anesthesia must stay banned, collapsing biological risk as the core justification and leaving only competitive-pressure paternalism. B then used that concession to show inconsistency with legal cosmetic surgery, show prohibition fails to eliminate relative comparisons or offshore elite access, and keep regulation plus institutional rules as the cleaner alternative. A’s collective-action and uptake points stayed live but never recovered a decisive reason to prefer categorical prohibition over the narrower tools B defended.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s R2/closing exploitation of A4.1: once safer-than-anesthesia recreational edits remain prohibited, the case is pure market-pressure paternalism and collapses against existing non-medical bodily modifications.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B won by extracting a decisive concession in Side A's second rebuttal: even a localized genetic edit proven safer than surgical anesthesia must still be prohibited. This collapsed Side A's biological-risk justification and exposed the ban as resting entirely on preventing competitive benchmarks from moving. Side B then showed that this prohibition is unprincipled relative to existing cosmetic markets, fails to protect non-buyers who still compete against natural genetics and wealthy offshore purchasers, and channels unavoidable demand into dangerous black markets. Side A's market-coercion argument remained coherent but could not overcome the demonstration that the ban criminalizes safe bodily autonomy without achieving the equality or protection it promises.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1-B4.3), which seized on Side A's admission in A4.1 that even a hypothetical ultra-safe edit would be banned, used it to eliminate biological risk as the justification for prohibition and reframe the entire debate as paternalistic benchmark-freezing.
