CON’s double-bind on PRO’s promised exemptions/funds (broad = gameable; tight = exclusionary) was never neutralized, and CON’s alternatives (targeted bans, preferences, finance while trade continues) kept a cleaner comparative out. PRO narrowed under pressure but did not dissolve the gate dilemma; CON crystallized it in closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4/A5: dropping unverifiable suppliers is de-risking, not proof of exploitation; PRO’s exemption/funding fixes either fail fraud control or exclude the least-capitalized first—so the mirror changes who can enter the race, not who races fair.

While Side A argued that a minimum floor protects decent smallholders from being undercut by exploitative rivals, Side B convincingly demonstrated that the compliance costs (audits, traceability, carbon accounting) inherently favor large, capitalized firms with compliance departments. This effectively excludes the decent but poor producers Side A claims to protect. Side B's distinction between prior licensing (mirror clauses) and targeted post-investigation bans was highly effective, and its closing rhetoric crystallized the clash memorably. Side A's attempt to resolve this via "importer-paid certification" and "exemptions" was successfully shown by Side B to be either easily gamed or practically unworkable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, specifically the point that importers will "de-risk against unverifiability, not guilt," which perfectly dismantled Side A's assumption that importer liability would protect smallholders from being dropped.
