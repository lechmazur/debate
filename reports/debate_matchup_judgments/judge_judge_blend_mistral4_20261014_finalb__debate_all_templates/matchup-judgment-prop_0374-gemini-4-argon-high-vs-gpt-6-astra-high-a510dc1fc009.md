### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): B opened strongly with vivid framing (sledgehammer/scalpel, "classroom's immune system") and concrete examples, and used Pressure Questions effectively to extract a real concession from A that "comparing historical accounts" isn't a safe pivot when history itself is the political target. But A consistently held the higher ground on the actual burden of the motion: proving self-censorship is *now more serious* than the content it polices. A repeatedly exposed that B's central structural claims—"paper trail" visibility guaranteeing correction, the "one dogmatic teacher vs dozens of cautious ones" ratio, and the causal attribution of all selective teaching to fear rather than conviction—were asserted rather than established. B never substantively answered A's sharpest point that "public availability creates an opportunity for correction, not a guarantee of correction," nor the causation-ambiguity argument that identical outcomes (selective evidence) can stem from conviction, not just fear, which undercuts B's clean dichotomy. A's closing crystallized this into a clean meta-critique—that B's comparison stacks self-censorship's worst case against distorted content's best case—landing uncontested as the final word. B's case remained vivid and emotionally compelling but rested on unproven scale assumptions that A methodically dismantled across rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 point [A2.2] that a visible worksheet can hide a missing counterargument just as effectively as a silent omission, and B's failure in Rebuttal 2 to answer this beyond reasserting "visibility enables correction," was the most decisive exchange—it neutralized B's core visible/invisible asymmetry argument.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): PRO built a clearer comparative mechanism showing that vague, punitive political pressure structurally incentivizes systemic self-censorship, disables the teacher as a corrective safeguard, and targets core historical inquiry. CON landed valid theoretical challenges—distorted content can be invisible in shared materials and teach bad epistemic habits—but conceded that avoiding foundational history is irreplaceable and never overcame PRO’s argument that political pressure also sanitizes shared materials while paralyzing the educators who could correct them. PRO’s “immune system” framing and exploitation of CON’s concession in the final rebuttals crystallized a decisive live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 2 and Closing leveraged CON’s admission that teachers must retreat from history to “less politically exposed material,” showing that political pressure disables inquiry precisely where it matters most, and turned CON’s “selective evidence” worry into a description of politically policed classrooms, cementing the comparative harm.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won the weighing. CON (A) made the strongest push that selective/distorted content is also invisible and teaches faulty reasoning, but PRO turned it: fear disables the very teacher correction CON relies on, sanitizes shared materials itself, and forces retreat from core history, not just contemporary politics. B's Pressure Q1 exposed A's pivot to "other historical evidence" and A4.1 conceded the loss doesn't replace slavery / civil rights learning. PRO kept a clearer asymmetric story: presence is correctable, systemic omission of inquiry is not, which better meets the "now more serious" comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q1 in B3 and B4.4 — if political pressure targets history itself, teachers cannot just pivot to comparing historical accounts, forcing A4.1 to concede valuable loss is not replaced. This collapsed A's claim that caution preserves analytical skills and let PRO reframe selective-evidence harm as the product of policing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built a more disciplined comparative case: it grounded "controversial content" in concrete institutional failures (ideological sorting, age-inappropriate material, activist displacement of core academics) rather than abstract hypotheticals, and it repeatedly exploited a genuine structural tension in PRO's position—PRO's own opening conceded that even eventual vindication does not prevent chilling [B1.2], yet PRO's remedy still relies on the same complaint/investigation machinery [B2.4], a contradiction CON pressed hard in Rebuttal 1, the Q round, and Rebuttal 2, and which PRO only partially answered by conceding "not every chilling effect is avoidable" [B4.1]—a concession that weakened PRO's attempt to cleanly separate "legitimate accountability" from "the harmful pressure" being debated. CON also successfully reversed PRO's visibility argument, arguing that unrecorded ideological framing is harder to catch than curriculum gaps caught by audits and state standards. PRO's strongest surviving point—that assessments testing factual recall don't prove students learned the reasoning/evidence-evaluation habits tied to an omitted discussion [B4.5]—was never fully neutralized by CON, which mostly reasserted a general "skills transfer elsewhere" claim without directly closing that gap. But on balance, CON's consistent, concrete weighing framework ("incomplete good" vs. "active corruption") and its effective use of PRO's own concessions gave it the cleaner, more resolved path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A1 in Rebuttal 2, which quotes PRO's own opening claim that "eventual vindication" doesn't stop the chill [B1.2] against PRO's proposed accountability-based remedy [B4.1], forcing PRO into the damaging concession that "not every chilling effect is avoidable" [B4.1]—significantly undercutting PRO's attempt to distinguish legitimate oversight from the harmful political pressure at issue.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing that politically amplified pressure produces a systemic, invisible chilling effect that degrades inquiry and reasoning across subjects, not merely eliminating a few partisan debates. Side B never adequately defended the narrow reach of self-censorship against A's mechanism showing how legitimate instruction on required topics gets stripped of evidentiary depth. While B landed strong rhetorical blows with the active-harm-versus-incomplete-good frame and the inevitability of caution under accountability, B repeatedly assumed without sufficient support that pressure confines itself to clear activism and that textbook-bound defaults preserve educational quality. A successfully showed that omission leaves less trace than explicit misconduct, that controversy is an unreliable proxy for educational harm, and that the comparison in the proposition favors the more systemic, less correctable threat. A's pressure questions exposed real gaps in B's audit and recovery claims, and A's closing cleanly crystallized the mismatch between political safety filters and actual educational need.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4), where A distinguished legitimate oversight from politicized complaint pressure, answered B's audit argument by showing that factual coverage does not equate to reasoning evaluation, and reframed self-censorship as a repeated, systemic degradation of ordinary teaching rather than an occasional skipped sidebar.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON wins the weighing by forcing PRO to concede its own enforcement paradox. PRO wants rules against coercion but defines self-censorship as triggered by mere complaints/investigations [B1.2]. A1-Q1 and A2-A4 exploit that: even screened, vindicated complaints still chill, so PRO's ideal accountability still produces the harm it calls more serious. PRO's concession that some caution is inevitable [B4.1] and that one omitted discussion is not worse than coercion [B4.3] lets CON narrow scope to textbook-bound restraint vs active miseducation with grading power, student chill, and displacement of foundational skills, which PRO never fully neutralizes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 A2.1 + Pressure Q1 and Rebuttal 2 A4.1: showing PRO's proposal to punish coercive grading requires the exact complaint/investigation mechanism PRO blames for chilling, and that PRO admitted vindication doesn't remove the chill, undermining PRO's claim to separate legitimate oversight from harmful political pressure.
