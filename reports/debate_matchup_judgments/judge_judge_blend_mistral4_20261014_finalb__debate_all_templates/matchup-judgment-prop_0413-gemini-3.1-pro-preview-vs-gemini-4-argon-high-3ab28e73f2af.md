### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): A built its case on a cleaner, harder-to-answer foundation: a contract between the state and birth parents cannot bind the non-consenting child, and B never squarely rebutted this—it kept restating the value of the reliance promise rather than resolving why that promise should override the adoptee's own claim to their origins. A also successfully turned B's own privacy rationale against it with the DNA-database argument (sealed records don't actually prevent exposure, they just remove any mechanism for the birth parent to set a boundary first), and B's "fence" analogy answered the state-agency point but not the practical failure of secrecy to deliver real protection. A's medical-necessity line (dead or unresponsive biological parents leave intermediaries useless) was also never fully neutralized, since B's "proximate cause only" response conceded that some genetic information is still lost under sealing. B had its own strong moment—the Safe Haven rebuttal in Rebuttal 2 exposed a real gap in A's chilling-effect answer (Safe Haven covers post-birth abandonment, not months-long prenatal crises)—but A's closing didn't need to re-litigate this to retain the stronger overall framing, since B's own central argument (contractual reliance trumping a non-signing adult's right to their own history) remained unresolved throughout. Both sides executed the Q&A format correctly and avoided fabricated sourcing, but A's rebuttals did more cumulative damage to B's core premise than B's rebuttals did to A's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's reframing of the DNA-database point—arguing that sealed records don't actually protect birth parents from exposure, they just strip away any mechanism for the parent to set a boundary before unexpected discovery—landed the most damage, because B's "fence" analogy answered the formal objection (state shouldn't be the active breacher) without resolving the practical claim that secrecy fails on its own terms.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): PRO won by keeping the decisive clash on non-consenting third-party identity plus concrete medical failure of intermediaries, and by flipping DNA from a CON inconvenience into evidence that sealed files already wreck the privacy CON defends. CON’s trauma/chilling route stayed live and forceful, but its answers on dead/unreachable parents and on post-exposure-only remedies were thinner; PRO’s binding-contact + Safe-Haven weighing and cleaner crystallizing closed the path more convincingly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s DNA/privacy flip (sealed records force surprise family matches with no preference warning; open records + enforceable no-contact let mothers set boundaries first) plus the death-certificate answer that intermediaries return nothing when a parent is dead or silent.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by decisively undermining Side A's core premise that sealed records protect birth mothers' privacy. By highlighting the reality of commercial DNA testing, Side B proved that sealed records merely shift the discovery process to an uncontrolled DNA match that exposes the secret to the birth mother's family without warning. Side B's comparative framing—that unsealing records with legally enforceable contact preferences allows for private boundary-setting rather than treating all adoptees as "stalkers in waiting"—was highly persuasive and directly answered Side A's privacy concerns. Side A argued strongly that post-exposure penalties are reactive and cannot undo psychological harm, but failed to overcome Side B's practical demonstration that the state's current promise of anonymity is already obsolete and actively harmful to both parties.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (A1), which directly compared the two worlds of discovery: under sealed records, an adoptee's DNA match shocks the birth mother's unsuspecting family, whereas open records with contact preferences allow the adoptee to see the boundary privately before taking action. This effectively neutralized Side A's privacy argument by showing sealed records actually increase the risk of uncontrolled exposure.
