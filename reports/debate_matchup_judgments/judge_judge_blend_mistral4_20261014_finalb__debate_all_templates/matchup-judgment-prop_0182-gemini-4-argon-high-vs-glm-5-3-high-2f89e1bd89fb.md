### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): CON built the more coherent and ultimately decisive case. Its multi-causal framing (attacker, unpatched environment, defective code) consistently undercut PRO's clean "vendor pays" story, and its sharpest moment came when it caught PRO conceding comparative-fault carve-outs for customer neglect—directly contradicting the "strict, no-fault" premise PRO had sold as superior to negligence law. CON also pressed the zero-day problem effectively: if liability attaches even to best-practice, memory-safe code, the rule stops incentivizing diligence and instead becomes a flat tax priced by exposure rather than care, favoring incumbents and squeezing startups. PRO's pressure questions were good and exposed a real tension, but CON's answers (A1/A2) handled them directly, turning the zero-day question back into proof that liability without fault can't discriminate between careful and reckless vendors. PRO's closing attempt to argue that cost-location changes incentives was met by CON's final rebuttal that a premium which doesn't vary with diligence is a tax, not an incentive—a clean, memorable rebuttal that went unanswered since CON had the last word. PRO's case, by its own final rebuttal, had accumulated comparative fault, containment standards, and mandatory insurance—elements CON persuasively argued just rebuild the existing negligence/insurance system with an added uninsurable-by-design burden. That convergence critique, pressed across multiple speeches and never squarely defused, is the decisive throughline of the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 point [A2.2–A2.4]: catching PRO's concession that "tort law already reduces recovery for user neglect" as smuggled-in comparative fault that destroys the bright-line promise of strict liability, combined with turning PRO's own zero-day admission into proof that the rule punishes even flawless best-practice engineering.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): B wins on the core contradiction. PRO promises strict liability ends negligence fights, then concedes comparative fault for unpatched customers, recreating the sprawl it claimed to delete [B2.2]. B's zero-day pressure shows strict liability prices attacker innovation, not diligence, so insurers charge for exposure not care, becoming a tax that hurts startups and gets passed back to victims via higher prices [B4.1]. PRO's containment/insurance defense never solves that and converges on status-quo machinery plus uninsurable risk [B4.5]. A never answers why victim-funded pooling via licenses is worse.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.2-B2.3 and B4.5: exposing PRO's comparative-fault concession as destroying strict liability and reframing PRO's final model as existing law plus adversary-driven tax.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively exposing the internal contradiction in Side B's case: Side B promised strict liability would eliminate sprawling negligence litigation, but then conceded comparative fault for user neglect, effectively reintroducing the exact forensic disputes they claimed to solve. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): The debate turned on whether B's version of "strict liability" was actually strict liability or a disguised negligence standard. A pressed this contradiction relentlessly: B first insisted strict liability requires "no proof of negligence" [B1.4], then introduced a "genuine best practices" defense [B2.1], then defined defect as failure to meet "contemporary security standards" [B4.1-B4.2] — which A correctly identified as the textbook definition of a negligence duty of care. B's attempted distinction (process vs. product defect) is legally plausible but never cleanly closed the gap A kept exposing, and it was never fully resolved in B's closing. A also landed a sharp, largely unanswered point on correlated systemic risk: one flaw can hit thousands of enterprises simultaneously, and B's fix (reinsurance, government backstops, aggregation caps) imports machinery outside the resolution, which A flagged as proof the regime as stated is uninsurable. B's strongest material — the externality framing ("who pays"), the comparative-fault analogy from real products liability, and the disclosure-chilling rebuttal — was solid and rhetorically clean, but it didn't fully neutralize A's two decisive threads (definitional collapse into negligence, and unaddressed correlated catastrophic risk). Both sides executed the format well and answered pressure questions directly, but A's progression across rounds did more to narrow and resolve the key tension in its favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's repeated exposure of B's shifting definition of "insecure code" — from "no proof of negligence" [B1.4] to a "contemporary security standards" defect test [B4.1-B4.2] — which A labeled as negligence by another name [A4.4, A5.2-A5.3], was the most damaging and unresolved exchange of the debate.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won the definitional core. B consistently showed A’s “insecure = fails contemporary standards / state of art” is a negligence test, so either strict liability punishes careful vendors for novel zero-days (uninsurable, anti-disclosure) or it collapses to negligence, which already covers egregious flaws like SQL injection. A never answered the correlated-risk pandemic point or why disclosure wouldn’t shift to silent patches, and its comparative-fault fix still leaves startups exposed to billions in simultaneous claims. That left B with the cleaner live route to rejecting strict liability as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.4-B2.5 / B4.4: B shows A redefines “insecure code” as failing contemporary standards, which is textbook negligence, and argues if strict is enforced as written – any exploited flaw = insecure – then transparency becomes a multi-billion-dollar confession and a single zero-day becomes every plane falling at once, an unanswered disclosure and insurability double-hit.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A decisively trapped Side B on the definition of strict liability versus negligence. When Side B attempted to soften strict liability by allowing a "state of the art" or "best practices" defense, Side A successfully argued this was just a negligence standard in disguise, rendering the proposition redundant. Side A also landed highly damaging, effectively unanswered points regarding the chilling effect on vulnerability disclosure (where publishing a CVE becomes a legal confession) and the uninsurable nature of globally correlated software risks. Side B's case relied on analogies to physical products that Side A effectively dismantled by highlighting the unique scaling and adaptive adversary dynamics of software.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first and second rebuttals, where it exposed Side B's contradiction on the "best practices" defense, effectively forcing Side B to either defend an uninsurable absolute liability or concede to a negligence standard that already exists.
