### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but B built a more concrete and harder-to-dismiss causal story. B's pressure questions (especially the denominator-gaming numeric example and the "objective metric for an unproven hire" question) forced A into a defense that worked for existing, measurable performers but left the "non-traditional candidate at hire" and "gaming the promotion denominator" problems only partially answered. A's strongest counter—"if pay flattens, it only proves prior exploitation"—is rhetorically sharp but logically question-begging, since B's wage-compression mechanism (peer friction, finite budgets, unquantifiable intangible value) doesn't require prior exploitation to operate. B's hospital-mortality analogy and the 10-hires-vs-2-hires example gave the judge a vivid, ungameable way to picture the perverse incentive, and A's closing never squarely rebutted the claim that entry-level stepping-stone jobs would be eliminated rather than merely "exposed." A's case remained coherent and principled throughout, but B's responses were more specific, better matched to the live pressure points, and left fewer loose threads by the final speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question Q2 (the 20%-vs-100% promotion-rate gaming example) combined with B4.2's follow-up was the most decisive exchange: it concretely illustrated how a well-intentioned entry-level employer could be punished relative to one that outsources junior work, and A's response ("an honest 20% rate is the truth") never actually addressed the behavioral change (elimination of entry roles) that B's example was built to expose.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won a solid but competitive debate. A established the intuitive value of reducing information asymmetry, but repeatedly overstated what disclosure guarantees and treated low promotion rates as proof that most workers will never advance. B more effectively showed that raw rates can obscure valuable stepping-stone roles and create gaming incentives. B also exposed A's reliance on easily quantified “objective” output, which poorly fits potential at hiring and intangible contributions in knowledge work. Some of B's claims blurred disclosure with rigid standardization, but A largely accepted that framing rather than exploiting the distinction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.3 was decisive: B answered both pressure questions directly, distinguished internal promotion from broader career value, and showed why A's proposed output metrics cannot fairly capture unproven potential, prevention, mentorship, or other intangible work.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won a competitive debate by making the decisive clash the mandate’s behavioral side effects on the same workers A claims to protect: risk-averse credentialism, flattening when intangibles cannot be publicized cleanly, and scorecard gaming that erases entry-level stepping stones. A’s information-asymmetry and fairness case was strong and rhetorically sharp, but never fully neutralized B’s hire-before-output timing point or the promotion-denominator destruction path after pressure. B’s path stayed live through closing and made the tradeoff easier to decide against the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1–B4.2 answering A’s pressure: discretion is needed to hire unconventional talent competitively before internal output exists, and a 20% promotion job is a valuable foothold the scorecard incentive would replace with outsourcing—not merely a “dead end” to disclose.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): A built a cleaner, more consistent case throughout: the opening framed disclosure as curing information asymmetry while preserving range-based flexibility, and every rebuttal round tightened that frame rather than widening it. The pivotal moment was A's pressure question catching B's apparent contradiction between "bands will shrink entirely" (B1.3) and "bands will be published exceptionally wide" (B2.2). B's save—external wide "mirage" bands versus internally enforced compression—was clever but created a new tension that A exploited effectively: if bands are genuinely wide and publicly audited, the claimed loss of flexibility to reward unique talent within them weakens, and top performers can detect the mirage and leave. B's rebuttal 2 also conceded "yes, applicants will see the marginal 2% raise" on the micro-tier point, which A turned into the decisive closing line, framing it as proof the deception fails on contact with disclosed data. B's closing reasserted "institutionalized mediocrity" and market-wide distortion without squarely defusing that admission or the contradiction, making it feel like restatement rather than resolution. B's case had real originality (wage compression psychology, title inflation, context-dependent promotion rates) and strong early rebuttal work, but it lost ground precisely where the debate's pressure concentrated, while A adapted and closed the loop on both pressure points cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 exploiting B's own concession ("yes, applicants will see the marginal 2% raise" in B4.2) and the unresolved wide-vs-shrinking bands contradiction, crystallized decisively in A's closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by showing that disclosure provides workers useful comparative facts without imposing fixed salaries, while Side B repeatedly treated transparency as though it mandated rigid compensation. Side B identified real risks of compression, contextual ambiguity, and metric gaming, but did not establish that these harms would be universal or worse than the existing information asymmetry. A also explained persuasively that contextualized data is still superior to unsupported corporate promises.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange paired the two disclosures against title inflation: if firms create hollow micro-promotions, the published next-tier pay band reveals their marginal value. Side B’s admission that applicants would see the tiny raise substantially weakened its claim that gaming would successfully deceive workers.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): A won by owning the proposition’s tradeoff and making the clash easier to decide: status-quo opacity is unaccountable leverage, while paired disclosure of bands plus promotion rates both constrains lowballing and exposes hollow ladders. B raised real gaming and compression risks and smartly resolved the wide-vs-shrink tension as external mirage/internal median, but never fully neutralized A’s competition and visibility counters; A’s sharper pressure use and concession-leveraging left imperfect disclosed metrics beating pure secrecy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s pressure and Rebuttal 2 on B’s shrink-vs-wide-band contradiction, then closing use of B’s concession that applicants will see 2% micro-tier raises.
