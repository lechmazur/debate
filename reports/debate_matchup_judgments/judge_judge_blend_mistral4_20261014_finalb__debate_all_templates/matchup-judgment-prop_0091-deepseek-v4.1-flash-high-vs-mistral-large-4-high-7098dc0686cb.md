### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B drew and consistently defended the clearest distinction: recurring fees are legitimate for continuing services, but not merely for activating local installed hardware. Side A’s lower-entry-price argument remained plausible, yet B answered it with different trims or permanent one-time activation. Most damagingly, Side A ultimately conceded that general cybersecurity costs are not caused by enabling the heater and should be charged separately, undermining its main justification for recurring hardware paywalls.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s first pressure question exposed that vehicle-wide maintenance costs do not justify a feature-specific recurring fee. Side A answered by agreeing that those costs should be unbundled and that a heater paywall is price discrimination, effectively conceding B’s central causal argument.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): PRO kept a clean, administrable line — subscribe for genuine ongoing services, own local hardware — and CON's core cost justification collapsed. PRO's Q1 forced CON to admit in B4.1 that cybersecurity/OTA costs exist regardless of heater use and that hiding them behind a heater paywall is price discrimination, not cost recovery. CON's disclosure/choice defense never restored function when hardware is paid for, while PRO answered lower-trim cross-subsidy with one-time activation or omit-hardware.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A3.1 pressure that platform maintenance costs exist whether heater is on, answered in B4.1 with concession that they should be transparently unbundled and that heater gating is price discrimination — undermining CON's main economic defense.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): Side A drew a much cleaner and more persuasive line between ongoing services (which justify subscriptions) and local hardware (which do not). B's concession that enabling the feature "costs nothing" and that the subscription is merely "price discrimination" fatally undermined their necessity argument. A's rhetoric was also sharper and more memorable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they clarified that the motion targets recurring subscriptions (not one-time activations or physical trim differences) and neatly separated platform maintenance costs from local hardware functions, effectively neutralizing B's "lower trim" and "cybersecurity" traps.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): B won by drawing the clearest principled line between fees for genuine external services and recurring charges that disable self-contained hardware. A offered a strong standardization and lower-price defense, but never fully justified why local functionality should remain perpetually revocable rather than be sold through an upfront price. B answered the pressure questions directly and made ownership, autonomy, and double payment the more decisive comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's distinction between installed local functions and ongoing cloud, cellular, or data services most effectively answered A's claim that permanent access would require manufacturers to fund connected services forever. A's configuration argument kept the debate competitive, but did not neutralize that limiting principle.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won the central clash by drawing a workable line: permanent access to local installed hardware, pay for genuine ongoing services. That directly answered CON's funding/server pressure in A4.1, while CON's answers conceded manufacturers may disable present hardware unless capability was purchased - which is the practice the motion targets. CON's compelled-purchase / higher-sticker-price point was never tied to why a self-contained heater needs rent.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.3 limiting principle that hardware ownership is permanent but data plans aren't, plus the omit-don't-rent reply to the base-trim first-charge argument, which neutralized CON's broad reading of the motion.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing the debate around the visceral consumer experience of "bricking" physical property and holding it hostage, which resonated much stronger than Side B's technical defenses of manufacturing efficiency and software licensing. Side A's limiting principle distinguishing local hardware from cloud services was reasonable and effectively neutralized Side B's slippery slope arguments, leaving Side B to defend the highly unpopular practice of gating already-installed physical features.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where they dismantled the "first charge" and "choice" arguments by reframing them as a "hostage negotiation" and pointing out that manufacturers can simply omit hardware if they want to sell a cheaper car, rather than installing it and demanding rent.
