### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B won by focusing on the proposition’s absolute word “only.” Its counterexample—intact families, blanket protection against deportation, and guaranteed subsistence—showed that substantive safety can exist without every specified destination-country entitlement. Side A persuasively established that enforceable individual rights are generally more durable and empowering, but did not prove they are indispensable in every legitimate transfer. Side B’s nonexistent reference labels and some overconfident claims about financial leverage reduced its margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s answer on family unity was decisive: screening out separated families can preserve existing unity even without a destination-country reunification procedure. Side A’s appeal to possible future spouses or relatives expanded the right into speculative future migration and did not rescue the universal necessity claim.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B built the clearer path against the absolute “only”: destination-family-rights are unnecessary when senders screen intact units/unattached adults, group recognition plus conditioned aid can secure non-refoulement without individualized appeals, and funded subsistence can prevent destitution without formal work rights. A’s irreversibility/rights-vs-favors framing and pressure on post-breach remedies were sharper and more memorable, but never fully closed the logical gap on the motion’s destination-specific absolute. B made the decisive clash easier to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1/B4.4 converting A’s case-selection concession into a direct defeat of the requirement that destination states supply family-unity rights.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side A won by decisively proving that externalized safeguards—such as donor funding, sending-state screening, and international monitoring—cannot substitute for individually enforceable rights in the destination country. Side A's rebuttals were devastating: on family unity, they showed that a single adult's circumstances change over time, rendering "case selection" insufficient; on work, they proved that "immunity without legality" leaves informal workers entirely at the mercy of their employers. Side A's rhetoric was sharper, framing the proposition not as Western perfectionism but as the bare minimum floor for safety, while Side B's pragmatic arguments about frontline states ultimately conceded that their model leaves the transferred individual holding nothing upon arrival.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 answer to Q2, explaining that "immunity without legality means no contract, no wage claim, no right to refuse dangerous work," which completely dismantled Side B's proposal to substitute formal work rights with stipends and prosecution immunity.
