### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued the biology competently, but B built a more sustained and specific challenge to the mandate's actual efficacy and fairness. B's core move—distinguishing "permission to sleep" from "guaranteed sleep," since evening activities (practice, jobs, homework, screens) shift along with the bell—was never fully neutralized by A, who kept reasserting the biological framing without closing the causal gap. B's pressure questions (rural student still waking at 6:30 after a midnight sleep onset; who funds bus/tier changes in poor districts) exposed real, unresolved costs, and A's answers in Rebuttal 2 ("funding is a policy choice," "we already pay for ER visits") were rhetorically forceful but did not actually show how underfunded districts absorb the transition, nor did they resolve the "floor forbids districts that need 7:45" problem B kept highlighting. A's closing was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, cutting off its final weighing moment, which is a real execution cost per the judging rules. B's closing, by contrast, was clean and crystallized the decisive asymmetry (certain family/equity costs vs. uncertain sleep gain) clearly. On balance, B did more of the comparative work the later rounds were supposed to do.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Questions and Rebuttal 2 exchange on "who pays" and the rural student waking at 6:30 was decisive: it forced A to concede logistics problems are real and fall back on "funding is a choice," without ever specifying a mechanism, leaving B's equity/funding critique as the most live, unanswered point in the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by distinguishing evidence that later starts can help from the stronger claim that 8:30 must be imposed everywhere. It showed that actual sleep gains remain conditional on bedtime, dismissal-linked activities, and commute length, while transportation, childcare, and work disruptions can be immediate and regressive. Side B established the importance of adolescent sleep but relied on categorical biological claims and largely asserted that funding and scheduling harms could be engineered away. Its clipped closing also imposed a modest execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal most decisively turned Side B’s bus-tier solution: moving younger children onto pre-dawn routes was PRO’s proposed transfer, not CON’s chosen price. A then sharpened the central comparison between merely permitting extra sleep and compelling certain family and district costs.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B won by converting the clash into a reliability-and-burden test for a universal “required” floor. A’s biology frame (early starts guarantee deprivation; logistics are engineerable) stayed live and memorable, but B kept narrowing it: later bells only permit sleep if evenings/jobs/homework do not slide; rural/long-commute students can still wake ~6:30; PRO’s own fixes transfer dark early routes and unfunded care onto elementary kids and poorer districts. After pressure, B answered more cleanly that rejecting compulsion is not endorsing deprivation, and that possibility is enough to recommend later starts locally but not enough to compel them where costs hit day one. A’s clipped closing and thinner who-pays answer left that comparative under-resolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s R2 permission-vs-guarantee turn: later start is only a possibility unless evenings hold, so a mandate needs a reliable gain—not PRO’s transferable tier-flip/care fixes—to override certain family and elementary harm.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): PRO built a consistent, escalating case around a single asymmetry—adjustable adult logistics versus unadjustable adolescent circadian biology—and used it to frame every exchange. In rebuttal, PRO offered a plausible mechanistic reply to CON's "bedtime compensation" claim (distinguishing the melatonin-bottlenecked evening from the alarm-bottlenecked morning), which CON never substantively dismantled, instead repeating "minutes not hours" with language ("well-documented effect," "real-world district studies") that edges toward unsupported authority without grounding. CON's strongest material—hourly-worker harm and evening displacement—was pressed hard and repeated across rounds, but PRO's counter that the status quo already taxes hourly parents (absences, tardiness, drowsy-driving costs) went largely unanswered in CON's later speeches, which mostly reasserted the same economic framing rather than engaging that comparison. Both sides handled the Q&A format correctly, but PRO's answers more directly closed the loop on the central clash (no enforceable alternative; why offset isn't total), while CON's answers largely restated prior assertions. PRO's closing crystallized the unanswered biological asymmetry; CON's closing restated certainty-vs-speculation framing without directly rebutting PRO's bottleneck mechanism. This gives PRO a clear, if not overwhelming, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2, explaining why bedtime is biologically bottlenecked (so it can't fully drift to cancel wake-time gains), directly undercut CON's repeated "bedtime compensation" claim, which CON never answered with more than restated assertion.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing a clear asymmetry between biologically constrained adolescent sleep and logistical burdens that institutions can redesign. B directly answered the strongest childcare and bedtime-offset objections, while A repeatedly escalated possible burdens into claims of bankruptcy and family ruin without adequately grounding their prevalence. A also leaned on unsupported references to documented compensation effects and district studies introduced during rebuttal. Most importantly, A's local alternatives did not solve the core problem of a mandatory early wake time, leaving B's case for an enforceable floor intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to the childcare challenge was decisive: secondary students often require less direct supervision, early drop-off can occur without beginning instruction, and predictable statewide timing enables coordinated coverage. This undercut A's central claim that an 8:30 start necessarily creates an unpayable childcare gap.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): A won by keeping a clearer decisive clash: unadjustable adolescent circadian delay versus adjustable logistics, plus the collective-action case for a floor. A answered B’s bedtime-compensation and hardship pressure directly, insisted that partial offset is not full cancellation, and showed B’s local alternatives leave the early timetable itself intact. B landed real costs and blunt-instrument concerns, but overplayed cancellation/certainty and never neutralized the biology-versus-schedule asymmetry as cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2/closing reply to bedtime compensation: wake-time delay is not erased unless bedtime shifts a full hour every night, so usable morning sleep still improves while local advice cannot fix a harmful start time.
