### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but B's consolidation argument proved decisive and largely unanswered. B's core move—that a default veto would help large catalogue owners (labels, stock libraries, platforms) clear licenses at scale while leaving individual, untraceable creators unable to negotiate or get paid—directly undercuts A's central justification that defaults protect the powerless. When pressed ("ask whom?"), A's answer ("an unreachable owner is not a yes") actually confirms B's consolidation worry rather than refuting it: untraceable individual creators' work simply becomes unusable or unpaid, while only aggregatable catalogues get licensed, reproducing exactly the concentration B warned about. A's strongest original point—that machine training operates at inhuman scale and (unlike search) substitutes for creators without sending traffic back—was never fully extinguished by B, but it wasn't enough to offset the unresolved structural problem with A's enforcement mechanism. B's closing crystallized this tension sharply, while A's closing repeated its consent framing without addressing the consolidation critique head-on. That left B with the cleaner, more resolved path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer to B's Q1 in Rebuttal 2 ("if you cannot trace permission, you do not get to take... an unreachable owner is not a yes") inadvertently validated B's consolidation argument rather than rebutting it, since it implies untraceable individual creators' work becomes unusable while only aggregatable catalogues can be licensed — the exact concentration B warned about.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the motion on consent-for-purpose and burden placement: public posting bought visibility, not free industrial substitution, and only a default stop makes the right real because black-box training cannot be policed after the fact. B's best live route—web-scale clearance and catalogue concentration—was real and well pressed, but A answered more cleanly (opt-in pools/licensed/public sources; today's free scrape already favors giants) while B partially conceded the detection/proof problem that undercuts opt-out-plus-later-remedies. The exchange left A's preventative default as the clearer workable consent rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure on black-box opt-out proof, then A4/A5 crystallizing that B's post-hoc logs/audits/output suits leave small creators with paperwork after irreversible substitution.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A won by decisively framing the distinction between "visibility" and "replacement," neutralizing Side B's analogy to search engines. More importantly, A won the practical enforcement clash: A successfully argued that an opt-out right is illusory because creators cannot audit black-box weights, making prevention at the point of ingestion (default refusal) the only workable mechanism. While B's "ask whom?" attack highlighted the friction of opt-in, A's response that platforms must adapt by using licensed or synthetic data was a coherent market shift, whereas B's reliance on regulatory audits conceded that the creator's individual enforcement power remains weak under an opt-out regime. A's rhetoric was also sharper and more memorable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing on the enforcement clash. A's argument that "prevention is possible at the point of taking; detection inside weights is not" perfectly crystallized why the default must be at ingestion, effectively neutralizing B's opt-out and audit proposals.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases and executed the Pressure Questions / A1-A2 format cleanly. The decisive moment came when A, answering B's Q1, clarified that the proposed veto "stops at the input, not at resemblance" — a necessary concession to avoid claiming ownership of style. B then showed in Rebuttal 2 and Closing that this concession guts A's own opening harm: a rival tool trained on other artists could still mimic the illustrator's style for her former clients, so the platform-specific input veto "stops nothing that matters." A's closing restated the scope limitation but never actually answered how the stated harm is still prevented, leaving this critique live and unresolved. B also offered a coherent (if not airtight) answer to A's "insignificant vs essential" dilemma via an aggregation/transaction-cost argument, which A did not push back on in closing. A scored real points too — reframing extraction as what entrenches giants, and arguing imperfect enforcement is still valuable — but these did not fully neutralize B's sharper, better-sustained effectiveness critique. The debate was close and high-quality on both sides, but B ended with the cleaner, less-answered path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 / Closing point (B4.3, B5.1) exploiting A's own A1 answer ("veto stops at input, not resemblance") to show the illustrator-style-mimicry harm from A's opening survives untouched — a concession-based gotcha that A's closing failed to directly rebut.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): A won a competitive debate by staying closer to the motion’s platform-hosting default and by making the choice easier: silence currently licenses commercial extraction after irreversible ingestion, so flipping the burden is cleaner than a buried post-hoc opt-out. A’s sharpest comparative move was the tension it forced on B—training either adds little value (licensed/consented corpora suffice) or a lot (creators should be asked first)—while cabining the right to inputs rather than style ownership. B landed real pressure on incomplete protection, gridlock, and free-hosting economics, and used A’s “input not resemblance” limit well, but those hits never fully neutralized the proposition as written about platforms’ own commercial training use.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 contradiction (training is simultaneously too trivial to need consent and too valuable to lose without charging/tiering creators), which B never cleanly escaped.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by exposing a fatal contradiction in Side B's economic framing. B argued simultaneously that training on public data is merely benign pattern-matching (implying the data lacks distinctive commercial value) and that free hosting would collapse without it (implying the data is essential and highly valuable). A capitalized on this tension in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing to prove that if the data is valuable, creators deserve a default right to consent, and if it is not, platforms can easily rely on licensed material. Furthermore, A cleanly handled B's pressure regarding the scope of the veto by conceding it applies to inputs rather than output resemblance. This perfectly aligned with the motion's specific focus on stopping platforms from using the work, neutralizing B's attempt to stretch the proposition into an unworkable global ban on style mimicry.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it explicitly called out Side B's contradiction regarding the economic value of training data ("shifts between saying training takes nothing valuable and saying free hosting collapses without it") and used it to collapse B's cost-benefit analysis.
