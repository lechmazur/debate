### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but A more successfully isolated and sustained the decisive "hard case" — real integration that lowers prices/preserves services while incidentally reducing demand for some local roles — and pressed two points B never fully neutralized: (1) the comparison problem (no common unit to weigh a speculative local wage forecast against concrete, broad consumer/access gains), and (2) the remedy/counterfactual problem (blocking a merger doesn't restore competitive wages; it can cause closures that leave workers worse off than before). B's pressure questions were sharp and forced a useful concession from A (wage-funded price cuts aren't a legitimate efficiency), but A cleanly cabined that concession to the "easy" transfer case and kept the harder integration scenario live. B's closing then overstated the concession ("they have conceded the case") without re-engaging the integration/remedy arguments, effectively dropping live clash rather than resolving it. A's closing, by contrast, directly crystallized why parity still fails in the case that matters, giving the cleaner final reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.4), distinguishing provable naked no-poach harm from speculative merger-based wage forecasts and reasserting the closure/remedy problem, which B's closing never substantively answered.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A built the stronger path to victory by establishing a principled symmetry: labor markets are markets, substitution and locality are already managed in consumer analysis, and competition law protects competition rather than shoppers alone. Side B’s central objection—that labor markets are too slippery to define—was squarely neutralized when A showed that product markets involve equally multidimensional trade-offs, making the call for a free pass a double standard. A also forced and then capitalized on B’s concession that a price cut funded solely by sub-competitive wages is a transfer, not an efficiency. B’s remaining “hard case” of genuine integration reducing labor demand was never shown to be monopsony harm rather than ordinary efficiency, and A’s “same test, same veto, no cross-subsidy” frame gave the cleaner decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s first rebuttal neutralized the “slippery labor markets” objection by demonstrating that product markets already involve equally complex, multidimensional substitution (price, quality, brand, service), so difficulty in line-drawing is an argument for careful measurement rather than a free pass for buyer power. This exchange defanged B’s core epistemic attack and established the double standard that persisted through the rest of the debate.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side A built a much stronger case by correctly applying existing antitrust doctrine (the prohibition on out-of-market efficiencies) to neutralize Side B's main argument about unresolvable trade-offs. Side A's rhetoric was sharper, effectively framing wage suppression as "extraction" rather than "efficiency," and cleanly answering the pressure questions. Side B's reliance on the "hard case" of real integration conflated reduced labor demand with monopsony power, leaving their core trade-off argument vulnerable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled Side B's trade-off argument by explaining that antitrust already forbids cross-subsidizing harm in one market with benefits in another, effectively turning Side B's "hard case" into a non-issue under standard competition law.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-organized cases, but B's framing (monopsony as the mirror of monopoly, and the "efficiency vs. extraction" test) proved more durable under pressure. The decisive moment came in the Pressure Questions exchange: B's Q1 forced A to concede that a straight wage cut passed through as lower prices "deserves zero credit" even under consumer-first review. B's closing correctly identified that this concession is in tension with A's earlier administrability argument—if reviewers can already distinguish extraction from genuine efficiency, they are already doing the labor-market analysis A claimed was too speculative to perform, which undercuts the case for treating wage effects as categorically secondary. A's closing reasserted the "no neutral scale" and "certain gains vs. speculative harm" points, which remained live and reasonably strong, but never squarely resolved the contradiction B exposed. A's rebuttals (market-definition skepticism, mobility arbitrage via staffing agencies, tools argument) were sharp and genuinely damaged parts of B's case, keeping this close, but B's closing crystallized the clash more effectively and left A's core administrability claim looking self-undermining.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question (Q1) forcing A to admit a pure wage-cut-passed-to-price scenario deserves "zero credit," which B then used in Rebuttal 2/Closing to show A's consumer-first framework cannot coherently police extraction without already doing the labor-market scrutiny A claimed was unadministrable.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by exposing a decisive contradiction in Side B’s case: Side B conceded that pay cuts driven by destroyed employer competition deserve zero efficiency credit, yet claimed consumer-first review can strip that extraction out without treating labor monopsony equally. Side A showed there is no coherent middle ground—if the harm is real enough to deny credit, it must be interrogated with the same seriousness as price effects. Side A also answered the pressure questions more directly, offered a workable test (credit only genuine output efficiencies, not buying-power transfers), and crystallized the clash in a memorable closing. Side B’s administrability and tradeoff objections remained competitive but never recovered from the collapsed middle position.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s closing crystallization that Side B’s concession—stripping out extraction as non-efficiency—is only enforceable if reviewers already treat labor monopsony with equal seriousness, rendering the consumer-first alternative self-defeating [A5.1–A5.3].
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A successfully trapped Side B on the critical distinction between genuine efficiencies and monopsony extraction. By forcing Side B to concede in the Q&A that pure wage suppression deserves zero credit as a consumer benefit, Side A demonstrated in the closing that regulators must investigate labor-market effects with "equal seriousness" to prevent firms from laundering extraction as efficiency. Side B's reliance on the difficulty of market definition and the tradeoff between consumer and worker harm was effectively neutralized by Side A's framework of applying the same evidentiary burden to both sides of the market.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which capitalized on Side B's Q&A concession that pure wage cuts deserve zero credit, proving that equal scrutiny is required to distinguish extraction from genuine efficiency.
