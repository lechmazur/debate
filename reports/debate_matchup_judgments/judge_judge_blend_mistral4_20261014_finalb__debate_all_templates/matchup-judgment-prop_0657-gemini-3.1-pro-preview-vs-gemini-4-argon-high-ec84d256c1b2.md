### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON (A) built a tighter, more technically coherent case by consistently distinguishing "sharing indicators/tear-line warnings" (already feasible, already status quo) from "sharing sensitive methods" (the motion's actual requirement), and exploited this distinction to expose real contradictions in PRO's case: PRO oscillated between claiming shared methods are local analytical models and claiming they catch foreign server farms pre-launch, and between "collective immune response" and "tiered access" that quietly recreates silos. A's pressure questions forced these contradictions into the open, and PRO's answers (digital exhaust detection, black-boxed sensors/liaisons) were turned against it in Rebuttal 2: A correctly noted that "digital exhaust" detection is routine cybersecurity rather than the sensitive method the motion demands, and that black-boxed sensors in infiltrated ministries create an oracle-attack vulnerability letting adversaries reverse-engineer the filter. PRO's closing never engaged these specific technical rebuttals, instead retreating to moral framing ("dying with perfectly kept secrets") without resolving the asymmetry argument (cheap, fast AI evasion vs. years-long intelligence development) that A had pressed throughout. A also answered PRO's own pressure questions directly and cleanly (A1/A2), while PRO's answers to A's questions were the ones later dismantled. Both sides used vivid, effective rhetoric, but A's metaphors tracked more precisely onto the surviving technical clash, making A's case easier to follow as a decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3-A4.4), which showed that PRO's "tiered access/black-boxed sensor" fix for front-line states effectively concedes that sharing sensitive methods with vulnerable democracies is too dangerous, and that the "digital exhaust" detection PRO relies on is ordinary cybersecurity rather than the sensitive method the motion requires—leaving PRO's closing unable to answer either point.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): CON won the necessity-vs-cost clash. PRO needed to show sharing sensitive methods is required for real-time defense, but CON showed tear-line outputs can already stream at machine speed while protecting the engine. CON's adversarial-training/oracle argument - sharing a detector lets the enemy train to bypass it - directly undercut PRO's radar analogy, and PRO conceded the motion in R2 by proposing black-boxed sensors "without handing the raw methodology" to front-line states. That admission plus the unresolved contradiction between local scanning vs pre-launch foreign detection left PRO without a live path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B R1/R2 exposing the internal contradiction [B2.3-B2.6] and the fatal concession in [B4.3] where PRO admits not sharing raw methods, plus the oracle-query argument that a shared detector becomes a training target for adversaries.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won by decisively controlling the technical definitions and strategic implications of sharing AI detection methods. They successfully distinguished between sharing threat indicators (outputs) and sharing the underlying algorithms (methods), proving that the former can be done at machine speed without the catastrophic risks of the latter. Side A's use of AI-specific concepts like adversarial training and oracle attacks on black-boxed models effectively neutralized Side B's attempts to mitigate the risks of sharing. Side B's eventual concession that adversaries would simply adapt to shared methods, framed as an inevitable arms race, failed to overcome Side A's argument that sharing the blueprint drastically accelerates the adversary's ability to bypass defenses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled Side B's "black-boxed sensor" compromise by explaining how adversaries could use it as a testing oracle to mathematically map and bypass the detection boundaries, while also highlighting the asymmetric economics of AI adaptation versus intelligence development.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases: CON's strongest thread was the asymmetry argument (adversary loses a disposable bot net, democracy loses an irreplaceable, years-cultivated access point usable across many security domains) plus the weakest-link vulnerability of a broad multilateral grid. PRO's strongest thread was that "outputs-only" sharing is operationally hollow against mutating generative-AI campaigns, and that officials need verifiable methodological proof (not a bare "trust us" conclusion) to act domestically before an election. The decisive turn was PRO's Q1 pressure question, which caught CON in a real inconsistency: CON's opening ([A1.2]) called forensic classifiers safe, shareable "outputs," but Rebuttal 1 ([A2.1]) recharacterized classifiers as dangerous "methods" that hand over the playbook. CON's answer to this pressure ([A4.1]) didn't resolve the tension honestly — it quietly narrowed the "safe" category down to a bare "static footprint or bot IP," which PRO seized on in closing ("Look at what CON's alternative finally shrank to... That single admission decides this debate"). This made CON's fallback position look increasingly thin against PRO's mutation-based critique, while CON's own asymmetry and weakest-link points, though live, were never quite enough to offset that erosion. PRO adapted better across rounds, converting the pressure-question exchange into a clean closing frame, giving a marginally cleaner, more resolved path to the vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure question exposing CON's shift from "classifiers are safe outputs" (A1.2) to "classifiers are dangerous methods" (A2.1), followed by PRO's closing callback that CON's position had shrunk to a near-useless "static IP."
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO won the central clash on outputs vs methods. CON's defense depended on sharing "forensic classifiers / telemetry" safely [B1.2], but PRO exposed that in AI warfare a classifier IS the method [A2.1] and static outputs are obsolete against mutating personas [A2.2]. CON's attempt to retreat to static IPs [B4.1] proved PRO's pressure Q1. PRO also answered the weak-link and asymmetry concerns with source/method separation and decisive-moment weighing (election loss irreversible vs depreciating detection tradecraft), while CON never answered the legitimacy need for verifiable proof or how dynamic feeds avoid method-reveal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Q1 in Pressure Questions and follow-through in R2 [A3.1][A4.3][A5.1] – pinning B between "we can share classifiers" and "sharing classifiers gives away the playbook" – collapsed CON's alternative of sharing only outputs.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by building a more logically rigorous and technically grounded case regarding the mechanics of intelligence sharing. CON successfully maintained the crucial distinction between sharing intelligence outputs (actionable warnings, threat feeds) and sharing methods (the underlying exploits and tradecraft). Side B (PRO) relied heavily on rhetorical flair and attempted to collapse this distinction, arguing that allies need the methods to track mutating AI. However, PRO's case fatally unraveled in the final exchanges when they claimed a compromised detection method could simply be "rebuilt." CON decisively exploited this by clarifying that sharing the method reveals the exploit, causing the adversary to patch the vulnerability and permanently destroying the underlying, multi-purpose intelligence access (the tapped server or human source). CON's weighing of the asymmetry—trading irreplaceable, multi-domain cyber access to stop cheap, disposable AI botnets—was decisive and largely unanswered by PRO's broader democratic stakes arguments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled PRO's claim that compromised methods can be "rebuilt," clarifying that sharing the method burns the underlying, irreplaceable cyber access, which PRO consistently conflated with the detection algorithm itself.
