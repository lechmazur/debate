### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but B's case proved more resilient under direct pressure. A's strongest weapon was the "apparent contradiction" charge (novices can't spot weak framing yet can bypass learning via re-prompting), but B defused it cleanly in Rebuttal 2 by explaining that surface-cue iteration doesn't require independent diagnosis—resolving rather than conceding the tension. Conversely, when B pressed A for a concrete, enforceable boundary distinguishing legitimate "assistance" from substitution, A's answer ("deep course-specific synthesis") remained vague, and B's follow-up (students can simply upload notes/context and let the model synthesize, making "supplying context" indistinguishable from "performing the synthesis") went unanswered in A's closing, which pivoted to a rhetorically effective but non-responsive "bans fail too" move rather than rebutting the certification-validity problem. B's throughline—that disclosure plus "ruthless grading" judges the product, not the capacity the credential is meant to certify—was the more decisive, unresolved framing by the end. A's practical unenforceability and equity arguments remained strong but didn't offset this core conceptual loss. B's second rebuttal being clipped for length is a real execution cost, but the substantive ground it had already covered (iteration via surface cues, pedagogical checks vs. self-reported transcripts, the boundary critique) had already landed before the cutoff.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answer to A's "contradiction" pressure question (A1: iteration via surface-cue selection rather than independent diagnosis) combined with its attack on A's "course-specific synthesis" boundary (B4.5–B4.6) was the most decisive exchange, neutralizing A's sharpest attack while exposing that A's core safeguard wasn't actually a constraint.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON won the certification clash. PRO's enforceability and transparency points are strong, but CON showed permission legally authorizes the substitution bans try to prevent, and product-quality grading cannot certify independent construction. PRESSURE answers were decisive: B showed students can outsource flaw-diagnosis itself via rubric-pasting, undercutting PRO's claim that strict grading forces higher-order critique, while PRO's "editorial judgment" concession proved CON's worry about what most coursework would actually certify. Clipped R2 cost CON some completeness, not the core win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1/A1 – B directly defused A's "must diagnose to prompt a fix" challenge by showing pasting the rubric and asking AI to identify weaknesses and generate alternatives yields improvement by surface selection, collapsing PRO's higher-order struggle claim.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully dismantling PRO's core mechanism. PRO relied on accountability for accuracy and reasoning to ensure cognitive rigor, but CON persuasively argued that iterative AI prompting can produce a flawless final product without the student performing the underlying cognitive work. CON's weighing in the closing, contrasting a leaky rule that preserves educational norms with PRO's policy that officially institutionalizes invalid assessment, provided a cleaner resolution to the enforcement clash. PRO's practical arguments about the impossibility of bans were strong, but CON's epistemic critique of what a university degree actually certifies carried the day.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A1), where it answered PRO's pressure question by explaining how a student can paste a rubric and ask the AI to identify weaknesses, effectively neutralizing PRO's claim that students must independently diagnose flaws to use AI effectively.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B built a tighter, more consistent case around the claim that drafting friction is itself the cognitive work being assessed, and used the Pressure Questions round to expose a real tension in A's position: A claimed instructors cannot reliably detect AI in polished prose, yet also claimed they could "distinguish permitted from excessive use." A's answer (A4.1-A4.2) tried to resolve this with staged drafts and spot-checks, but in doing so conceded that the motion permits AI to generate "structural outlines or first-draft prose" in most coursework. B seized on this concession decisively, drawing the vivid and damaging comparison that a human tutor doing the same thing would be called fraud, and that this is categorically different from Socratic scaffolding. A's closing pivoted to "what should universities certify" without directly rebutting the tutor/fraud analogy, leaving B's sharpest point live and unanswered. A's opening was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost. Both sides argued competently and stayed on the actual motion, but B's progression across rounds — introducing the detection contradiction, pressing it with pointed questions, and crystallizing the concession in rebuttal and closing — gave the clearer, more resolved path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1-B4.3), which used A's own concession that AI may generate "structural outlines or first-draft prose" to argue that this exceeds what human tutors are permitted to do, directly undercutting A's claim that accountability safeguards preserve substantive authorship.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): B won the central clash on what drafting does. PRO's best defense that assistance is light feedback collapsed when A admitted in A4.2 that the motion does permit AI-generated structural outlines and first-draft prose in most coursework while also conceding brief disclosure alone does not prove mastery. That concession let CON's core arguments land: outlining/objecting/revising is the thinking itself, recognition ≠ mastery, and tutor norms already forbid what PRO now allows. On enforcement, PRO's claim that bans are unenforceable was turned around via B2.3/B4.2 - once machine drafts are legal, version history becomes meaningless and spot-checks cannot ensure accountability at scale.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.3 enforcement contradiction plus B4.1-B4.3: B forced PRO to admit AI may write outlines/first drafts and that disclosure doesn't prove mastery except 'if questioned', then leveraged tutor analogy and version-history baseline to show PRO's safeguards are rarer and weaker than the unaided norm it replaces.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively trapping Side A on the definition of "drafting." Through sharp pressure questions, Side B forced Side A to concede that the motion permits AI to generate structural outlines and first-draft prose. Side B then effectively demonstrated that this concession collapses Side A's accountability mechanisms: if pasting AI drafts is legal, version-history checks become meaningless, and instructors in large classes cannot orally question enough students to verify actual mastery. Side B also cleanly neutralized Side A's comparison to human tutors by distinguishing between Socratic scaffolding and generating the actual text. Side A's arguments on enforceability were strong, but they could not recover from the structural damage to their accountability framework in the later rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled Side A's answers to the pressure questions by showing that legalizing AI first drafts renders version history useless and reduces accountability to easily evaded spot-checks in large lecture courses.
