### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): The debate turned on a dilemma CON established early and sustained: any Arctic treaty robust enough to matter (binding militarization limits, outside enforcement) would be rejected by major powers or override Indigenous/sovereign authority, while any version PRO could plausibly defend (Indigenous consent as precondition, no outside veto, limits only in "agreed zones") collapses into the status quo with new letterhead. PRO's opening and first rebuttal built a coherent Antarctic analogy and correctly noted gaps in current Arctic governance (non-binding Council, US non-ratification of UNCLOS), but under pressure PRO's concessions (A2.1, A4.4) let CON land the decisive line in B4.3: "the enforcement currency is identical; only the stationery changes." PRO's closing tried to spin this as a concession to PRO's favor, but never showed concretely why a new forum succeeds where existing binding SAR, spill, and shipping agreements already operate, especially given CON's point that a NATO-Russia crisis would suspend any treaty regardless of its label. CON's pressure questions (B3.1/B3.2) were sharply targeted at this exact tension and were answered by PRO but not neutralized. CON's closing cleanly crystallized the standing dilemma, while PRO's closing restated its framing without closing the gap CON exposed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.3 ("the enforcement currency is identical; only the stationery changes") directly undercut PRO's core claim that a binding treaty offers a materially different remedy than the current patchwork, and PRO's closing attempt to reframe this as a concession did not resolve the underlying problem.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): After concessions on sovereignty, Indigenous self-government, and non-total demilitarization, PRO’s Antarctic-style treaty collapsed into either a superficial relabeling of existing patchwork agreements or an unenforceable wish list. CON effectively showed that the Antarctic analogy fails because the Arctic is inhabited, that current targeted binding law already addresses shipping, search and rescue, and spill liability, and that PRO’s compulsory dispute settlement offers no greater enforcement leverage against a defiant great power than the sanctions and countermeasures already available. CON kept the decisive clash focused on mechanism rather than aspiration, and PRO’s closing reliance on “illegality” as a distinguishing feature did not overcome CON’s argument that the enforcement currency is identical.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s exposure across Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 that PRO’s necessary concessions gutted the Antarctic model: if the treaty preserves settled sovereignty, Indigenous self-government, local development authority, and only limits militarization in “agreed zones,” what remains is not an Antarctic constitution but the existing Arctic order with stronger adjectives.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Side B decisively won the clash over the Antarctic analogy. By highlighting the Arctic's permanent populations, Indigenous rights, and existing binding agreements, B forced A into a dilemma: either apply an uninhabited-wilderness model to a populated region, or concede so many exceptions that the treaty ceases to be similar to the Antarctic Treaty System. B effectively showed that A's adapted model relied on the exact same enforcement mechanisms as current international law, while adding bureaucratic friction and risking great-power rejection. B's grounding in specific Arctic realities was superior to A's more generic international law claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where they systematically dismantled Side A's retreat, showing that every concession A made to protect Indigenous rights and local sovereignty stripped away the defining features of the Antarctic model, reducing it to a redundant layer over the existing Arctic-led order.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides executed the format cleanly, with properly structured Q&A rounds. PRO built a coherent case that the Antarctic model could be borrowed as a legal technique for the contested ocean core without touching land sovereignty, and used the pressure round effectively to force CON to admit that incremental deals also depend on non-coerced compliance. But CON's rebuttals were sharper and more damaging: it exploited PRO's own scoping answer (freeze/demilitarize/ban only the "central Arctic Ocean") to show that the real flashpoints—the Northwest Passage, Barents shelf disputes, the Kola military bastion—sit outside or beside that narrowed core, producing a dilemma PRO never squarely resolved: the treaty is either too narrow to touch the actual competition or too broad to ever be ratified. CON's concrete, vivid lines ("a red line a nuclear power won't sign is not a red line; it is a communiqué") and its turn of PRO's own pressure question back on itself ("they wouldn't; that is the point") landed as decisive, unanswered blows. PRO's closing reasserted a "default behavior" framing but did not directly rebut the narrow/broad dichotomy, leaving CON's central structural critique live at the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 response to PRO's scoped treaty definition (A4.1) — pointing out that the Northwest Passage, Barents shelf, and military bastions sit in or beside the excluded zones, producing the "irrelevant or impossible" dilemma — was the most decisive exchange, and PRO never fully neutralized it in the closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing a persistent feasibility dilemma that PRO never resolved: an Antarctic-style treaty must either cover the actual flashpoints—making it unratifiable by Russia and the United States—or shrink to a narrow "shared ocean core," rendering it irrelevant to the militarization and territorial competition it claims to prevent. CON's concrete inventory of working incremental governance (Barents Sea settlement, Hans Island resolution, Polar Code, hotlines) offered a credible alternative, while the "red line a nuclear power won't sign is a communiqué" line crystallized the enforcement gap. PRO's elegant "flip the default" framing and Antarctic precedent were rhetorically powerful, but their admission that no treaty can physically compel a non-ratifier undercut the claim that bindingness materially changes behavior in the Arctic's hard-security context.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4), where they answered PRO's pressure questions and landed the coverage-versus-compliance dilemma alongside the "red line/communiqué" line, decisively shifting the burden back onto PRO to show the treaty could both matter and bind.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing the fatal geographic and geopolitical flaw in Side A's proposal. Side B's "coverage versus compliance" dilemma effectively demonstrated that a treaty restricted to the central ocean would miss the actual flashpoints (like the Northwest Passage, continental shelves, and the Kola Peninsula), while a treaty covering those areas would never be ratified by the bordering nuclear powers. Side A's theoretical argument about changing the "baseline" of international law was less persuasive than Side B's grounded defense of adaptive, incremental governance that actually accounts for the Arctic's inhabited and strategic reality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled Side A's "shared ocean core" compromise by pointing out that the actual disputes and military bastions sit in or beside the excluded zones, rendering the proposed treaty either irrelevant to the competition or impossible to ratify.
