### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): A built a consistent throughline: large-audience influencers are commercial broadcasters exploiting parasocial trust, and the fix (spoken/visible disclosure) is cheap and proportionate. A's pressure questions exposed a real tension in B's case—claiming audiences are "reflexively skeptical" while also claiming spoken disclosure "destroys authenticity"—and B's answer (that authenticity is about a mediated relationship, not awareness) actually conceded the deception point, which A exploited decisively in closing ("that value is built entirely on consumer deception"). B never directly answered A's strongest rebuttal that broadcast disclosure rules don't require scheduled breaks or licensee structures (the "couch endorsement" example), instead reasserting the same structural objection in closing without engaging it. B's threshold-gaming and enforcement-architecture concerns were substantive and only partially answered, but they weren't enough to offset A's cleaner reframe and sharper closing synthesis. Both sides executed the Q&A format correctly and professionally.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Q1 pressure question (users "recognize commercial context instantly" vs. disclosure "breaks authenticity") forced B into an admission that the perceived value of influencer endorsements depends on concealing the paid relationship — which A then converted into the decisive closing line that such value "is built entirely on consumer deception."
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): Side B won by framing strictness as equivalent consumer protection rather than mechanically converting influencers into broadcast licensees. Side A repeatedly relied on that parity-of-form strawman, while its strongest threshold and compliance objections were answered through scalable criteria and the motion's focus on large commercial creators. Side A's concession that viewers may not know which recommendation is paid ultimately reinforced B's case for explicit disclosure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pressure on authenticity was decisive: if identifying a paid endorsement destroys its value, that value partly depends on concealing the commercial relationship. Side A distinguished general commercial awareness from knowledge of a specific sponsorship, but that distinction strengthened rather than defeated the need for clear disclosure.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (PRO)): B won by narrowing to large-audience commercial creators, defeating A's crush-enforcement and hobbyist strawman, and turning A's authenticity defense into a concession that the pitch's value depends on preserving an unmediated-friend illusion after a paid contract. A scored real points on format mismatch, threshold arbitrage, and existing "clear and conspicuous" standards, but never neutralized B's comparative claim that broadcast-scale reach warrants unmistakable verbal/visual honesty rather than parity of broadcast architecture alone.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's A1/A2 and follow-on in R2/closing: if naming the paid pitch "breaks authenticity," and general skepticism still fails to identify which product is contracted, authenticity is the deception mechanism—not a speech interest worth protecting.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON)): Side A built a clean equivalence principle (equal influence, equal accountability) but never resolved the operational tension B exposed: when A bit the bullet on the "two-year-old gifted microphone" example in A2.3, it implied broadcast-style strictness would require disclosing even passive, non-endorsing use of old gifts—an absurd overreach. B's Pressure Question forced A to choose between abandoning that example or accepting its implications, and A's attempted clarification in Rebuttal 2 (distinguishing "endorsement" from "mere background use") directly contradicted its own earlier example, which B caught cleanly in Rebuttal 2 (B4.1-B4.2). B also sustained the sharper point that current consumer-protection law already bans buried #ads, so A's retreat to "clear, conspicuous notice" collapses into describing existing practice rather than justifying a distinctive new import of broadcast mechanics. B's medium-specific argument (controlled studio vs. live unscripted streaming, no lawyer can pre-vet spontaneous speech) was original, concrete, and never squarely neutralized by A, who mostly answered with "large influencers have resources" without addressing the live/unscripted nature of the content. Both sides executed the format well (clear A1/A2 answers), but B's rebuttals did more damage to A's core route to victory, making B's case the comparatively cleaner, more resolved position by the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1-B4.2), which caught A's attempt in A4.1 to redefine A2.3 as covering only "active endorsement," directly quoting A's own earlier language to show the microphone example was about passive ongoing use—exposing a genuine self-contradiction that A never fully resolved.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won because equal strictness need not mean mechanically importing every broadcast procedure. Its endorsement-versus-incidental-background distinction largely defeated Side A's wardrobe-inventory and blanket-disclaimer scenario, while the comparable reach, trust, and consumer-harm case remained intact. Side A sharply exposed B's misleading claim that the alternative was merely voluntary, but relied too heavily on broad, unsupported assertions about broadcast practices and did not show why clear disclosure of a material connection is unworkable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's distinction that disclosure attaches to an endorsement rather than every gifted object in view was decisive. It narrowed the proposed rule enough to blunt Side A's disclosure-fatigue hypothetical, although B's earlier microphone wording made the clarification less clean than it could have been.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (PRO)): A won by consistently forcing B into a dilemma: either "as strict as traditional media" already collapses into existing clear-disclosure rules (so the motion adds nothing) or it imports unworkable broadcast mechanics (ongoing gifted-item liability + pre-screening unscripted life) that produce disclosure fatigue. B's equality-of-influence frame was clean but never escaped the medium-fit problem or the retreat-and-rewrite cycle on the microphone example; A made the decisive comparative case clearer across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.6) locking B's microphone concession against B's later attempted rewrite and showing that both horns of the dilemma sink the proposition.
