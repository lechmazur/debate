### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, mechanism-driven cases and executed the pressure-question format cleanly with direct A1/A2 answers. PRO's "concentration not churn" story (one accountable senior plus software replaces a junior team) and its spreadsheet-distinction rebuttal were sharp and original. But CON's counter-mechanism—that liability and accountability are themselves scarce, non-scalable bottlenecks that force firms to add reviewers, client-facing staff, and exception-handlers as volume grows—directly answered PRO's central pressure question and was never fully dismantled. More decisively, CON's reframe that PRO's own metric (fewer staff per unit output) measures productivity, not net employment loss, exposed a real gap in PRO's proof of "net" displacement, and PRO's closing only reasserted the ratio argument rather than closing that gap with anything beyond restatement. CON also landed an unanswered structural hit: PRO's concrete policy proposals (portable benefits, wage insurance, apprenticeships) are indistinguishable from the general mobility policy CON itself endorses, which undercuts the practical stakes of the motion's policy clause. PRO never engaged that "no daylight" critique in its closing. On balance, CON's later-round framing made the dispute easier to resolve and left fewer live, unanswered threats to its position than PRO's case retained.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange where PRO pressed "why would liability-driven bottlenecks cause more hiring rather than squeezing more output through the same few accountable seniors" (A3.1/A4.4) against CON's direct A1 answer (B4.1) that review time and client/competitive pressure force added reviewer and exception-handling labor—this was the fulcrum of the debate, and CON's answer, paired with its productivity-vs-total-employment reframe, left PRO's concentration logic asserted but not clearly victorious.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by separating productivity per unit from aggregate employment. A persuasively showed why individual teams may shed junior roles and made the stronger insurance case for flexible preparation, but never adequately established that bounded demand would outweigh new clients, expanded output, and human accountability roles. B directly answered both pressure questions and showed that A's recurring examples proved leaner production, not net white-collar job loss.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal was decisive: it identified A's fewer-workers-per-output metric as productivity rather than evidence of total employment decline, then explained who could hire displaced workers and how entry-level training could shift toward verification, intake, and client work.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won the decisive metric: net white-collar employment versus leaner teams. PRO’s firm unbundling story (keep seniors, cut juniors) was vivid and sticky, but repeatedly proved composition/productivity, not aggregate elimination once demand, accountability, and quality bottlenecks expand. CON answered pressure with a coherent bottleneck-and-recomposition path; PRO’s own 5–10-year “fewer entry staff per unit” test and largely general mobility policy list undercut the motion’s “likely net loss, prepare for collapse” package. Closing crystallized that clash cleanly for A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3–A4.5: fewer juniors per unit is productivity (spreadsheets), and PRO’s concrete programs largely collapse into the flexible mobility CON already defended.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly without fabricated evidence, but A built a tighter structural case: info-processing tasks are the substance of white-collar work, creation is concentrated/senior while destruction is broad/junior, and partial automation shrinks headcount within surviving occupations. A's closing crystallized the decisive gap — B's entire rebuttal rests on an unproven assumption that new demand will grow fast enough to outpace substitution, a point B never quantifies or secures beyond analogy. B's historical examples (spreadsheets, word processors) were double-edged — B even conceded word processors "destroyed typing pools," undercutting its own precedent. In the pressure round, A's questions forced B to concede that job replacement won't be "same title, same pay," which A exploited effectively, while A's own answers (especially reframing the hiring-freeze question as proof of real substitution rather than policy-induced panic) neutralized B's strongest trap. B fought well and kept the "lump of labor" framing alive, but never closed the demand-sufficiency gap A kept pressing, leaving A with the cleaner, more decisive route to the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing line "CON shows demand can grow. That is not enough" crystallized the unresolved core clash — B never established that demand growth would outpace substitution, which A had been pressing since Rebuttal 1's ratio argument.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): A wins a competitive debate by showing that partial automation can reduce headcount without eliminating occupations and that CON's examples establish possible demand growth, not growth sufficient to offset sharply lower labor needs per unit. More decisively, A defends early preparation as low-regret support rather than a restriction on deployment. B's claim that training, portable benefits, and disclosure would themselves cause hiring freezes remains largely asserted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response to the hiring-freeze objection was most decisive: a firm freezing junior recruitment because AI can replace junior tasks demonstrates the displacement risk, while portable benefits and transition training do not themselves prevent hiring. This left B without a persuasive downside to early preparation.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): B won by keeping the decisive clash on substitution intensity and workers-per-unit output, not mere task speedup. Historical tool analogies were neutralized as amplification needing operators, while every CON expansion example still showed fewer people doing more. A never showed demand growth would be large and broad enough to offset the junior/mid-level losses, and B’s pressure answers plus closing crystallized net loss and low-regret insurance without throttling.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s R1 tool-vs-substitute split (spreadsheets still needed human operators; AI composes) plus R2 answers that entrants and new markets still hire far fewer per output unit.
