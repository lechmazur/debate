Side B also preserved itemization while defeating the idea that separate accounting justifies later revelation. Side A’s clipped opening was an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response to conditional charges was decisive: a solo diner can see the solo price, while a large party can see its applicable all-in price before ordering. This exposed Side A’s one-price-or-formula dilemma as a straw man and returned the debate to timing rather than accounting labels.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON)): PRO maintained the decisive timing frame - comparison and consent must occur at the menu, not after ordering - and defused CON's strongest live route on conditional auto-gratuities by showing applicable-price display ("party of six: $21 all-in") is ordinary menu practice, not checkout revelation. CON's singular-price literalism and food-vs-transaction split never explained why a payment the diner must make to eat is not price, and its clipped opening cost clarity. PRO also preserved itemization within all-in pricing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 answers to B's pressure questions - showing conditional all-in prices solve the large-party problem and that "$18 includes $3 service" is one displayed price, not a formula - directly neutralized CON's core conditionality objection.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Large 4 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B wins decisively by framing the debate around the timing of consumer consent and the reality of price comparison. Side B effectively dismantles Side A's primary argument—that conditional charges like large-party gratuities cannot be included in a single menu price without creating confusing formulas—by pointing out that menus already handle conditional pricing (e.g., kids' menus, lunch specials) and that displaying the applicable total before ordering is fundamentally different from revealing it on the bill after eating. Side A's reliance on a pedantic reading of "the displayed menu price" as a single universal number felt evasive and failed to address the core harm of bait-and-switch pricing. Furthermore, Side A suffered an execution cost with a clipped opening. Side B's rhetorical framing was highly effective, memorable, and grounded in common sense.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they crystallize the temporal distinction: reading a menu note happens before the commitment to order, whereas the bill arrives after the diner is already trapped, exposing Side A's defense of bill-stage disclosure as protecting "itemization after commitment."
