- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but B built and sustained a sharper epistemic objection that A never fully neutralized: that "the principal reason" a complex model relied on is often not a single verifiable fact but an unstable post-hoc approximation, so any court-mandated explanation either collapses into existing data-disclosure duties (redundant) or manufactures false certainty about causation (dangerous, since it can misdirect correction and launder bias as a "neutral" stated reason). - **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): CON won a competitive debate by making the decisive clash easier to judge: for complex systems, a court-ordered “principal reason” is often an unverifiable post-hoc ranking, so the right either collapses into existing data/file duties CON already accepts or becomes a legal fiction courts cannot faithfully test. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly within the rules, but CON built the more resilient structural case. PRO's strongest mechanism — a "principal factors" explanation — was pressed hard in B3.1 (unfalsifiable boilerplate) and B3.2 (what legal hook authorizes this outside statutory contexts), and PRO's answers (A4.1-A4.2) relied on vague appeals to "due process" and "arbitrary denial" prohibitions that don't clearly exist for private landlords, lenders, or clinics. CON converted this into a decisive fork in B4.4-B4.6: wherever a statute already forbids the denial, a reason duty already exists (redundant); wherever none exists, courts must invent the wrong and remedy together (illegitimate judicial overreach). PRO's closing never directly engaged this fork, instead restating the general "unanswerable no" framing rhetorically rather than logically rebutting it. CON also successfully reframed PRO's own verification tools (pay stubs, vendor logs, applicant comparisons) as exactly the audit/file-correction machinery CON proposed, suggesting the explanation itself added nothing beyond those tools. PRO had strong moments (silence vs. stated-reason asymmetry, the "insufficient income" example), but CON's progression across rounds was tighter, more responsive to pressure, and ended on an unanswered structural objection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.4-B4.6 fork — "every version of this right that works is a statute; every version courts can issue alone is a story" — built directly on PRO's A2.1 concession and PRO's closing never squarely answered it.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): CON won by converting PRO’s own narrowing—“factors the decision-maker will stand behind”—into a live institutional and epistemic fork: judicial explanations become true-but-unfalsifiable stories unless statutes standardize models and forms, while the tools that actually catch errors and proxies are file access and outcome audits. PRO kept a strong moral stake (an answerable denial vs silence) and usefully argued complements, but never neutralized redundancy-where-statutes-exist / invention-where-they-do-not, nor showed why the explanation itself does work the audit machinery is not already doing. A’s comparative close was cleaner and decided the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 1–2 treatment of B2.1: “stand behind” reasons + credit analogy become post-hoc boilerplate unless legislatures constrain models; verification tools PRO invokes are already CON’s alternatives.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Muse Wonder 1.0 (high) (PRO)): Side A built a decisive logical fork that Side B could not escape: either the right to explanation relies on existing statutory prohibitions (making it redundant, as discovery and burden-shifting already apply) or courts are inventing new substantive rights without a legal hook. Side A also effectively neutralized Side B's "file correction" argument by pointing out that full file access allows correction without needing the model to pinpoint the exact trigger, and that the verification tools Side B relied on to police boilerplate were actually the audit mechanisms Side A advocated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's answers to the pressure questions, showing that Side B's verification mechanisms were just Side A's audits, and that Side B's legal hook conceded the redundancy/judicial overreach fork.
