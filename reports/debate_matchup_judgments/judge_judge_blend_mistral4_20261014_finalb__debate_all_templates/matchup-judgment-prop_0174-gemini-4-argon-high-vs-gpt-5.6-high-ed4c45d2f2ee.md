CON exploited this in Rebuttal 2 and Closing to show the entire policy collapses into accounting compliance rather than guaranteed safety outcomes—an appropriation, however reclassified or inflated, satisfies the lock with no actual repair required. CON did suffer real execution costs from being clipped twice for exceeding word limits, losing some closing flourishes, which tempers the margin, but the core substantive exchange still favors CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's concession in Rebuttal 2 ([B4.4]) that lawmakers can "unlock" megaproject approval "tomorrow" by tripling the budget "today," which CON seized on in Rebuttal 2 and Closing to show the fiscal lock guarantees spending allocation rather than actual safety remediation, undermining PRO's core causal claim that the rule forces infrastructure to be fixed before new projects proceed.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 4 Argon (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but CON (A) landed the more decisive blow by exposing a genuine structural flaw in PRO's (B) sequencing mechanism: PRO insisted maintenance must get "first claim" on scarce engineers and contractors, yet also claimed urgent megaprojects could be approved "in the same budget cycle," a tension CON's pressure question sharpened and PRO never fully resolved. CON's follow-up—that chemically dead bridges or urgently needed storm barriers could be blocked merely because legislators doubled rather than tripled the maintenance budget—forced PRO to bite a bullet ("replaced... but only after the broader network's maintenance commitment is secured") that undercuts PRO's own safety-first framing. CON's closing crystallized this unresolved tension cleanly, while PRO's closing mostly restated its priority claim without directly re-litigating the condemned-bridge/storm-barrier edge case. This made CON's path to victory clearer and more resolved by the end.
