### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases—B on kinetic-energy physics and "street as enforcement," A on concentrated harm, deterrence, and incapacitation—and both followed the Q&A format correctly. The decisive divergence came in the later rounds: A's pressure question forced B to defend how design-first policy beats a fast, universal statute given multi-year rebuild timelines. B's answer split "lower defaults" from "redesign," claiming defaults alone work "tonight" citywide. But B had earlier conceded (B2.6) that a posted number is merely "advisory" while the lane geometry stays wide — the exact reason B gave for ranking redesign above penalties in the first place. A explicitly caught this (A4.6, reiterated in closing) and B never reconciled the tension, instead just re-asserting the "tonight" claim in its closing without addressing the contradiction. A also landed a clever, largely unanswered selection-effect point (A2.1) that penalties' prevented deaths are invisible by construction, which B answered only with an unsupported "tail not bulk" assertion. B's foundational causal story about systemic speed/design remained intuitively strong throughout and was never refuted on its physics, but A's sharper, more consistent rebuttal work exposed a real crack in B's implementation defense that tipped the comparative case A's way.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's callback in Rebuttal 2/Closing (A4.6, A5.3) to B's own earlier concession that speed-limit signs are "advisory" without redesign (B2.6), used against B's later claim that lower defaults work "tonight" — an internal contradiction B never resolved.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): PRO built the stronger comparative case by anchoring the debate in kinetic energy and the inevitability of ordinary lapses, showing that lower defaults and redesign make crashes survivable before they happen while penalties arrive too late for the first unplanned crash. CON's deterrence and incapacitation framework was analytically sharp but conceded that harsh penalties are not built for first-time lapses on legal fast streets, leaving its comparative advantage resting on an unquantified share of chosen-violation deaths that PRO plausibly characterized as the tail rather than the body. PRO effectively neutralized CON's delay and untreated-arterial objections by noting citywide defaults take effect immediately and that even impaired drivers arrive slower when geometry constrains them. The decisive clash resolved in PRO's favor: the tool that changes whether an impact is survivable outranks the tool that only prices it afterward.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal answers (A4.1–A4.3), where PRO showed that lower defaults apply citywide immediately, that redesigned geometry slows even non-compliant drivers, and that leaving streets lethal until every offender is punished is the wrong ranking—directly undermining CON's "untreated arterial" and "advisory limit" pressure.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A built a much stronger causal story around the physics of crashes and the reality of human error. By distinguishing between "chosen violations" and "ordinary lapses," Side A effectively neutralized Side B's deterrence argument, pointing out that penalties cannot prevent the first crash of a distracted or late driver, whereas street design makes that same crash survivable. Side A's rhetorical framing of the "everyday geometry of death" was highly persuasive, epistemically grounded, and consistently applied across all speeches, making the comparative choice much clearer for the reader.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, specifically the point that "geometry does not need to sober anyone" but simply reduces the speed at which an impaired driver arrives, brilliantly dismantled Side B's claim that street redesign is useless against drunk or racing drivers.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued with unusual discipline—clean A1/A2 and Q1/Q2 formatting, punchy aphorisms, and sustained clash on the same crux (whether harsher post-crash penalties add deterrence, and whether lower defaults/redesign work on unrebuilt streets). But B's case held together more coherently under pressure. A's central load-bearing claim—that ordinary speeding tickets only "bite" because a harsher ladder of post-crash ruin exists above them—was asserted repeatedly but never actually explained mechanistically, while B's counter (tickets deter through proximity: the stop, fine, and points, independent of sentencing severity for fatal crashes) is the more plausible real-world mechanism. More decisively, A conceded in A2.4 that "people already ignoring jail also ignore a bump," which B correctly weaponized in the closing: if the worst offenders already treat existing penalties as irrelevant, an escalation in harshness cannot be doing the marginal work A claims. A's attempt to recover ("the unrebuilt city is exactly where a known ruinous price is the only citywide instrument left") never resolves this internal contradiction—if that price is already ignored, adding harshness to it changes nothing, whereas B's design/physics mechanism ("a street that will not hold forty removes the killing speed whether anyone prices a sentence or not") works regardless of the offender's psychology. B also correctly reframed the motion as a ranking/prioritization question rather than abolition of penalties, blunting A's "suggestions cannot punish" rhetoric. A's geographic-incompleteness point about unrebuilt streets was a real and serious challenge, but B's answer (citywide immediate default + ordinary enforcement, with redesign phased onto high-injury streets) adequately answered it without needing fresh evidence. The debate was close and both sides wrote strong, quotable prose, but B's mechanism was more resistant to its own internal contradictions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's closing exploitation of A's own concession that "worst drivers already treat jail as optional," used to show that ranking harsher post-crash penalties above prevention cannot logically add deterrence value A claims for it.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by showing that harsher post-crash penalties arrive too late to prevent deaths and cannot deter the worst offenders that Side B itself admits already ignore jail. Side A’s distinction—that existing sanctions can remain while prioritizing prevention—defanged the claim that tickets require ever-harsher ladders of ruin, and the immediacy of citywide lower speed limits answered Side B’s critique that redesign is slow and incomplete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 turned Side B’s concession that worst offenders ignore existing jail time into a reason harsher penalties fail, while showing that lower defaults and street geometry remove killing speed regardless of whether reckless drivers price a sentence.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by sharply distinguishing between existing post-crash penalties and the motion's specific focus on *harsher* penalties. Side A successfully proves that ordinary enforcement of lower speed limits does not require escalating post-crash ruin to remain credible, and that the worst offenders (who Side B admits already ignore jail) are not deterred by harsher sentences. Side B's "ladder" argument collapses under this distinction, as it conflates maintaining current penalties with the motion's mandate to prioritize making them harsher. Side A's logic is airtight, its rebuttals are devastating, and it controls the core clash from start to finish.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of the "ladder" argument in Rebuttal 2, clarifying that ordinary tickets derive their bite from proximity and existing rules, completely neutralizing Side B's claim that prevention cannot outrank harsher post-crash punishment.
