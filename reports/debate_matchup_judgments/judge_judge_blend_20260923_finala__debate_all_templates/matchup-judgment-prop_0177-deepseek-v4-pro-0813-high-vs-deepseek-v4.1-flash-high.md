### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won by exploiting a self-inflicted wound in PRO's case. When pressed for a concrete durability threshold, PRO answered "two-year median function plus retransplantability" — a specific, honest answer to the pressure question, but one CON immediately turned into a decisive weapon: a two-year median with repeat procedures is a chronic bridge population, not a routine replacement for the organ shortage, and it still leaves patients competing for human organs. PRO's closing attempt to neutralize this by analogizing to imperfect human allografts was weak, since it ignored the scale gap (years vs. decades) and never answered CON's earlier point that a failed xenograft can sensitize a patient and foreclose a later human transplant. CON also raised a third-party consent/zoonotic-transmission problem that PRO's "conditional authorization with automatic suspension" answer only partially addressed — suspension is reactive and cannot undo exposure to non-consenting caregivers, a point CON pressed again in rebuttal 2 and closing without a real answer from PRO. CON's framing that PRO's own evidentiary logic is circular (needing monitored cohorts to prove the durability claimed necessary for "routine" status, but only being able to run those cohorts by treating patients as the evidence base) was never cleanly resolved by PRO. Both sides executed the Q&A format well, but CON's answers and follow-through better neutralized PRO's core claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turning PRO's own pressure-question answer (two-year median function, retransplantable) into proof that xenotransplantation would create a "chronic xenograft population" rather than end the shortage, combined with the unanswered point that suspension-based safeguards cannot undo third-party zoonotic exposure.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON won by keeping the motion’s conjunctive burden live: “routine solution to the shortage within fifteen years” plus accelerated pathways now. PRO was strong on urgency and the cohort-bind, but lost the decisive definitional and policy comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s conversion of PRO’s “meaningful survival over waiting” and later two-year median answer into a bridge-not-solution strike (esp. A2.1 and A4.3–A4.5), which left PRO defending provisional access rather than routine shortage resolution.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully forcing the debate onto the conjunctive burden the motion actually sets: "routine solution" AND "accelerated pathways now." From Rebuttal 1 onward, A caught B's language sliding from "routine solution" to "bridge therapy" and never let go of that gap, culminating in a closing that cleanly framed B's own concessions ("several years" of function, "not permanent") as proof the motion's first prong was unmet. A also landed an unanswered structural point: unlike a recalled drug, a failed living xenograft causes irreversible harm (sensitization, broad exposure) that accelerated pathways would multiply before the science is settled — B's regulation-vs-black-market reframe never squarely addressed this asymmetry. B's case was coherent and made good reframes (the "self-sealing standard" charge, waitlist mortality as comparator, versioning to avoid clock-resets), and its Pressure Questions were sharp, but its answers (A1/A2) conceded exactly the ground A needed: an admission that the deliverable is bridge-level survival, not durable routine replacement. That concession, repeatedly exploited by A, is the decisive thread of the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 point [A2.1] — catching that "months to years" as a bridge is not a "routine solution" and that failed xenografts create sensitization harm — set the frame that dominated the rest of the debate and that B's later concessions (B4.3, "routine never meant permanent") only reinforced rather than escaped.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by holding the proposition as written: “routine solution” to shortage requires durable, scalable replacement, not a conditional multi-year bridge. PRO’s strongest urgency and adaptive-path story never neutralized sensitization risk, biological follow-up time, or the late concession that payoff may be “several years” of function. PRO’s paralysis charge was rhetorically sharp but could not convert comparator-to-death into meeting the motion’s burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON A2.1/A4.5: months-to-years bridge plus possible sensitization is not a routine organ-shortage solution, and PRO’s later “several years” admission locks that gap.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively distinguishing between a temporary "bridge" therapy and a "routine solution" to the organ shortage, which was the core burden of the proposition. Side B's introduction of the sensitization risk—that a failed xenograft could ruin a patient's chances for a future human transplant—fatally undermined Side A's fallback argument that a multi-year bridge is still better than dialysis. Side A's rhetorical push on the moral cost of delay was strong, but Side B's biological and definitional rigor controlled the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they exposed that Side A's "versioning" and "bridge" concessions meant the 15-year window would only yield data on a single early construct, and that a failed bridge actively harms the patient via immunological sensitization.
