### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): PRO built a clean initial case (the lender/financial-anxiety example, a scoped trigger, a proposed notice text) and pressed CON well on the "sponsored ≠ AI-optimized" distinction. But CON's central dilemma—that any workable notice either restates "Sponsored" (because ordinary lookalikes/bid optimization/variant selection are the ad stack itself) or, to be meaningful, must gesture at the sensitive inference (e.g., predicted anxiety) that justified the rule in the first place—was never cleanly escaped. When pressed in Pressure Questions and Rebuttal 2, PRO's materiality test explicitly refused to exclude lookalikes and bid adjustment, conceding the very overbreadth CON attacked, while the offered notice text stayed generic enough to fit CON's "wallpaper" charge. CON also sustained a second live point—that inconsistent, platform-only enforcement makes silence read as false reassurance—that PRO answered only in principle, not in the specific institutional sense CON raised. PRO's closing tried to spin CON's Q1 answer as a "concession," but CON's closing directly and convincingly denied that framing, keeping its position coherent to the end. CON's Rebuttal 1 being clipped for length is a real but modest execution cost, not enough to offset the stronger, more consistently pressed structural argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Questions (Q1/Q2) forcing PRO to either produce the exact lender notice or admit its materiality test wouldn't exclude ordinary ad-tech tools—PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer confirmed both horns of CON's dilemma (redundant-with-"Sponsored" or sensitive-inference-revealing), which CON then closed out decisively.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A won by maintaining a cleaner live route to victory on the core clash: a method-level notice about AI-optimized, behaviorally targeted persuasion conveys information that a simple "Sponsored" tag does not. Side B never fully bridged the gap between knowing an ad is paid and knowing that a specific message variant was selected by AI around one's individual data. B's privacy dilemma was effectively exposed as a false dichotomy when A showed that broad, non-diagnostic disclosure ("using behavioral activity") can inform user caution without stamping sensitive inferences onto the feed. B's concerns about speech-sorting and false assurance remained plausible but largely speculative, and they could not overcome A's concrete account of user interests plus B's own execution cost from a clipped Rebuttal 1 that exceeded the word limit. A's closing crystallized the decisive comparison by leveraging B's pressure-question answers, while B's closing restated earlier points without resolving the live burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and closing dismantling of B's claimed trade-off between sensitive specificity and useless generality. A demonstrated that a standardized method notice ("AI optimized targeting or message selection using behavioral activity") adds meaningful, non-redundant information beyond "Sponsored" without exposing private psychological profiles, and held that line through the final speech.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully distinguishing between the advertiser's purchase of AI tools and the recipient's right to know how their behavioral data was used to optimize the message. Side B's argument that "Sponsored" already implies AI optimization failed to bridge the gap between the buyer's knowledge and the user's awareness. Furthermore, Side B suffered a notable execution cost when its first rebuttal was clipped mid-sentence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's direct answers to the pressure questions, where it quoted the exact label text and explained how it warns users without exposing a sensitive psychological dossier, decisively blunted Side B's core trap regarding specificity and privacy.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): The pivotal clash was whether an AI-optimization label tracks a genuinely distinct harm or merely a technique. CON's "narrow nightmare vs. broad rule" critique (B2.1) landed early and was never fully resolved: PRO's proposed definition covers even one-time automated selection, not just continuous adaptive experimentation, yet PRO kept defending the mandate by pointing to the more extreme adaptive case. When pressed on whether a human decision-maker choosing between two appeals based on predicted persuasive effect differs meaningfully from a model doing the same, PRO's answer ("human rules can be stated as rules; a buried ranking of susceptibility cannot") was essentially asserted rather than justified, and CON's closing correctly identified this as question-begging ("Calling one choice a rule and another a ranking does not establish a different relationship to persuasion"). CON also successfully turned PRO's enforcement argument back on itself by noting symmetric verification limits, while still holding the more targeted, technique-neutral alternative (disclose who paid, why targeted, sensitive-trait use) as a coherent competing framework. PRO's opening was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost. Both sides argued skillfully and stayed on topic, but CON built the more penetrating, harder-to-answer structural objection and sustained it cleanly through the pressure round and closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1 point that PRO's actual proposed definition (automated generation/selection scored for a user or micro-group) does not require the continuous adaptive experimentation PRO's examples relied on, later crystallized in B4.5/B5.2 showing PRO's human-rule/model-ranking distinction was asserted rather than established.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B demonstrated that the mandate is mismatched to the harm it invokes: either it is overbroad (bundling simple one-time predictions with intensive experimentation under one label) or underinclusive (letting functionally equivalent human persuasion escape). A failed to establish that labeling the technique conveys a recipient-relevant relationship comparable to "sponsored," and A's "buried ranking" distinction was blunted by B's argument that human-directed campaigns also select appeals for predicted persuasive effect.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal (B4) exposed the mismatch between PRO's narrow horror story of continuous adaptive experimentation and the broader mandate's actual scope, arguing that the label collapses meaningful differences while separating functionally equivalent persuasion, and asked what benefit justifies expanding disclosure to a machinery badge rather than the underlying targeting relationship.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully isolating the unique harm of AI-optimized persuasion—the opaque scoring and selection of message variants based on predicted individual susceptibility—and proving that Side B's proposed alternative (disclosing the targeting basis) completely fails to capture this dynamic. Side A's rebuttals were sharp, directly answering pressure questions and effectively dismantling Side B's equivalence between human media buying and algorithmic optimization. Side B's argument that the mandate was overbroad (catching one-time predictions) was noted, but Side A successfully argued that even one-time algorithmic ranking of susceptibility is a distinct, hidden relationship that users deserve to know about. Side A's closing crystallized the clash memorably and decisively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's pressure questions (A1 and A2 in Rebuttal 2), where A clearly distinguished between stateable human rules and a model's buried ranking of susceptibility, exposing the critical blind spot in B's alternative disclosure regime.
