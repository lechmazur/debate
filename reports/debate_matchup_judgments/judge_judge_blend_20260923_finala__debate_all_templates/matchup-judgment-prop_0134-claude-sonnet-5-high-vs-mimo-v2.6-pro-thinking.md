### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B narrowly won by sustaining the clearest causal path: restrictions remove the nightly-rental incentive, return at least some scarce housing, and prevent some future conversions, while the opposing income and spending losses are more substitutable and less severe than displacement. Side A sharply exposed uncertainty about how many units return and correctly distinguished outside visitor spending from resident spending, but its key claim that STR-heavy tourist cores are geographically separate from displacement was asserted rather than established. Its retreat from calling ownership exit modal, plus two clipped speeches, also weakened execution. B's unsupported 15-20% threshold and overclaim that fewer evictions were guaranteed kept the result close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to the geography argument was decisive: it argued that tourist-rich, transit-connected, amenity-heavy neighborhoods are often themselves active fronts of gentrification and displacement. Side A continued asserting that relief misses peripheral renters but never sufficiently answered why displacement within the regulated high-demand neighborhoods should be discounted.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing a decisive asymmetry in the severity of harms (irreversible displacement vs. substitutable income loss) and by trapping Side A in a contradiction regarding magnitude (if STRs are a large enough share to cause significant economic harms when banned, they are large enough to affect the housing supply). Side A struggled with word limits and leaned on a geographic mismatch that Side B reasonably contested.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (A2) masterfully neutralized Side A's strongest attack by explaining that while an owner-occupier sale is a one-time stock adjustment, STRs create a repeatable eviction churn, and restriction fundamentally stops the forward-looking incentive to evict.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): PRO kept a coherent mechanism that survives CON's best pressure: even if some units exit to owner-occupancy, the incentive to evict for nightly profit is removed, which is forward-looking displacement prevention. CON scored hard with the supply-return vs churn distinction and external-money injection point (resident spending is reallocation, visitor spending is injection), unanswered directly. But CON's geographic mismatch claim was weakly grounded and its two clipped turns hurt execution. PRO's irreversibility asymmetry - losing a home vs substitutable host/hotel income - remained live and won the comparative weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1-B2.6 exposing that A2.2 concedes the rent-supply return fails when units go to owner-occupiers/second homes and that resident vs visitor spending is not a one-for-one substitute.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side B won the central counterfactual. Side A repeatedly claimed that selling an STR to an owner-occupier shrinks the rental pool, but the unit was already outside the long-term rental pool; the sale instead returns housing from tourist use to residential use, while leasing adds rental supply directly. B also applied A's scale argument symmetrically and gave stronger weighing between partly substitutable tourism income and durable displacement. A made the debate competitive through its uncertainty argument and its sharp point that citywide hotel substitution may still drain neighborhood spending. B's repeated clipping was a meaningful execution cost, but not enough to overcome its stronger causal account.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to the sale objection was decisive: restrictions need not force every unit into a lease because either leasing directly expands rental supply or a sale restores the unit to residential occupancy. Side A's answer treated an existing STR as though it had already been a long-term rental, weakening the premise of its closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side A wins by systematically dismantling Side B's causal link to rent reduction. By forcing Side B to concede that restricted units may be sold to owner-occupiers, Side A successfully argues that this shrinks the long-term rental pool, directly undermining the proposition's requirement that the policy 'reduces rents.' Side A also executes flawlessly, while Side B suffers from clipping in nearly every speech.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 pointing out that a sale to an owner-occupier shrinks the rental pool, meaning the displaced renter gets nothing while the owner loses income.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): PRO kept the comparative frame alive: if STRs are a small share, aggregate income loss is small too, while at near-zero vacancy marginal units determine displacement. CON’s strongest hit - that sale to owner-occupier removes a unit from rentals - damages PRO’s original lease mechanism, but PRO’s reframe that ending nightly tourist bidding helps renters even via sale plus the reversible flow vs irreversible stock loss (home/school/network) outweighed CON’s certainty-vs-uncertainty weighing. Clipping costs for A prevent a larger margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s scale-consistency turn [A2.1] + irreversible tenure vs reversible dollars [A4.4] vs CON’s rental-pool shrink attack [B2.2]/[B4.4] that PRO sale = no lease.
