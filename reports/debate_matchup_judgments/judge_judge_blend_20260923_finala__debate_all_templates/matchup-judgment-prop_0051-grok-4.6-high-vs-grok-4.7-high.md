- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by showing that batch identity and factory traceability are prerequisites for meaningful testing, recalls, audits, and bans. B also demanded that transparency itself prove wages, while A more plausibly defended traceability as the necessary first step for enforcement. B's targeted-sampling alternative remained useful but did not fully answer evasive storefronts or hidden production chains.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange was its response that sampling without batch and factory identity can delist a storefront while leaving the production run and mill untouched. Side B's payment-identity answer mitigated this, but did not fully explain how its system reaches the underlying supply chain or enables labor enforcement.

A never squarely explained how platforms would verify mill names or batch codes against fraud, while B's alternative of targeted sampling and account-level enforcement used already-verified platform data. B's closing crystallized the core flaw: the obligations would tax every adult tee for a recall they cannot perform and a wage they will not pay.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 and Pressure Questions reframing A's transparency requirements as a "paperwork filter, not a safety filter" and exposing that a "mill name is not a wage," which forced A into a narrower, less convincing "named subcontractor" position without a credible verification mechanism.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by showing why Side B’s reactive enforcement model generally requires a failed sample, complaint, or recall after risky goods have entered homes. A plausibly defended documentation as traceability and deterrence rather than a perfect substitute for testing, while explaining why platforms are the practical choke point for seller identification, batch matching, repeat-offender control, and factory disclosure. Side B effectively showed that paperwork can be false and that compliance burdens lawful small exporters, but it too often treated imperfect detection as proof that stronger obligations have no value. It also did not adequately answer the narrower case for labor transparency. A’s clipped turns and occasional conflation of unmatched goods with unlawful goods reduced the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange was its answer to the copied-certificate objection: bind records to sellers and batches, sample against them, and penalize mismatches. This did not eliminate Side B’s capacity critique, but it converted paperwork from an empty PDF into an enforcement trail and exposed that Side B’s alternative remained predominantly reactive.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): A won by demonstrating that B’s preferred reactive enforcement cannot outrun the volume of untested goods that platforms actively rank and ship, whereas A’s proposed gates close the arbitrage gap and establish parity with domestic safety standards. B’s “paperwork doesn’t test fabric” critique was incisive but never fully answered why cross-border listings should remain exempt from rules domestic retailers already follow, nor did it provide a convincing preemptive alternative to A’s liability shift.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s answer to B’s pressure question on copied certificates (A4.1–A4.2), reframing the clash from “does a file test fabric?” to “does the platform keep promoting the mismatch?” by binding listings to named sellers, batch records, and knew-or-should-have-known liability—a turn B never fully recovered from.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B wins the central trade-off. PRO's volume argument that marketplaces create the flood customs can't sample is strong, but CON shows the file doesn't test dye, is copyable, and both sides ultimately rely on sampling/complaints. Adding a pre-market accredited-record + batch-match queue delays the typical cheap shirt where unmatched is the small exporter's default, not proof of hazard, and pushes costs to low-income buyers and small sellers while giants absorb paperwork. Labor naming suffers the same fiction problem - refusal to name ≠ coerced stitching - and CON's targeted signal of failed sample / recall / repeat offender answers PRO's pressure about flags without catalog-wide delay. Clipped PRO openings cost clarity, and A's concession that no duty tests dye damages its live safety route more than B's late-enforcement concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's file-does-not-test-fabric line in B2.1/B4.2-B4.5 + A3/B3 pressure: copied certificate onto untested dye, unmatched = small-exporter default, extra queue taxes parent without pulling sample sooner, which A4.1 admits.
