### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won a close remedial clash. Both sides agreed dark-pattern obstruction is real; the live question was whether “as easy as sign-up” is the right rule. CON made that standard decisive: it is either literal (then gameable by degrading enrollment and overbroad on legitimate confirmations) or effectively anti-obstruction (then targeted bans already do the work). PRO’s vivid harm story and light-in/heavy-out incentive reply were strong, but they never dissolved that dilemma or proved parity necessary and robust across gyms, B2B, and small providers. CON answered pressure more surgically and left the cleaner vote reason: symmetry is not relief.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.5–A4.7 dilemma: once PRO admits warnings/consequences, parity ceases to be literal; if it is literal, firms can make both doors heavy—so the motion is either unnecessary or unbound.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins because its structural parity standard better targets the root business incentive for dark patterns—easy capture plus obstructed exit—while convincingly answering CON's gaming objection through firm-level conversion economics. A's rebuttal that degrading sign-up destroys the impulse funnel defangs B's central "dial" critique, and A's whack-a-mole argument shows targeted enforcement is insufficient against fragmentation-by-friction. CON's dilemma and overbreadth objections are analytically sharp but cannot overcome the demonstrated asymmetry in consumer welfare.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over whether parity is gameable by degrading sign-up, where A's argument that impulse-dependent businesses will not sacrifice easy enrollment—and that doing so would undermine the exploit model anyway—decisively answers B's strongest structural objection.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins because it successfully defends the core mechanism of the proposition (symmetry) against Side B's primary attack (gameability via degrading sign-up). Side A convincingly argues that market incentives for impulse conversion prevent firms from degrading the front door, and that parity objectively anchors the exit process to the entry process, preventing "engineered inertia" that targeted deception laws miss. Side B's alternative of targeted anti-obstruction law is less effective because, as Side A notes, structural friction itself is the harm, not just outright deception. Side A's rebuttals were sharper, adapted better to the evolving clash, and directly neutralized Side B's best points.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to the "make sign-up harder" gameability argument, pointing out that businesses relying on impulse conversions will not degrade their front door, effectively neutralizing Side B's strongest structural critique of the parity rule.
