### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully proving that outcome-based enforcement is impossible if workers and regulators cannot see the rules generating the outcomes. Side B landed heavy blows by pointing out that disclosing thresholds—even post-penalty—would leak fraud triggers. However, Side A executed a brilliant pivot in the closing, arguing that if a company uses the same secret signal for both theft detection and routine scheduling, that convergence is the exact abuse of unaccountable authority the motion prevents.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Closing was decisive. By pointing out that relying on discovery "reverses the actual order" (forcing a worker to file a claim before knowing if a rule was broken) and by reframing Side B's overlapping fraud/performance triggers as a dangerous loophole rather than a shield, Side A perfectly neutralized Side B's two strongest objections.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): PRO kept a live path: you cannot know you have a bias or unlawful-quota claim from a pay stub alone when the rule is hidden. Its layered fix - plain factors beforehand, post-decision specifics to the affected worker, full thresholds confidentially to regulators - directly neutralized CON's thin-vs-thick dilemma and gaming/fraud leak objection. CON never solved how a worker detects that a safety break or gate pause was scored, and its answer that publication equals evasion guide was undercut by PRO's timing separation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 answering B's dual-use theft/shift cutoff question by splitting timing: general notice upfront, specific pause + effect after adverse action, fraud cutoff never pre-published but regulator-visible - which turned CON's strongest fork into a workable disclosure design.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully navigating the central clash over gaming and fraud evasion. By proposing a layered disclosure model—general notice beforehand, specific post-decision explanations for the worker, and confidential full-model access for regulators—Side A neutralizes Side B's "evasion guide" objection. Side A also decisively exposes the flaw in Side B's reliance on ex-post legal discovery, pointing out that workers cannot identify a valid legal claim (like bias or illegal quotas) if the algorithmic rule causing the injury remains hidden. Side B's argument that post-decision disclosure equates to full publication was less persuasive than Side A's practical framework for accountability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.4), which introduces the layered disclosure model (post-decision specific disclosure to the worker, confidential thresholds for regulators) to collapse Side B's "thin vs. thick" disclosure fork and resolve the fraud-evasion dilemma.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully proving that Side B's post-harm accountability alternative relies on a catch-22: a worker cannot demand a remedy for an algorithmic decision if they do not know the system exists to begin with. Side A also effectively neutralized Side B's strong 'gaming' arguments by framing metric-gaming as a problem of bad employer proxies rather than a justification for secrecy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing exposed the flaw in Side B's post-injury alternative: an employer answering a complaint about lost hours will not voluntarily confess to a secret algorithm without prior disclosure rules forcing them to do so.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): A kept a workable scope — systems whose output can change pay/hours/discipline — and turned B's gaming and maze claims: if a metric collapses when named it is a trap, and reverse-engineering already favors insiders. B's adverse-action-only alternative was undercut by A's pressure on quiet hour cuts and unknown existence: you can't demand reasons for a score you were never told exists, and B conceded the factor list is needed later in audits, making advance notice the precondition, not a cheat sheet. Clipped B speeches added execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4/A5 exchange: existence notice plus principal factors is what a later complaint cannot supply — proof the system exists and a map of what it can move — so if the list is safe for B's post-harm audit, it is safe as pre-notice; otherwise the appeal never opens.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by decisively capturing the information asymmetry inherent in algorithmic management. While Side B argued that workers could simply demand explanations after a concrete adverse action like a pay cut, Side A effectively demonstrated that without advance disclosure, workers would not know an algorithm was involved and employers would have no incentive to volunteer that information. Side A also neutralized the gaming argument by pointing out that easily gamed metrics are inherently flawed and secrecy merely shields them from correction. Side B's clipped turns were a minor execution cost, but Side A's superior handling of the notice-versus-remedy clash secured the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled Side B's adverse-action alternative by pointing out that a worker seeing a shorter paycheck will not know to ask about the hidden algorithm, making advance disclosure the only reliable way to trigger a meaningful appeal.
