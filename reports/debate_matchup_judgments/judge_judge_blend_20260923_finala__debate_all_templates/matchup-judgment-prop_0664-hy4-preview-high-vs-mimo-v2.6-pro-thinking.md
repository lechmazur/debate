### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): CON built a sharper, more consistent case by tying its argument tightly to the motion's actual wording ("more" U.S. and allied forward deployment vs. Taiwan's own organic defenses), which let it neutralize PRO's "decisive concession" charge with a coherent escalation-geography mechanism (striking Taiwan is already war; striking Okinawa/Guam/Philippines widens it to new state actors) rather than merely asserting a distinction. PRO's dismissal of that distinction as "paint schemes" [B4.5-4.6] never actually engaged with the widening-the-war logic, leaving a live CON argument unanswered. CON also handled the pressure-question exchange cleanly, using A1/A2 answers to reinforce its emissions-clock/first-strike-incentive argument, while PRO's parallel answer (passive sensors, dispersal) was asserted but not pressed further when CON reasserted the target-multiplication point in Rebuttal 2. PRO's opening was vivid and concrete (Bashi Channel, minefields, physics-vs-political-will framing) and its critique of sanctions timing was a real point CON had to work to answer, but two of PRO's speeches were clipped for exceeding word limits, cutting off developing arguments, which is a real execution cost. Overall CON's tighter scope discipline, stronger handling of the pivotal "concession" pressure, and cleaner closing comparison give it a clear but not dominant edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.1/A4.1 response distinguishing Taiwan's organic self-defense from "more" U.S./allied forward deployment, and explaining why striking allied territory widens the war, effectively defused PRO's "paint schemes" concession charge — a charge PRO never adequately re-engaged after CON's clarification.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B won by maintaining a tighter comparative framework focused on conflict prevention rather than warfighting, successfully distinguishing Taiwanese organic defenses from U.S. and allied forward deployment on allied soil, and developing a coherent crisis-instability mechanism—centered on first-strike incentives against emitting radars and networked nodes—that Side A never fully dispelled. Side A made a strong intuitive case for deterrence by denial, but overreached by claiming Side B had conceded the motion on Taiwan’s indigenous capabilities, a reading that conflicts with the proposition’s explicit reference to “U.S. and allied” systems. Side B’s rapid-economic-measures response and its consistent pressure on the unique escalation risks of forward deployment created a cleaner live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s first rebuttal (B2), which clarified that Taiwan’s own defenses are not U.S. or allied forward deployments and reframed the debate around prevention versus warfighting, decisively blunted Side A’s attempt to force a concession and established the geographic and escalation distinction that governed the rest of the exchange.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a highly persuasive comparative frame: physical denial relies on physics and geography, while economic deterrence relies on fragile political will and coalition discipline. Side A effectively exploits the timeline mismatch between a rapid amphibious fait accompli (days) and the crippling effects of economic sanctions (months), arguing that a rational planner will price sanctions as a postwar cost. Side B's "crisis instability" and "escalation bait" arguments regarding forward-deployed radars were theoretically sound, but Side A successfully neutralized them by explaining how dispersal, passive sensors, and shoot-and-scoot tactics mitigate first-strike incentives. While Side A suffered minor clipping in early rounds, the core arguments were fully developed and decisively answered the most important burdens of the debate. Side B's late pivot to distinguish between Taiwan's organic defenses and allied soil was a good clarification, but it did not rescue the fundamental vulnerability of relying solely on economic threats against a revisionist power.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of the "escalation bait" argument by pointing out that being undefended is the ultimate invitation for conquest, and that dispersal actually lowers the payoff of a first strike, effectively flipping Side B's crisis instability claim.
