### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B systematically dismantled Side A's mechanisms by exposing a structural flaw in the motion: if reports are published "alongside papers," rejections are never seen. Side B then brilliantly turned Side A's fallback arguments, showing that both the "audit" of captured journals and the "shadow of publication" push reviewers toward safe acceptances. Side B's persistent word-count clipping was a noticeable execution cost, but their comparative logic was overwhelmingly dominant.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing turn of Side A's "shadow of publication" argument, pointing out that a harsh report only sees daylight if the reviewer is overruled, thus making "no" the dangerous answer and "yes" the safe one.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing that PRO's transparency mechanism cannot reach the actual harm it invoked—arbitrary rejections—because reports are only published alongside accepted papers. CON showed this creates a perverse asymmetry where a critical review becomes public and signed primarily when it is overruled, while payments and rehiring power from acceptance-driven journals push reviewers toward "yes." PRO never recovered from CON's demonstration that the audit mechanism is blind precisely where capture happens, since harsh rejections never appear on article pages, and that the reforms burden the most vulnerable reviewers while leaving powerful ones covered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's turn that rejected reports never reach article pages, so PRO's public audit cannot detect journals culling tough reviewers, and that the system makes "no" the dangerous answer and "yes" the safe one.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B wins decisively by exposing a fatal structural flaw in Side A's case: the motion publishes reports alongside accepted papers, meaning the reviews for rejected papers (the exact harm A complains about) remain hidden. B brilliantly extends this logic to dismantle A's "audit" defense for paid reviews, noting that the rejected reports from tough reviewers would never be published, blinding any public audit to journal capture. A's attempt to patch this with voluntary preprint disclosures was weak and outside the motion's text. Although B suffered execution costs from repeated word-limit clipping at the ends of several speeches, the core logical traps were fully delivered and decisively neutralized A's primary routes to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of the "transparency" mechanism in Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2. By pointing out that reports for rejected papers are never published, B completely neutralizes A's claim that transparency stops senior reviewers from secretly burying junior work, and later uses the exact same mechanic to destroy A's claim that published reports allow the community to audit journal bias.
