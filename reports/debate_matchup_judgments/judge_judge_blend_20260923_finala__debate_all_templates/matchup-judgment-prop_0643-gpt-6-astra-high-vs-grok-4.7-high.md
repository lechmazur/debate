### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases without resorting to fake sourcing, and both handled the Pressure Questions round competently with clear A1/A2 answers. But CON (Side A) sustained a tighter, more integrated line across every round: the incentive-mismatch argument (worker directors answer to an electorate that feels closures first), the confidentiality-accountability gap, and the informal-power-via-committees mechanism all reinforced one central dilemma—if worker directors' influence is negligible, the mandate is just the consultation PRO called inadequate; if it's substantial, it is a legally compelled transfer of control over capital those directors don't own. PRO's closing correctly flagged this as a potential false dichotomy (any minority could be similarly indicted), but CON's final closing answered that challenge by distinguishing a legally reserved, cohesive bloc with institutionalized committee leverage from an ordinary dispersed minority—closing the loop CON opened in Rebuttal 1. PRO's rebuttals were strong but increasingly relied on design fixes (equal cross-border ballots, independent election administration, enforceable loyalty duties) that go beyond what the motion as written actually guarantees, which CON exploited effectively. CON's Rebuttal 2 was flagged as clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost, but the core content and the closing recovered the argument's thrust, so the cost is modest rather than decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 point that a one-third bloc "does not need a veto to exercise power" via committees, consensus norms, and traded delay—reinforced through the pressure questions and crystallized in the closing dilemma ("cheap override fails the voice promised; costly override is the reallocation itself")—was the most damaging sustained line, forcing PRO into defensive, additive institutional patches rather than a clean rebuttal.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO kept a consistent path: one-third worker directors share information and duties, shareholder majority retains final votes, so scrutiny without veto. That directly answered CON's paralysis and incentive attacks and survived pressure. CON's strongest points - committee delay, confidentiality democratic deficit, residual-owner principle, and clipped R2 - left the dilemma B posed unanswered: if the majority can set a deadline and approve, paralysis fails; CON's alternative of councils with teeth either lacks enforcement or also constrains shareholder control for the same reason. Clipped final rebuttal was an execution cost and left the meaningful-vs-controlling trade-off unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's A1/A2 in R2 plus Q2 pressure: showing majority can override, distinguishing influence vs obstruction, and forcing CON to admit its own "teeth" either means ignorable consultation or a similar constraint on shareholder control it deems illegitimate.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by establishing a decisive dilemma regarding the practical power of the worker minority: either the minority has real power to delay and obstruct through board norms (making it an illegitimate reallocation of control over capital), or it lacks such power (making the mandate mere consultation, which PRO explicitly rejected). Side A's formalistic reliance on majority voting fails to address Side B's strong points about board consensus norms and committee gatekeeping. Side B's rebuttals were sharper, and analogies like "a speed limit is not a second driver" effectively crystallized the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which forcefully applied the dilemma that PRO's mandate must either be practically powerless (failing its own purpose) or practically obstructive (illegitimately reallocating control), effectively neutralizing PRO's attempt to claim minority influence without minority veto.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): CON built a tighter, more disciplined case: it separated the "information" problem from the "compulsory voting authority" problem early, offered a coherent alternative (enforceable consultation/negotiated seats), and consistently forced PRO to show why votes beat voice rather than just asserting it. CON's strongest blow landed in Rebuttal 2/Closing: PRO wants the casting vote to "cap" the downside of worker influence (job-protective resistance) while still crediting worker influence with the upside (blocking bad investments) — an asymmetry PRO never resolved. PRO's closing reasserted the same claim ("the downside is capped") instead of answering that internal tension, which is a real unaddressed logical gap. PRO's opening was also clipped by the word limit, an execution cost that cut off its own summation. PRO's German/Nordic evidence and vivid lines ("a seat changes the count," "voice without a vote is how blindness survived") were rhetorically strong and its answer to the "significant" definition question (roughly one-third) was a fair, direct response to pressure. But CON correctly noted this only shows compatibility, not causation or necessity, and PRO never fully closed that gap. On balance, CON's rebuttals did more damage to PRO's live routes than PRO's rebuttals did to CON's, giving CON a clear, if not overwhelming, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point that PRO "cannot count the influence as a benefit while treating residual shareholder control as eliminating its downside" [A4.4], which exposed an asymmetry in PRO's casting-vote defense that PRO's closing reasserted but never actually resolved.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): CON held the burden gap: PRO showed worker information and horizon matter and that German-style representation is survivable, but never closed the distance to a universal mandatory voting floor. A kept pressure on information vs authority, alternative enforceable consultation, and concentrated worker conflicts, and exposed that the casting vote cannot simultaneously be touted as preventing capture while guaranteeing influence only helps. B's "seat changes the count" is memorable but A shows changing the count does not equal improving the decision across diverse firms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing on casting vote both ways and vote vs decision: showing minority seats can be outvoted like consultation, and can also build a blocking coalition against needed restructuring, so capped conflict does not selectively erase costs.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing a fatal logical flaw in Side A's reliance on the shareholder casting vote. Side A argued that worker influence is beneficial because it forces the board to assemble a coalition, yet claimed the casting vote caps the downside of workers resisting necessary restructuring. Side B sharply demonstrated that influence is symmetrical: if workers have enough leverage to block short-termism, they have enough leverage to block necessary reinvestment. Side B successfully defended enforceable consultation as a sufficient alternative for information sharing, leaving Side A unable to prove why the additional, symmetrical voting risk was justified.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, specifically the argument that PRO cannot count worker influence as a benefit (swinging a coalition against shortsightedness) while treating residual shareholder control as eliminating its downside (swinging a coalition against necessary restructuring).
