### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but PRO's scoping move—limiting liability to reachable institutional assets, scaled to role and ability to pay—proved more resilient under pressure than CON's core claim that only the state should pay. The decisive moment was PRO's Pressure Question exposing that CON's own case required churches to "return land they hold" while simultaneously forbidding scaled cash contribution from the same continuing institution. CON's attempted distinction (specific parcel vs. fungible cash) was a reasonable but incomplete answer, and CON's response to the companion question about a solvent order's refusal to pay voluntarily sidestepped rather than resolved the tension, asserting the state's duty survives without clarifying whether the church's own share simply evaporates. PRO's answers throughout were cleaner, directly labeled, and progressively narrowed the dispute to "reachable assets capped by ability to pay," which blunted CON's parishioner-tax and ministry-closure objections more effectively than CON managed to blunt PRO's institutional-continuity and joint-operator arguments. CON's builder analogy, once countered in Rebuttal 1, was never revisited or repaired. The debate was close and both sides argued in good faith without fabricated evidence, but PRO ended with the more internally consistent position and the last unanswered rhetorical punch in closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question (Q1) forcing CON to explain why compelled land return is legitimate but scaled cash contribution from the same continuing institution is not—a tension CON never fully resolved, only partially distinguished via an in-kind/fungible-cash line that PRO's closing directly exploited.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by effectively establishing entity liability and weaponizing Side B's concession that churches should return land. By proving that land return is functionally an extraction of institutional wealth, A dismantled B's claim that cash levies inherently punish 2024 parishioners. A also clearly delineated the division of harm (state = compulsion; church = custody/method), securing a robust mandate for shared repair.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question 1 and Rebuttal 2 effectively trapped B on the contradiction of supporting compelled land return while calling scaled cash contributions a raid on new parishioners.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a robust framework of institutional entity liability that cleanly separated corporate obligation from individual parishioner guilt. The decisive moment was Side A's pressure question exposing Side B's contradiction: Side B conceded churches must return institutional land, but claimed cash from the same institutional balance sheet was an unjust raid on current donors. Side B's argument that the state should bear 100% of the cost because it compelled attendance failed to overcome Side A's strong "joint operator" framing, and Side B's reliance on voluntary giving effectively conceded Side A's point about public subsidies for institutional denial.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled Side B's distinction between compelled land return and compelled cash contribution, proving both draw on the same institutional estate and exposing Side B's contradictory stance on what constitutes a "raid" on present worshippers.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly around the same core tension: whether "central role plus retained benefit" is a workable, non-collective-punishment standard, or an unlimited levy dressed in limiting language. B's key strategic move was conceding early and often that the claim fails where assets cannot be separated from later bequests, converting A's "no limit" attack into a demonstration that the rule is narrower than feared rather than broader. This concession, repeated in the PQ answers (A1) and closing, gave B a concrete, bounded mechanism: disgorgement of a traceable, valuable retained benefit (e.g., the farm/land preserved by unpaid child labor), which A never fully dislodged beyond asserting "preservation is not creation" and that consumed subsidies aren't banked gains. That rejoinder is decent but doesn't fully answer why land/buildings whose maintenance costs were literally offset by forced unpaid labor isn't a quantifiable retained benefit — A relies on repeating the assertion rather than engaging the mechanism. A's strongest point — that "ordinary liability" (sue case by case) is what's left once you strip the levy language, and that this makes the state the proper payer — lands rhetorically in A5.1, correctly noting B's own closing (B5.1) essentially concedes that pure case-by-case suits fail practically, which is a real tension B doesn't fully resolve: B wants disgorgement without full civil-suit rigor but also wants to avoid being an "open account." B papers over this with "a tribunal with appraisal power" (B4.3), which A calls a "role tax" in different clothing — a fair rebuttal, but B's concrete farm example gives judges a tangible image to hang the ruling on that A's more abstract "consumed subsidies" framing doesn't match. On pressure questions, B's Q1/Q2 forced A to articulate a real standard (identifiable gain), which A did directly and with a clear A1/A2, satisfying the format requirement, but that answer also somewhat vindicates B's model (proved wrong, causation, gain) more than it destroys it, since B then shows how the farm case satisfies exactly that test. A's own PQ push B into conceding the bequest exclusion, which A leverages well in the final speech, but by then B has already used that same concession to sharpen its own case in closing, neutralizing much of the damage. Overall B ends with a slightly cleaner, more concrete affirmative path (traceable farm/land benefit meeting A's own stated test) while A's closing repeats "no honest limit" without demonstrating that B's narrowed rule is actually unworkable in the paradigm case B chose. It's a close, high-quality debate, but B's narrowing move plus the concrete example gives it a modest edge on decisiveness and clarity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's use of A's own concession ("owe for what it actually did," "specific unjust gain") combined with the school-farm example in Rebuttal 2/Closing to show a concrete case meeting A's stated test, versus A's repeated "preservation is not creation" reply that never fully rebuts why saved maintenance costs aren't a measurable retained benefit.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B successfully trapped Side A between contradictory burdens. When Side A conceded that liability stops strictly at identifiable gains and fails if mixed with later gifts, Side B expertly pointed out that this either renders the reparative fund empty (since endowments are overwhelmingly mixed) or forces Side A to rely on generalized metrics like 'children housed' which act as the very collective tax Side A swore to avoid.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal and closing systematically weaponized Side A's concession about tracing against them, showing that Side A's rationale for a fund (lost files and dead staff) fundamentally conflicts with their promise to only extract specifically proven unjust gains.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by systematically dismantling the financial mechanism of Side A's proposition. Through sharp pressure questions, B forces A to concede that contributions must be strictly limited to identifiable, separable gains, excluding mixed innocent patrimony. B then effectively argues in later speeches that this concession collapses A's fund into either ordinary tort liability (which A admits is inadequate for survivors) or an unworkable standard that fails on typical mixed endowments. While A's moral framing and "avoided cost" argument were strong, B's logical trap in the closing decisively neutralized A's live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where B capitalizes on A's concession about separable assets to show that A's reparative fund either reduces to ordinary liability or fails entirely on mixed endowments, perfectly capturing the contradiction in A's mechanism.
