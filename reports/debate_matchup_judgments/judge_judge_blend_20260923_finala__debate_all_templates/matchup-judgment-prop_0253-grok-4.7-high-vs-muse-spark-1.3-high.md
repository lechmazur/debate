### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built and sustained the decisive dilemma: PRO's mandate either does nothing to end "lock-in" (if platforms may freely discount or ignore an import, as B2.1 conceded) or it functions as an effective rubber stamp that pressures desperate platforms into skipping their own vetting to win recruits (the exact mechanism PRO needed for exit pressure to matter). A2.1 first exposed this fork, A4.3–A4.4 tightened it into a clean either/or, and A5.2 carried it unresolved into the close. PRO's attempted escape—labels are merely descriptive, so probation is never actually waived—never squares with PRO's own reliance on competitive/exit pressure as the thing that forces platforms to honor imports; if that pressure is real, it cuts toward skipped vetting just as CON predicted. PRO's liability answer (exporter certifies, importer who displays owns the choice) also drew a sharper CON response (A4.5) noting the credential was legally compelled rather than freely chosen, weakening the analogy to ordinary hiring. PRO's positive case (captivity, phone-number analogy, free-rider response) was solid in the opening but did not fully survive the sustained pressure, and PRO's Rebuttal 2 was clipped for exceeding the word limit, cutting off its final answer mid-sentence — a real execution cost under the judging rules. CON's case was more internally consistent across rounds and gave the cleaner reason to vote CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1's "escape hatch gives the game away" fork (imports are either inert or a rubber stamp) — repeated and never fully defused by PRO through R2 and closing — was the single most decisive exchange.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by trapping Side B in a decisive dilemma: if platforms can freely discount imported ratings, the mandate fails to cure the lock-in Side B complains about. But if competitive pressure forces platforms to honor those ratings to recruit workers, it causes a race to the bottom that validates Side A's safety concerns. Side A's final weighing of "friction vs. injury" cleanly resolves the round in its favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 decisively closed the trap on Side B's "exit" argument, demonstrating that Side B cannot rely on competitive pressure to force adoption without simultaneously validating the safety risks of waiving probation.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully dismantling Side B's central "useless vs. dangerous" dichotomy. A clearly distinguished between transferring labeled, contextualized work history and forcing platforms to waive their own safety vetting, proving that the mandate reduces switching costs without mandating blind trust. Side B's argument relied heavily on the assumption that importing platforms would either ignore the data entirely or recklessly skip probation to fill shifts. Side A effectively countered this by pointing out that platforms can use the verified data to avoid the "blank file" penalty while still enforcing local screening, and that platforms already skipping vetting are doing so on thin evidence regardless. Side A's framing of ratings as the worker's earned resume rather than the platform's proprietary asset was highly persuasive and grounded. Side B performed very well and kept the clash sharp with the "borrowed halo" concept, but A's resolution of the core fork was more logically sound and directly answered the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's resolution of the "useless vs. dangerous" fork in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, clarifying that transferring labeled history replaces a blank file without forcing platforms to waive their own local screening or rubber-stamp foreign scores.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON's central structural move — the "toothless or compulsory equivalence" dilemma (A2.2, sharpened in A4.5) — became the load-bearing clash of the debate and was never cleanly escaped. PRO's attempted middle ground ("send and show, not obey," B4.1/B4.3) tried to preserve receiver discretion while banning "erasure," but CON's rebuttal that a displayed, non-discountable badge functions as compelled trust ("Probation does not stop customers from reading a displayed badge as cleared history... That is compulsory equivalence, politely labeled") landed and was not directly neutralized in PRO's closing, which mostly reasserted "display is honesty" without resolving how discounting-to-zero differs from forbidden erasure. CON also successfully dismantled PRO's opening analogies (phone numbers as mere identifiers, credit files as regulated/disputable, unlike proprietary ratings), and PRO's later substitute analogies (resumes, licenses) were asserted rather than defended against the same disanalogy. CON's free-rider/liability point (rival platforms displaying a signal they cannot audit, while bearing the cost if it misleads) also stayed largely live through the closing. PRO's opening had strong moral framing ("leash vs. record") and decent answers on ownership of facts, but by the final exchange CON had the cleaner, more consistently pressed throughline and the harder-to-answer dilemma.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.2/A4.5 sequence — forcing PRO to choose between "toothless" discretion (which guts the stated benefits of bargaining power and cold-start relief) or compelled equivalence (which reintroduces the liability/audit problems) — was the decisive exchange; PRO's "send and show, not obey" reply never cleanly escaped this bind.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively springing a trap on Side B's middle-ground stance. When Side B argued that importing platforms wouldn't be forced to obey the ratings but merely to "display" them and not treat veterans as beginners, Side A successfully demonstrated that this still amounts to compulsory equivalence. Side A showed that forcing a platform to display unauditable safety signals generated by a rival's unique rules forces it to vouch for trust it cannot verify.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive, specifically the argument that if a platform is mandated to ingest and display a rival's "no safety strike" attestation to customers, it is forced to present silence from a weak system as proof of safety, validating the compulsory equivalence trap.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully trapping Side A in the "toothless or compulsory equivalence" dilemma. While Side A argues for a middle ground where platforms must display but can discount imported ratings, Side B effectively demonstrates that in a consumer-facing marketplace, displaying a "clean" record forces the receiving platform to accept a competitor's un-auditable quality control. Side B's rebuttals systematically dismantled Side A's analogies and middle-ground compromises, showing that the mandate either does nothing or creates a race to the bottom in verification standards.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantles Side A's "display with context" compromise by showing that displaying an unauditable badge to customers is de facto compulsory equivalence, and that the receiving platform cannot inspect the underlying logs to verify the exporter's definitions.
