### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but A (CON) executed the later rounds more effectively. A's central dichotomy—that any ban's exemption regime is either broad enough to let unpermitted cars through (undermining the "ban" label) or narrow enough to strand people who fit no category—was raised early, restated in Rebuttal 2, and never squarely resolved by B, whose answer (self-declared, audited-after-the-fact permits) actually concedes the stranding risk A identified. A also backed the "design without prohibition" claim with concrete, plausible examples (Barcelona superblocks, Oslo parking cuts) that directly rebutted B's Pressure Question challenge, whereas B's own Q2 was logically self-defeating (demanding a "car-free zone" that keeps cars "lawful on every street"), which A exposed as a contradiction. On funding, A's "asphalt is right-of-way, not a sellable lot" rebuttal to B's land-value/bond financing went unanswered in B's closing. B's moral framing (payment-based filter vs. need-based default, "filter vs. decision") was original and rhetorically sharp, and its equity critique of pricing was never fully neutralized, but B's practical mechanism for implementing a ban fairly and funding it kept unraveling under pressure, leaving A with the cleaner, more defended path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's "porous, unpaid, and audited after" dichotomy in Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.4), which showed B's exemption regime for the ban collapses into either a non-ban or the same stranding problem B accused pricing of causing, was the most decisive and unanswered exchange.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by exposing the ban’s central dilemma: broad, self-declared exemptions make it porous and abandon pricing of road harms, while narrow or audited exemptions risk criminalizing legitimate trips. A also showed that targeted closures and street redesign can coexist with a lawful fallback network, weakening B’s claim that only a total ban unlocks transformation. B’s equity framing was memorable, but discounts and waivers reduced its force, and B never resolved why an uncategorized necessary trip should have no legal fallback.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s answer to B’s self-declared permit proposal was decisive: if categories are broad, cars continue entering without paying for congestion; if enforcement is meaningful, changing circumstances can produce fines or stranded travelers. This directly undercut B’s claim that need, rather than exclusion, would be the ban’s default.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully trapping Side A on the practical implementation of the ban's permit system. While Side A offered a compelling moral critique of congestion pricing as a regressive "tiered subscription," Side B demonstrated that pricing allows a legal fallback (paying the toll) for edge cases that a rigid ban would either strand or ignore. When Side A attempted to solve this with "self-declared and audited" broad exemptions, Side B expertly showed this forces a choice between a porous, ineffective ban and a strict ban that fines people for unforeseen circumstances. Side B's closing crystallized this dilemma perfectly, neutralizing Side A's egalitarian framing with administrative reality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which dismantled Side A's "self-declared and audited" permit solution by showing it forces a lose-lose choice between a porous, unpaid ban and a strict ban that penalizes people for unlisted edge cases.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and followed the Q&A format cleanly. CON's strongest moves were exposing the tension in PRO's own exemption scheme (permits as "the policy" once granted, converging toward the status quo) and pressing the displacement-to-neighbors problem, which PRO's "mode shift before the ring" answer addressed only thinly. But PRO's case proved more resilient across rounds: it consistently reframed the core question as "who has the standing right to occupy the street," turned CON's "adaptability" pitch into an admission that pricing's flexibility just means the harm never actually ends ("adjustable failure is the policy"), and capitalized hard on CON's own concession (A4.3) that the fee does not itself end injury, noise, or curb-land harms, only promising that reduced volume "makes possible" future reform. CON's closing pivoted back to a rights-vs-permits framing without squarely re-defending against that weaponized concession, leaving PRO's sharpest closing line — that a priced core stays a car district no matter how the price is tuned — as the more decisive, unresolved point standing at the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's use of CON's own admission that pricing "doesn't claim to end injury, noise, or curb land by itself" (A4.3), turned in Rebuttal 2 and Closing into the charge that pricing's adjustability is really just an admission that the harm is never removed — a reframe CON's closing did not directly rebut.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the decisive distinction clear: pricing reduces traffic but preserves ordinary private cars' ability to occupy central streets, while a ban changes the default and enables actual street reclamation. Side A created credible pressure on permits, spillover, and administrative durability, but overstated bans as having only one setting and never fully answered why willingness to pay tracks need. Its late universal claims about every pricing scheme and no city sustaining a ban were unsupported, and its nonexistent P4 references added an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange exposed the inconsistency between Side A's claim that prices discover need and its later reliance on politically designed discounts. B then tied that flaw to the central comparison: under pricing, money remains a standing waiver for discretionary cars, whereas narrow permits do not restore the ordinary commuter fleet.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a stronger normative and practical framework for what a city center should be, effectively arguing that congestion pricing merely creates a "priced queue" that preserves the physical harms of cars for those who can pay. Side A brilliantly turned Side B's pragmatism argument—that pricing "works adequately even when run badly"—by pointing out that its "adjustable failure" is precisely the policy of leaving cars in the core. Side B made a strong case about the political vulnerability of permit lists, but Side A's rebuttals on functional permitting, the inherent boundary effects of any cordon, and the fact that willingness to pay does not equal need were more decisive and damaging to B's core claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's turn of Side B's "works adequately even when run badly" argument in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, reframing it to show that pricing's "adjustable failure" is simply the permanent policy of leasing the street to ordinary private cars.
