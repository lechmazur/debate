### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by keeping the debate focused on the proposition’s financial-incidence requirement. It persuasively argued that tax-funded compensation can absorb ordinary premiums while municipal indemnification retains the largest settlements, leaving taxpayers responsible for most actual dollars. Side B showed meaningful incentives from surcharges and nonrenewal, but repeatedly labeled excess liability a “remainder” without establishing that insurance would cover most misconduct costs. Its accountability case was stronger than its cost-shifting case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s sharpest response was that a remainder larger than the policy can absorb is still the main cost. This directly undercut Side B’s attempt to distinguish first-dollar insurance from municipal excess coverage and exposed the missing proof that taxpayers would cease to bear most of the bill.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): CON successfully showed that the bulk of misconduct costs would remain with taxpayers. PRO conceded that average premiums could be folded into tax-funded wages and that catastrophic excess claims would still be indemnified by the city, leaving only a thin officer-facing layer of deductibles and surcharges. CON crystallized that for multimillion-dollar settlements—the paradigm case PRO opened with—the municipal excess is the dominant dollar amount, so "size is the incidence." Nonrenewal was also shown to affect future employment without erasing past-act indemnification, meaning the public check stays primary. PRO's "remainder" framing could not overcome the reality that the main financial exposure still lands on city budgets.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's closing reframe [B5.1] that "for a multimillion-dollar settlement, size is the incidence," which exposed that the municipal excess PRO characterized as a minor "remainder" was in fact the bulk of the cost, gutting PRO's claim that the financial burden would stop falling mainly on taxpayers.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively attacking the core mechanism of the proposition: the math of the "financial cost." Side B correctly identifies that police misconduct settlements are often multimillion-dollar events, while personal liability policies have relatively low limits. Therefore, the "tail" (the excess above the policy limit) constitutes the vast majority of the actual financial cost. Because municipalities are generally legally bound to indemnify officers for acts within the scope of employment, that massive tail remains on the taxpayer. Side A's attempt to redefine "mainly" as a conceptual "incidence" or "first-dollar" coverage rather than sheer dollar size was unconvincing against Side B's direct mathematical and legal rebuttal. Side B also effectively neutralized Side A's points on collective bargaining and exposure-rating, making the CON case much more grounded in practical municipal realities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantle Side A's "remainder is not mainly" argument by pointing out that in a multimillion-dollar settlement, the multi-million dollar excess is objectively the "main" financial cost, rendering Side A's semantic distinction hollow.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by drawing the clearest distinction between the ordinary public cost of staffing police and the additional cost created by an officer's repeated misconduct. Its same-beat comparison preserved a plausible role for individualized surcharges and insurability screens despite assignment risk. Side A sharply pressed reimbursement, noisy claims, and vacancies, but too often treated any continuing policing expense or future city liability as proof that replacing a high-risk officer cannot reduce misconduct costs. B's clipped first rebuttal was an execution cost, but its later answers directly restored the central mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange was its answer that a vacancy is not itself another misconduct judgment: removing an uninsurable repeat-loss officer can prevent that officer's next payout, while comparing partners on the same beat helps separate assignment risk from personal patterns. Side A never fully neutralized that distinction.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): CON won by showing that personal insurance cannot actually move the main financial cost of misconduct off municipal taxpayers. CON's "function not names" rebuttal neutralized PRO's claim that removing uninsurable officers ends the liability, and CON's "either fork" frame demonstrated that reimbursement (preserving the cost) and vacancies (creating new costs) both leave taxpayers holding the bill. PRO's "named surcharge" transparency argument was rhetorically effective but did not prove the cost shifts, while CON consistently tied the debate back to the motion's core metric.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response to PRO's vacancy argument: showing that policing is a public function on a city-commanded beat, so removing one officer does not end taxpayer liability—the next settlement from the same assignment still arrives, and overtime for the empty car adds cost.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by drawing a decisive conceptual distinction between the cost of policing (staffing, overtime, base premiums) and the cost of misconduct (settlements, surcharges). Side B repeatedly conflated the two, arguing that taxpayers still pay for "overtime for the empty car" if a bad officer is priced out. Side A successfully neutralized this by pointing out that a vacancy is a normal municipal expense, not a misconduct judgment. Side A also effectively countered the "cities will just reimburse" argument by noting that paying a specific surcharge for a repeat offender would be a visible, politically toxic named transfer, thereby breaking the hidden subsidy. Side B's practical critiques of the insurance market (pricing presence over skill) were strong, but Side A's "two files on one beat" analogy provided a sufficient counter. Side A's Rebuttal 1 was slightly clipped at the very end, a minor execution cost, but it did not materially damage their overall case progression.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing to Side B's "empty car" argument. By sharply distinguishing the cost of replacing an employee from the cost of their civil rights violations, Side A dismantled Side B's claim that taxpayers still fund the misconduct even if the officer leaves.
