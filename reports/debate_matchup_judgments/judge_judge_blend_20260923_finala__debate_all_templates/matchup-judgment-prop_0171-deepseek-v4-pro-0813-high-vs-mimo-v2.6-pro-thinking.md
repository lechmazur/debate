### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B opened strongly with concrete, workable examples (malaria/cholera/typhoid challenge models) and a coherent "smaller monitored risk vs. larger unmonitored risk" frame. But Side A's rebuttals landed the two most decisive, unresolved blows: (1) B repeatedly conceded challenge studies cannot settle severe disease, hospitalization, transmission, or elderly protection, meaning the field trial still follows regardless — so the study is an added risk layer, not a substitute for "natural uncontrolled exposure," collapsing B's core baseline comparison; and (2) the filter can err in both directions (discarding candidates that would work in vulnerable populations, not just catching dead ones), a point B never squarely answered. A's pressure question on the ratchet ("what stops marginal approval") exposed that B's safeguard language ("effective early treatment," "capacity to intervene") is vague and negotiable, and B's answer in Rebuttal 2 was circular ("if it did qualify, it wouldn't be marginal"). A's closing crystallized these unanswered points cleanly. B's closing reasserted its frame but did not resolve the double-error objection or the vagueness charge. A's case therefore had the more damaging, less-answered rebuttals, though A's presentation suffered a real execution cost from being clipped in three of its five speeches, which tempers the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's [A2.3]-[A2.4] point that a truncated challenge study can both discard viable candidates and pass dead ones, combined with the pressure question exposing B's vague, circular rescue-criteria safeguard in Rebuttal 2, was the most decisive exchange, since B never squarely resolved either.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON won by forcing the motion’s real scope and making PRO’s case do work it could not finish. CON showed the tool does not settle licensing questions, can discard viable candidates as well as stop dead ones, and that burden-reversal plus soft rescue language is a ratchet, not restraint. PRO was strong on animal-model failure and monitoring, but kept defending a narrower already-permitted practice while under-answering dual-error triage and soft criteria. Clipping hurt CON’s polish, not the comparative path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.4/A4.3–A4.6: challenge studies add a monitored layer rather than replace the large trial, and the young-adult artificial filter errs both ways, collapsing PRO’s proportionality claim.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON successfully reframed the debate by showing that challenge studies are an added risk layer rather than a substitute for field trials, and that their two-way error rate (discarding viable candidates for the elderly while catching dead ones) undermines PRO's proportionality claim. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, competitive cases, but CON (A) sustained the more damaging pressure on PRO's central mechanism: the claim that challenge studies act as a reliable "filter" reducing total exposure. A's repeated point that screened, healthy-adult challenge signals can generate false confidence that pushes weak candidates toward vulnerable populations, backed by the norovirus example (decades of challenge work, no licensed vaccine), was never fully neutralized—PRO's replies (B4.1, B4.5) largely asserted the filter works and called the false-positive risk "unproven" without independently rebutting the generalizability gap. A also pressed the structural point that "animal models inadequate" is a near-universal trigger, meaning "broader" permission is not meaningfully bounded; PRO's answer (a rule-bound severity ceiling in B4.3) actually converges substantially with A's own stated conditions for narrow permission (A4.3), which PRO never exploited to show CON was conceding the substance. Execution also favored A: CON's speeches were clean throughout, while PRO's opening and closing were both flagged as clipped for exceeding word limits, costing PRO content and continuity at exactly the moments meant to frame and crystallize the case. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions format correctly with proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 labeling, so that was a wash, but the substantive unanswered epistemic critique plus the repeated clipping tips the debate to CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's repeated use of the norovirus case (A1.3, A2.4, A5.2) to show that challenge-study signals in healthy screened adults have not reliably translated into licensed products was the most damaging recurring exchange; PRO's rebuttal that "without challenge work there would be no candidates to fail" (B2.4) reframed but did not resolve the translation problem, leaving CON's core mechanism-level objection live through the final speeches.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): PRO won by keeping the decisive comparison live: small, informed volunteer risk as a candidate filter versus larger, less-informed field exposures once animal models fail. CON landed real pressure on trigger breadth, false positives, and unknown late harms, and answered the “de facto ban” question with concrete conditions, but never fully neutralized the displacement arithmetic or showed that intermediate gates reliably kill weak candidates earlier. Clipped PRO turns cost polish, not the core route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO Rebuttal 2 A1/filter answer: challenge reduces how many candidates ever reach vulnerable groups, flipping CON’s “extra risky layer” into net risk reduction.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by showing that PRO's central "filter" mechanism—where challenge studies protect vulnerable groups by weeding out bad candidates—relies on signals that are artificially clean and often fail to translate to real populations. CON's norovirus example and the false-positive confidence argument created durable doubt about whether broader permission would reduce net harm, while the critique that inadequate animal models are the norm in complex disease showed the policy would be far more expansive than PRO conceded. PRO never adequately limited the trigger or rebutted the translation gap, leaving CON's ethical boundary argument intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal showing that challenge studies in screened healthy adults can generate false-positive confidence and fail to translate to vulnerable groups (A2.3, A4.2), which decisively undermined PRO's claim that broader challenge work reliably filters harmful candidates before they reach larger trials.
