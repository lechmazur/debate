### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): CON built a tighter comparative case: rather than denying the abuses, it argued that new esports-specific mandates would be gamed or evaded (jurisdictional gaps for foreign/cross-border actors, substitution of private grinding for capped scrims, portfolio-style development costs that can't be itemized to one player) while targeted enforcement of existing fraud/coercion/misclassification/child-labor law avoids those failure modes. PRO's pressure questions were sharp and forced CON to specify standards, and CON's answers (declining "documented recovery" buyout, cap on compelled hours) were reasonable first moves, but PRO never rebutted CON's strongest late points — that shared/portfolio investment costs can't cleanly attach to one player's buyout, and that ordinary competitive selection pressure (benching underperformers) reproduces burnout without triggering any "compulsion" test. CON's closing crystallized this substitution-effect logic cleanly, while PRO's closing reasserted its "limit the actor who compels" framing without closing those specific gaps. PRO's case also suffered two word-limit clippings (Opening and Rebuttal 1), a real execution cost that likely cut supporting material the judge never saw.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's [A4.1]/[A4.3] answers to PRO's pressure questions — noting that jurisdictional reach problems undercut PRO's new rights symmetrically, and that "documented recovery" buyouts ignore shared/portfolio development costs across a roster — landed the most damage, and PRO's closing never directly resolved the portfolio-cost objection.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): PRO kept a live path: control-as-employment, declining documented buyout ending captivity after recovery, and a cap on compelled bootcamp/off-season load. CON's strongest hits - portfolio risk and substitution - showed costs but did not refute that domestic teams/leagues that fine, house, and field minors are reachable, and that lowering the mandatory floor reduces forced burnout even if voluntary grinding remains. CON's enforcement-only alternative collapses given its own fragmentation concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.4-A4.5 separation of mandatory vs voluntary load and tying enforcement to control/punishment CON accepted in B2.1, blunting B3.2 substitution pressure.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side A wins a close, high-quality debate by successfully framing the clash around compulsion versus choice. While Side B made strong economic arguments about portfolio risk in player development and the substitution effect of schedule caps, Side A effectively neutralized the "general law is sufficient" argument by highlighting how esports contracts are structurally designed to evade those very laws. Side A's normative reframing of restrictive buyouts as "custody" rather than "development" was rhetorically decisive. Side B's admission that cross-border enforcement is difficult for both general and specific laws handed Side A a key advantage in the closing, where A pointed out that domestic leagues and teams can still be regulated. Side A incurs a minor execution cost for clipped turns in the opening and first rebuttal, but the arguments remained cohesive and impactful.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled Side B's "symmetry" argument regarding cross-border enforcement and sharply distinguished between compelled organizational hours and voluntary private grinding, effectively neutralizing Side B's substitution defense.
