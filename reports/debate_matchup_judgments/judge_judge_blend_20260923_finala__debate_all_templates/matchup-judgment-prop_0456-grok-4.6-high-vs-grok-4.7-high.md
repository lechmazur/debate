### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent frameworks, but A landed the more decisive blow in the endgame. A's distinction between discrete, bounded compulsory licenses (mechanicals, retransmission) and training's perpetual, corpus-consuming competitor was never fully answered by B's insistence that training is "the same class of problem." More critically, A's pressure question about unclaimed payments exposed a structural flaw B could not escape: B's answer (A2 in Rebuttal 2) conceded that unclaimed money escheats to the state or other creators rather than the actual rightholder, and that training proceeds regardless of whether the specific owner is ever found or paid. A then correctly hammered this as proof that the "license" is fictional for exactly the vulnerable parties (session players, missing co-writers) B claimed to protect — the scheme clears the studio without ever compensating the person whose work was used. B's closing simply reasserted the escheat point without resolving why that counts as protecting the unfound author, leaving this a live, unrebutted route for A. B's case also suffered an execution cost: Rebuttal 1 was clipped for exceeding the word limit, weakening its development of the mechanical-license analogy and holdout argument right when it needed reinforcement. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions format correctly, but A's answers were tighter and more directly exploited by its own follow-up, while B's answers, though on-format, ultimately reinforced rather than defused A's strongest attack.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 point that "a check that escheats is not leverage; it makes the author optional" — directly exploiting B's own A2 answer that unclaimed funds don't return to the specific rightholder, exposing the compulsory scheme's core weakness that training proceeds without ever actually compensating the affected creator.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing Side A’s false choice between compulsory licensing and unpaid scraping. B consistently argued that unlawful training can instead be prohibited and that willing catalogs, commissioned material, and public-domain works provide a lawful route without erasing refusal. A answered implementation questions well, but never justified why a representative model requires access to unwilling owners’ works. A also weakened its case by claiming the scheme spends only one corpus while defending a standing permission that would cover future works whenever payment enters the pool. The clipped first rebuttal was a minor additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response that “already happening” describes a violation rather than a legitimate baseline was decisive. It undercut A’s repeated comparison of a public royalty with a private zero and forced A to justify compulsion against consent-based licensing, a burden A never fully met.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON won the core liability vs property clash. PRO's transaction-cost / market-failure case was strong but was undercut by CON's distinction that a compulsory cover is one song while a model is a permanent substitute, and by PRO's own concession in R2 that training continues when payment goes in but the owner is not found [B4.3]. That validates CON's tariff-on-confiscation frame and leaves PRO's promise to pay unseen session players unfulfilled. PRO's Pressure Q about representativeness was answered weakly, but A's Pressure Q about rate capture and unclaimed checks exposed a contradiction. B's clipped R1 added execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Rebuttal 2 exchange on unclaimed owners: A asks who keeps the check when it goes unclaimed and whether training continues [A3.2]; B admits money escheats to state/similar creators and training continues because condition is payment in [B4.3]; A turns that into "author optional" and "right satisfied without the person" [A4.4][A5.5].

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built tight, escalating cases, but B's throughline held up better under pressure. B's opening grounded the compulsory-license logic in real precedent (mechanical/blanket licenses), and when A's sharp Q1 forced the admission that even a locatable, refusing owner gets trained on for a fee, B answered honestly in proper A1/A2 form and folded it back into its own stated framework ("a refusal that cannot be administered is not the product copyright is defending") rather than being cornered. A's case—exclusivity as the product, leakage undermining the scheme's stated beneficiaries, and irreversible competitive harm from training—was strong and consistently rhetorically sharp ("if it shrinks the corpus, the corpus was never theirs"), but it never fully answered B's distributive-fairness point that independents and unknown owners get nothing under a pure "get a yes" regime, nor did it fully engage with why "buy the work" is administrable at training scale. B's closing crystallized the decisive comparison more cleanly than A's, which mostly restated its opening anchor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question forcing B to admit that a findable, all-price-refusing owner is still trained on under the scheme, and B's direct A1 answer owning that as the scheme's intended "forced sale" rather than evading it—this exchange defined the rest of the debate's clash.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): B won by exposing A’s false choice between unpaid scraping and compulsory licensing. Ordinary copyright offers a third path: use licensed material and omit works whose owners refuse. A candidly admitted that its scheme overrides even a findable illustrator’s explicit no, while never establishing why enforcement problems uniquely justify that forced sale or why the same enforcement cannot target unpaid scrapers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s decisive turn was that the unfound owner is merely the justification while the found, refusing owner bears the loss. B also showed that compulsory licensing does not uniquely solve evasion: the scraper remains unlawful either way, while the scheme affirmatively legalizes ingestion by firms that deposit a fee.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): CON won the core autonomy clash. PRO's case depended on orphan works and holdout costs, but conceded in A1 that a findable living illustrator who refuses every price is still trained on if the levy is paid. A turned that admission into the deciding frame: the unfound are the justification, the found are the sale. A also neutralized the scraper argument by showing unpaid training is already illegal, so a pot legalizes the same ingestion for payers rather than uniquely catching free riders. B never justified why leakage-prone collective payment should strip a locatable no.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3-A4.4 leveraging B4.1 — findable illustrator still trained — to reframe the scheme as a forced sale of the found to solve the unfound.
