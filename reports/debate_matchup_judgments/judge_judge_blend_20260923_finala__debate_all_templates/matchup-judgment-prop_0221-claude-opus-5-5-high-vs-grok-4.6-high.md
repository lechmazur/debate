- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the cleaner per-dollar comparison: tutoring targets larger marginal needs with a cheaper and broader labor pool, while class-size reduction requires many more certified teachers and rooms for modest universal gains. Side A’s reply relied on calling scaled tutoring “empty chairs” rather than establishing that collapse comparatively.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): PRO won by maintaining the per-dollar comparative frame and extracting a damaging admission: CON conceded that California’s scaled class-size rollout produced only “modest average gains.” PRO leveraged that ceiling with a built-in buffer—tutoring effects could be substantially discounted and still outperform that modest baseline on a per-dollar basis—while CON never proved that tutoring fully collapses at scale rather than merely shrinks. PRO’s targeting argument showed why concentrating dollars on the highest-need students generates larger marginal gains per total budget than spreading them thinly across every child, and PRO rebutted workforce fragility by showing class-size reduction faces the same hiring pressures with a scarcer, costlier input. CON’s denominator challenge was sharp but insufficient to overcome the conceded modesty of scaled class-size effects.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s pressure questions in Turn 5 forced CON to admit California’s statewide class-size reduction produced only “modest average gains,” and PRO crystallized that concession in the closing to establish a comparative ceiling that discounted tutoring could still surpass.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): B won by tying targeted instructional minutes and cheaper staffing more directly to gains per dollar. A created meaningful pressure around implementation leakage, but treated scaling failures asymmetrically and never established that modest universal class-size benefits outweigh much larger targeted gains, especially in grades 3–8. A’s clipped opening and weak answer on later grades also mattered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal was decisive: it showed that labor scarcity and implementation risk also burden class-size reduction, while the latter requires more certified teachers, permanent salaries, and classroom space. This substantially weakened A’s claim that scale uniquely destroys tutoring’s advantage.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by controlling the "funded at scale" frame and the per-dollar metric more convincingly. It established a clean, unrebutted asymmetry: unfilled class-size positions draw no salary, so the per-dollar ratio stays intact, whereas paid-for missed tutor sessions waste money for zero gain. Side A's retreat into a definitional dodge—that scaled failures "did not buy high-dosage tutoring"—allowed Side B to show that Side A was counting only the dollars that worked, evading the motion's actual cost accounting. Side A's targeted-intervention theory and thimble framing created real pressure, but Side B's structural-reliability argument and the live K–3 tension proved more durable after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 answer distinguishing unfilled teaching positions (no salary spent, per-dollar efficiency preserved) from paid-for missed tutor sessions (money lost, zero gain), which Side A never recovered in its closing.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A built a highly persuasive case around the "at scale" modifier, distinguishing between the structural reliability of class-size reduction and the logistical fragility of tutoring. Side A decisively won the clash on implementation leakage by pointing out that districts still pay for the upfront costs of tutoring even when sessions are missed, effectively dismantling Side B's attempt to define away poorly executed programs. Side A's pressure questions and closing were exceptionally sharp.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it dismantled Side B's claim that a district failing to deliver 3 sessions "didn't buy high-dosage tutoring" by pointing out that the district still paid for the coordinators, training, and infrastructure, thus ruining the per-dollar ratio.
