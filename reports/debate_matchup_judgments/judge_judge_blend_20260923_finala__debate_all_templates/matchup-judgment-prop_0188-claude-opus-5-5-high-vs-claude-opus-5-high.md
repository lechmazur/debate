### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A convincingly showed that results deposits alone cannot expose outcome switching and that preregistration supplies the baseline needed for auditing. Side B also offered a credible narrower alternative for the file-drawer problem and sustained its overbreadth objections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest turn was converting Side A’s enforcement answer into the journal-licensing objection: guaranteeing publication through pre-data acceptance means journals must approve funded projects before they begin. Side A’s reply that grants already undergo peer review did not neutralize the added editorial gate.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): B won by making the decisive clash easier to decide: the motion’s publishing half kept shrinking under pressure into conditional acceptance, then into pre-data journal gatekeeping that B framed as a licensing veto over public inquiry. A’s clipped turns were a minor execution drag; B’s route stayed tighter after the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2): converted A’s funder–registered-report enforcement answer into “journals become the licensing authority,” neutralizing the “regardless of outcome” half and raising the motion’s institutional price.

While CON landed damaging blows on the journal gatekeeping risks of PRO's mechanism, this cost did not outweigh the benefit of distinguishing prediction from post-hoc discovery across both clinical and social science research.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): A won narrowly by preserving the central distinction between screening study quality before results and selecting publication after seeing outcomes. B offered a strong targeted-registration and repository alternative, but it did not answer A's point that the influential journal literature would remain outcome-selected. B also leaned too heavily on treating the commitment as compulsory publication of every study regardless of design, rather than publication regardless of outcome after protocol review.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer to B's journal dilemma was decisive: journals may reject weak protocols before results exist without violating an outcome-independent publication commitment. That substantially weakened B's claim that the policy must either publish everything or leave its guarantee hollow.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): CON won the decisive clash on feasibility and burden. After conceding publication bias, B showed that the motion’s load-bearing half—journal “publish regardless of outcome”—cannot be delivered by funder mandates over independent journals, and that PRO’s “reject before results / send to another journal / norms and reputation” answers reduce the guarantee to existing voluntary Registered Reports plus exploratory paperwork that PRO admits constrains little. A’s crystal (“do they already know the result?”) is rhetorically strong but never solved the control problem or the already-funded protocol timing hole. B’s narrower funder-triggered registration plus universal results posting kept visibility where confirmatory work matters without monoculture or conscripted journals, and answered A’s pressure more cleanly than A answered B’s reject/file-drawer and secondary-data questions. Clear but competitive edge to B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s reject-vs-publish distinction and the follow-on dilemma (if journals may reject protocols the guarantee is hollow; if not, journals are conscripted), which forced PRO to walk the motion back toward voluntary Registered Reports.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by exposing that Side B could not defend the journal publishing guarantee as a genuine mandate. Side B admitted journals may still reject protocols, which collapses the "regardless of outcome" promise into existing voluntary Registered Reports, while Side A showed that its alternative of targeted confirmatory registration plus universal results posting actually guarantees public visibility without conscripting independent journals. Side A consistently sharpened this structural impossibility, and Side B never recovered from the dilemma that its guarantee is either hollow or unenforceable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 (Turn 4) framing the dilemma that allowing journals to reject protocols either sends null results back to the file drawer or conscripts publishers, which Side B never resolved and was forced to walk back in later speeches.
