### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): B won a competitive debate by showing that indirect burdens on poor households strengthen the case for targeted compensation rather than justify subsidizing affluent consumption. B also offered the clearer comparison between planned reform with payment infrastructure and abrupt subsidy collapse during fiscal shocks. A's strongest objection was the uniform five-year deadline across weak and heterogeneous states, which B never fully resolved, but A repeatedly assumed transfers must arrive after prices rise despite B's explicit sequencing and focused too heavily on preserving nominal prices rather than household purchasing power.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer that bakers and drivers need not receive separate payments because compensated consumers can pay the higher prices directly undercut A's central cost-chain objection. A preserved a real exclusion-risk concern, but its later-check framing did not squarely answer B's proposal to enroll and pay households before price changes.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by demonstrating that the specific mechanism of the motion—targeted cash replacing universal subsidies within five years—fails at the point of impact. CON showed that pass-through price rises for bread, transport, and clinic power hit before cash reaches households, and that bakers, minibus drivers, and other price-setters raise costs for everyone regardless of enrollment status. This temporal mismatch and non-household gap in the subsidy chain remained unresolved by PRO, whose theory that household cash cushions neutralize pass-through never fully overcame the fact that costs are incurred and prices reset upstream. CON also kept alive the heterogeneity objection—one rigid deadline cannot fit both wealthy Gulf rentiers and fragile North African importers like Libya—and exposed a fiscal dilemma PRO never squared: indexing transfers to inflation recreates the fiscal exposure, while leaving them unindexed erodes the cushion in the very crises that make subsidies unaffordable. CON's alternative of cutting premium gasoline while maintaining a universal floor under diesel and transport fuel provided a coherent, non-status-quo option that blunted PRO's attempt to frame CON as defending paralysis. PRO landed strong blows on regressivity and the chaos of sudden subsidy collapses, but without a convincing answer to how targeted cash prevents pass-through price spikes in real time, or how five years survives fiscal stress in the region's weakest states, PRO's path to victory remained incomplete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (A4), which sharpened the pass-through critique by showing that minibus owners and bakers pay the new diesel price before any transfer lands, that fares and bread prices reset for enrolled and unenrolled alike, and offered the premium-gasoline/diesel-floor alternative as a direct answer to PRO's pressure questions.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins the decisive clash on what the replacement does: universal fuel holds down bread, fare, and generator costs for everyone automatically, while targeted cash only reimburses some households after prices reset and misses price-setters and the unenrolled. That timing/coverage critique survived B's sequencing defense, exposes the Gulf vs North Africa capacity gap, and is paired with a credible alternative (cut premium gasoline, keep diesel/transport floor) that answers B's fiscal-crisis pressure. B's regressive/value-dump and legible-bargain arguments are strong but do not neutralize the pass-through problem or justify a uniform 5-year deadline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 and Closing: the baker/minibus driver pays new diesel before any transfer lands, so fares reset for enrolled and unenrolled alike; luxury vs staple split preserves fiscal saving without deleting the economy-wide stabilizer - directly undercutting PRO's "repairable engineering" and "more legible rentier bargain" claims.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): B won the central comparisons. It clearly distinguished broad categorical cash from a fuel subsidy that rewards higher consumption, and plausibly showed that a binding five-year destination can coexist with flexible intermediate steps. A raised meaningful capacity and shock concerns but overstated categorical coverage as equivalent to universality and largely ignored the subsidy’s regressivity, fiscal exposure, and poor fit for households using little fuel. Most damagingly, A made the absence of an observable reform threshold its centerpiece while sidestepping B’s direct question asking A to supply such a threshold for its own indefinite conditional approach. B’s two clipped rebuttals were an execution cost, but their visible substance remained coherent and decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions: categorical transfers track vulnerability rather than liters, while the deadline binds the destination rather than every interim step. A then failed to answer B’s reciprocal threshold question and instead repeated its demand that B provide one, weakening A’s main closing argument.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by exposing two critical flaws in PRO's plan. First, PRO's categorical fallback for unverifiable informal incomes effectively recreated a near-universal subsidy for the majority population, contradicting the promise of precise targeting while adding bureaucratic exclusion risk. Second, PRO never specified the observable trigger that would pause the five-year clock during currency or oil shocks, leaving a rigid deadline that risks landing precisely when states are weakest. PRO's conceptual case remained appealing, but CON landed sharper, more damaging rebuttals and framed the stakes more memorably.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 turning PRO's categorical-payment fallback into a rebuilt universal subsidy, and pressing the undefined threshold for PRO's supposed path flexibility, decisively undermined the feasibility of "targeted" transfers within the rigid five-year window.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): PRO kept the core comparison live: a fuel subsidy pays by liters - most to rich drivers - while even a broad categorical cash grant pays flat to vulnerable groups and excludes the rich, and a liter burned can't be clawed back but a missed household can be added. On indexing, subsidy bills explode when currencies collapse, making a freeze most costly exactly when CON wants it. CON's strongest flip that categorical = universal failed to answer the distribution difference, and its flexibility defense offered no bound, leaving "when conditions allow" equal to indefinite delay.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.2 answer to A2.1/A3.1 - distinguishing consumption-proportional subsidy from flat need-based categorical grant - turned CON's best attack that PRO had rebuilt universality into a demonstration that even broad proxies are more progressive and correctable.
