### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): B won the decisive clash on remedy fit and motion scope. A powerfully showed defaults are sticky distribution and that hidden one-tap exits reach few users, but repeatedly had to expand the rule (ban paid slots, mandate neutral setup) to escape B's dilemma. B kept returning to the motion as written: self-preferencing bans leave Google-style paid defaults or familiarity-driven choice screens that still crown giants, while access-plus-one-tap fixes every concrete harm A named. B's clipped R1 cost some polish but did not erase the cleaner comparative path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B turning A's Google-pays-Apple evidence against the motion: that deal is paid preferencing the rule does not bar, so A's strongest market-rigging example sits outside the proposition while B's access remedies still cover hardware/switching harms.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): A won because it kept its structural argument live: defaults are immensely sticky and create a self-reinforcing moat of distribution, data, and developer attention. CON conceded that defaults are worth billions, which undermined its own claim that one-tap reassignment and access remedies neutralize the harm. PRO successfully showed that access without default neutrality is theater, and that breaking inherited defaults is the only way to make rivals contestable even if they do not win instantly. CONs strongest counter—that the ban auctions the throne to other giants—was sharp, but PROs response that structural OS control is permanent while commercial slots are contestable, combined with the mechanism of neutral setup moments, gave the clearer path to victory. CONs Rebuttal 1 being clipped also left its comparative weighing slightly underdeveloped.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PROs rebuttal that CONs own admission that defaults are bought and sold for billions proves that one-tap reassignment cannot neutralize default power, crystallized in the line that access without default neutrality is permission without users.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively exposing the fatal flaw in Side A's interpretation of the motion. Side B proved that barring self-preferencing does not bar paid preferencing, meaning an OS maker would simply auction the default slot to the highest-bidding non-OS giant (like Google), replacing one incumbent with another. Side A's attempt to argue the ban "naturally covers" paid deals was a weak evasion of the motion's explicit text. Side B's alternative of access remedies and one-tap reassignment directly solved the specific harms Side A identified without the unintended consequences of Side A's ban. Side B's closing crystallized this perfectly, demonstrating that the motion merely auctions the throne rather than dismantling it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it turned Side A's own evidence regarding billions paid for search defaults against it to prove that stripping an OS maker of its default slot would just result in an auction won by another tech giant, leaving startups exactly where they started.
