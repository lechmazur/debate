  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing perfectly neutralized Side A's "zero-balance wall" and spend cap arguments by explaining that on a product with no pause, the cap only fires when the unreflective sitting is already over.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully defending the proposition's premise that the harm profile of ultra-fast gambling is distinct, focusing on the psychological loss of control and dissociation caused by the deletion of natural pauses. Side B clarified that product-level design constraints are necessary to restore the pause, and that regulating fast products "more aggressively" means adding these specific design brakes on top of baseline protections, rather than abandoning slow products to a "lighter" regime. Side A's reliance on financial ruin as the sole metric of harm was too narrow to overcome Side B's broader, mechanism-based framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing to Side A's "zero balance wall" argument. Side B pointed out that relying on a financial cap to stop a fast session means letting the dissociative "conveyor" run until the money is gone, allowing the psychological harm to occur, whereas slower products naturally have pauses where baseline protections and reflection can work before a hard cap is ever hit.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that rapid, continuous re-staking creates an additive risk profile: it compresses losses into emotionally heightened sessions and removes opportunities for reflection or intervention. Side A offered credible universal loss and credit controls, but largely reduced harm to total money lost and did not establish why baseline protections preclude additional product-specific friction. Its claim that distinct harm must mean an entirely different ultimate injury set too demanding a burden for the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its answer that casino loops and micro in-play markets can require different tools while sharing the same mechanism of continuous in-session re-staking. The baseline-plus-extra-brakes distinction directly answered Side A’s halo objection and preserved the case for differential regulation.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully proving that the psychological capture (the 'trance' and continuous loop) of ultra-fast gambling is a distinct harm requiring distinct, targeted friction. Side A's proposal of universal loss and session limits was strong, but B successfully forced A to admit that temporal controls are necessary to stop overnight binges, framing this as a concession that speed matters.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Closing effectively weaponized A's Rebuttal 2 concession about needing 'overnight stops', cleanly pointing out that A ultimately had to rely on time/velocity regulation to solve the binge B identified.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling the comparative burden of the motion. The proposition required proving that ultra-fast products have a "distinct" harm profile justifying "more aggressive" regulation than slower forms. Side A successfully argued that the core harm (financial ruin via bingeing) is not distinct to fast products, and that universal brakes on losses and credit solve the problem without creating a regulatory "speed caste." Side B's strongest trap—arguing that Side A's proposed session limits are essentially time regulations—was expertly defused in Side A's closing. Side A clarified that universal session caps apply equally to all gambling and do not rank products by spin rate, meaning hitting a universal loss ceiling sooner on a fast slot is not a "second ruin" requiring harsher, differential regulation. Side A's logical progression and sharp weighing in the final speeches made the decisive clash clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing response to Side B's "clock" trap, distinguishing universal session limits from the motion's demand for a differential speed caste, and proving that hitting a universal loss ceiling sooner does not constitute a distinct harm requiring harsher regulation.
