### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Both sides built genuinely strong cases and this was close, but A did the better job of converting pressure into progress. A's pressure questions forced B into a real bind (the "undeserved aura of authenticity" line), and although B's defusal ("scheme-created risk, not a concession") was clever, it read as a partial dodge rather than a clean resolution. A also did more constructive work under B's strongest attack—the vagueness/enforcement charge—by offering a concrete threshold (material alteration + audience-targeting analogues to existing sponsorship/age-targeting practice) and a scalability/undetectability rationale for why AI-specific regulation isn't arbitrary underinclusion. B's best point—that A's theory equivocates between "false product claim" and "impossible body ideal," and that equivalent harms via makeup/posing go unregulated—was never fully dismantled by A, but A's authenticity-of-the-image framing gave a coherent enough answer that the equivocation charge didn't collapse A's case. A's closing crystallized the comparative stakes ("who bears the cost, the child or a four-word label") more memorably than B's more diffuse "follow the claim, not the toolchain" close. B's case suffered a minor execution cost from a clipped rebuttal. On balance, A narrowed the dispute more effectively and left the cleaner decision rule standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question Q2 ("doesn't that concede undisclosed enhancement itself misleads minors?") forced B to walk a fine line between defending disclosure-skepticism and admitting the underlying harm; B's answer was inventive but only partially defused the trap, leaving A's authenticity framing intact as the more decisive live route.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's practical examples. When B pushed the 'protein powder next to an impossible waist' example, A cleanly absorbed it by pointing out that if it makes an implied claim, existing law can ban it regardless of editing technique. Furthermore, A expertly neutralized B's pressure question by explaining that the 'aura of authenticity' for unlabeled images is a scheme-created risk, not a concession of inherent deception.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's explanation in Rebuttal 2 that the 'aura of authenticity' is a scheme-created risk directly neutralized B's strongest pressure question.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing the motion regulates invisible production methods rather than the actual harm of deception, leaving identical beauty ideals untouched while creating unverifiable compliance burdens and a false sense of authenticity for unlabeled images. PRO never overcame the tailoring problem: the same persuasive effect and beauty standard can be achieved through posing, makeup, or lighting, yet only digital alteration triggers the rule. PRO's enforcement mechanisms relied on weak tools like creator attestation, and its intuitive "product is the body" framing collapsed into mere workflow regulation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's distinction between an implied false causal claim (already regulable) and a mere production method, which denied PRO's claim of a regulatory gap and exposed that the motion targets how pixels were made rather than what was falsely communicated to minors.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO built the more decisive frame: disclosure shifts the burden of uncertainty from the deceived minor to the party who possesses the concealed information, and PRO used the pressure-question exchange effectively to expose a real gap in CON's alternative — non-explicit-claim influencer content that presents a fabricated body as personal reality, which existing false-advertising/endorsement law may never reach. CON's answer to that pressure ("if nothing is sold, this isn't advertising regulation") essentially conceded the gap by narrowing the scope of what counts as covered "influencer content," which sits awkwardly with the motion's explicit inclusion of influencer content alongside advertising. CON's vagueness and chilling-effect case was strong and consistently pressed, but PRO neutralized much of it with a workable tu quoque (CON's own "implied product-body promise" test is equally contextual) and by conceding manageable line-drawing rather than perfection. CON's claim that disclosure achieves nothing because "the image still lands" was met adequately by PRO's point that safeguards need not eliminate harm to be worthwhile. PRO's Rebuttal 2 being clipped for exceeding the word limit is a real execution cost, but the core content had already landed by that point, so it did not decisively undercut PRO's path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure question about non-commercial influencer content presenting a fabricated body as personal reality, and CON's response effectively conceding that such content falls outside its preferred enforcement regime, was the most decisive exchange — it exposed a real hole in CON's "existing law suffices" case that CON never fully closed.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON won by decisively answering PRO's charge that both sides rely on subjective standards. CON successfully distinguished between ex post fraud enforcement (where a subjective standard is a narrow trigger) and ex ante platform mandates (where vague adjectives cause platforms to over-censor small creators). By forcing PRO to concede that platform overreach is an "implementation problem," CON secured the stronger structural argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal distinguishing ex ante mandates from ex post fraud enforcement was the most decisive exchange, as it completely neutralized PRO's strongest defense of the policy's vagueness.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO successfully turned CON’s vagueness attack by showing that CON’s own preferred alternative—relying on “implied product-body promises” under existing law—requires equally contextual, borderline judgments while leaving a larger coverage gap. CON never recovered from this symmetry: if borderline cases doom PRO’s rule, they also doom CON’s. PRO then exposed that existing false-advertising law often fails when an influencer silently presents a synthetic body as personal reality without an explicit product claim, a gap CON’s closing never squarely filled. PRO’s framing of who should bear epistemic uncertainty (the concealing creator, not the child viewer) gave a cleaner comparative warrant, and CON’s perfectionist standard—that disclosure must eliminate all harm to be worthwhile—was less persuasive than PRO’s incremental-benefit account. While CON’s platform-overenforcement point had some force, PRO’s cumulative qualifiers (“aimed at minors” plus “heavy” material alteration) provided enough definitional structure to survive as a workable rule. The clip on PRO’s Rebuttal 2 was a minor execution cost, but the contained answers and argument were sufficient.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s pressure question Q2 and subsequent R2 turn asking why CON accepts contextual judgment for “implied product-body promises” but rejects it for “material body alteration,” thereby exposing that CON’s alternative is both vaguer and narrower. This exchange did decisive damage to CON’s live route to victory by collapsing the distinction between the two sides’ epistemic standards while highlighting the larger enforcement gap under CON’s regime.
