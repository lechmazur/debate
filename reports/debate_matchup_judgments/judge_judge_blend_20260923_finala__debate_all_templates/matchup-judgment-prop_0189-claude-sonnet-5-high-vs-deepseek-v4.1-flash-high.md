### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won by replacing A’s risk-versus-perfection framing with the more relevant comparison of residual harm per scarce dollar. A plausibly established that sewage overflows are especially dangerous, but repeatedly overstated separation as a one-time fix with a merely puddle-like failure mode. B showed that separated stormwater remains polluted, sanitary sewers can still spill, both networks require maintenance, and adequate runoff treatment adds further cost. Most importantly, A never established that full separation should prevail across sites despite opportunity costs to other urgent water and health investments. A’s opening was also clipped, while B consistently narrowed the debate around the motion’s costly mandate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was its answer to A’s “bounded failure” framing: separation does not reduce failure to harmless street water, because untreated stormwater still reaches waterways and the sanitary network can still overflow. That directly undermined A’s central asymmetry while reinforcing B’s residual-risk-per-dollar standard.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): CON won by keeping the decisive clause—separation even when upfront cost is much higher—live and unanswered as a universal mandate. PRO built the stronger health/failure-mode story (raw-sewage spill vs. runoff; design feature vs. exception; treadmill billing), but repeatedly slid into arguing separation is often better or cheaper long-horizon, which does not carry the hard “even when” cases. CON’s residual-risk-after-spending and opportunity-cost framing, plus PRO’s own narrow no-case concession, made the motion look like a rigid pipe mandate rather than max harm reduction. Competitive on substance; clearer decision rule for CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 / closing on “even when”: once PRO admits no-cases and that bare separation needs a second treatment layer, residual risk per dollar beats permanent-risk-elimination rhetoric.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by establishing and defending a clearer comparative calculus: bounded, non-toxic failure modes versus an unbounded, worsening public-health crisis engineered into combined systems. CON effectively pressed the "even when" clause and opportunity costs, but PRO preserved the mandate by distinguishing mere sticker shock from technical infeasibility, and their framing of overflow management as a permanent subscription with no ceiling remained standing. CON's attempt to equate residual risks did not fully overcome the categorical asymmetry between designed-in sewage overflow and exceptional separated-system failures.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2 ([B4.1]–[B4.2]), where they crystallized that separation delivers a bounded failure mode (water on a street) compared to combined-system overflow management's unbounded public-health crisis (sewage in the river), directly answering CON's pressure question while sharpening the core comparative weighing.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by keeping the comparison focused on eliminating sewage-stormwater mixing rather than pretending separation eliminates every form of runoff pollution. A directly answered the cost, phasing, and stormwater-management objections and distinguished a finite transition from indefinite overflow mitigation. B raised strong disruption, opportunity-cost, and adaptability concerns, but repeatedly conflated ordinary polluted runoff with raw-sewage overflows and overstated that A had left runoff untreated despite A expressly proposing retention, infiltration, wetlands, or targeted treatment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s response to B’s stormwater objection was decisive: separated systems still require management, but that does not negate their central benefit of keeping sewage in the treatment system during storms. B never established that road runoff and combined sewage overflows were equivalent in severity, and its closing mischaracterized A’s answer.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): A won a competitive debate by keeping the motion’s decision rule central: even at higher upfront cost, ending the mixing defect is better than permanently managing it. A’s pathogen-vs-runoff distinction and finite-transition vs forever-rent framing survived pressure better than B’s cost-stacking and adaptability attacks, which damaged A’s “expensive once” rhetoric but never fully erased the end-state advantage. B landed sharper tactical hits on phasing and continuous untreated runoff, yet A answered the required A1/A2 structure more cleanly and left B without a residual-sewage standard that residents should accept indefinitely.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 1 false-equivalence break: combined overflows dump raw sewage; separated stormwater is not raw sewage and need not be unmanaged “straight to the river.”
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by leveraging the motion's built-in cost concession and framing the debate around a permanent structural defect. B established that combined sewers inherently mix raw sewage with stormwater, creating a recurring public-health risk that overflow management never removes, whereas separation treats sewage regardless of weather and moves stormwater to controlled pathways. A landed solid blows on the tension between B's "expensive once" slogan and the reality of multi-decade phasing, and on the fact that separated stormwater still carries pollutants. However, B successfully distinguished raw sewage from stormwater runoff and showed that A's alternatives are themselves a form of perpetual overflow management, not a genuine fix. B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly—recurrence versus replacement—and A never fully justified why a city should accept intermittent raw-sewage overflows indefinitely rather than eliminate the root mixing defect.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal that combined systems convert rain into raw sewage while separation treats sewage regardless of weather, and that green infrastructure on combined systems still receives sewage when capacity is exceeded, materially damaged Side A's claim that overflow management delivers equivalent environmental benefits at lower cost.
