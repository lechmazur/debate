### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B won because Side A conceded the substantive duty at issue: former powers should pay for conservation, archives, and capacity under recipient control. A’s case therefore rested on claiming that including support within restitution necessarily creates donor certification and a veto, but B consistently offered simultaneous return, recipient-set priorities, controlled funds, and waiver. Those mechanisms preserve unconditional physical return while preventing former powers from declaring moral closure before financing the harms they created.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s answer to the waiver fork was decisive: an injured party’s ability to waive an owed remedy does not show that the duty never formed part of repair. This undercut A’s strongest closing argument, while A never established why recipient-controlled funding necessarily lets the former power decide when an object is “officially home.”
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON won the decisive definitional clash. PRO built a strong moral/practical case that bare shipping dumps colonial costs on recipients, but never escaped CON’s fork: if recipients may waive funded support and still treat return as complete, funding is not what completes restitution; if they may not, former powers retain a completion veto. CON absorbed PRO’s conservation/archive concerns into unconditional title/custody plus a later claimable, recipient-controlled debt, and made that comparison cleaner and more decisive across the later rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s use of B’s waiver concession [B4.4] to collapse “funding as completion” into either optional aid (CON’s model) or donor leverage over when return counts.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B wins by exposing a structural fork that Side A could not escape: if recipient nations can waive funding and still consider restitution complete, the motion collapses into unconditional return plus optional aid; if they cannot waive it, former colonial powers retain leverage as adjudicators of completion. Side A's concession that waivers are possible without negating the duty failed to show why the motion's framing adds anything beyond ordinary reparations. Side B maintained a cleaner alternative—unconditional return plus enforceable, recipient-controlled reparative obligations—that better protected sovereignty without restoring donor conditionality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 introduced the decisive waiver dilemma, showing that Side A's model either reduces to unconditional return or gives the former power a veto over completion.
