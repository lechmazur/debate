- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by sustaining a concrete core case about the warehouse worker losing coverage exactly when illness strikes, repeatedly turning Side B's proposed patches into concessions that public financing must rescue the sick anyway.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Q1-Q2 and closing turn: when the warehouse worker gets sick, CON funds her from other people's payroll then a public backstop, so the job tie only works until you need it - and a program only for the jobless sick is the queue she cannot leave.

### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B successfully neutralized A's core coverage-loss argument by showing employer-based systems can sever the access-work tie through universal backstops and portability (Germany/Japan), then established a live structural harm under single-payer—national queues with no competitor or exit—that A never fully answered. B's pressure questions exposed that single-payer reintroduces a wealth tie when queues grow, and B's closing crystallized that both systems can promise universal membership, but only B's model contains failure when a payer underperforms. A's principled argument about financing architecture remained audible but could not overcome B's comparative showing on access resilience, choice, and risk diversification.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's exploitation of A's admission in the second rebuttal that single-payer queue escape requires private wealth, which B weaponized in the closing to show single-payer replaces a work-status tie with a wealth tie; paired with B's reframing of Germany's employment-linked model as a billing channel rather than an access gate.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): CON wins the narrow framing. PRO establishes that raw work-tie = membership denial, but CON recasts well-built employer-financing as mandatory universal with state-paid backstop, so layoff is billing shift not gate. PRO then concedes near-universal enrollment is not the motion [A4.4] and retreats to ledger purity — floor that never asks employer. CON's pressure on queues exposes that single-payer's escape is private pay wealth tie [A4.1], while CON keeps contained failure and fund choice. After exchange both sides can secure the floor; CON gives the cleaner second-order reason: diversified job risk plus ability to switch funds when one payer errs beats one national budget queue with no exit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turn [B4.5-B4.7] — seizing PRO's "near-universal is not the motion" to collapse PRO to an accounting preference and flipping PRO's own private-pay concession into a wealth tie — was the most decisive exchange.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully shifting the comparison from a flawed US-style employer system to a robust German-style multi-payer system. This neutralized Side A's primary attack (the layoff coverage cliff) while preserving the benefits of competition and risk diversification. Side B also effectively exploited Side A's concession regarding private escape routes for single-payer queues, turning it into a "wealth tie" vulnerability. Side A's rhetorical flourishes were strong, but Side B's structural arguments and comparative weighing were more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side A's "queue vs membership" distinction by pointing out that belonging to a single, inescapable national queue is not meaningful access, and highlighting that Side A's proposed escape route (private pay) simply replaces a work tie with a wealth tie.
