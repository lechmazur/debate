### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won the central incentive clash: producers choose packaging formats and can redesign them, while taxpayers and shoppers cannot. Side A raised strong concerns about regressivity, fee design, and fixed collection costs, but these mostly challenged implementation rather than showing why producers should continue facing little end-of-life cost. Its alternative also shifted responsibility to consumers even when they lacked realistic control over packaging design. Side B’s repeated word-limit clipping was an execution cost, but its core case remained clear and comparatively stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s pressure question and closing response on the unrecyclable pouch were decisive. Side A answered that the shopper chose and discarded it, but Side B showed that the producer chose whether the package was recyclable in the first place. That made Side A’s disposal-charge model look indirect and poorly targeted compared with charging the actor able to redesign the waste stream.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by demonstrating that producers are the only actors with the power to redesign packaging, meaning a cost signal must land on them to reduce waste. Side B argued effectively that shelf charges are regressive and that pay-as-you-throw (PAYT) systems could shift costs to discarders, but Side A's closing decisively dismantled this by pointing out consumers cannot send a demand signal against unrecyclable packaging if that is all the producer offers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing refutation of Side B's PAYT alternative, pointing out that billing the shopper for disposal penalizes the party with no power to redesign the product while exempting the boardroom that engineered it.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by decisively proving that only producer-side liability forces the upstream redesign of unrecyclable packaging. While Side B landed strong critiques on the regressivity of flat fees and the mechanics of deposits, Side A's closing argument—that consumers cannot be penalized for disposal when they have no choice over the packaging design—fatally undermined Side B's consumer-side alternative. Side A incurs a slight execution cost for repeated clipping at the end of three speeches, but the core argumentative clash was resolved in their favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing dismantling of Side B's "shopper creates the rubbish" claim by pointing out that shoppers face a supermarket full of pre-designed unrecyclable laminates, making consumer-side disposal charges a penalty for corporate design choices rather than a true incentive for waste reduction.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by tying payment responsibility to producers' unique control over packaging design and by showing that standards alone neither fund ongoing collection nor create continuous incentives to reduce waste. Side A made a strong regressivity case and correctly noted consumer pass-through, but it never fully answered why taxpayers should retain the annual bill or why pass-through eliminates firms' incentive to redesign. B also benefited from A's explicit concession that taxpayers would continue funding collection. A's two clipped rebuttals and B's one clipping were execution costs, while B's mistaken reference labels were a minor flaw.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered both questions, explained that lower-packaging consumers can pay less, and turned Side A's standards alternative by arguing that standards can coexist with producer funding but do not resolve who pays the recurring collection bill.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's defense of the status quo. While Side B landed a very sharp distinction between one-off regulation costs and ongoing fee costs, Side A decisively won the philosophical clash over who should pay. Side A's argument that packaging waste is a socialized private cost—not a true public good like roads or sewers—effectively neutralized Side B's claim that progressive taxation is the fairest funding mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing beautifully dismantled Side B's "public service" principle by contrasting genuine public goods (sewers/roads) with the socialized private cost of dealing with a marketing department's multi-layer pouch.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built a more compelling and principled case for why the burden of waste management should shift to producers. A effectively neutralized B's pass-through argument by reframing it as a necessary mechanism to stop the cross-subsidization of high-packaging consumers by low-packaging taxpayers. A's closing crystallized the clash memorably, dismantling B's analogy between waste sorting and universal public services like roads. While B made strong analytical points about the regressivity of flat fees and the efficiency of direct standards, B ultimately failed to defend the status quo of taxpayer funding for private packaging decisions as persuasively as A attacked it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's "universal public service" analogy in the closing, sharply distinguishing between inherent public goods like sewers and the socialized private costs of marketing-driven packaging choices.
