- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B won by consistently turning Side A's mechanisms and examples against them. B effectively showed that A's premier example (Mexico's IMMEX) succeeded by reducing friction, not by adding the protectionist mandates the motion defends.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's distinction between IMMEX (which cheapens imported inputs) and local-content mandates (which act as a second national wall) surgically dismantled Side A's core historical precedent.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A wins because its phased, export-tested industrial policy survives the central clash more intact than Side B's public-goods-only alternative. B never fully resolves the funding contradiction A exposes: if fiscal constraints forbid targeted incentives, they also forbid the broad infrastructure B prescribes, and A offers a more credible path from anchor-tenant zones to widened tax bases.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Turn 4 rebuttal introducing the tight/loose dilemma—"conditions tight enough to anchor investment deter the plant; conditions loose enough to land it collapse the strategy"—is the debate's sharpest analytical blow, though Side A's Turn 7 response on phased implementation and geographic cost advantages keeps A's route to victory alive.

While A provided a practical "phased" defense, B successfully argued that this merely delays the inevitable costs and risks of the surcharge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, specifically the answers to the pressure questions where B distinguished between general public goods and firm-specific collateral, exposing the circularity of staking a substation on a single tenant's hoped-for exports while the wider port still clogs.
