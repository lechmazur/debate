### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON's decisive move was flipping PRO's own school-zone analogy: if the driver of harm is the living-room context (impulsivity, family audience, unavoidable clock), then every gambling ad sharing that context should face the same restriction, not just sportsbook ads — treating unlike products alike within a genuinely dangerous zone is exactly what the analogy demands, not forbids. PRO never fully resolved this. When pressed (A's Q2) on whether "more aggressively" means a tighter cap or a feature ban, PRO answered "both," which let CON close by showing the cap portion is an unequal speed limit inside the same zone (unjustified under PRO's own logic) while the feature-ban portion, though better justified by the in-play/referee-function distinction, doesn't by itself vindicate the broader "more aggressive" standard the motion requires. PRO's "checkout at the whistle" and "referee the match" framing was vivid and identified a real mechanistic difference (live in-play synchronization), and its answers to pressure questions were direct and non-evasive, but it left the cap/ban tension and the youth-exposure symmetry argument (kids see casino/lottery ads in the same living room too) insufficiently answered. CON's case suffered an execution cost from a clipped Rebuttal 1, but recovered its core points in Rebuttal 2 and closing, and its final trap on the cap-vs-ban inconsistency was the sharpest unresolved point standing at the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's reversal of the school-zone analogy (B2.4 to A2.2/A4.1-2) — arguing that if the living room itself is the hazard, every gambling ad in it deserves equal restriction, not a special penalty for sportsbook ads — forced PRO into an admission (B4.3, "both a cap and a ban") that CON then exploited in closing to expose the unresolved logical tension in PRO's proposed rule.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by identifying a distinctive interaction between product and context: the live sportsbook prompt converts the unfolding event itself into an immediate wagering opportunity before a broad, youth-facing audience. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): The decisive clash was whether sports-betting ads during live play are mechanistically distinct from other gambling ads, or whether shared audience capture means all gambling ads deserve identical treatment. CON (A) ran a clean, consistent "equal products, equal rules" line and scored real points on displacement and on the concession that casino/lottery ads reach the same captive audience. But CON's central rebuttal to PRO's mechanism claim was repeatedly just "branding, not a proven toxin," which never engaged with PRO's concrete structural point: in-play odds are embedded live in the broadcast graphics, update with the score, and are sold as fan loyalty in real time — a temporal/structural integration a static casino or lottery spot cannot replicate. PRO (B) built and sustained a floor/ceiling framework across rounds (shared exposure justifies a common floor for all gambling ads; distinctive mechanism justifies a higher ceiling only for in-play sports betting), which directly answered CON's "why not one universal cap" pressure question rather than dodging it. CON's closing kept restating the equal-treatment slogan without cracking that framework. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions cleanly with proper A1/A2 structure, but PRO's answers did more actual conceptual work, while CON's answers mostly asserted skepticism. The debate was close and well-argued on both sides, but PRO's mechanism-based distinction proved more resilient under repeated challenge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 answer in Rebuttal 2 (B4.3) — reframing CON's "why not one tighter cap for all gambling ads" pressure question as a floor/ceiling distinction — most effectively neutralized CON's strongest consistency argument, and CON's repeated "branding, not toxin" response never fully answered the structural claim about live, real-time odds integration.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by distinguishing shared exposure from distinctive potency. Its floor-versus-ceiling framing showed that restricting all gambling ads does not preclude tighter limits for prompts synchronized with live play, immediate odds, and existing fandom. Side A effectively established that casino and lottery ads can reach the same captive audience, but repeatedly calling event-linked betting mere branding did not neutralize B’s plausible mechanism of greater immediacy and integration.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s answer that shared reach justifies a common floor but not an identical ceiling decisively answered Side A’s strongest objection. It preserved concern for other gambling ads while explaining why live, event-linked sportsbook prompts warrant differential treatment.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B successfully distinguished between a baseline "floor" of restrictions for all gambling ads and a higher "ceiling" for sports-betting ads, grounded in the unique mechanism of in-play betting and live odds integration. Side A repeatedly relied on the "same room, same rule" slogan but failed to effectively rebut the structural differences in how sports-betting ads integrate with the live event, dismissing them as mere "branding." Side B's conceptual clarity and direct answers to pressure questions made for a more convincing and nuanced case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it introduced the "floor vs. ceiling" distinction to neutralize Side A's primary attack that stricter sports-betting rules would leave casino ads under-regulated.
