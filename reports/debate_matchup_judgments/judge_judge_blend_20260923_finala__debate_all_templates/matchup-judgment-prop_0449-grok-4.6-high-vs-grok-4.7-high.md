### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a close, high-quality debate by showing that enforceable penalties need not guarantee every plant closes to improve incentives at borders and loan desks. Side A powerfully exposed hardship for late developers, but too often treated the Chinese holdout as defeating the entire policy while offering only the already-inadequate alternative of waiting until substitutes arrive voluntarily. B better weighed partial but substantial progress against continued unpriced free-riding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s decisive response was that demanding penalties forcibly shut every self-financed Chinese plant mistakes enforcement for guaranteed compliance. Its argument that the hardest holdout does not justify leaving coal unpriced everywhere else blunted Side A’s main route, though A’s blackout-country objection remained significant.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully decoupling 'incentives' from 'capacity,' arguing convincingly that financial penalties cannot overcome physical infrastructure bottlenecks like monsoon seasons or reactor build times. B expertly weaponized A's concessions to show the policy would fail to curb the biggest emitters while actively punishing the poorest for structural realities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive, taking A's explicit admission that penalties would still apply during load-shedding to prove the policy punishes genuine physical shortage, not just a lack of willpower.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by decisively neutralizing Side B's two main offensives: the "China holdout" and the "developing world blackout" arguments. Side A effectively demonstrates that border and finance penalties do not require a military siege of Beijing to be effective; they change the economics for new plants and protect compliers from being undercut. More importantly, Side A exposes the fatal moral hazard in Side B's demand for leniency: waiving penalties for countries that fail to build replacements by 2040 would simply incentivize governments to intentionally under-invest in alternatives so they can plead a blackout and keep burning coal. Side B relies heavily on the soundbite "expense does not pour concrete," but fails to overcome Side A's point that the threat of the penalty is precisely what forces capital allocation toward grids and storage today. Side A's logical dismantling of Side B's "escape hatches" in the later rounds makes their path to victory much cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it systematically dismantles Side B's pressure questions by showing that accepting either of Side B's "escape hatches" (sparing the giant or waiving the blackout) would render any dated climate rule unenforceable, while firmly defending the necessity of the hard deadline to prevent moral hazard.
