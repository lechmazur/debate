### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): B won by identifying a unique benefit that A's regulatory floor did not provide: locally negotiated substantive limits on workload, targets, retraining, and cumulative changes. B's framework-bargaining answer substantially reduced A's continuous-update objection, while A's late statutory cumulative-review alternative was underdeveloped and introduced too late. A raised credible concerns about delay, litigation, and unequal council leverage, but overstated that any meaningful bargaining power entails paralysis. A's two clipped turns and questionable claim that night-shift mis-scoring automatically creates anti-discrimination liability were additional execution costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer that councils negotiate a governing framework rather than every model retraining was decisive. It answered the freeze objection while turning A's incremental-update example into a reason for negotiated cumulative caps, exposing that disclosure and review alone may document a workload ratchet without limiting it.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won because it established that direct regulation can only provide procedural protections (the floor) while bargaining is uniquely able to set the substantive, locally-calibrated limits on AI-driven pace and targets that statute cannot write. CON’s counter that the duty would trigger paralyzing delay and litigation was blunted by PRO’s comparative point that large unionized employers already operate under identical impasse machinery without paralysis, a distinction CON never fully leveraged against in the weighing. CON further suffered material execution costs from clipped opening and closing turns that left its final crystallization incomplete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 2 (B4): turning CON’s ratchet pressure question into an argument for framework bargaining, and pressing CON’s failure to explain why existing impasse machinery already used by large unionized employers does not produce the paralysis CON predicted.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by establishing a highly compelling distinction between procedural rights (the "floor") and substantive outcomes (the "number"). PRO's argument that an explanation right is practically useless if the underlying algorithmic target is inherently unfair resonates strongly and justifies the need for negotiation. CON's best argument—that negotiation creates delay and distributes benefits based on local bargaining power—was analytically clever, but PRO successfully parried it by pointing out that this is a general feature of all collective bargaining, which already functions without paralyzing large employers. CON's failure to adequately distinguish AI from existing bargaining subjects, combined with execution costs from clipped opening and closing speeches, secures a solid victory for PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2, where they answer the pressure questions by distinguishing between procedural rules and substantive targets ("the number"), and explain that bargaining a governing framework once solves the continuous retrain problem, effectively neutralizing CON's delay and ratchet concerns.
