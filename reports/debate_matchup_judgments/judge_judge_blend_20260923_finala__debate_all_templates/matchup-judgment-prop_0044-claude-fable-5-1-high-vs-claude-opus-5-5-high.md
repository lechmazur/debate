### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by isolating the mandate's distinctive liabilities: individualized guarantees encourage conservative promises, while physical packaging is stale or unavailable before online purchases. Side A established real consumer harms and offered strong responses, but never fully defeated B's distinction between passive hardware durability and the continuing expense of servers and patches. B also showed how a uniform legal floor plus flexible point-of-sale disclosure could preserve comparison without attaching extra liability to longer forecasts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to the warranty challenge was decisive: laptops can keep functioning after repair duties expire, whereas cloud-dependent devices require ongoing spending and can be deliberately switched off. That distinction rehabilitated B's warranty analogy and directly supported the claim that a guaranteed date could become a shutdown date.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): CON successfully showed that the motion's mechanism—self-selected, legally binding dates on physical packaging—would likely backfire by creating perverse incentives to lowball and treat the printed date as a shutdown target, using the warranty market's long stagnation at one year as evidence. PRO's efficiency-label analogy failed because support dates are forecasts of future corporate behavior, not current measurements, and PRO never recovered from CON's point that stale shelf dates would punish firms that extended support. CON's alternative of a uniform regulatory floor plus updatable point-of-sale disclosure also survived PRO's attempts to collapse it into the motion, preserving competitive information while avoiding the liability structure that drives numbers down.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal distinguishing passive hardware warranties from active software support obligations, using the warranty market's stagnation at roughly one year to prove that printed floors become ceilings when firms bear liability for self-selected numbers.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by making sharper, more decisive logical distinctions. B's differentiation between hardware warranties (where the product keeps working passively) and smart device support (which requires active, ongoing server expense) effectively dismantled PRO's main counter-argument. Furthermore, B successfully defended their alternative in the closing by clarifying that point-of-sale disclosure still allows for competition, just without the perverse incentive of self-chosen legal liability. PRO argued well but ultimately relied on the optimistic assumption that marketing would override legal risk, which B's warranty analogy convincingly disproved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they distinguished hardware warranties from software support. By pointing out that laptops don't die at month 13 because hardware runs passively, whereas servers require active expense that firms will drop the moment a legal duty lapses, B decisively neutralized PRO's strongest pressure question and analogy.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side A narrowly won by defending the relevant comparison: even a conservative guaranteed floor gives buyers more actionable information than silence, while solvent firms can be held to it. Side B compellingly identified lowballing, dishonest firms, and online-packaging limitations, but often treated an imperfect guarantee as worthless and never established that known short support is worse than undisclosed short support. Its local-function alternative was compatible with the motion and did not answer the continuing security-update problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s closing answer to the local-function remedy was decisive: offline functionality may prevent total shutdown, but it does not provide future security patches. The Revolv comparison also showed that enforceable dates matter against surviving firms, while Side B’s “scheduled funeral” response overlooked that advance warning is precisely the disclosure benefit.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won because it exposed the structural flaw in unverifiable long-term box guarantees: they create perverse incentives where honest firms lowball and fly-by-night firms inflate, while offering shoppers no verifiable signal at purchase. Side A’s concession that unknown boxes are indistinguishable on day one, combined with clipped constructive speeches, left its case unable to recover from B’s sharper comparative framing and market-entry verification alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal distinguished its market-entry design requirement (testable before sale) from Side A’s post-hoc promise, turned the importer-liability point into a consolidation moat, and exploited Side A’s admission that the label provides no usable information on day one for unknown brands.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B systematically dismantled PRO's core mechanism by demonstrating that a legally binding "guaranteed" label creates perverse incentives: honest firms lowball to limit liability, while fly-by-night firms inflate the number, creating a "liar's costume" that misleads shoppers at the exact moment of purchase. PRO's reliance on long-term track records effectively conceded that day-one buyers receive no reliable information, fatally undermining the motion's premise. Side B also offered a structurally superior alternative (factory-verified local functionality) that cleanly sidestepped the enforcement gap PRO tried to weaponize, while PRO's late-stage security patch argument was not enough to salvage the label's day-one utility.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's "liar's costume" argument, which proved that a legally binding guarantee forces honest firms to lowball while rewarding fly-by-night firms for inflating dates, rendering the label actively misleading at the shelf. Side B's closing response to the Revolv example—noting Google would have simply printed a short date and legally killed the device on schedule—was also a devastating neutralization of PRO's best evidence.
