- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B won a competitive debate by showing that vetoes do not merely preserve future choice: blocking infrastructure, fiscal reform, or waste disposal can itself create irreversible costs. A’s claim that elected governments, rather than the commissioner, choose those harms was too formalistic, especially because the required supermajority prevents an ordinary successor government from readily correcting a wrongful veto. B also directly answered the clean repository case with enforceable but amendable legislation, preserving accountability under uncertainty. A offered the more memorable representation frame, but never fully resolved the veto’s own legitimacy and error costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange paired the “delay is not refundable” response with the override dilemma: if an ordinary majority can reverse the block, the veto adds little; if only a supermajority can, the commissioner may impose lasting harms that the next elected government cannot correct. This substantially weakened A’s central asymmetry claim.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): CON won the asymmetry clash and the enforcement question. PRO's core moves - advisory fails, don't foreclose is knowable, blocking is reversible - were met by B's concrete symmetry argument that delay is itself irreversible (pools age, coal runs, interest compounds) and that a negative veto still compels budgets and designs. B answered A's pressure with statutory tools - bonding, trusts, sunset/break clauses - showing ordinary law is enforcement, not advice, while A never resolved B's supermajority dilemma: if override is easy veto doesn't bind, if hard one official deletes the next majority's ability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.5-B2.6/B4.5-B4.6: delay is not refundable escrow - vetoing storage extends temporary waste, energy, and fiscal harms - so the veto commits the same irreversibility it claims to prevent, collapsing A's asymmetry and foreclosure-standard defense.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully trapping Side B on the alternative. Side B's reliance on ordinary statutes (e.g., "enact the rule" for retrievability) conceded the need for constraints but relied on a mechanism that Side A effectively showed is vulnerable to the exact same short-term electoral pressures the debate is about. Side A's closing crystallized this perfectly, noting that a rule yielding to an impatient majority is not a rule. Side B's arguments about the irreversible costs of delay were strong, but Side A's framing of the veto as a negative power that merely prices foreclosure via supermajority override was more robust and structurally sound.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled Side B's "enact it in law" alternative by pointing out that ordinary law is repealable by the very impatient majorities the policy is meant to restrain, exposing the core weakness of Side B's counter-model.
