### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side A won by systematically neutralizing Side B's functional justifications for maintaining a small fee. A successfully demonstrated that finite handsets require scheduling under either model, muting B's rationing advantage, and persuasively argued that relying on families as 'unpaid auditors' is a poor substitute for state accountability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Closing definitively dismantled Side B's rationale for a fee, pointing out that since both models face hard physical limits and require oversight, Side B's fee does no unique managing or securing—it merely collects money from innocent families.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): CON won the core tradeoff. Both sides agreed kickbacks should end and family contact helps reentry. PRO framed the choice as state vs grandmother, but CON narrowed it to free vs regulated cheap: capped pennies deliver the first hours of contact PRO cares about while PRO conceded a managed level, scheduling, and added screening [A4.6]. CON's answers that a trivial price is self-rationed by the caller - keeping custody-hearing calls while trimming idle hours - and that time queues bill the working mother in a non-refillable currency, plus the invisibility argument that free hides vendor migration in a bulk invoice and silences itemized-bill whistleblowers, left PRO's "pennies never bind so fee does no work" dilemma turned against itself.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal A1/A2 - soft price vs hard schedule and utility-style regulation keeping the watchdog alive - directly neutralized A's equity and capture arguments and became the debate's decisive weighing.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B wins by introducing two highly effective, original arguments that Side A fails to adequately neutralize. First, the "watchdog" reframe: B argues that itemized bills were the very mechanism that exposed vendor kickbacks, and making calls free removes this oversight, leaving the state (which was complicit in the kickbacks) as the sole, blind customer. A's appeal to legislative oversight is easily dismissed by B noting that such oversight failed during the kickback era. Second, the rationing clash: B sharply distinguishes between a "soft" price cap (pennies) that trims idle use and a "hard" scheduling cap that entirely denies a call when slots fill. A's defense of timetables ignores B's point about working mothers and hard denials. Furthermore, Side A suffers multiple execution costs, with several speeches (including the closing) clipped mid-sentence, dulling their rhetorical impact. Side B's case is tighter, more original, and better executed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal to Side A's scheduling argument, distinguishing between a "soft" price boundary that allows a fifth call for thirty cents and a "hard" scheduling boundary where the fifth call simply does not happen, effectively neutralizing PRO's claim that timetables are a fairer rationing mechanism.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by effectively conceding Side B's strongest points (that free minutes might get cut in lean years, and that the vendor remains) and turning them into comparative victories. Side A successfully proved that a state-managed contract with trimmed minutes in lean years is still better than silent poverty-rationing where the poorest families get zero.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing beautifully weaponized Side B's claim that per-minute billing makes each added phone "earn money," pointing out that this is just the shakedown's profit motive with a regulator's signature.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): PRO kept the decisive equity and public-safety route alive: fees tax innocent families who fund reentry, marginal telecom cost is tiny versus incarceration cost, and NYC/CT show state-paid/vendor-carried free contact works. CON conceded the shakedown but its remedy — ban commissions, charge pennies at cost — leaves the monopoly to define cost and rations by poverty every year, while free provision rations by turn via scheduling prisons already do. CON's strongest budget-cut and queue points were blunted by clipped turns and by its own admission that the same procurement office that banked kickbacks would police costs, while PRO directly answered Qs about outsourcing and capacity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Q2 plus R1 [A2.2][A3.2] on "whose cost" and what forces a monopolist to add phones — CON's reply [B4.3] that per-minute pennies incentivize capacity still assumes the monopoly won't pocket margin, and leaves poverty-rationing unanswered.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by systematically dismantling the practical mechanics of Side A's "free" model. B effectively argues that state-funded free calls lead to silent budget cuts, prison-yard rationing, and a lack of incentives to expand capacity, whereas near-cost pricing preserves flexibility and funds infrastructure. Side A's concession that vendors would still be used under their plan weakens their stance on the motion's text, and their reliance on state procurement ignores B's sharp point that the state itself created the corrupt commission structures. Despite minor execution costs from clipping, B's structural and incentive-based arguments were more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to Side A's pressure question about state auditing. B pointed out that state procurement offices were the ones who signed the corrupt commission deals and banked the kickbacks, making families with phone bills the actual auditors who exposed the shakedown. This flipped A's accountability argument on its head.
