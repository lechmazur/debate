### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side A won narrowly by exposing the contradiction between Side B's claim that workers control permissions and notifications and its insistence that a common channel must reach everyone. If deleting, silencing, or denying tracking makes a worker noncompliant, those controls do not answer the core objection to conditioning employment on use of personal property. Side B strongly showed that company phones do not eliminate after-hours culture and that portals can reduce convenience, but it under-addressed mandatory tracking and never fully displaced A's segregation principle.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest response was that sandboxing and settings are not meaningful safeguards when using the app and remaining responsive are employment conditions. This directly weakened Side B's recurring claim that the app was a tenant the worker could evict at will.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by exposing a fatal contradiction in Side B's alternatives. A successfully argued that if workers leave company phones in lockers, they suffer an information blackout (missing the 6 a.m. shift change), and if they use web portals at home, they are still using their personal phones. Side B failed to reconcile how workers can simultaneously disconnect completely and still receive urgent scheduling updates.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 expertly dismantled B's defense of the locker, pointing out that leaving a company phone at work creates the exact information blackout PRO wants to avoid, trapping PRO between missing urgent alerts or using the personal phone.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side A built a more logically robust case by exposing the practical flaws in Side B's alternatives. Side A's "information blackout" argument effectively dismantled Side B's "locker" defense, showing that leaving a company phone at work actively harms hourly workers who rely on shift swaps and schedule alerts. Furthermore, Side A successfully framed Side B's distinction between an app and a web portal as "policing the container, not the contact," and capitalized on Side B's concession that after-hours pings are a cultural issue rather than a device issue. Side B's rhetoric was highly engaging, but Side A won the decisive clash on the comparative harms of the alternatives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of the "locker" defense by introducing the "information blackout" for hourly workers, and the "policing the container" argument exposing the functional equivalence of an app and a web portal on a personal device.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): A won the central comparison: essential communication can remain mandatory through an employer-provided device, while ownership separation uniquely avoids commingling private data, hardware costs, and employer software. B persuasively showed that sandboxed profiles reduce many risks and correctly challenged A's characterization of the issued-phone exchange as a concession. However, B never established why an on-call worker cannot reliably carry and answer a company phone; inconvenience and a second device were insufficient to justify compelled access to personal property.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest response was that safety-critical channels should be staffed with issued tools, just as other indispensable work inputs are. B answered that a second phone must still follow the worker home, but that only showed continued reachability, not the distinctive personal-data and ownership intrusion A identified.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won by successfully trapping Side A between voluntary use and employer mandates. Side B established that if single-device convenience is truly preferred by workers, voluntary installation survives the motion; if the channel is mandatory for safety, the employer can simply issue a device. Side A's "changing glass" argument was effectively neutralized by Side B's distinction between merely being reachable on call and permanently housing employer software on the same device as personal data.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1 cleanly dismantled A's "changing glass" argument by clarifying that the harm is not merely being reachable at a recital, but the collapse of work and personal data into a single inseparable object.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won by establishing a clear, defensible bright line (physical separation of devices) and effectively neutralizing Side A's "sandbox" argument. Side B correctly pointed out that an issued phone, even if carried off-shift, does not contain the worker's personal data, bank logins, or photos, dismantling Side A's claim that the intrusion "merely changes glass." Side B also successfully framed the motion as removing coercion rather than banning the apps, and handled the pressure questions with much greater clarity, particularly on how 2 a.m. coverage is achieved through staffing and issued devices rather than conscripting personal property. Side A's reliance on the reliability of employer-controlled sandboxes left their case vulnerable to Side B's sharp distinctions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 answer to A1, where they decisively explained that an issued phone riding to a recital carries no personal accounts or private history, entirely defanging Side A's claim that the intrusion "merely changes glass."
