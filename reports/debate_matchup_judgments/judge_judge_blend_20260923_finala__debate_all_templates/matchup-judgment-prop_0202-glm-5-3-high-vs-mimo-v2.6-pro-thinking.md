### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but CON sustained the sharper, more mechanistically grounded critique throughout. CON's core throughline—that the gatekeeper inevitably ends up judging its own rivals' traffic (the "self-judging" conflict), and that regulatory review operates too slowly to catch subtle throttling or degraded delivery ("packet speed vs proceeding speed")—was never squarely neutralized by PRO, whose answer relied on asserting that DMA-style "burden reversal" and complaints channels would discipline discretion without engaging the specific claim that invisible degradation escapes ex-post review. CON also landed a clean hit on PRO's MVNO analogy, turning it into support for the "client not competitor" framing, and PRO's rebuttal did not fully re-engage CON's "layer separation is asserted, not shown" point about encryption/identity/group semantics being core rather than decorative. PRO's strongest surviving asset was the unrebutted monoculture/single-point-of-failure security argument and the effective "compared to what" baseline framing in closing, plus a clever exploitation of CON's PQ concession (B4.3) about accepting a neutrally-specified mandate. But CON's closing directly contested that the DMA's actual mechanism (gatekeeper-authored spec under ex-post review) differs from the accepted hypothetical, preventing PRO's move from being fully decisive. On balance, CON's rebuttals were more consistently pointed and better answered the pressure exchanged, giving it a narrow but clear edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 response to the MVNO analogy and layer-separation claim [B2.3–B2.5], which converted PRO's own example into support for the "client not competitor" framing and forced PRO into an unresolved tension between claiming core-feature interoperability drives contestability while pushing all quality differentiation "above the bridge."
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by showing that the security and quality costs of DMA-style interoperability are architecturally embedded at the interface boundary—metadata accumulation at seams, version skew, spam laundering, and product stagnation—while PRO's "managed interoperability" remedies operate ex-post and too slowly to catch subtle gatekeeper sabotage like throttled delivery or degraded spam scores. A's "packet speed versus proceeding speed" distinction crystallized the decisive clash: the motion requires gains to exceed losses, but PRO only demonstrated that losses could be litigated after the fact, not that they were prevented. PRO's attempt to reframe CON's costs as features of unsupervised interop was parried by A's sustained argument that supervised discretion still leaves the gatekeeper as real-time arbiter of rival traffic. A's opening dependency thesis and email spam dilemma remained live through the close, whereas PRO's clipped speeches and unanswered quality stagnation burden left its comparative weighing incomplete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 (Turn 4, A2.6-A2.8) exposed the decisive contradiction in PRO's spam answer: conditional access requires the gatekeeper to vet rival traffic, creating either an email-style arms race or a chokepoint censor, with the middle unshown. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but CON's rebuttals cut deeper into PRO's core mitigation strategy. PRO's analogies (email, TLS deprecation, payment fraud, app-store certification) were clever and grounded in plausible general knowledge, and PRO answered CON's pressure questions concretely with real-world governance examples. But CON exploited the weaknesses in those analogies with sharper distinctions: (1) payment fraud is reversible/insurable while leaked conversations and social graphs are not, which reframes the "managed cost" argument against PRO; (2) the mechanisms PRO cites (app stores, root programs, TLS deprecation) all work because a single operator retains unilateral ejection power, whereas a DMA-style legal entitlement lets entrants resist deprecation and appeal to regulators, undermining the very governance PRO relies on. CON also defused PRO's "toothless vs. catastrophic" dilemma cleanly by distinguishing the one-time act of compelling an open interface from the unbounded task of policing its ongoing security. PRO's closing pushed back on the "load-bearing wall" point but did so with a bare assertion rather than a mechanism, leaving CON's ownership/entitlement distinction largely intact. CON's Rebuttal 2 was clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost that slightly tempers its polish, and its closing overstated that its case stood "unrebutted" when PRO had in fact directly engaged it — but this did not erase the substantive edge CON built through the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 distinction between reversible/insurable monetary fraud and irreversible privacy/social-graph harm, combined with the ownership-vs-legal-entitlement point undercutting PRO's app-store/TLS/root-program analogies, was the most decisive and least fully neutralized exchange in the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by exposing a structural disanalogy in PRO's core case. PRO argued that security risks are manageable by citing app-store review, payment-network certification, and TLS/HTTP deprecation. CON showed that every one of those governance mechanisms works because a single operator owns the system and can eject laggards unilaterally, whereas DMA-style mandates turn interfaces into legal entitlements that entrants can defend against deprecation—producing exactly the ossification email illustrates. This "load-bearing wall" critique was never answered on PRO's side. CON also landed a decisive distinction between reversible, insurable payment fraud and irreversible privacy losses in messaging, undermining PRO's fraud analogy. PRO's closing tried to reframe the debate as "managed risk versus guaranteed failure," but CON had already demonstrated that the risk-management tools PRO relied on are disabled by the mandate itself, leaving PRO with no DMA-specific mechanism to prevent security degradation. The contestability gains remained contingent on enforcement PRO could not show would overcome gatekeeper resistance, while the security costs were shown to be certain, permanent, and structurally irreversible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's demolition of PRO's governance analogies by showing they all depend on operator ownership and unilateral ejection—contrasted with DMA's severing of exactly that control while assuming governance survives (the "load-bearing wall" and legal-entitlement ossification argument).
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's core analogies and mechanisms. While Side B relied heavily on payment networks and TLS deprecation to prove that security risks in federated systems are manageable, Side A brilliantly distinguished between owned, voluntary systems (where operators can unilaterally deprecate legacy tech or absorb reversible financial fraud) and mandated legal entitlements (where entrants can use regulators to block deprecation, causing irreversible privacy harms and protocol ossification). Side A also cleanly resolved Side B's "toothless vs. catastrophic" regulatory dilemma by distinguishing between the binary act of forcing an API open and the unbounded, ongoing task of policing its security. Side B's case remained structurally sound regarding the harms of lock-in, but ultimately failed to prove that the mandate's specific mechanisms wouldn't degrade security and quality more than they improve contestability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's TLS and payment analogies by highlighting that mandated interfaces become legal entitlements that entrants can use to resist deprecation, thereby legislating the exact ossification seen in email.
