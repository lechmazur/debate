### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): CON (Side A) won the decisive clash: whether OS/stack-level diversification actually breaks the correlations that cause catastrophic failure. PRO's opening (CrowdStrike, portfolio/crop analogies) was compelling but CON's rebuttal that the real correlation lives in shared libraries, firmware, and cross-platform kernel agents — which stack diversification passes straight through — was never squarely answered. PRO conceded in Rebuttal 2 and Closing that diversification is only "partial" and should be "layered alongside" measures like canary deployment and SBOMs, which let CON's closing turn that hedge into a full concession: the layering measures do the decorrelating work, while mandated diversification adds the costs (three patch regimes, scarce specialists, a minority platform patched slowest and stranded when its niche vendor fails) without fixing the actual problem. CON's distinction between one-time resilience costs (generators, reserve capital) and the continuous tax of maintaining multiple software stacks was also never rebutted. PRO's closing attempt to redefine the motion ("both sides agree government should intervene") was cleanly exposed by CON as smuggling in a different, unfalsifiable question. Execution also favored CON: PRO's speeches were clipped for length in four of five turns (opening, both rebuttals, closing), losing potential force at exactly the moments meant to crystallize the case, while CON was clipped only twice and delivered a clean, on-limit closing that directly answered PRO's reframe.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 (A2), which reframed PRO's own bank-portfolio analogy against diversification ("regulators target the correlated exposure, not brand variety") and introduced the frequency×severity×recovery accounting critique — a point PRO never fully answered and which CON's closing later exploited by noting PRO's own concession that diversification is merely a "partial," "layered" measure.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): B wins because CON sustains the core distinction between label diversity and exposure diversity. PRO proves partial decorrelation helps but never answers that OS quotas pass through shared libs, firmware, and cross-platform agents, while B supplies a concrete alternative regulatory mechanism - staged/canary rollouts, SBOMs, caps, kill-switches - that targets the actual correlated deployment model. B's accounting of recovery speed and permanent staffing/tax costs on minority stacks outweighs PRO's frequency-only expected-loss frame, and PRO's closing tries to absorb CON's measures into "active diversification," losing the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 answers to pressure – detailing staged rollouts/SBOM/caps as regulation of exposure not brands, plus turning PRO's zero-day/insolvency scenarios into an indictment of mandated heterogeneity as the slowest-patched, worst-staffed, first-stranded stack.

CON effectively argued that "label diversification" (mixing OS brands) fails to decorrelate the actual shared dependencies (open-source libraries, cross-platform agents) that cause systemic failures, while imposing severe continuous costs (scarce specialists, poorly patched minority stacks). ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): The debate turned on whether operational-hygiene remedies (backups, staged rollouts, segmentation) can substitute for a live, running alternative platform during a common-mode failure. PRO's pressure question on zero-days in shared OS/library code forced CON into a crucial concession: its toolkit presupposes gradual/update-channel failure with an "escaped" unaffected copy, or offline data recovery. PRO's closing sharpened this into a clean, memorable point that CON never fully answered: a backup restores data onto the same vulnerable stack, so it cannot serve as a running fallback during simultaneous failure — only a second live platform can keep dispatch or payments running while the primary is remediated. CON's staffing/competence critique was strong and repeatedly pressed, and PRO's "targeted premium" reframe (critical layer only) was a reasonable but somewhat underdeveloped answer to it, especially regarding who staffs the hot standby. Still, CON's final closing was cut short by the word-limit clip right as it pivoted to its strongest counter-move, weakening its ability to close the argumentative loop. On balance PRO's core mechanism (running fallback vs. inert backup) survived rebuttal more cleanly than CON's staffing objection survived PRO's zero-day pressure, giving PRO a clear but not overwhelming edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure question on zero-days in the shared OS/library, and CON's implicit concession that its remedies work only for gradual/update-channel failures, followed by PRO's closing point that backups restore onto the same vulnerable stack rather than provide a running fallback.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): PRO kept the decisive clash on correlated failure vs operational hygiene. Its pressure on zero-day in the shared stack exposed that staged rollouts, delayed patching and backups all presuppose an unaffected copy of the same stack exists. CON's answer that zero-days are rare concedes the tail risk PRO says governments must insure, and that air-gapped backups restore data but not a live service, leaving CON without a continuity answer. CON's competence/fragmentation and seam arguments were strong but were narrowed by PRO's critical-layer-only scoping and asymmetry framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4/A5 sequence that a backup is not a running service and restores onto the same vulnerable stack, so only a heterogeneous live fallback provides continuity when every identical system fails at once, which directly neutralized CON's cheaper-alternatives ROI claim.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A won by decisively distinguishing between data recovery (backups) and service continuity (failover). While Side B effectively highlighted the costs, complexity, and staffing burdens of maintaining diverse stacks, Side A's closing argument—that restoring a backup onto a fundamentally flawed or zero-day compromised monoculture merely recreates the failure and leaves critical services like 911 offline—exposed a fatal gap in CON's alternative toolkit. Side A's framing of common-mode failure as a distinct category of risk that requires heterogeneous failover, rather than just better hygiene or staged rollouts, remained the most compelling and unresolved clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing argument that "a backup is not a running service" and that restoring data onto the same vulnerable stack fails to provide the continuous uptime required for critical systems like emergency dispatch, effectively neutralizing CON's reliance on air-gapped backups for zero-day scenarios.
