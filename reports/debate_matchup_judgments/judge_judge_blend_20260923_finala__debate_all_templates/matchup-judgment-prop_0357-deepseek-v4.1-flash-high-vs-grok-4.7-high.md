### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): PRO decisively won by leveraging CON's own example of a 'one-time pairing fee' to prove the mandate does not require free lifetime cloud service, but merely bans tying repair to a recurring connectivity subscription.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized CON's cost arguments by pointing out that CON already conceded automakers could charge a one-time fee, turning a 'free vault' into a simple ban on subscription kill switches.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by maintaining a narrow, intuitive frame: the proposition simply bans automakers from using a lapsed connectivity subscription as a kill switch for core repair. B successfully showed that authenticated offline paths and one-time fees preserve security and traceability without demanding free lifetime service. Side A (CON) raised legitimate concerns about compelled infrastructure and liability, but critically conceded that automakers may sell repair access, transfer accounts, or charge one-time pairing fees—concessions that B exploited to prove the mandate does not force a free vault. A never recovered from that tension, and its Rebuttal 1 was clipped for length. B’s second rebuttal decisively separated security-based refusal from billing-based refusal, leaving A without a persuasive reason why an unpaid traffic bill should block hardware repair.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal (Turn 7), which separated security rejection of stolen or counterfeit parts from subscription-based rejection of legitimate parts, showed that CON’s own acceptable alternatives (one-time fees, credential transfers) satisfy the proposition, and crystallized the clash as a choice between a trust service and a tollbooth on ownership.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) kept the proposition narrow: not free cloud, but no repair kill-switch tied to a lapsed data plan. It answered both pressures directly - security can still refuse stolen/counterfeit parts [B4.1], legitimate same-class parts need an authenticated non-subscription path [B4.2-B4.3]. Side A (CON) conceded a one-time pairing fee or transferred credential solves the kill-switch [A2.5] yet never explained why an unpaid traffic bill should decide repairability, and its R1 was clipped. The core comparison - safety refusal vs billing refusal - remained live for PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's R2 A1/A2 distinction between failing authentication vs failing billing, plus crystallizing CON's concession that one-time fees preserve security, turning cost/complexity objections into support for the mandate.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by cleanly neutralizing Side B's "free perpetual backend" argument. A effectively argues that if automakers choose a server-dependent architecture for repair, they must price that into the sale rather than use a lapsed subscription as a lockout. Side B concedes that a lapsed plan shouldn't switch off repair but tries to argue for point-of-sale regulations instead; A successfully defends the motion as the exact remedy needed for post-sale lockouts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4's distinction that core repair is a condition of sale, and that automakers can simply price the necessary backend into the vehicle or the replacement part if they choose a server-dependent architecture, effectively defeating B's core "free lifetime service" objection.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by keeping the moral and conceptual core of ownership clearer through the exchange. CON's technical objections about backend costs and vagueness were answered by PRO's distinction between optional services and repair rights, and by framing the requirement as a design condition of sale rather than free perpetual service. CON's point-of-sale alternative was sensible but never fully answered PRO's concern about second owners and post-sale term changes, and PRO's pressure answers were more direct.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 reframe that the mandate stops automakers from moving repair functions behind a subscription after purchase and that the cost belongs in the original purchase price neutralized CON's "free lifetime service" objection and clarified why the subscription-lapse trigger matters.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): PRO kept a live, narrow path: core = diagnosing and restoring sold systems/like-for-like parts, cost priced into sale, not free cloud forever. CON's perpetual-backend and security critiques were answered by scoped credentials and restoration vs invention limit, and CON's own concession that no sold right lets a lapsed plan kill repair left no independent reason to reject the lapse-triggered rule. Alternative at-sale disclosure never solved post-sale shifting to subscription.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 false-dichotomy exposure: either tools live on car (rule pointless) or on server (free service) ignores manufacturers moving same function across that line after sale, so offline-at-sale alone cannot stop second-owner locks.
