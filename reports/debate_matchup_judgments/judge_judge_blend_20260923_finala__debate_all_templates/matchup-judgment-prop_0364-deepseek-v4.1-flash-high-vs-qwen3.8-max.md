### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and executed the Q&A format correctly, but PRO (B) established the more resilient throughline: standardization only mandates disclosure of whatever metric the contract already chose, not a new compensation formula, which defused CON's core "false precision" attack more cleanly than CON ever neutralized PRO's leverage argument. PRO's central claim—that a low-leverage creator (e.g., a first-time director) cannot realistically enforce individualized audit rights against the only party holding the data—went unanswered in substance; CON's repeated fallback to "enforceable disclosure," "union-administered review," and "sampling audits" implicitly concedes the need for something like the collective, standardized mechanism PRO proposes, without explaining why that mechanism works without common definitions. CON's strongest original contribution, the "gravitational default" worry (that standardized categories become de facto litigation targets), was a good point but was met adequately by PRO's "rebuttable, contract-governs" clarification and the "lottery ticket vs. real upside" reframe, which made the flat-fee/buyout cost look like a fair trade rather than a decisive harm. PRO's closing crystallized the clash more sharply and tied it directly back to the motion's compensation-verification logic, while CON's closing, though strong, still leaned on the unresolved gravitational-default point without fully answering why contract-based audits solve the asymmetric-leverage problem. This gives PRO a clear, if not overwhelming, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Q1 (A1: neutral multi-stakeholder, rebuttable standard) combined with CON's failure across both rebuttals to explain how a low-leverage creator enforces individualized audit rights without common reporting — this exchange most decisively favored PRO's core leverage argument.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side A won by maintaining that if compensation depends on reach, the payer cannot be the sole witness. A successfully distinguished standardized reporting formats from standardized compensation triggers, neutralized the small-streamer objection by framing opaque reach-based pay as a lottery ticket written by the payer, and exposed that CON’s contractual-audit alternative collapses into trust for weak creators. B’s “false precision” and “gravitational default” arguments were sharp but persistently blurred that distinction, and B never overcame the black-box problem. A’s closing crystallized the decisive clash cleanly: CON preserves flexibility for the payer and mere trust for everyone else.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal framing of unverifiable reach-based pay as “a lottery ticket written by the payer,” combined with the argument that “verification without standardization” is a slogan because verification requires a common record to verify against.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO kept a live principle—if pay depends on reach, reach must be verifiable in a common, confidential form—and answered leverage and cost pressure directly with guild-level checking and marginal-cost reporting. CON’s core that standardization creates false precision and drives platforms to flat fees was turned: B showed opaque reach-based pay is not real upside, and contract-specific audit without a common record leaves low-leverage creators trusting the payer. That comparison decided it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 on flat fees as lottery tickets and that verification without standardization is a slogan—neutral multi-stakeholder, rebuttable reporting vocabulary directly neutralized CON’s gravity/default and small-streamer harm claims.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON's central thesis—"standardization is not verification"—survived the exchange better than PRO's response to it. CON consistently pressed two structural weaknesses: (1) that mandated disclosure still lets the platform define the underlying metric, and (2) that parties can simply relabel reach-based pay as flat fees to escape the mandate entirely. PRO's answers to these (a "functional reading" that catches any relabeling, and a baseline that still includes "the platform's own definition of a view") were exposed by CON as either vague/unenforceable or self-undermining, since a disclosure regime that lets platforms keep defining "view" concedes CON's core claim that the payer still controls the count. PRO's "exhibit that makes the trial possible" reframe in the closing was a good rhetorical move but didn't fully answer why an unaudited, self-reported exhibit is meaningfully better than the status quo when the mandate can be circumvented. CON's closing crystallized this gap cleanly, while PRO's closing largely reasserted the disclosure-enables-verification framing without resolving the buyout-escape and definition-capture problems raised in cross-examination.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.6), especially the point that PRO's own baseline still includes "the platform's own definition of a view" [B4.1], which CON used to show the mandate concedes the very control problem it claims to solve—paired with the unresolved buyout-escape pressure question, was the most damaging exchange.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): CON demonstrated that standardized disclosure is not verification: a formatted report assembled by the payer under contested definitions is merely a more legible black box. PRO’s claim that standardization enables enforcement collapsed against CON’s sustained argument that without log-level access and contestability, creators receive a press release, not evidence. CON also showed the mandate creates perverse incentives to eliminate reach-based pay, undermining the very upside PRO sought to protect.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s rebuttal that standardized dashboards without log-level access amount to evidence versus a press release, undermining PRO’s claim that standardization enables verification.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON wins the verification clash. PRO establishes fairness intuition that pay tied to reach needs visibility, but CON shows standardized summaries don't deliver verification – logs and contestability do – and are still built by the payer. PRO never fully answers who writes the standard (capture risk) and that tying the duty only to reach-based pay creates an incentive to drop backend, harming non-stars it claims to protect. That perverse-incentive + definitional-monopoly chain survives both rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2 point that standardized aggregates can be gamed above the logs and that [B2.4] inviting platforms to switch to flat buyouts eliminates the upside PRO wants to protect.
