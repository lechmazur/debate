### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully proving that the companies make ongoing, controllable choices (like using an audio beep rather than a software speed-lock in restricted zones) that justify comparative fault. Side A gave an exceptional defense using the principle that "collection is not causation," but Side B convincingly showed that this isn't just about deep pockets—it's about the company's active role in mitigating risk through its software and hardware.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal dismantling the Hertz car rental analogy by highlighting the unique operational model of micromobility (geofencing, app-based speed control, and sidewalk placement) decisively established the company's ongoing control over the vehicle.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing that comparative liability should attach to companies when their operational choices—weak geofence enforcement, vehicle design, profit-driven deployment in crowded areas—contribute to pedestrian injuries in restricted zones, especially given the chronic uncollectibility of at-fault riders. A crystallized the debate around a concrete tourist-hit-and-run scenario where B was forced to concede the pedestrian likely recovers nothing, and A successfully framed this as a system design problem rather than an acceptable feature of tort law. B's "collection ≠ causation" objection remained philosophically tidy but failed to answer why companies that profit from and control the riskiest aspects of the service should bear zero comparative responsibility, and B never overcame A's "beep versus lock" operational comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7) where A directly answered B's pressure questions by distinguishing insolvency-as-trigger from graded comparative fault, listed dischargeable safety duties (hardware limiting, repeat-offender bans), and exposed B's inconsistency in claiming firms could verify riders but could not implement remote speed controls—crystallizing that the real disagreement was "who pays when mitigation fails."
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A built a decisively stronger case by focusing on the company's controllable mitigation choices (e.g., using an audio beep instead of a wheel lock in declared danger zones) and framing liability as comparative fault rather than strict liability. Side A also effectively flipped Side B's argument that companies would "draw fewer maps," pointing out that a duty tied to declared zones actually incentivizes real enforcement. Most importantly, Side A relentlessly pressed the practical reality of the insolvent or untraceable rider (the tourist), forcing Side B to concede that under their rule, the injured pedestrian simply absorbs the loss. This admission fatally undermined Side B's normative framework. Side A's minor clipping at the very end of two speeches did not obscure its fully developed core arguments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's reversal of Side B's "drawing fewer maps" argument, noting that the status quo already punishes honesty about danger, and that tying comparative duty to declared zones gives firms a reason to actually enforce them with hardware limits rather than treating maps as liability shields.
