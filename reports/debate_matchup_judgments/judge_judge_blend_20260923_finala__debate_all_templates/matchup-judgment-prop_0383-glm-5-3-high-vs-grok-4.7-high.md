### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but B built the more disciplined and resilient case. B's central move—arguing that "most wasteful" is a property of the discarded item, not a guarantee about the substitute, and that the motion's "even if" clause authorizes bans even when the replacement is worse or the small vendor cannot absorb the cost—was never fully dislodged. A's response retreated to "beats on balance of harms" and leaned on the assumption that transition windows and consultation are built into the motion, but never showed this is actually in the text, and never answered B's clean distinction between toxic industrial bans (lead paint, CFCs) and a sterile default packaging item [B1.7], a point that went unaddressed in later rounds. B also sustained a consistent, refined alternative (scaled, refundable litter fees/deposits) across every round, while A's claim that fees are "permanent" sat awkwardly next to A's own earlier praise of deposit schemes as effective mechanisms. B's pressure questions extracted a strategically limited concession (foam) without surrendering the substitution-quality principle, and B's closing cleanly crystallized the clash. A's case had real strength (externality framing, transitional-vs-permanent framing), but suffered from two clipped speeches—including the closing, cut off exactly at the final weighing sentence—which is a genuine execution cost per the judging rules. On balance, B made the debate easier to decide and left fewer of its live points unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 response to A's "clearly better substitute" claim [B2.1–B2.3], showing the motion's text never actually binds phase-outs to superior substitutes, combined with B's unanswered lead-paint/CFC distinction [B1.7], formed the most damaging and never-fully-neutralized line of attack.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully proving that the motion's wording pre-authorizes bans even when local substitutes (like compostables in cities without industrial composting) fail to actually reduce lifecycle waste. Side B's execution suffered from word-count clipping and a chronological error in Rebuttal 2, where they attacked Side A for not answering a question before Side A's turn to answer had occurred.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Closing reversal of Side B's speed-limit analogy ("A speed-limit sign states a limit. This motion excuses the failure in advance") perfectly crystallized Side A's winning argument that the proposition lacks a binding environmental gate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully narrowing the motion to the worst single-use items that already have lower-impact substitutes (e.g., foam to fiber), which defanged Side B’s central lifecycle critique about heavy reusable tubs being worse for emissions and water. Once Side B conceded foam could be phased out conditionally, Side A crystallized the debate into whether regulation inherently includes assessment windows—which it does—making the remaining dispute procedural rather than principled. Side A also landed the decisive comparative point that fees “correct without change” by perpetuating manufacturing and landfill costs, whereas a phase-out shifts the baseline and front-loads a cost that decays as markets adapt. Side B’s vivid pressure on small vendors and the night-shift worker was insufficient to overcome this framework after the substitute-viability objection was neutralized. Two clipped speeches from Side A were a minor execution cost but did not leave core burdens unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s first rebuttal distinguishing reusable mandates from the actual motion’s target of the “most wasteful” single-use items, showing that lower-impact single-use substitutes are already on the shelf and that the lifecycle critique only bites against dumb swaps the motion does not require.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but B built a more distinctive, multi-pronged case (substitution effects, jurisdictional compliance lottery, regressive small-business burden, hygiene logistics, definitional creep) and used the pressure-question round more surgically. B's Q2 forced A to concede in Rebuttal 2 that grants, lead times, shared hubs, and procurement must be "in the same law" as the ban — effectively admitting the workable version of PRO's case requires a full support apparatus, not the bare phase-out the motion states. B's closing exploited this concession cleanly ("the version with training wheels"), giving the debate a sharper final frame. A's case remained consistent and forceful (cumulative harm vs. finite adjustment, "accounting first as veto"), and its closing point about permanent exemptions was strong, but it never fully closed the structural gap B identified between the literal motion and the elaborate implementation A had to construct to make the policy workable. That unresolved tension, combined with B's crisper rebuttal work and more memorable framing, gives B the edge, though the margin is not large because A's underlying environmental-cost argument remained substantively live throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 (turning "functional line" into a "jurisdiction lottery" and noting the support cushion "arrives late or not at all") combined with the Pressure Questions extracting A's concession that grants/hubs/procurement must be legislated in the same law — which B's closing then used to reframe A's whole case as defending an unstated, fuller policy package rather than the bare motion.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully exposing that Side A's demands amounted to an impossible veto. When pushed on what would justify a phase-out, Side A required a substitute with 'no new logistics' for small operators, which Side B correctly identified as violating the motion's premise that some transition costs are acceptable. Side B also cleanly resolved Side A's 'jurisdiction lottery' argument by explaining how local compliance lists function.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing brilliantly capitalized on Side A's answer to Q2, showing that Side A's demand for a perfectly finished replacement with zero friction was essentially a permanent exemption for harmful waste.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that the motion explicitly tolerates transition costs and that cities can administer the standard locally through posted lists of what their own plants can process, avoiding the impossible universal-jurisdiction test Side B demanded. Side B’s “accounting first” criterion, exposed in the pressure exchange, functioned as a veto requiring perfect substitutes and zero logistical friction before any phase-out could begin, effectively freezing known ecological failures indefinitely.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 answers to Side B’s pressure questions, where A demonstrated that local compliance lists and sequenced rollouts are normal policy tools rather than a smuggled infrastructure program, and that requiring one packaging format to satisfy every jurisdiction globally is a standard the motion never imposed.
