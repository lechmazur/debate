- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that prioritizing corridors does not eliminate targeted removal or employer enforcement.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest response was that the same payroll, visa, and biometric chokepoints Side A cited for deportation could instead register wanted workers, leaving a smaller residual for removal. This exposed Side A’s tendency to equate necessary enforcement with prioritizing mass deportation.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): B won a close, high-quality exchange by controlling the decisive comparison: under persistent labor demand and admitted weak capacity, mass-deportation-first remains a revolving door while corridor-first uses the same chokepoints to convert the wanted majority into registered status and shrink residual enforcement. A landed a sharp non-self-enforcing-status critique and strong sequencing pressure, but never neutralized the job-refill / capacity-asymmetry problem; B’s targeted-regularization answer plus the wanted-workers reframe made the proposition’s hierarchy easier to affirm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s R2 capacity flip (A1/A2): hollow inspection makes mass roundups harder than phased registration at existing payroll/permit points, turning A’s hollow-systems attack against deportation-first.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by demonstrating that mass deportation faces a deeper capacity and self-defeating logic problem than corridors: labor demand persists, jobs refill informally, and mass roundups require more state coercion than registering workers at existing chokepoints. PRO effectively turned CON's "hollow systems" objection into an argument for better corridor design, showed that regularization shrinks the enforcement target to a manageable residual, and maintained that corridors use employer and origin-state incentives rather than fighting them. CON's enforcement-first sequencing was coherent but struggled to explain how mass deportation actually breaks the informal-market cycle without employer sanctions that are at least as capacity-intensive as corridors, and it never squarely answered PRO's point that the irregular population largely consists of wanted workers whose removal would simply regenerate demand.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's turn of CON's "hollow systems" point [B2.1], arguing that Gulf irregularity stems from employer-tied visas and weak exits that better corridors would fix, while mass deportation demands even greater enforcement capacity than the hollow states CON describes.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal directly answered both pressure questions: breaches could trigger status and future-access losses plus targeted removal, while recurring quotas preserved leverage over origin states.
