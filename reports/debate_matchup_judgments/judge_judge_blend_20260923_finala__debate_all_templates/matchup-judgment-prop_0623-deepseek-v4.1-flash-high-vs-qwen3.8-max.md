### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, competitive cases, but CON ultimately controlled the decisive comparisons. PRO's opening and first rebuttal were strong (fissured-industry mechanism, extension covering non-members, Australian/European examples), but its case increasingly conceded ground: admitting sectoral schedules require differentials "not single rates" let CON argue PRO's system is just a bureaucratized version of a statutory minimum, and PRO's admission that unviable firms "should not be preserved by poverty pay" handed CON a direct concession that the mechanism does produce job losses despite the promised uniform floor. CON exploited this cleanly and repeatedly, and its closing crystallized the strongest unanswered line: if sectoral bargaining is really just a tailored floor, legislate it directly rather than delegate it to councils whose corrections require navigating classifications and exemptions instead of a simple statutory fix. PRO's institutional remedies for outsider capture (reserved seats, elections, petitions) remained speculative against CON's more concrete appeal to legislative accountability and publicity. On format execution, PRO's rebuttal after questions properly opened with labeled A1/A2 answers, while CON's answers, though substantively responsive, were not explicitly labeled — a real but minor execution cost that does not overcome CON's stronger overall comparative framing and sharper closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's exploitation of PRO's concession that unviable firms "should not be preserved by poverty pay" (B4.1), turned in A4.3 into an admission that the promised uniform floor collapses into job losses or a subsidy/exemption bureaucracy — a point PRO never fully neutralized before the debate ended.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): CON won a competitive debate by keeping the decisive burden on mechanism and accountability: PRO’s undercutting and multi-standard case stayed live, but CON showed a high, differentiated statutory floor plus direct scheduling/safety/joint-liability rules can stop the race without incumbent councils, extension without consent, or continuous exemption politics. PRO answered pressure honestly and forced CON to expand its alternative, yet never fully neutralized capture or the job-loss/exemption tradeoff beyond design promises; CON made the choice clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON turning PRO’s “schedules not single rates” defense into the bureaucracy/accountability indictment (B2.1–B2.5 / B4.1–B4.2), showing equivalence of tailoring fails once control and correction differ.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO won the central comparison. CON attacked sectoral schedules as a bureaucratic maze with exemptions and insider capture, but PRO showed CON's own alternative – indexed, regionally differentiated statutory minima plus subsidies, inspectors, hardship relief and direct scheduling/safety laws – needs the same tailoring and hardship judgments with less coordination. PRO's pressure Q1/Q2 exposed that inconsistency and its A1/A2 answers gave concrete design replies (sunset phase-ins, inclusive representation, portable benefits) while preserving its decisive mechanism: changing the unit of competition so undercutting is unprofitable for firms that could pay but won't. CON's accountability point was strong but never solved the fissured-industry race to the bottom that a single-number floor leaves intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.3/B3.1 – PRO turns CON's "maze" attack by showing the high, regionally differentiated statutory minimum has the same viability and complexity costs with less tailoring and no coordination to stop undercutting.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON built and sustained a clean structural dilemma: a sectoral floor calibrated finely enough (by region, size, occupation) to avoid harming marginal firms collapses into an ordinary minimum-wage schedule, while a floor strong enough to change industry-wide competition necessarily prices out weaker firms and entry-level workers. PRO never resolved this tension; its repeated answer that variation is "not surrender" and that boards will have "safeguards" (representativeness, review, small-firm voice) restated administrative hopes rather than showing how the floor stays both meaningful and harmless. CON also converted PRO's own concession—that costs might be absorbed via "better public contract pricing"—into evidence that the mechanism relies on taxpayer subsidy rather than being self-financing, a sharp and decisive rebuttal move that PRO's closing did not neutralize. PRO's strongest surviving point—that universal minimums alone don't stop firms from winning contracts by paying the legal floor and churning staff—was reasonably answered by CON's alternative package (higher enforced floors, portable benefits, misclassification rules, procurement standards), which addresses the same undercutting dynamic without the structural costs CON identified in sectoral bargaining. CON's cartel/consent argument (binding nonunion firms and dissenting workers to a board they never chose, with large incumbents best positioned to absorb costs) also went under-answered beyond assertions that it is "public governance, not collusion." Both sides followed the Q&A format properly, so no execution penalty there, but CON's rebuttals did more actual damage to PRO's core mechanism than PRO's rebuttals did to CON's dilemma framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's dilemma in [A2.1]-[A2.2] and its reprise in [A4.3]-[A4.4], which forced PRO's "better contract pricing" answer into an admission that sectoral gains require external subsidy rather than being cost-neutral, was the most damaging and unresolved exchange of the debate.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): B won by keeping a live structural dilemma at the center: a floor strong enough to change industry competition imposes real costs on marginal/rural/nonprofit providers, while a floor flexible enough to avoid that becomes a politicized minimum-wage patchwork with extra boards. A never fully escaped that trade-off, and A’s “better public contract pricing” and administrative-safeguard answers partly conceded fiscal and capture risks. A’s race-to-the-bottom story and pressure on undercutting at the legal minimum were sharp, but B’s alternative package (higher universal floors, portable benefits, misclassification/procurement enforcement) remained a credible non-sectoral path, and B crystallized the choice more cleanly across rebuttals and closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s flexibility paradox (esp. B2.1/B2.7 and closing): if sectoral floors are tailored by region/size/occupation enough to spare marginal firms, they stop doing PRO’s work; if strong enough to lift industry norms, they price out the workers and small providers PRO claims to protect.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): PRO retained a live comparative advantage: a universal minimum still lets firms win contracts by paying only the floor and churning staff. CON's answer — raise the floor plus portable benefits, scheduling rules, procurement — replicates the complexity it attacks and concedes public contract repricing is needed. CON's strong dilemma (floor too strong = harms marginal, too weak = redundant) was partly met by B's calibrated-but-binding floor with public review and safeguards, while PRO's race-to-bottom framing survived both rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Exchange on undercutting at the legal minimum (B2.1/B4.6 vs A4.1) and the mirroring dilemma on flexibility (A2.1/A4.4 vs B4.1-B4.3) — B's point that a transparent industry floor removes labor cost from competition answered CON's sufficiency claim, while CON's promise-based safeguards critique never fully neutralized that mechanism.
