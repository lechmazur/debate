- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won because the motion requires allowing trading, not creating an unregulated general right. B’s clipped opening and oversimplified treatment of utility margins reduce the margin but do not reverse the result.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s regulatory dilemma was decisive: safety rules, metering, and wires charges do not negate peer trading; they describe how it can be responsibly allowed. A repeatedly converted “allow” into an unregulated legal entitlement, leaving B’s regulated model largely intact.

CON’s cost-shift/shared-grid case was strong early but drifted into “if regulated it is redundant,” which undercuts a ban rather than proving one. PRO’s pressure answers (two cost pools; monopoly incentive) and closing crystallized that CON conceded workable safeguards yet still demanded prohibition. CON never fully showed why the residual margin must belong to the utility once network costs are recovered, so the proposition’s “allow” burden was met more cleanly by B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1–B4.2 two-pool answer to A’s tariff dilemma: wires recovery vs. shared retail–wholesale margin, then using CON’s own feasibility assumptions against a ban.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO neutralized CON's core cost-shifting claim by showing P2P can include a wires charge, splitting the existing retail-wholesale spread rather than defunding the grid. ### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that grid charges, curtailment, and standardized settlement are conditions of network access rather than reasons to prohibit voluntary pricing and contracting. Side A sharply argued duplication and transaction costs, but repeatedly assumed regulation would erase meaningful choice without proving that access rules consume the trading margin or that utility programs reliably provide equivalent access. Its late breach-risk argument was also weakened by Side B’s point that standard contracts can make curtailment an explicit condition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s answer to the claim that regulated P2P merely becomes a VPP was decisive: the grid operator can retain dispatch control while peers still choose counterparties, prices, and whether to trade. That preserved a concrete benefit—consumer initiative and competitive pressure—that Side A’s utility-program alternative did not guarantee.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO won the motion as written: allow-with-rules beats prohibition once grid charges, curtailment, and consumer protections are treated as conditions of access rather than reasons to ban. CON’s collapse-to-VPP attack was the debate’s sharpest move and forced a narrow residue fight, but PRO preserved a live unique benefit—enforceable consumer initiative and competitive pressure versus discretionary utility programs—and handled pressure by cleanly separating energy price from wire rules. CON showed extra contract friction and thin residual freedom, not net harm unique enough to defeat permission.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 1 collapse: regulated P2P becomes a mediated VPP with bilateral dispute costs; PRO’s Rebuttal 2 recovery (toll-road / neighbor still sets price) kept the permission path alive.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) kept the burden on prohibition and answered CON's core cost-shifting and complexity claims with a concrete regulatory fix: allow trading but charge transparent grid-use fees and preserve curtailment. CON's strongest move - that regulated P2P collapses into a VPP [A2.2][A5.1] - was directly met by B's toll-road distinction that access conditions are not price controls [B4.1] and that VPPs are discretionary while P2P is an enforceable right [B4.3][B5.2]. CON never shows why duplication alone justifies a ban.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's A1 in Rebuttal 2 - toll road/traffic light analogy distinguishing peer-set energy price from regulator-set grid-use and curtailment - undercut A's collapse-into-VPP thesis and reframed harm as utility control loss.
