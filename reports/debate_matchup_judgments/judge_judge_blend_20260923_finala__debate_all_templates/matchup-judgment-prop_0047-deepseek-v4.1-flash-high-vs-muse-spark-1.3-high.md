### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent narratives, but CON did more of the real argumentative work. PRO's "who pays" framing was compelling in the opening, but under pressure its answers grew speculative ("someday" competitiveness, "calibration choice" on definitions) and it never squarely defended the shift from "local manufacturing" to "subsidizing producer-region jobs too," a move CON explicitly flagged as rewriting the motion's own terms. CON's pressure questions forced PRO into hand-wavy territory on definitions and leakage, while CON's own answers (direct regulation of effluent, dumping, and labor standards as an alternative route to making polluters pay) offered a concrete mechanism PRO never fully neutralized in its final speeches. CON's closing image—"you cannot take coats off children this winter to promise cleaner mills later"—crystallized the decisive clash more sharply than PRO's more abstract "hidden bill" framing, and CON consistently tied the arithmetic (unit costs, cash constraints, timing) back to the motion's explicit concession that prices rise, which PRO conceded but never fully resolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.4/B4.3 exchange, catching PRO's pivot to "producer-region jobs" as inconsistent with the motion's "local manufacturing," combined with CON's direct-regulation alternative in A1 of Rebuttal 2, which PRO's closing never squarely rebutted.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): B won by showing that producer subsidies do not solve immediate cash constraints or guarantee affordable clothing, while the tax predictably raises prices for necessities. A established genuine environmental harms but repeatedly treated affordability, tax definition, leakage, and displaced garment workers as manageable design issues without sufficiently proving that the promised replacement supply would arrive cheaply or quickly. B also supplied a plausible direct-regulation alternative that weakened A’s claim that this tax was necessary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered A’s pressure questions by distinguishing opposition to all price increases from opposition to a heavy necessity tax, proposing regulation of effluent and waste, and explaining why durability and future jobs cannot cover a present checkout shortfall.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the decisive clash on the motion as written: a heavy production tax that funds local manufacturers while admitting higher prices for lower-income buyers. A’s externality and “honest pricing” frame was coherent and polished, but never neutralized B’s cash-constraint, outgrowing-clothes, and mills-not-shoppers objections, nor the producer-country job loss and feasibility points. B’s direct-regulation alternative and arithmetic/timing rebuttals left A defending a speculative later supply against certain checkout harm now.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s R1/R2 insistence that durability, local jobs, and avoided cleanup cannot be spent at checkout, and that motion revenue funds mills not shoppers—forcing A into definitional carve-outs and “someday compete” promises.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and followed format well, including proper A1/A2 answers to pressure questions. PRO's "dishonest cheapness/honest prices" framing was rhetorically strong and consistently reasserted, and its answer that a lump-sum rebate preserves relative price signals while existing repair/secondhand infrastructure can be funded immediately was a genuine attempt to dissolve CON's rebate dilemma. But CON built a sharper, more sustained logical trap: the same tax revenue cannot simultaneously fund industrial subsidies, rebates, and repair programs, and the motion's own "prices rise for the poor" clause concedes the core harm is certain while the durable-supply benefit is speculative and delayed. CON's Rebuttal 2 directly exposed a contradiction in PRO's phase-up promise (tax now vs. subsidy funded only once alternatives exist), and PRO's closing, constrained to crystallization, never fully closed that loop, leaving CON's "certain harm vs. speculative benefit" framing as the last word on the decisive clash. That edge in analytical tightness on the central tension gives CON a clear, competitive win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4.4) pinpointing the sequencing contradiction in PRO's phase-in funding mechanism — "Delay the tax and the subsidy lacks reliable funding; start it and poor families pay before alternatives scale" — which PRO's closing did not directly resolve.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): B won by focusing the debate on immediate affordability, sequencing, and fiscal tradeoffs. A correctly explained that lump-sum aid can preserve relative price signals, but never fully resolved how tax revenue could simultaneously subsidize manufacturing, protect poor consumers, and fund repair while alternatives scale. B also offered a more targeted route for addressing environmental harms without taxing every cheap necessity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s sequencing challenge was decisive: delaying the heavy tax weakens the funding needed to create alternatives, while imposing it immediately makes lower-income families finance an uncertain transition. A’s phasing proposal softened but did not eliminate that dilemma.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): B won by making the decisive clash clearer: the motion’s accepted price rise hits lower-income buyers immediately while durable supply and full protection remain uncertain, and the same revenue cannot simultaneously fund factories, rebates, and repair at scale. A’s honest-prices and per-wear framing was strong and answered externalities well, but never neutralized B’s sequencing/arithmetic pressure or the bluntness charge; B’s targeted EPR/standards alternative stayed live and comparatively cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second-rebuttal crushing of the rebate dilemma and phase-up contradiction (same dollar cannot both shield the poor and subsidize industry; delay tax or front-load pain).
