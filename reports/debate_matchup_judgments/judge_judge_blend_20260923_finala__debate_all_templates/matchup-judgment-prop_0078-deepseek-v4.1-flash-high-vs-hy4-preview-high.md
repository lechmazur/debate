### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued with discipline and clarity, but PRO (B) built the more resilient structure. B's public/private distinction and "bridge, not destination" framing anticipated CON's core "access vs. adoption" move, and B's flip of CON's own vulnerable-women example—arguing that permanent optionality empowers community gatekeepers rather than protecting isolated women—landed as a genuine, largely unanswered pressure point; CON's reply pivoted to state officials rather than confronting the internal-gatekeeper mechanism directly. B also handled the pressure questions cleanly with explicit A1/A2 answers, conceding candidly that naturalization (not basic rights) is the real stake, then reframing that concession as a bounded, exemption-protected default rather than a "loyalty test." CON's strongest counter—exploiting that concession to argue any contingency is a "grading mechanism"—was sharp and nearly closed the gap, and CON's "grandmother with an interpreter" example was an effective concrete illustration of supported access. But CON never fully neutralized B's gatekeeper argument or B's public-conduct/private-belief distinction, leaving B with the cleaner, more complete case by the closing exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 turn of CON's "isolated women" example ([B2.3]: gatekeepers benefit when language stays optional, so expectation-plus-support is the real rescue) was the most decisive exchange; CON's later reply addressed state coercion but never squarely rebutted the internal-gatekeeper mechanism B raised.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by defending a limited, reciprocal expectation rather than cultural conformity: unconditional basic rights, funded support, reasonable exemptions, a common public language, and civic norms defined through conduct rather than private belief. Side A effectively exposed that B would delay naturalization for some slow learners, making the contest competitive, but never adequately defended why interpreter-dependent access is an equal long-term substitute for direct public participation. A also relied on the contentious premise that full civic membership is owed immediately, while its conscience objection weakened once B clarified that devout dissent remains protected.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s answer that equal dignity concerns public conduct and legal standing, not compelled private belief, decisively narrowed Side A’s assimilation objection. Side A’s later claim that officials would infer motive did not show why ordinary, generally applicable antidiscrimination rules uniquely impose ideological conformity on immigrants.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B built a more persuasive and practically grounded case. They successfully defended the proposition by distinguishing between basic rights (which remain unconditional) and full civic naturalization (which reasonably requires language and civic norms). Side B's rhetorical framing of Side A's model as "participation on permanent loan" effectively neutralized Side A's argument that interpreters provide full equal membership. Side A made a strong philosophical case for immediate supported access, but struggled to overcome Side B's practical points about the limitations of permanent translation and the necessity of shared public norms to prevent enclave segregation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they directly answered Side A's pressure questions by clarifying that naturalization is delayed (but not basic rights) and that norms apply to public conduct rather than private conscience, effectively defusing Side A's main offensive traps.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued with unusual sophistication and stayed within the evidence rules, but PRO's case held together more coherently under pressure. PRO built a durable frame distinguishing the "public operating code" (language, due process, nonviolence) from private culture, and turned CON's central equity worry back on itself: an optional model lets "the confident cross over anyway" while the isolated remain permanently dependent on interpreters and brokers — a point CON never fully neutralized. Critically, CON's own answer to PRO's Q1 ("None of those norms requires giving up a faith, dress, or mother tongue") conceded away much of the rhetorical force behind CON's opening "policing identity" charge, and PRO exploited that concession cleanly in the closing to dissolve the "redundant or dangerous" dilemma CON tried to spring. CON's recurring equity point (the mother working two jobs punished by an exam) remained a live and forceful objection, and CON's "toll gate" closing image was vivid, but it targeted implementation/fairness of enforcement rather than fully rebutting PRO's stratification argument about what happens when expectation is absent entirely. On balance PRO answered pressure more directly, converted an extracted concession into leverage, and closed with a tighter, more consistent throughline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure question, forcing CON to admit none of the listed norms requires abandoning faith, dress, or language — a concession PRO then used in its closing to defuse CON's "redundant or dangerous" framing and reassert that the expectation is functional (public code) rather than identity policing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B won by consistently distinguishing public civic competence from private cultural conformity and by combining support with a meaningful expectation. Side A compellingly highlighted unequal capacity and the risks of punitive exams, but repeatedly treated B as offering tests without infrastructure even after B explicitly endorsed classes, interpretation, and anti-discrimination measures. A also blurred speaking a mother tongue at home with lacking public-language competence. The erroneous closing references were a minor execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal directly answered the coercion dilemma by specifying a public-language and civics threshold while excluding scrutiny of home speech, dress, or worship. A exposed the harshness of tying this to settlement rights, but its “milestone without support” response did not fit B’s stated support-plus-milestone model.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively trapping Side B in a logical dilemma regarding the definition of "core civic norms." Side A successfully argued that if these norms merely mean obeying the law, the proposition is redundant; if they require internal adoption, they constitute an unworkable and dangerous policing of identity. Side A also dominated the pressure question exchange, using Side B's concession of a "naturalization exam" to show that the proposition effectively punishes the poor and working class for scarcity rather than refusal. Side B's rhetorical polish could not overcome Side A's sharper analytical framework and superior handling of the core clash over capacity versus coercion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's list of civic norms by proving they were already covered by existing laws, thereby rendering the motion's "adoption" requirement either redundant or an invasive loyalty test, while simultaneously exposing the punitive reality of Side B's proposed language exam for the working poor.
