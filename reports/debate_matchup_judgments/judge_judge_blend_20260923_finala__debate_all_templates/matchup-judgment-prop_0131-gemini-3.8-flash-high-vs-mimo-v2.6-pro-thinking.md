### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): PRO built a plausible headline case (unpaid care is unsustainable, formal aides are scarce, unpaid labor is an invisible subsidy) but largely repeated these claims across rounds rather than engaging CON's sharpest mechanism. CON's decisive move was distinguishing a "generous wage for existing hours" from targeted income support/pension credits, arguing the former crowds out funding for formal capacity and creates a perverse incentive for the marginal lower-earning spouse to exit the labor force — exactly the group PRO says the policy protects. PRO's answers to pressure questions (A4.1-A4.2) asserted that exits are driven by care need rather than incentives and that pension attachment fixes the gap, but never rebutted CON's rejoinder (B4.3-4.4) that pension credits can be delivered without a wage that induces exit, nor did PRO's closing address this narrowing at all. CON's closing crystallized the clash cleanly ("judge the motion by its instrument, not its sympathy"), while PRO's closing largely recapped its opening. CON also directly answered PRO's pressure questions with a coherent reallocation logic, whereas PRO's answers to CON's questions were more assertion than mechanism. B's opening being clipped is a real execution cost, but it did not blunt the analytical edge CON built and sustained through the later rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.3-B4.4, which isolated PRO's pension-gap fix as a separable instrument (pension credits/social insurance) that undercuts PRO's need for a "generous wage" at all, while showing the wage itself induces the labor-force exit PRO claims to prevent — a point PRO never answered in its closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by distinguishing financial recognition from actually reducing the care burden. A persuasively showed that unpaid care causes poverty and instability, but never adequately compared the total wage bill for millions of caregivers against targeted respite, equipment, benefits, and formal-care capacity. B also exposed that A's pension and solvency answers depended largely on social insurance and grants rather than generous wages themselves. A's unsupported avoided-cost claims and erroneous reference IDs further weakened its response, though B's clipped opening was an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's decisive response was that A compared caregiver wages with costly institutionalization only for those who might enter institutions, rather than comparing system-wide spending per additional care hour. B then narrowed A's strongest remedies—pension credits, equipment support, and respite funding—into targeted alternatives that did not require paying wages for hours already supplied.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won by locking the decisive clash on the motion’s instrument: generous pay purchases hours already delivered and crowds out capacity that actually reduces load, while PRO’s defensible aims (solvency, pension security, equipment) can be met by grants/credits/respite without the wage. PRO kept a strong collapse-cost story and shortage premise, but never neutralized the opportunity-cost/rewriting charge; A’s progression from category error through fiscal comparison to the final instrument test made the vote clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2 (A4.1–A4.5) and close: force-separating income support/pension credits from a market wage, then showing the wage both crowds out formal-pay raises and induces exit that shrinks the aide pool PRO itself says is empty.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A built a sharper, more adaptive case: it reframed the debate correctly around the actual comparator (status quo unpaid care vs. paid care), repeatedly exposed that B's "professional system that relieves families entirely" is an aspirational alternative not actually on the ballot, and answered pressure questions concretely with design mechanisms (pension accrual, hour caps, respite funding, independent assessment) that directly neutralized B's gender-trap and dependency objections by showing those traps already exist unpaid and are worsened, not created, by payment. B's case was coherent and consistent across rounds but largely repeated the same three arguments (gender trap, clinical inadequacy, infrastructure "alibi") without ever squarely rebutting A's central charge that B was comparing real policy against a hypothetical utopia rather than the actual status quo baseline. B's closing failed to engage this framing at all, while A's closing crystallized it clearly. A's case suffered real execution costs from repeated word-limit clipping (opening, rebuttal 2, closing all cut off), which is a legitimate format penalty, but the substantive content delivered before each cut was still more decisive than B's steady but static repetition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 turn on the "revenue stream/oversight" point [A2.5]: A argued paid care creates a formal relationship (assessments, caseworkers, review) that unpaid care completely lacks, flipping B's "financial dependency" fear into evidence for payment's oversight benefits — a move B never fully answered except by repeating the dependency claim in later rounds.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by consistently comparing compensated care with the actual baseline of unpaid family labor, while Side B leaned too heavily on an ideal professional system that was neither required by nor clearly exclusive with the proposition. A directly answered the gender and dependency objections through pension protection, respite, assessment, and oversight, showing that these risks justify careful design rather than continued nonpayment. B raised credible fiscal and incentive concerns, and A incurred real execution costs from three clipped turns, but B never established why professional services preclude compensating the residual care families still provide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to the gender trap was decisive: women already leave work because care must be provided, so refusing payment preserves rather than prevents the harm. A then comparatively improved that baseline through pension accrual, respite, hour limits, and job protection. B identified a possible marginal incentive to leave employment but did not overcome A’s status-quo comparison.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): B won by locking the debate to the proposition as written: stop assuming unpaid family care will absorb the burden versus pay more generously. A repeatedly replaced that baseline with an idealized full professional system that relieves families “entirely,” which never became a real on-the-table alternative. B’s residual-care and already-trapped-unpaid rebuttals, plus direct A1/A2 design answers on pensions, hour caps, respite, and independent assessment, kept neutralizing A’s live routes. A remained sharp on gender incentives and fiscal alibi risks, but never squared the residual labor problem or showed why unpaid invisibility beats paid formalization under the actual motion. Clipping costs hurt B’s polish, not the path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Turn 3/7 false-choice and residual-care replies: unpaid confinement is the status quo, so designed payment is strictly better than the same exit for free—and even full professional systems still leave nights/weekends/coordination with kin.
