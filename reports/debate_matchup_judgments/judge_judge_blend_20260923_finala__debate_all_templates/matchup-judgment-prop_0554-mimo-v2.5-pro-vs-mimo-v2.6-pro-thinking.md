### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): This was a genuinely close, substantive debate. A built a coherent case (democratic mandate, cost/urgency, infrastructure parity, and a concrete answer to B's own pressure question via SuedLink/SuedOstLink surviving elections). B built an equally serious case around the motion's key word "even," arguing that overriding strong opposition converts stakeholders into mobilized opponents, that compensation without real bargaining power is coercive, and that the "infrastructure parity" analogy actually cuts against A since rail/roads are routinely rerouted and delayed by objection too. B's reframing of A's flagship SuedLink example — turning it from "override succeeds" into "override still produces litigation, delay, and forced undergrounding" — was the sharpest single rebuttal in the debate, blunting A's best evidence without fully conceding the political-survival point. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions round properly with clear A1/A2 answers. What tips this is execution and closing discipline: A was clipped in nearly every substantive round (opening, both rebuttals, and closing), and its closing literally cuts off mid-sentence before delivering its final synthesis, weakening the last impression. B stayed within limits except once, and its closing lands cleanly with a memorable, motion-anchored decision rule ("fast-with-consent against forced-then-reversed"). Given comparably strong substance on both sides, B's cleaner execution, sharper reframing of A's central evidence, and more complete closing give it the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 turning A's SuedLink/SuedOstLink example (cited as proof override survives elections) into evidence of litigation, cost escalation, and forced undergrounding — reframing A's strongest data point as confirmation of B's backlash mechanism rather than a clean win for override.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): Side B won by centering the debate on the word "even" and showing that override exactly where opposition is strongest breeds litigation, cost escalation, and political reversal rather than speed. B effectively turned A's own examples: the UK 2015 moratorium was reframed as backlash from prior developer-appeal override, not local-veto capture, and Denmark/Germany's fast deployment was shown to rely on voluntary buy-in that the motion would undermine. B also crystallized that A's "how, never whether" threshold strips communities of the leverage needed for genuine consent, converting potential stakeholders into mobilized opponents. A's infrastructure analogy and democratic public-interest framing were plausible but could not fully recover after B demonstrated that the cited override successes (SuedLink) were actually marked by delay and cost escalation, and that the fastest European buildouts came through consent rather than imposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 combination turning the UK 2015 moratorium and Denmark/Germany evidence against A, demonstrating that A's examples of success depended on buy-in mechanisms incompatible with forced override.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): PRO won the decisive clash on backlash and veto. CON argued override creates moratoria, but PRO showed the 2015 English moratorium arose under the veto-friendly regime CON defends, then answered CON's Q1 with German SuedLink/SuedOstLink and French RTE grid cases that survived elections. PRO's threshold – how not whether, mitigation/compensation but no permanent block – aligned wind with normal motorway/rail practice and exposed that CON's modular-relocatable defense collapses on grid, the subject of PRO's strong Q2. CON's compensation-as-bribe point was sharp but never explained why wind deserves a unique veto other infrastructure lacks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO Rebuttal 1 & 2 turning the 2015 moratorium from evidence of override backlash into evidence of minority capture under local-veto rules, distinguishing fuel-tax diffuse costs from village-identifiable compensation, and meeting the name-a-case challenge with SuedLink corridors.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Both sides built a coherent case, but PRO handled the decisive pressure point more directly. CON's sharpest move—pointing out that PRO's "even when opposition is strong" clause is in tension with claiming benefit-sharing/ownership works—was answered concretely: PRO isolated grid transmission as the residual category where no siting alternative or ownership stake is possible, turning it into a monopoly-cost problem rather than a preference dispute. CON's counter-proposal (exhaust alternatives, then hand hard cases to an "independent planning authority") is a real decision rule, but PRO's closing correctly noted this authority already exists in the form of the inspectorate/inquiry process that produced the decade-long freeze, and CON never cleanly distinguished how its version avoids that outcome. CON's historical counter-narrative on the Cameron freeze (top-down political calculation rather than local veto) was plausible and not fully refuted, keeping backlash risk alive as a real cost, but it wasn't enough to offset PRO's fairness/regressive-cost framing, which went largely unanswered after the early exchange. Execution also favored PRO: its closing was delivered in full and crystallized the clash cleanly, while CON's closing was clipped mid-sentence right as it tried to state its final "why CON wins" thesis, costing it the strongest possible final impression.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's move exposing that CON's own praised examples (Denmark/Scotland ownership) are themselves a form of benefit-sharing, forcing CON to concede the mechanism works while still needing a residual "hard case"—which PRO then filled concretely with the grid-transmission monopoly example in Rebuttal 2/A1.

B’s three clipped turns, including a truncated closing, left key burdens unresolved and weakened crystallization.
