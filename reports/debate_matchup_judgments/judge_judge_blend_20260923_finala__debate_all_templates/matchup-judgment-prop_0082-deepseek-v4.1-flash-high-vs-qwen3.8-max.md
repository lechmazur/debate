### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but A built a more resolved case. A's central move—reframing enforceability around testable, direction-based metrics (holdout removal of a feature, displacement tests distinguishing thin vs. dense markets)—directly answered CON's core "impossible standard" objection with a concrete, plausible mechanism rather than hand-waving. A's pressure questions also exposed CON's weakest link: reputation/exit discipline cannot punish suppression a user never sees. CON's answer to that question (patterns of churn/complaints reveal harm) never actually explained how users distinguish ordinary bad luck from hidden suppression, and its own answer to A's second question effectively conceded that A/B-style evidence could justify limits—undercutting its repeated claim that the standard is unworkable. CON's late point about conflicting metrics (matches up, conversation down) was a genuine unanswered wrinkle in A's closing, giving CON a strong final beat, but it doesn't fully unseat A's broader framework, which already required convergent negative signals before triggering action. On balance, A's mechanism-driven rebuttals did more damage to CON's core claims than CON's framing did to A's, producing a clear but competitive win for A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Questions (Q1 on undetectable suppression) and CON's own answer conceding that A/B evidence "removing paid visibility raises total mutual matches" could justify limits—an answer that undercut CON's central "impossible standard" argument.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully resolving Side B's strongest argument regarding 'hidden counterfactuals.' Side A effectively explained that consumers judge opaque systems through aggregate outcomes rather than needing to see the exact hidden match. Furthermore, Side A successfully pressed the ambiguity of Side B's metrics, demonstrating that relying on platforms to balance conflicting data imposes a subjective model that could harm users who need visibility boosts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal, where it neutralized the 'hidden counterfactual' argument by pointing out that users experience the visible consequences of a rigged system (fewer replies, constant pay prompts), and if the harm were truly undetectable, it couldn't justify limits anyway.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO kept the motion conditional and made it testable: distinguish expansion (total mutual matches rise) from auction (total flat/falling while revenue rises) via holdouts and nonpaid floors. That directly neutralized CON's best liquidity/thin-market defense and answered the enforcement objection. CON's reputation/exit argument never solved the invisible counterfactual problem pressed in Q1, while PRO's false-positive vs false-negative weighing made trust-as-product decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 displacement test and Q1 on undetectable buried profiles - it turned CON's thin-market expansion example into a reason to keep some boosts, while showing reputation cannot punish what users never see.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and handled the pressure round with proper A1/A2 formatting. PRO's throughline — that dating apps are "trust markets" where paid-priority and engagement loops corrode the belief that a match reflects compatibility rather than payment or impulse — stayed consistent and was reinforced by concrete, non-exotic platform metrics (reply rates, conversation depth, churn, complaints) as the operational trigger. CON's strongest move was the unfalsifiability critique (repeated pressure that "demonstrably" has no real threshold and could be triggered by ordinary market noise like skewed ratios or harassment), and its boosts-help-the-invisible mechanism was a genuine, underused counterweight. But CON's own remedy (disclosure plus filters) never squarely answered why a visible auction restores trust rather than merely naming the distortion, and its "banning boosts just hides the ranking in an opaque algorithm" line has a logical soft spot, since the underlying organic algorithm is already opaque with or without boosts — it doesn't get newly hidden by removing them. PRO's closing crystallized the trust-market framing more cleanly and tied its evidentiary answer directly back to the standard, giving the cleaner final reason to vote, even though CON's vagueness critique remains a live, only partially neutralized concern.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Pressure Questions exchange: PRO's Q1 (what would even count as demonstrable harm) forced CON to concede a workable evidentiary path in principle, and CON's Q1 forced PRO to specify metrics and decision-maker — but PRO's follow-up answer in Rebuttal 2 offering platform data (reply rates, conversation depth, churn/complaints, A/B testing) as an operational trigger was the more concrete resolution, while CON's counter that these metrics can't isolate design from market composition landed but wasn't decisively pressed further.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A wins by demonstrating that the alternative to paid boosts is an opaque ranking algorithm that inherently favors conventional attractiveness. Side B relies on the moral argument that users shouldn't have to pay for visibility, but Side A successfully argues that removing the paid lever leaves marginalized daters invisible and actually reduces transparency.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's argument in Rebuttal 2 that banning boosts does not eliminate unequal visibility, but merely hides prioritization inside an unaccountable algorithm.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): CON won the implementability and remedy clash. PRO’s conditional limit rests on “demonstrably,” but never supplies a falsifiable threshold or decision-maker independent of noisy trust complaints. CON answered pressure with a concrete test (controlled rollout on match-to-conversation, unmatching, churn) and showed PRO’s metrics don’t isolate design. On substance, CON’s visible-auction point – banning paid boosts doesn’t remove ranking, it hides prioritization, removing labels/filters/opt-outs and hurting older/less-conventional users who use boosts as a lever against invisibility – was never neutralized. PRO’s reply that labels leave distortion in place conceded hidden ranking persists.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4’s A1/A2: specifying a workable harm test while exposing PRO’s reply rates/complaints as non-isolating, and reframing a banned boost as less inspectable than a disclosed boost with filters and audits, which directly undercut PRO’s trust remedy.
