### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. B established that voluntary turnout systematically filters out less engaged demographic groups and plausibly argued that parties become accountable to a broader electorate when nearly everyone participates. A’s attendance-versus-outcomes distinction was sharp, but it relied too heavily on treating compelled votes as random or counterfeit without establishing that most reluctant voters lack genuine preferences. B also answered the enforcement dilemma with a capped, excusable civil penalty; imperfect deterrence does not make a legal duty meaningless. A’s repeated clipping was an additional execution cost, while B’s mistaken C-reference was minor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was its answer to A’s claim that absent voters still discipline politicians. B replied that chronic non-voters exert little present accountability because politicians can rationally ignore an empty chair. This directly weakened A’s alternative and clarified why access reforms alone do not remove the self-selection filter.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B narrowly won by successfully contrasting 'representative attendance' with 'representative outcomes'. While Side A gave a strong defense of why a full sample is better than a biased one, Side B expertly used Side A's concessions ('usually none', 'some noise') to prove that the mandate would mostly generate 'counterfeit consent' and donkey votes rather than truly representative policy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal and closing powerfully turned Side A's admission about 'noise among the newly included' into the decisive 'counterfeit consent' voting issue.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively attacking the link between mandatory turnout and "representative outcomes," effectively arguing that compelled attendance yields either spoiled ballots (changing nothing) or "donkey votes" (adding noise). Side A also trapped Side B in a strong enforcement dilemma (debt trap vs. theatre), which Side B struggled to resolve cleanly. While Side B made a strong principled case for demographic representation, Side A's sharper rebuttals and superior framing of the motion's specific burdens secured the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they capitalized on Side B's concession that a spoiled ballot changes "usually none" to prove that the outcome gains rely entirely on "noise" and "counterfeit consent," effectively neutralizing Side B's core link to the motion.
