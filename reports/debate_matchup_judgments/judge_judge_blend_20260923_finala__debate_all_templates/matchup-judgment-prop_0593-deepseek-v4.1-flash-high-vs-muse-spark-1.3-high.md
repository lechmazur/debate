### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides fought over the same pivot point—Rana Plaza and whether making audits public/standardized fixes or merely relabels the auditor-capture problem—but CON's resolution of that clash proved more durable. PRO's strongest tactical move, the "toothless brochure vs. causes pullouts" contradiction charge, was cleanly defused by CON's "toothless for reform, sharp for risk" framing: sanitized boilerplate is unprosecutable while candid admissions become litigation exhibits, so firms rationally sanitize or exit rather than fix. PRO never fully answered why mandated categories stop firms from filling them with unprosecutable vague language, nor why public payment-and-scope-by-the-audited-firm would break the same capture dynamic that persists in financial audits. PRO's answers increasingly fell back on "visibility is better than nothing" and appeals to unspecified enforcement bodies, which CON repeatedly noted lack demonstrated capacity or will. CON also kept the causal chain—disclosure risk to cost to supplier exclusion to worker harm—intact through both rebuttals and the pressure round, while PRO's parallel claim that exit becomes "visible" and thus accountable was conceded to not help workers who lose the contract. Both sides handled the Q&A format correctly, but CON's answers did more actual work resolving the tension PRO had exposed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's resolution of PRO's "contradiction" charge—reports are simultaneously shields (via sanitized boilerplate) and litigation risks (via candid admissions), so the rational strategy is sanitize-or-exit—was the turning point; PRO never neutralized this synthesis in the final rebuttal or closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by establishing public, standardized reporting as enabling infrastructure for enforcement rather than a complete substitute for unions or liability. Side A’s candor-versus-sanitization dilemma and supplier-exit harms were powerful, but it did not fully answer why those risks justify preserving opaque private diligence or how its preferred enforcement finds hidden supply-chain targets without comparable information.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its answer that CON’s enforcement alternative depends on information: unions and regulators need identifiable sites, risks, and claims to investigate. Side A sharply replied that honest reports expose firms while vague reports shield them, but B more successfully argued that standardization, auditing, and public cross-checking make omissions and clean claims contestable rather than leaving ignorance entirely private.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): B won by turning the debate onto firm incentives and worker outcomes rather than the desirability of transparency in the abstract. After A pressed the alleged contradiction that reports cannot be both toothless and pullout-causing, B's “toothless for reform, sharp for risk / sanitize or exit” distinction became the live deciding frame and held through closing: vague clean filings are hard to prosecute, candor becomes liability, and the rational response is sanitization or exit, with costs pushed onto vulnerable suppliers. A kept a real infrastructure claim—unions and regulators need discoverable sites—but never neutralized B’s points on auditor payment/capture, tier-three opacity and shifting names, unprosecutable boilerplate, or the fact that visible exit still leaves workers unpaid. B made the comparative choice clearer: this mandate rewards clean paper and distant suppliers more reliably than it produces floor-level remedy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's reply that sanitized reports shield while honest ones expose, so rational firms sanitize or exit (B4.1–B4.2), converting A's contradiction pressure into B's decisive incentive argument.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but PRO's central mechanism—that standardized, audited disclosure supplies the verifiable facts needed to trigger bans, lawsuits, and prosecutions—survived the exchange better than CON's core claim that the mandate is inherently a "compliance shield." CON's sharpest weapon was the "different policy" charge (that PRO's defenses of remediation, auditor liability, and responsible exit go beyond the motion's text), pressed effectively across B2.1, B3.2, B4.4, and B5.4. But PRO had a real answer: "due-diligence report" is a term of art that already implies an identify-act-remediate-report cycle, not mere disclosure, so invoking enforcement teeth for false audits isn't smuggling in a new policy so much as unpacking what audited due diligence means. CON never squarely engaged this semantic rejoinder after it was raised in A4.1-4.3, instead repeating the same structural objection in the closing without adapting. Meanwhile PRO's pressure question about where CON's preferred remedies (bans, prosecutions, worker monitoring) get their triggering facts was answered by CON only with a list of alternative fact sources (subpoenas, whistleblowers, journalists) that don't resolve how those mechanisms get initiated without any baseline transparency duty — a genuine gap PRO exploited in both rebuttal rounds and the closing. Both sides handled the Q&A format correctly, but PRO's answers did more actual work resolving the exchange, giving it the cleaner path to a decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The critical exchange was CON's Pressure Question about what duty is actually breached if a firm's audited report discloses forced labor but it keeps sourcing anyway, forcing PRO to expand "due diligence" to include remediation and auditor liability — and PRO's follow-up definitional defense that this expansion is inherent in what a "due-diligence report" already means, which CON never directly rebutted afterward.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by distinguishing mandatory reporting from actual remediation and repeatedly showing that Side B relied on additional duties, penalties, and enforcement mechanisms not guaranteed by the proposition. Side B persuasively argued that imperfect disclosure can aid outside enforcement, but overstated reports as the necessary source of proof and never fully answered the risks of hidden abuse, false assurance, and box-ticking.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal directly answered the claim that the alternative was secrecy by identifying targeted investigative sources of facts, then exposed Side B’s reliance on remediation duties and auditor penalties beyond the reporting mandate.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): B won the decisive clash on the motion as written: standardized audited reports, without secured remediation duties or bite, mainly produce box-ticking and a liability shield. A’s necessity-of-facts case and financial-audit analogy were strong, but replies repeatedly imported extras (remediation, responsible exit, auditor liability) that the proposition does not lock in, so B’s camouflage-versus-remedy comparison stayed live and clearer by the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1–B2.8: showing A rescues the mandate only by adding rules beyond publish-and-audit, so A is defending a fuller policy than the motion.
