### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly and stayed on-motion, but A (CON) built a more durable structural attack: the exit-financing mechanism (dominant platforms are often the only realistic buyer for pre-revenue, scale-dependent startups) and the "double bind" charge that B's presumption punishes the same unknowable future that B says regulators cannot use to justify blocking a deal under existing law. A pressed this contradiction in Pressure Questions and repeated it effectively through both rebuttals and the closing, and B's answer—that acquirers can document internal integration roadmaps while regulators cannot forecast external markets—never squarely closed the gap A kept exposing: a roadmap shows intent/capacity, not the "substantial consumer benefit" welfare verdict the motion actually demands. B's alternative-buyer answer ("mid-tier firms, rival ecosystems") was also thin against A's concrete twelve-person, pre-revenue example, which A reused effectively across rounds. B's case (information asymmetry, error-cost asymmetry, "once tipped stays tipped") was rhetorically strong and coherent, but the central logical tension A identified was never fully resolved, leaving A with the cleaner, more resilient path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's repeated "double standard" point—first raised in Pressure Questions Q1 and pressed again in Rebuttal 2/Closing—showing that B simultaneously argues regulators can't forecast an early-stage startup's future while demanding the acquirer prove a similarly unknowable future benefit; B's attempted distinction (internal roadmap vs. external forecast) did not resolve why a roadmap satisfies a welfare standard rather than merely showing intent.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): CON wins the decisive burden clash. PRO's core - that platforms can easily prove benefit from internal roadmaps - is undercut by B's distinction between a roadmap and a contested welfare finding about privacy/quality/innovation years later. B's pressure Q1 exposes the double standard (future unknowable for regulator = excuse, but unknowable for firm = proof of harm), which A never resolves. On startup pipeline, B shows blocking kills distribution-dependent products, while A’s claim that mid-tier buyers fill the gap is asserted, not defended. Error-cost framing stays live for both, but B turns it: false condemnation kills the exit that funds entry.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 answers and B3.1 - roadmap is planning, not welfare verdict; if slide deck suffices killer deals pass, if real future benefit must be proved the test is impossible and waiting kills the startup, showing uncertainty ≠ confession.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by decisively resolving the epistemic clash. While Side B effectively argues that proving future consumer benefit is just as impossible as proving future market harm, Side A sharply distinguishes between the regulator's burden (forecasting an external market counterfactual without proprietary data) and the acquirer's burden (documenting its own internal integration plans and operational synergies). This distinction neutralizes Side B's core argument that the presumption creates an impossible standard. Side A also maintains a cleaner comparative framework on error costs, emphasizing the permanence of tipped digital markets. Side B's arguments regarding the chilling effect on venture capital and startup survival were strong, but Side A's structural critique of the "acquihire" incentive model and its epistemic defense of the burden shift secured the victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A1), which dismantled Side B's "unknowable future" argument by distinguishing between external market forecasting and internal integration planning.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): B built a tighter, more coherent case around a repeated asymmetry frame (a blocked deal can be conditioned, licensed, or sold elsewhere; a swallowed challenger cannot be restored) and never let go of it. Crucially, B directly neutralized A's flagship example, Android, by showing it would likely clear the presumption under B's own framework, and A never returned to defend or re-engage that example after Rebuttal 1. B's answers to pressure questions (A1/A2) offered a concrete, non-impossible evidentiary standard (mechanism, necessity, binding commitments; overlap/foreclosure tests), which blunted the "impossible negative" charge better than A's answers blunted B's core asymmetry argument. A's Catch-22 and "everything overlaps" critiques were real and forced B to clarify, but A mostly reasserted the impossibility claim rather than demonstrating it against B's specific proposed standard, and A's own examples (Instagram/WhatsApp) went largely unaddressed by A's side of the ledger in later rounds. Closings crystallized the clash well, but B's closing framing was sharper and left the decisive question (who should bear the cost of uncertainty) more clearly answered in B's favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 distinguishing Android ([B2.3]: "A buyer with that plan can prove substantial benefit... Deals paid for by deleting a rival path fail; complements that deliver otherwise unavailable gains clear") effectively defused A's central example, and A never fully recovered that ground in later speeches.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won the burden-asymmetry clash. After CON exposed a tension between dismissing synergies and allowing Android, PRO clarified a workable standard - dated mechanisms, necessity vs licensing/other buyers, and enforceable commitments - and answered both CON pressure questions directly. CON repeated the impossible-burden and sole-buyer claims without undermining that narrowed test, and its existing-law-sufficiency claim was undercut by PRO's private-information/tipping argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's R2 A1/A2 answers that replace prophecy with mechanism/necessity/commitment and define complement vs threat by overlap, documents, and alternative paths, dissolving CON's Catch-22.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully defending the workability of the presumption against Side B's "Catch-22" attack. Side A provided concrete, legally grounded examples of how an acquirer could prove consumer benefit (e.g., engineering records, binding commitments, internal documents) without needing a finished product. Furthermore, Side A won the decisive final clash on asymmetry: while Side B argued that blocking deals kills unfunded innovation, Side A persuasively countered that a blocked acquisition leaves the technology intact for licensing or alternative buyers, whereas a completed anti-competitive acquisition permanently destroys the rival path. Side B's VC chilling effect argument was strong, but Side A's structural and evidentiary responses were cleaner and more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they directly answered the pressure questions by specifying the exact types of pre-closing evidence (engineering records, dated mechanisms, binding commitments) that would satisfy the presumption, effectively neutralizing Side B's claim that the standard was an impossible evidentiary trap.
