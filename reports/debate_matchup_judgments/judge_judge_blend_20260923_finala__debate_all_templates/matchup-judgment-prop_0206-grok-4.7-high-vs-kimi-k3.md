Side B attempts to argue that flat taxes force the poor to subsidize the rich, but Side A neutralizes this by consistently defending a progressive tax design.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's first rebuttal and closing masterfully isolate the core tradeoff: money can be progressively targeted, while time cannot, leaving Side B with a fundamentally regressive mechanism.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B won the central comparison.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2's lawyer-vs-cleaner comparison - one instrument bills the person who lives longer, the other bills the person who dies sooner - plus the point that disability exits erase the promised contributions, turning PRO's fiscal saving into hardship without revenue.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): B won by showing that average-life-expectancy indexing imposes missing-wage, missing-pension years on workers who did not receive the longevity gain. A never adequately resolved the tradeoff between broad, administratively difficult carve-outs and narrow gates that strand worn-out workers. B also offered a more progressive payroll-tax design and successfully turned A’s claimed “ceiling” advantage. A’s labor-cost and intergenerational arguments kept the debate competitive, but its closing misleadingly treated B’s statement that taxes need not rise forever as a concession that they must rise without limit. B’s clipped first rebuttal was an execution cost, but not enough to offset its stronger comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was the response to A’s ratchet argument: productivity and wage-base growth mean tax rates need not rise for every longevity gain, while automatic age indexing itself has no fixed ceiling. This directly undercut A’s central “cure with a ceiling” framing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side A won by effectively neutralizing Side B's attacks on the payroll tax and winning the core clash over vulnerable workers. Side A used productivity gains and uncapped earnings to explain why taxes need not rise infinitely or regressively, points Side B largely ignored. Side A also successfully demonstrated that Side B's policy forces the short-lived to subsidize the wealthy, and that narrow exemptions are insufficient to fix this structural inequity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A1 response in Rebuttal 2 cleanly disarmed B's pressure on job market hireability by pointing out that the goal for worn-out workers is a accessible pension, not remaining cheap to hire.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): PRO won the central comparison: indexing treats longer retirements directly while payroll taxes are regressive, tax work, and have no ceiling. A turned CON's equity heartland by showing the caregiver pays more under payroll taxes from first dollar, and answered the gates dilemma as majority savings with minority carve-outs. CON's strong uneven-longevity pressure landed, but B's R1 was clipped, its A1 on hiring costs was weak, and its admission of no terminal rate was seized in A5 to crystallize cure-with-ceiling vs ratchet-without.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.2-A2.4 flipping CON's worn-out worker: diagnosing inequality then prescribing a flat first-dollar payroll tax that hits that same low-wage worker hardest while raising hiring costs that screen older workers out.
