### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a competitive debate by showing that similar reliability in sparse districts requires materially lower vehicle utilization and then explaining why cross-subsidy is unstable in a market with competing ride options. A also offered mandatory phased coverage plus density-matched anti-bias enforcement, weakening B's claim that the alternative permits total exclusion. B made the stronger equity appeal and had cleaner rhetoric, while A's repeated clipping was a real execution cost, but B never adequately resolved the opportunity-cost and competitive-leakage objections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's use of B's own staged-car estimates was decisive: fewer rides per vehicle implied substantially higher per-ride costs, and A sharply distinguished competitive robotaxis from protected universal-service systems such as mail and utilities. B's assertion that fleets could simply blend those costs did not answer riders switching to competing services.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing that the motion bundles geography with equity and imposes unsustainable economics, while offering a sharper alternative. CON distinguished sparsity from discrimination, demonstrated with PRO's own numbers that sparse-district rides cost roughly two to three times as much, and explained why cross-subsidies leak away in a competitive market—an argument PRO never answered in the closing. PRO's closing mischaracterized CON's alternative as permitting zero service, but CON had already accepted phased coverage of named neighborhoods plus binding density-matched audits, making that attack stale. PRO's public-infrastructure framing and need-based appeals were strong, but CON's feasibility critique and the evolution of its targeted alternative created the cleaner live route to victory after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's turn in Rebuttal 2 (A4.5-A4.6), where CON accepted PRO's staging numbers (5–8 rides per night versus 15–20 downtown), derived the resulting 2–3× cost-per-ride gap, and then showed why PRO's cross-subsidy analogy fails because robotaxi riders can simply switch to competing apps—an objection PRO left unaddressed in the final speech.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively dismantling Side A's economic mechanism. Side A relied heavily on analogies to utilities, broadband, and rural mail to argue that robotaxi fleets could cross-subsidize low-demand areas through blended fares. Side B effectively countered that those analogies involve protected monopolies or universal service mandates with captive audiences, whereas robotaxis face direct competition from human ride-hail and transit. As B pointed out, raising downtown fares to fund sparse-area parity would simply drive riders to competitors, breaking the cross-subsidy. Side B also smartly separated "coverage" (which they accepted via phased build-out and anti-discrimination audits) from "reliability parity" (the actual motion), showing that the motion's specific requirement is economically destructive without being necessary to prevent redlining. Side A's closing attacked a strawman of B's audit that B had already patched, leaving B with the cleaner, more realistic path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal in Turn 8 destroying the rural mail cross-subsidy analogy by highlighting that robotaxis operate in a competitive market, meaning downtown riders would switch apps if fares rose to fund the mandate, causing the subsidy to leak away.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by grounding the low-demand problem in fleet geography: similar reliability requires costly idle capacity or repositioning, which can raise fares or deter entry. Side B compellingly argued that vouchers do not guarantee prompt arrival, but never adequately resolved A's central dilemma that a meaningful coverage floor has real costs while a cheap, modest rule may accomplish little. B's anti-gaming fare condition closed one loophole but restored A's cost objection, and its claim that cross-subsidy would cost only a negligible amount was asserted more than demonstrated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange showed that B's single-fare-schedule patch prevents denominator gaming only by forcing operators to absorb the underlying low-demand coverage cost; A then tied that cost to defection toward unregulated alternatives and reduced entry in marginal cities.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) constructed the clearer route to victory by demonstrating that the reliability mandate was either hollow (if limited to modest gaps) or costly and gameable (requiring fare controls and idle cars), and that any real cross-subsidy would collapse once riders defected to unregulated competitors. Side B (PRO) never resolved this double-bind or the marginal-cities exit problem, while A showed that provider-neutral vouchers converted low-demand zones into paid demand that dispatch algorithms already serve, delivering actual affordability rather than a dashboard metric.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 8) most decisively framed the central clash by showing PRO's rule could not be both real and cheap at once, that vouchers turned sparse requests into optimizing fleet targets, and that cross-subsidy failed without monopoly power—setting up the closing comparison of the two nurses and the "guarantees a number" crystallization.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully framing the debate around the leverage of public permits and the physical reality of algorithmic dispatch. Side A's decisive point—that a voucher is useless if the algorithm refuses to dispatch a car to the neighborhood—effectively undermined Side B's primary alternative. While Side B landed sharp attacks on how wait-time metrics could be gamed, Side A's regulatory response (uniform pricing to prevent zone-shifting) was a plausible and standard non-discrimination fix. Side A's closing effectively crystallized the choice between using permit leverage versus spending taxpayer money to buy back service on public roads.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal to the voucher alternative, arguing that "a voucher for a car that never arrives is worthless," which exposed the physical dispatch flaw in Side B's affordability-focused solution.
