### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO built a coherent externality case (Mirai, race-to-the-bottom, invisible promises) and successfully adapted it to CON's pressure by proposing class-specific minimums, a functional risk test, and support bonds/escrow to answer the insolvency problem. CON's strongest material—bureaucratic complexity, fines not patching orphaned devices, updates≠safety—landed real damage in the middle rounds, but CON's own rebuttal-2 alternative (risk-based market access with enforceable support plans for high-risk devices, disclosure for low-risk ones) left it exposed to PRO's closing pincer: either the alternative covers cheap devices with enforceable support (reinventing PRO's mandate) or it doesn't (leaving the Mirai-style orphan pool intact). CON's closing never answered that collapse, instead re-asserting earlier points, so the decisive late-round clash went unresolved in CON's favor. PRO's answers to the pressure questions were also cleanly formatted and substantive, while CON's alternative, though original, ultimately looked like a relabeled version of the same mechanism it was attacking.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing pincer in A5.1 ("if it doesn't cover cheap devices, the orphan pool remains; if it does, CON has just reinvented PRO's mandate") was the most decisive exchange, and CON's closing failed to rebut it directly.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side A won by establishing an unpriced security externality and defending fines as prospective deterrence rather than an after-the-fact repair tool. Side B identified genuine implementation costs and heterogeneity, but did not show that these outweighed the benefits, and its late risk-based alternative left low-risk devices capable of creating aggregate network harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A decisively answered the claim that fines cannot patch abandoned devices: their purpose is to change the earlier decision to abandon. Side B kept returning to the lack of retrospective repair without neutralizing that ex ante incentive mechanism.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO kept a live externality + deterrence path: cheap devices create aggregate botnet risk that consumers cannot price. CON's breadth and cost objections were answered by class-specific floors and internalization, while CON's own risk-based alternative either re-creates a minimum or leaves low-cost orphan devices that drive Mirai-style harm unaddressed. That aggregation point was never squarely neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Closing clash on who bears cost for orphaned cheap devices – A shows labeling them low-risk does not eliminate aggregate network harm, undermining B's risk-tier alternative and making the all-device floor the only mechanism that prices abandonment ex ante.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): CON built and sustained the sharper structural critique: a "minimum guaranteed period" is a calendar, not a security guarantee, and PRO's own attempts to make it meaningful (signed updates, authenticated servers, "constructive abandonment," and especially the A4.3 promise of replacement/refund/wind-down when suppliers vanish) kept smuggling in a broader quality-and-warranty regime that CON correctly flagged as exceeding the stated motion. CON's regressive-pricing and ecosystem-failure points were never squarely rebutted—PRO's answer that cheap makers "can absorb modest costs... or exit" effectively conceded the market-shrinking effect CON predicted. PRO's timing/externality framing was compelling and well-argued, and its pressure questions landed reasonably well, but its rebuttals conceded more ground than they recovered, while CON's closing crystallized the clash ("the calendar is not the cure; it is the disguise") more cleanly than PRO's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.5 catch that PRO's own answer to supply-chain failure (refund/replacement/wind-down) turns the "minimum update period" into an open-ended warranty regime was the most decisive single exchange, exposing scope creep PRO never fully resolved.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a clear preventive market-failure case and showing why labels and post-harm liability poorly address diffuse, difficult-to-trace harms. Side B raised credible concerns about cost, universal scope, and enforcement, but its central claim that the mandate requires only cosmetic calendar updates strained the proposition’s explicit requirement for security updates. A also directly accepted and justified higher prices as internalizing real support costs, while B never showed that its alternatives would protect users better.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange was its answer that cosmetic patches do not qualify as security updates, paired with the comparison that Side B’s liability regime depends on tracing diffuse botnet harms after damage occurs. This substantially weakened B’s calendar-versus-security framing, although B effectively exposed A’s overreach when A added refunds and replacements beyond the stated mandate.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): CON won the core calendar-vs-security clash. PRO's externality/market-failure case was strong but never solved B's decisive pressure: a minimum period can be met with cosmetic patches, and making it meaningful requires regulators to define critical flaws and patch adequacy [B2.1][B3.1]. PRO's answer that "security updates" plus constructive abandonment covers it [A4.1] conceded sprawling adjudication, and its answer on supply-chain failure that sellers must refund/replace [A4.3] turned the update mandate into a warranty and validated B's regressive-cost claim. B kept the alternative - punishing false supported claims and demonstrable harm - alive as targeting outcome not date.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5/B2.1 exposing that PRO needs quality standards beyond the written mandate: either the rule rubber-stamps cosmetic patches or it becomes an open-ended product guarantee, with exit of cheap makers as regressive cost.
