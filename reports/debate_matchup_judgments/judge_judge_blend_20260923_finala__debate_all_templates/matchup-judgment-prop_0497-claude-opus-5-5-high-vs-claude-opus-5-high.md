### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): PRO won by identifying a critical gap in CON's "permission and retail purchase" alternative: server code is never sold publicly, making online software impossible to preserve without a mandate. CON attempted to neutralize this by arguing that PRO's launch trigger only captures obsolete code and its end-of-service trigger fails when companies go bankrupt. However, PRO effectively countered that solvent publishers frequently shut down live-service games—a major vector of cultural loss that CON left unaddressed. This left PRO with a decisive, unique preservation benefit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's counter to CON's alternative, concisely crystallized in closing as "Permission to copy is worthless for what you can never buy" because "no shelf sells server software."
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by systematically narrowing PRO's claimed unique benefit to a thin marginal gain—launch-day server binaries that miss years of live patches, configs, and operational know-how—while exposing persistent costs that funding cannot fix: third-party licensing collisions, unfunded dark warehouses, and an unenforceable end-of-service duty against bankrupt publishers. PRO's "two trigger" defense and film-deposit precedent were plausible but could not overcome the contract-law ceiling or the "letter to a dead address" enforceability problem, and CON's turn showing PRO's small-creator exemption was really permission in disguise defanged the mandate's reach over digital art.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (A4) was decisive: it collapsed PRO's itch.io exemption into a permission mechanism, showed bankruptcy renders end-of-service duties useless, and narrowed the mandate's entire unique contribution to a static launch binary—leaving PRO with a high-compliance, low-capture regime.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): PRO wins by decisively proving that CON's alternative ("permission plus funding" to buy retail copies) cannot capture essential, non-commercial assets like server code. PRO's trap regarding delisted online games was perfectly executed, and CON's rebuttal that launch server code is just a "first draft" was weak compared to PRO's point that CON's plan yields zero server code. While CON made strong points about the scope of "digital art" and contract collisions, PRO's core mechanism for preserving complex software was far more robust and uniquely solved by the mandate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantle CON's claim that archives can just "buy and preserve digital-only titles while they are on sale" by pointing out that server software is never sold on any shelf, leaving CON's plan with no way to preserve the actual running environment of an online game.
