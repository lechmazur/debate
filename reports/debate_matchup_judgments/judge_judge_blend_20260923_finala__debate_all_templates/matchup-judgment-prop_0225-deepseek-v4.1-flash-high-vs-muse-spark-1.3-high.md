- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won a competitive debate by turning Side B's strongest objections into manageable design constraints: broad employment measures, rolling review, disciplinary floors, appeals, and capacity-sensitive redirection. B's alternative also never supplied an enforceable labor-market constraint when programs persistently produce poor outcomes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal was decisive: it answered the political-discretion question with contestable criteria and answered the capacity question by funding bottlenecks rather than blindly adding seats. This weakened B's claim that redirection necessarily means inaccessible programs, while preserving A's central criticism that B offered disclosure and review without a binding response to persistent mismatch.

PRO had the cleaner opening frame—public subsidy, mismatch debt, shortage capacity—and kept forcing the useful burden that CON’s status quo has no hard stop on low-outcome expansion. But across rebuttals CON made the decisive comparison: PRO’s “correctable cap” is not actually correctable or cleanly deliverable. Metric redefinitions still punish rural/public-service outcomes; rolling review does not reverse lost faculty and programs; and once PRO conceded capacity bottlenecks may require holding funds, the promised redirect became certain loss of flexible seats today for maybe seats tomorrow. PRO’s design add-ons (floors, appeals, capacity-first) absorbed some heat but also showed the simple motion cannot carry without politics and lag. CON’s alternative is softer than ideal, yet better survives the exchange as the less destructive public choice.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s reply to rolling review/reversibility and capacity: gutted departments are not un-gutted, while shortage expansion past clinical/lab limits collapses quality—and PRO’s later “hold funds and wait” concedes the equity trap.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing that PRO's cap-and-redirect mechanism, even with its accumulated safeguards, would destroy flexible access and departmental capacity today while offering only speculative, deferred benefits. PRO's core diagnosis remained vivid, but the operational weight of the debate shifted against the intervention because its benefits became contingent and complex while its harms stayed concrete.

CON turned this into the decisive weighing frame that PRO trades certain, immediate loss of flexible degrees for only possible future capacity.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by sustaining the finite-budget tradeoff and explaining that redirected funding can purchase the instructors, placements, and facilities that shortage programs need. Side B raised serious forecasting, access, and adaptability concerns, but its call to fund bottlenecks directly never adequately answered where that money comes from. Its repeated claim that an English seat cannot become a clinical placement overlooked A’s fungibility argument and the proposition’s explicit funding redirection. A also limited the force of the institutional-destruction objection by defending reviewed caps and retained core departments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.2-A4.3 was decisive: A conceded that cuts do not magically produce nurses, then clarified that the released subsidy buys the actual bottlenecks. This directly exposed the unresolved financing problem in B’s preferred direct-investment alternative.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won the decisive mechanism clash: caps do not convert English seats into clinical placements, instructors, or retention capacity, so the proposition’s causal path fails. PRO pressed finite budgets and first-gen debt strongly, but never showed why rationing contested “low-employment” seats is necessary once direct bottleneck funding is available, while CON better answered rebuild risk, metric contestability, and equity-as-rationing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s repeated dismantling of the clinical-capacity analogy and fungibility claim—especially A2.1/A2.5 and the closing use of B’s own “money” answer—that capping history does not buy a nursing placement.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won by proving that capping enrollment does not actually solve skills shortages: cutting humanities seats does not convert into nursing clinical placements, qualified instructors, or labs, so PRO's redirect was an accounting fiction. CON also showed that employment forecasting is unlike measurable clinical capacity, exposed the regressive caste-like effects of rationing access, and offered a coherent alternative of direct bottleneck investment without caps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's takedown of PRO's clinical-capacity analogy and mechanism gap, showing that capping history does not create nursing placements because bottleneck constraints are specific and non-fungible, which left PRO's core redirect argument without a workable causal link.
