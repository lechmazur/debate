### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): B won by showing that corporate fragmentation does not itself produce genetic diversity: patents, farmer demand, and biological convergence persist, while smaller firms face the same costly research and approval pipelines with less revenue. A compellingly exposed the incentives created by seed-chemical integration, but never adequately solved the financing problem or proved that more owners would put more distinct genetics in fields. B also showed that A still relied on public funding, licensing, and merger controls, making targeted reform comparatively less disruptive. B's two clipped rebuttals reduced the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was its answer that ownership diversity is not genetic diversity, followed by the comparison that both plans require licensing, public breeding, and merger control, while break-up additionally risks thinner pipelines and transition disruption.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively subsuming Side B's mechanisms. When Side B answered pressure questions by pointing to public breeding for innovation and merger control to prevent foreign acquisitions, Side A cleanly demonstrated that Side B's world relies on the exact same regulatory and funding tools Side A proposed, leaving the break-up itself as an unnecessary source of fixed-cost fragmentation and transition shock.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 flawlessly turned Side B's pressure question answers into offense, pointing out that if B relies on merger control and public funding, B is just using A's tools but with added demolition.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by effectively turning Side A's own concessions against it. Side B demonstrated that Side A's break-up plan still relies on the exact same conduct tools (merger control, mandatory licensing, public funding) to prevent Chinese acquisition and foster diversity, but adds the "wrecking ball" of destroying the R&D economies of scale needed for decade-long pipelines. Side B's comparative framing ("Same tools, minus the wrecking ball") crystallized the clash perfectly, while Side A struggled to overcome the arithmetic of fragmented R&D budgets and the reality that farmers still converge on the highest-yielding genetics regardless of corporate logos.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it highlighted that Side A's plan requires merger control to stop fragments from being swallowed by China, exposing that Side A relies on the very conduct tools it dismissed, while adding the collateral damage of broken R&D pipelines.
