B's second rebuttal was also cut off mid-sentence for exceeding the word limit, an explicit execution cost per the judging rules, weakening its ability to fully land its strongest counter before the closing had to do double duty. A's case was slightly more internally consistent across rounds (practice/judgment distinction sustained from opening to closing) and answered pressure more cleanly, giving it a narrow but clear edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 answer (A4.1-A4.2) redefining "main source" as default authority rather than comment volume, combined with B's clipped Rebuttal 2 failing to fully rebut that reframing before the closing, was the most decisive exchange—it left A's framing of the central term less damaged than B's.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by collapsing the artificial boundary between mastery practice and writing feedback, showing that judgment develops through repeated revision cycles rather than deferred authority. Side B never overcomes the scaling reality that restricting AI to mechanics rations substantive feedback into silence, and Side A's reframing—requiring students to dispute AI comments against teacher-set criteria—better protects judgment than sparse human conferences. Side A's "silence kills it" frame and its reversal of the "writing for the algorithm" concern prove more durable after the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A in Rebuttal 1 [A2.3/A2.7] turns Side B's triage proposal against itself, arguing that confining AI to drills leaves most drafts with only mechanics and peer response, effectively producing the algorithmic, risk-averse writers Side B fears.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by grounding the debate in the practical realities of secondary education (teacher overload) and effectively redefining "main source" as the provider of timely feedback during the drafting process. Side A's distinction between "owning the standard" (the teacher) and "providing the feedback" (the AI) dismantled Side B's semantic argument. Side B relied on an idealized, unscalable version of human feedback, and its proposed "triage" conceded that most drafts would receive no human feedback on substance. Side A's closing was exceptionally sharp, and despite a minor clip in Rebuttal 2, its practical framing was decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's attack on Side B's "triage" model, pointing out that restricting AI to mechanics and rationing human feedback to "key moments" means students get no substantive feedback on most drafts, effectively industrializing surface correctness while starving the judgment Side B claims to protect.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases, but A found and sustained the decisive fault line: B's own model requires teachers to lack time for feedback (hence AI must supply volume) while simultaneously requiring teachers to have enough time to spot-check and audit AI comment threads across a class of thirty (to catch the model's "taste for safer structure"). A named this tension explicitly in Rebuttal 1 ("Spot-checking thirty multi-draft threads spends judgment on the model's paperwork") and pressed it again in the pressure questions and Rebuttal 2, and B never resolved it—B's answers (flag uncertainty, offer alternatives, teacher overrides) reassert the audit mechanism without explaining how it scales given the very time scarcity that motivated AI's role in the first place. A also successfully weaponized B's own definitional concession ("main means primary volume, not sole authority") to argue that regardless of the "editor-in-chief" label, the design itself is what the motion prohibits—students revise against the model many times before any human judgment intervenes. B's strongest counter, the equity argument that the alternative is "no reader at all," was met head-on by A's point that the same underserved students are least equipped to distinguish real feedback from AI taste, and B's closing concession ("That is why AI should be main") effectively embraced rather than escaped A's framing. A's Rebuttal 1 was clipped for length, a real execution cost, but the surviving content still carried the key rebuttal work, so the penalty is modest rather than decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's repeated exposure of the internal tension in B's design—teachers too time-constrained to give direct feedback, yet somehow available to audit AI-generated feedback threads at scale—first raised in Rebuttal 1, sharpened via Pressure Q2, and left unanswered through Rebuttal 2, was the most damaging and unresolved exchange of the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by offering a coherent, workable hybrid—AI as the high-volume formative reader under teacher-designed rubrics and spot-check authority—that directly answered PRO’s central fear of students writing for the model. CON’s safeguards (question-form feedback, required student justification, teacher override) were concrete, while their equity argument exposed that PRO’s alternative of fewer essays, rotating conferences, and peer response amounted to rationing that would leave most students, especially those without home editors, with almost no iterative feedback. PRO’s “who shapes the next sentence” frame was pedagogically powerful, but they never resolved the scarcity problem realistically, and CON successfully crystallized the clash around “who gets a second reader before the deadline.”
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal answers to PRO’s pressure questions (A4.1–A4.3), where they proposed constraining AI to question-form prompts, requiring students to log accept/reject decisions, and having teachers spot-check contested AI comments—effectively neutralizing PRO’s claim that AI-as-main-source necessarily trains students to write for the algorithm.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively framing the debate around the practical reality of feedback scarcity in secondary classrooms. By defining "main source" as primary formative volume and forcing Side B to defend a model that requires rationing feedback (fewer essays, peer review), Side A made the equity and pedagogical harms of the proposition clear. Side A also provided concrete safeguards (question-based AI feedback, student justification logs) that blunted Side B's "compliance" offense. Side B's philosophical point about "writing for the model" was strong but ultimately collapsed under the weight of the practical staffing constraints Side A exposed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they answered the pressure questions with concrete pedagogical safeguards (AI asking questions instead of rewriting, requiring student justification) and successfully framed Side B's alternative (fewer essays, peer response) as "honest rationing" that disproportionately harms students without home editors.
