### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by combining irreversible intergenerational risk with a stronger comparative account of scale. Side A showed that bans cannot eliminate offshore editing, but repeatedly treated imperfect prohibition as no governance and never established that licensing would reduce total harm rather than add legitimate infrastructure and normalization. B also better weighed the rare medical benefit against unconsenting descendants and available reproductive alternatives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to the tourism argument was decisive: neither regime controls permissive foreign clinics, but a ban still keeps reputable domestic hospitals, investment, training, and commercialization out. This undercut A’s claim that leakage makes prohibition merely symbolic.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by effectively turning Side B's primary argument about irreversibility. Side A successfully demonstrated that because an edited lineage cannot be recalled, the complete absence of registries, mandatory tracking, and data under a ban is more dangerous than a regulated regime. Side B conceded that a ban wouldn't stop offshore fertility tourism but failed to prove how a domestic ban resolves the irreversible harms to returning lineages better than Side A's proposed governance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which definitively turns the "unrecallable" argument by pointing out that a ban cannot recall an edited child either, making the lack of registries under a ban a fatal flaw.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won a competitive debate by making irreversibility decisive against CON’s “licensed control” path. From the opening through closing, A kept the manufacture-vs-treat and cannot-recall differences live, repeatedly showing that post-birth suspension/registries do not protect the already-created lineage and that authorizing a domestic severe-disease channel still builds tools, staff, and demand that undercut disease/enhancement walls. CON’s tourism/black-market and instruments-of-governance case was strong and well sustained, and both sides answered pressure cleanly, but CON never fully neutralized the boomerang that creating home permission normalizes embryo shopping while still failing to bind offshore clinics. A’s narrower prevention rule therefore left the cleaner reason to affirm the written ban.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2/A4 on irreversibility: suspending licenses after harm does nothing for living unrecallable lineages, collapsing CON’s regulate-like-a-drug control story more than CON’s “ban can’t recall either” reply could repair.
