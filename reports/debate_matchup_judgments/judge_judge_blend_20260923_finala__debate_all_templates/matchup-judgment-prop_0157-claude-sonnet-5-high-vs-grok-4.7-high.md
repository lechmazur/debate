- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. B also preserved a substantial innovation-incentive objection: A showed that compensation and royalties were possible, but the motion did not secure them.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal, where they seized on Side B's claim about recipient liability to argue that a mandate simply forces originators to 'teach badly and walk away' without skin in the game.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won the central clash: CON has no answer for a capable country that is simply refused, exemplified by South Africa's mRNA hub. ### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that manufacturing readiness and a legal transfer duty are complementary rather than alternatives: governments can build capacity, yet firms still need a binding deadline to share usable know-how. Side B raised credible incentive, safety, and qualification concerns, but its claim that the statute becomes decorative once capacity exists overlooked the distinct holdout problem. A also directly answered liability and preserved a plausible early-return window, while B never established that predictable post-authorization sharing would outweigh the harms of prolonged global scarcity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange was against B’s readiness dilemma: pre-qualification addresses competence, while the statute separately prevents firms from withholding technology. B’s concession that no voluntary term would require transfer before rich-market orders were filled reinforced A’s claim that negotiation alone preserves the queue.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by cleanly separating physical capacity-building from proprietary tech transfer, escaping Side A's pre-qualification dilemma. Side B also effectively used the Pressure Questions to force Side A to admit that voluntary deals will always prioritize rich markets first, successfully framing this as a dangerous queue that breeds variants rather than a necessary incentive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing response (B5.2) to Side A's admission (A4.3) that no voluntary contract would force early transfer. Side B brilliantly flipped this to prove that voluntary mechanisms merely defend the queue, necessitating the mandate.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): CON won the decisive clash on feasibility. PRO requires transfer to "qualified" producers with full assistance, which CON showed makes a fixed window either unexecutable (failed batches) or long enough to gut the early-return window it claims to preserve. PRO's fix of pre-qualifying before authorization relocates the whole mandate upstream to voluntary capacity-building, leaving CON's decorative-or-dangerous fork intact. CON also turned PRO's Q2 by admitting no firm would accept early deprioritization of rich markets, which is exactly the R&D incentive chill CON warned about, while PRO never supplied a concrete contract term that would have moved know-how before rich orders without a statute.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 [A2.1-A2.2] and Rebuttal 2 [A4.1-A4.2] dilemma: a clock cannot be simultaneously binding/short and competence-ready; pre-qualification makes the statute decorative if capacity exists voluntarily or dangerous if it doesn't.
