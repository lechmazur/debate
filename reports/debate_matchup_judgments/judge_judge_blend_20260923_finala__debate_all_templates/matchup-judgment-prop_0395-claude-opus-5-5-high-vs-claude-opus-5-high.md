### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): B won a competitive high-level exchange by keeping the motion’s “most occupations / routine tool” burden live and forcing A into either special-case enclaves or paternalistic “mercy termination.” B’s fixed-term vs birthday-clause distinction and “appraisal at every other age” prove-too-much reply repeatedly damaged A’s bargain and dignity frames; A’s labor-economics forks and handshake-vs-file close were sharp but partially clipped and never fully reclaimed ordinary at-will/market jobs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 1–2 sequence: appraisal-as-ordeal proves too much, plus fixed terms/pensions may be bargained but birthday defaults may not—narrowing A to the “sliver” or unrefusable salary-costing “protection.”
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by controlling the scope of the motion. CON's best case rested on back-loaded professional bargains—tenure, partnerships, seniority pay—that make a binding endpoint economically necessary. PRO absorbed this in the opening by conceding that bright lines may survive in narrow, dangerous, or highly specialized roles, then systematically showed that "most occupations" are not structured that way. In Rebuttal 2, PRO crystallized the clash by noting CON had retreated to precisely the sliver PRO already conceded, leaving no defense for the accountant, pharmacist, or lab technician fired by calendar. PRO's distinction between bargained fixed-term endpoints and a default birthday rule, combined with sharp pressure on why the state should coerce workers into retirement "for their own good," exposed CON's paternalism. CON never squaredly answered why a worker who wished to keep contributing should be forbidden from doing so merely to preserve a compensation structure they might not even have, and two clipped speeches compounded the execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (B4), where they exposed CON's retreat to judges, tenured professors, and equity partners as the very sliver PRO had conceded, then demanded CON defend the birthday rule as routine across the ordinary labor market—shifting the burden and collapsing CON's claim to be speaking for "most occupations."
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins by successfully decoupling the economic "back-loaded bargain" (seniority pay, pensions, tenure) from the biological "birthday clause." PRO demonstrated that fixed-term contracts, pension accrual caps, and collective agreements can preserve the workforce structures CON defends without relying on an arbitrary age trigger. CON's defense of the mandatory age line rested heavily on paternalism (sparing older workers from the indignity of performance reviews) and conflated age limits with the need for tenure reform. PRO neatly dismantled this by pointing out that tenure already allows removal for cause, and that objecting to blocked seats is an objection to permanent seats generally, not the occupant's age. PRO's rhetorical framing of "protection you cannot refuse, paid for with your salary" effectively neutralized CON's dignity argument, making the comparative choice clear and decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they clearly distinguished between a defined term (which they accept) and a birthday clause (which they oppose), and dismantled CON's tenure argument by exposing it as an attack on permanent seats rather than a defense of age-based dismissal.
