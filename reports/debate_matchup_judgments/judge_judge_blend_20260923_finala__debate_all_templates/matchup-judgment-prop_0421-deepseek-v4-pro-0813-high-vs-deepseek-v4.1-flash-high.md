- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO built the stronger route to victory by establishing that most fees are already computable before payment and that conditional fees can be disclosed through clear rate or trigger rules without resorting to vague estimates. CON conceded that hiding exact known fees serves no consumer interest, undermining its own position, and never convincingly showed why an earlier rule-based disclosure would mislead rather than inform. PRO's framework of exact-when-computable and explicit-when-conditional dissolved CON's "noise" objection, while the comparison-timing and sunk-cost arguments retained force through the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal (Turn 7), where PRO answered CON's pressure questions by distinguishing exact-dollar disclosure for computable fees from rate-or-trigger disclosure for conditional fees, giving a workable standard that undercut CON's "noise" and "reverse bait-and-switch" objections and sharpened the debate decisively.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): PRO won the comparison-harm clash: mandatory fees are often already computed yet withheld until payment priming. CON conceded no consumer interest in hiding known exact fees [A4.1], and B's A1/A2 answers showed exact-when-computable plus rate/trigger solves conditional-fee worry. CON's redundancy/noise defense leaves false comparison intact, while PRO explains why late exact total cannot retrofit fairness.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 answers to A3 pressure plus B2 dissolution of contingent-fee objection were decisive, turning CON's accuracy attack into support for early exact disclosure.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): PRO built a significantly stronger case by focusing on the search and comparison phase of consumer choice. PRO correctly identified that disclosing the true price only at the final commitment point does not protect the initial comparison process, as consumers filter and select based on the initially listed prices. CON's argument relied on a chronologically confused claim that "exactness at commitment prevents false comparison," ignoring that consumers compare before reaching the final checkout screen. PRO also handled the pressure questions effectively, providing a workable framework for conditional fees, and dismantled CON's objections with clear, grounded reasoning.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing and second rebuttal, where they explicitly pointed out that "a price disclosure that arrives after comparison cannot protect comparison," effectively neutralizing CON's entire "exactness at the pay button" defense.

### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): PRO wins because it established a cleaner comparative framework: disclosure before the final checkout screen allows meaningful price comparison and informed consent, whereas waiting until payment exploits behavioral commitment. CON's strongest objections—fee variability and implementation cost—were blunted by PRO's dynamic-disclosure answer and the point that platforms already compute these charges. CON's defense that buyers can "compare at checkout in two apps" implicitly confirmed PRO's harm (comparison is distorted until very late), and PRO successfully showed that a review screen with updated totals and renewed consent is functionally different from a payment screen. The debate stayed focused, and PRO's central route—transparency as a precondition for honest competition—survived the exchange intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 directly answered CON's pressure questions by distinguishing the review screen from the payment screen, showing dynamic disclosure plus renewed consent neutralizes the price-lock objection, and converting CON's own "compare at checkout" argument into proof that hidden fees systematically distort choice.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO won the central timing-vs-accuracy clash. B showed mandatory charges are knowable after cart/address/seat inputs and before payment, so displaying them on a review screen is feasible dynamic disclosure, not a speculative early estimate. CON's best response – compare totals by reaching checkout in each app – conceded the competition distortion PRO alleged. PRO answered the price-lock pressure with a hold-or-reconsent logic, leaving CON with only implementation anxiety and an unstable “accurate at payment is enough” standard that a third-party reader sees as a trap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B3.1/B4.4 and B2.1-B2.2: Q1 exposing that A2.6 requires checkout-to-compare proves hidden-subtotal advantage, plus the dismantling of variability argument as justification for reserving the first accurate total for payment.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO built a much stronger and more persuasive case by effectively framing the behavioral economics of consumer commitment and the market distortion caused by hidden fees. CON's argument that consumers could simply "reach checkout in multiple apps" to compare prices was a major strategic misstep that PRO expertly exploited to highlight the exact friction and bait-and-switch the proposition seeks to prevent. PRO also cleanly resolved the dispute over app flow by identifying the order review screen as the logical point for disclosure, neutralizing CON's dynamic pricing concerns without conceding to CON's "price-lock mandate" slippery slope.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's dismantling of CON's claim that consumers can just compare final totals by reaching checkout in multiple apps (B4.4 and B5.3). PRO correctly identified that this concession proved the existence of the deceptive friction the proposition aims to solve, effectively turning CON's own defense into proof of the harm.
