### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases without fabricated sourcing, and the debate stayed tightly on the motion throughout. CON's strongest contributions were the "exploit as information, not steel" disanalogy and the accountability/attribution mechanism (a named commercial vendor can be sued, sanctioned, and produces discoverable artifacts a closed state program does not). PRO's strongest contributions were the documented pattern of systematic abuse, the structural-distrust argument, and—critically—the detection-asymmetry point (misuse-based rules can only catch abuse where a free press or independent forensics can expose it, which is precisely absent in the worst regimes) that CON never squarely rebutted. The pivotal exchange came in Pressure Questions: PRO demanded a concrete security benefit from selling to non-democracies, and CON candidly admitted it could not produce one, conceding that its "shared counterterrorism" and "leverage" pillars rest on an unfalsifiable claim. CON's counter-pressure (exposing tension between "substitutes are imperfect" and "cost is nearly empty") drew a decent but somewhat circular answer from PRO ("genuine partners don't need vendors"), and CON's follow-up calling this "no true partner" reasoning was sharp—but it didn't fully recover the conceded benefit-claim gap. PRO's closing crystallized the stakes more memorably ("exposure after the fact is not protection—it is an autopsy") and left CON's substitution defense looking like "someone worse will sell it," a weaker fallback than the original leverage claim. On balance, PRO did slightly more damage to CON's core planks than CON did to PRO's, giving PRO a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question demanding a concrete security benefit from selling spyware to non-democracies, met by CON's honest but damaging concession that it could not name one—paired with PRO's unrebutted detection-asymmetry argument in Rebuttal 2.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A compellingly argued that commercial vendors create attribution and legal leverage, but B showed that imperfect substitution still means a ban raises the cost and lowers the reliability of repression. B also established that a misuse-only rule acts after victims are targeted and is least effective where oversight is weakest. A identified plausible security costs, yet never grounded them well enough to outweigh B's documented abuse and ex ante prevention case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was the imperfect-substitutes dilemma: if alternatives are equivalent, the claimed leverage from licensed sales is weak; if they are inferior, the ban meaningfully degrades autocratic surveillance. A answered that commercial visibility remains valuable, but B persuasively weighed that benefit as delayed and reactive rather than preventive.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): PRO kept two live routes: complicity matters even with substitution, and misuse-only is structurally blind where it matters most because detection requires a free press. CON's core lever defense conceded a non-zero victim tally [B4.1] and could not name a concrete security benefit beyond classified generality [B4.4], while its Mexico over/under-inclusive point was answered by the visibility-asymmetry claim [A4.3]. Raising cost and removing NSO-grade quality for the worst regimes outweighed the "relocation" objection on balance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.3-A2.4 contradiction + A4.3 detection-blindness vs B2/B4 visibility-lever – PRO turned CON's "vendor as pressure point" into post-hoc autopsy and forced CON to defend victims lost while waiting for the lever to move.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but CON (A) landed the more damaging exchanges. A's core move — forcing PRO to admit in the pressure-question answer that the ban's actual mechanism is only marginal degradation (price, freshness, scale) rather than real prevention — let A reframe the whole trade as "surrender all seller-side legal leverage (audits, revocation, subpoenas) for a slightly worse exploit," and PRO never fully neutralized that framing, even in closing. A also pressed effectively on the democracy/accountability line, showing that Spain and Greece remain eligible buyers despite documented abuse, which A repeatedly cast as "punishing geography, not conduct." PRO's defense — that regime type is a proxy for whether corrective institutions (courts, press) can even exist — is intellectually defensible but was asserted rather than sharpened against A's repeated attack, so it read as circular by the closing. PRO's opening and moral framing (silent break-in kits, diffusion/leakage risk, second key to Western phones) were strong and original, and PRO followed the Q&A format cleanly, but A's rebuttals did more concrete work turning PRO's own concessions into leverage. A was clipped twice for length (Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2), a real execution cost, but the substance delivered before each cutoff was still complete enough to land the key points, so the penalty is modest rather than decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 turning PRO's pressure-question answer ("mechanism is price, freshness, scale") into "the entire benefit of this motion is a somewhat higher cost per intrusion, purchased at the price of eliminating every legal handle on sellers" was the pivotal exchange; PRO's closing response reasserted the value of degradation but did not resolve the leverage-cost tradeoff A had exposed.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that a ban need not eliminate every exploit to reduce repression: removing lawful financing, maintenance, training, and fresh exploits raises costs and reduces the scale and reliability of attacks. Side A offered plausible licensing leverage and vendor lock-in, but never established that Western sales displace Chinese or gray-market tools rather than add another capability, nor that opaque end-use conditions meaningfully restrain authoritarian buyers. B also defended regime type as a relevant proxy for institutional accountability. A's two clipped rebuttals added an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's answer to A's “who is protected?” challenge was decisive: the marginal journalist or exile benefits when turnkey zero-click tools become costlier, slower, and less reliable. This exposed A's repeated assumption that anything short of eliminating all spyware counts as no benefit.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): PRO kept a live path: ban removes the legal, financeable, maintained tier, raising price, degrading freshness and scale, protecting the marginal journalist/exile even if tools still arrive. CON's leverage claim was undermined by PRO's pressure showing the cited democratic scandals were exposed by press/courts, not licenses, and revocation can't unwind infection. CON's China lock-in and line-drawing points had force but did not show Western sales displace Beijing rather than add a second key, and the accountability proxy (investigation possible vs impossible) survived.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Q1/R2 deconstruction of B's visibility argument — that licensing didn't produce Catalonia/Predator exposure while still claiming a ban destroys needed visibility, reframed as "permission with a letterhead" vs "prohibition without eyes."
