### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Both sides fought the genuinely hard question—whether "even when it reduces local review and litigation" is a defensible tradeoff or a confession that core protections become optional. A built a clear mechanism: mandatory surveys stay a precondition for permit issuance, a narrow post-decision challenge can still vacate a defective approval, and what dies is only the indefinite, duplicative reopening of a closed record. B's strongest counter was that the agency defining "required" and "complete" controls what the record ever sees, so the backstop is hollowed out from within. This is a real point, but A directly answered it in R2 (A4.3-A4.4) by tying the survey's adequacy to enforceable conditions of approval with a live remedy (remand/vacatur) rather than just a filing date, and B's closing repeats the "they define required" line without fully engaging that the same complaint (lead agency capture) would undermine B's own preferred staffing/coordination fix just as much. A's pressure questions forced B into a genuine tension between B2.6 (delay isn't the cause of coal burning) and B2.9 (a well-sited line next year displaces coal) — B's answer that "both survive" because litigation-caused delay is about bad siting, not endless review, is coherent but doesn't fully dissolve the tension A kept pressing in R2 and closing: if any objection can reopen the file indefinitely, there is no "next year." B never cleanly explains how a finality point can exist under its model. A's closing crystallizes this asymmetry more sharply than B's, which restates the theater/backstop framing without resolving the endpoint problem. Both sides avoided fake citations and stayed appropriately grounded; B's clipped opening is a minor execution cost. Overall a close, high-quality debate, but A's tighter closing loop on the finality/endpoint question gives it a clear, if not overwhelming, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2 response (A4.1-A4.4) answering B's pressure question about who enforces reject/reroute/compensate — tying mandatory surveys to enforceable conditions of approval with vacatur/remand as the remedy — combined with A's closing exploiting the unresolved tension between B2.6 and B2.9, was the most decisive exchange, since B's closing never fully closed that gap.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A showed that indefinite local review defaults to fossil incumbency, offered a coherent limiting principle—mandatory surveys plus bounded litigation—and exposed B’s unresolved tension between saying process is not the blocker and admitting a well-sited line next year would displace coal. B’s local-knowledge fears were real but never overcame A’s status-quo asymmetry or explained how endless review ever reaches a decision. A’s closing crystallized that a rule unable to finish is theater, and the dirtier default finishes instead.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7), answering B’s pressure questions and reframing the clash: a constraint that cannot close is not environmental protection but a win for the incumbent fossil plant.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a superior weighing mechanism and successfully defending the motion's "even when" clause. A's central frame—that infinite, overlapping review acts as a veto that defaults to the status quo of fossil fuel emissions—was highly compelling and consistently applied. While Side B effectively attacked the "even when" clause by arguing that reduced litigation pathways would render environmental constraints unenforceable, A decisively answered this in Rebuttal 2 by clarifying that streamlining removes duplicative, open-ended vetoes while preserving mandatory surveys and timely, narrow challenges. A's rhetoric was also sharper, with memorable crystallizations like "A protection that never becomes a decision protects the plant already running" and "An open objection is not a siting standard." Side B argued well but ultimately conceded too much on the cost of delay and struggled to resolve the tension in their own claims about why coal plants keep burning.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they directly and cleanly answered Side B's pressure questions by distinguishing between a mandatory survey with a timely challenge (which survives streamlining) and an open-ended, overlapping local veto (which is cut), effectively neutralizing B's core attack on the "even when" clause.
