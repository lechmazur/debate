### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B won by establishing a principled distinction between training and the compulsory-license analogies A relied upon. Covers, broadcasts, and photocopies reproduce or deliver protected expression; training, on B's account, extracts unprotected patterns without retaining or delivering that expression. A showed that a revenue-scaled scheme could be administered, but did not adequately prove that creators rather than catalog owners would meaningfully benefit or that value extraction alone justified creating the new right. B's clipping and late voluntary-market claim were costs, but not enough to overcome its stronger core case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response to the sampling analogy was decisive: sampling can allocate royalties for an already defined, countable performance right, but it cannot establish that training is itself a copyright use. B then leveraged A's admission that the payment rule must be invented and exposed the unresolved gap between a revenue levy and meaningful payment to individual creators.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's proposed mechanism and proving that a compulsory license would fail to achieve A's own goals. Side B effectively argued that without a traceable unit of expression being delivered, pooled revenues would inevitably be distributed by catalog size, thus enriching the major incumbents Side A originally sought to disempower.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal expertly caught the contradiction in A's "revenue-based" pivot, pointing out that without tracing (which A conceded was impossible), distribution must default to catalog size, leaving small creators with the pennies A promised to avoid.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side A wins by decisively dismantling Side B's core mechanism. Side B relied heavily on analogies to blanket performance and mechanical licenses, arguing that collective sampling can solve the problem of uncountable uses. Side A brilliantly countered that those licenses still sample countable units of "delivery" or "copies" (plays, pressings), whereas training extracts patterns without any delivery or copy, leaving a denominator of zero. When Side B pivoted in Rebuttal 2 to a "percentage of revenue" tax, Side A correctly pointed out this conceded the impossibility of sampling and merely created a toll that flows to major catalog owners. Side A's conceptual distinction between protected expression (which is priced upon delivery) and unprotectable patterns (which are learned) was rigorous, legally grounded, and effectively neutralized Side B's "floor beats a zero" rhetoric. Although both sides suffered minor clipping at the ends of their speeches, Side A's core arguments were fully delivered and conceptually superior.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it answered Side B's pressure question on sampling by explaining that sampling requires a unit to sample (a play or a copy), and since training involves no delivery or retention of expression, the denominator is zero, exposing Side B's pivot to a revenue tax as a concession.
