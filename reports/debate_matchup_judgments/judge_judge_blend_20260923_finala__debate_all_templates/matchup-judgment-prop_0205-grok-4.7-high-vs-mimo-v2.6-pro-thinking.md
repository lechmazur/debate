### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases, but A maintained a cleaner throughline: a narrow remedy (neutral eligibility bar plus non-badged, non-self-assigned placement) that repeatedly answered B's security, permission-mandate, and "barely formed category" objections without conceding the underlying harm. A's pressure questions forced B to admit that ordering and rule-writing are unavoidable, and A's Rebuttal 2 explicitly converted that admission into support for its own distinction between "writing the bar" and "filling the ballot" — a distinction B never fully dislodged. B's strongest weapon, the claimed contradiction between "defaults are market-sealing" and "rivals can still win," was a clever pressure point, but A's closing explanation (stickiness justifies removing self-assignment, not denying that a fairly-earned slot can still be beaten) defused it more than B's final rebuttal acknowledged. B's closing "redistribution wearing the costume of neutrality" line was memorable and the integration-loss argument was real, but it rested on the unresolved assumption that neutral/randomized placement necessarily degrades certified quality, which A had already answered by keeping the same eligibility bar in place. Both rebuttal speeches were clipped for length, an even execution cost, but A's structural clarity and more decisive handling of the pressure-question exchange gave it the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.6), where it answered both of B's pressure questions directly (A1/A2) and then reframed B's own questions as conceding that "someone will be first and someone will write the rules," turning B's strongest pressure point into support for A's bar/ballot distinction.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): Side A wins because it maintained a coherent structural-conflict framework—leveraging OS control into downstream markets through sticky defaults—while showing the remedy was narrow (choice screens, not bans). A successfully absorbed B’s integration and security objections by arguing that objective eligibility bars preserve those benefits without preselection, and answered pressure questions more directly. B’s attempt to paint A with a self-contradicting inertia theory mischaracterized A’s position, and B’s closing wrongly claimed A never identified a consumer benefit, overlooking A’s sustained argument about merit-based competition and reduced leverage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over whether a security and integration bar could replace preselection. A showed that hardware binding, fraud liability, and privacy-stack requirements could be written as neutral rules applying to all, so no safety benefit required crowning the platform’s product. B never explained why an objective bar could not capture the same integration standards, leaving its primary consumer-welfare defense undermined.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): PRO maintained a clean separation between setting a security bar and occupying the default slot. Its answers to pressure – platform still writes the bar [A4.1], randomize/neutral ordering with no paid placement [A4.4] – neutralized CON's vacuum/regulator and lottery objection. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): This was a tightly matched, substantively engaged debate, but A ultimately controlled the decisive clash: whether switching friction is genuine security or disguised self-dealing. A's opening grounded the case in a familiar, plausible mechanism (default bias, gatekeeper self-preferencing) analogous to real-world remedies like mandated choice screens and third-party wallet/NFC access, giving it strong real-world traction. In the pressure round, A's Q1 forced B into a concession-shaped corner ([B2.2]'s "any chooser preferences its designer"), and B's answer in rebuttal 2 that the standard is "a clear confirmation for any change of handler" was exploited devastatingly in A's closing: that standard cannot distinguish a certified rival from malware, and it never fires for the incumbent precisely because it is already the default — exposing the asymmetry as status-quo protection rather than risk-based security. B never supplied a symmetric, risk-based account to rebut this, instead reasserting that "nothing is changing in factory settings," which effectively concedes A's point. B's own pressure questions (on secure-element access and the "fallback is still a default" problem) landed well and pushed A to admit the remedy requires more than a settings-screen tweak, straining A's "narrow" framing — a real and unresolved cost for A. But this scope-creep critique, while damaging, is less fatal than A's unanswered asymmetry point, since forcing genuine handler access is consistent with how real regulatory wallet-access mandates already work. A's case did suffer an execution penalty from being clipped twice (opening and rebuttal 2), losing some concluding force, but the surviving content still carried the stronger comparative weight into closing. B fought hard and exposed a genuine tension in A's case, but did not fully neutralize A's core "asymmetric toll" argument, leaving A with the cleaner path to the vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing exploitation of B's "clear confirmation for any change of handler" standard — showing it fails to distinguish a certified rival from malware and never burdens the incumbent — was the most decisive exchange, and B's response ("not about who profits," "nothing changes in factory setting") effectively conceded the asymmetry rather than refuting it.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): PRO won by proving that CON's defense of default friction collapses into self-dealing. CON conceded that any change of default handler triggers a warning regardless of certification, which PRO crystallized as a control that tracks incumbent benefit, not risk. PRO also showed that CON's objection to any chooser—claiming it "preferences" its designer—would outlaw every external remedy and permanently enthrone the gatekeeper. CON raised real concerns about integration and fallback defaults, but could not explain why a working fallback plus a real chooser is worse than the status quo, nor escape the asymmetry trap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over PRO's pressure question about what concrete standard lets a vetted rival avoid the scare prompt. CON answered that any change of handler requires confirmation, and PRO weaponized this in the closing to show the warning is a toll on rivals rather than a genuine security control.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): PRO's moat vs security frame was strong, but CON landed the structural hit that PRO never resolved: if you bar a default, you still need a handler when the buyer skips, and A4.1's 'fallback not a protected default' renames the default while keeping vendor preferencing in that slot. Coupled with B4's transfer-of-trust argument and showing A4.3 expands the rule beyond settings into secure-element/system context, CON made easy reversible switching look like the narrow, workable remedy while PRO's categorical bar collapsed into either a chooser that still has an author (B2.2) or a disguised default.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4/B5 contradiction: fallback is still a default, so motion cannot both ban defaults and keep a working phone, plus requiring secure-element access makes the 'narrow settings rule' expand.
