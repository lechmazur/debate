### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): CON built and sustained the more damaging structural critique: that forfeited money is not removal capability, and that PRO's mechanism requires forfeitures to be simultaneously rare (proving deterrence) and frequent/large enough to fund government removal fleets — a tension PRO never squarely resolved, including in its closing. CON also turned PRO's strongest scenario (unforeseeable technical failure) against it, showing a bond does nothing to move a stranded satellite while offering a concrete counter-mechanism (pre-contracted removal servicers, redundancy mandates). PRO's opening framing (tragedy of the commons, bond analogies) was solid, and its pressure question on technical failure was sharp, but its rebuttals leaned on assertion ("forfeitures fund removal capacity") without closing the scale/consistency gap CON kept exposing. PRO also lost content to repeated word-limit clipping across three speeches, including the tail of its strongest rebuttal-2 push, which is a real execution cost. CON's closing crystallized the decisive clash cleanly ("prices what it cannot prevent and funds what it cannot perform"), while PRO's closing largely restated earlier claims without answering the unresolved contradiction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's repeated pressing of the deterrence-vs-collection contradiction (B2.1, sharpened in B4.7 and the closing) was the pivotal exchange: PRO's attempt to resolve it as "dual logic" (A4.1-A4.3) never explained how rare forfeitures could fund large-scale removal capacity, leaving PRO's central mechanism internally unsettled through the final speech.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's mechanism. A exposed a fatal flaw in B's funding logic: if forfeitures are rare, they cannot generate enough capital to cover $100M removal missions, build government fleets, and cover shortfalls simultaneously. A also successfully distinguished satellite bonds from terrestrial mining bonds, proving why the analogy fails.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 expertly distinguished the mining analogy by pointing out that terrestrial cleanup is estimable and procurable, whereas orbital removal is neither, and trapped PRO in a mathematical paradox regarding rare forfeitures funding massive shortfalls.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): CON won by collapsing PRO’s mechanism at the decisive points: forfeiture money does not deorbit a dead satellite; deterrence-rare and cleanup-fund-frequent cannot both be true; and bonds fit mining/construction only where cleanup is local, estimable, and procurable—none of which holds for orbital debris. PRO’s strongest live route (post-launch thruster failure escapes licensing) was answered: neither tool recovers the object retroactively, but ex-ante servicer contracts/redundancy via licensing are preventive in a way a post-failure receipt is not. PRO’s dual-function defense stayed standard and never resolved the contradiction; several PRO turns were also clipped. Comparative weighing favored CON’s “prices what it cannot prevent / funds what it cannot perform.”
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s rare-vs-frequent forfeiture contradiction (A2.1–A2.2, A4.7, A5.3) paired with the dead-satellite scenario: a treasury deposit leaves the hazard aloft while still requiring incompatible forfeiture rates.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases, but PRO ultimately controlled the decisive clash. PRO's dilemma framing—either the bond deters risky engineering or, failing that, still funds cleanup—answered CON's "cost of doing business" pressure question more cleanly than CON answered PRO's "where does the money come from" question. CON's strongest weapon, the "no institutional capability to deploy bond funds" objection, was met with a plausible (if not airtight) answer: the licensing/flag-state authority that already exists is the natural obligor, and escrow demand can catalyze a servicing market, paralleling how mining bonds funded reclamation industries. CON never fully neutralized this, instead repeating the "vacuum" framing without engaging the demand-creates-supply logic. Meanwhile PRO's closing landed a sharp, unanswered point: CON's own fallback of "pooled licensing fees" re-creates exactly the externality-socialization CON spent the debate condemning, just more diffusely and with less individual accountability. CON's regressive-burden and legacy-debris arguments were real but were substantially blunted by PRO's tiering/refundability response and by the reasonable point that the motion concerns future operators, not historical polluters. Both sides suffered clipping penalties roughly equally, so that did not decide the outcome. Overall PRO's rebuttals did more comparative work and left CON's alternative looking structurally similar to (or weaker than) the bond it was meant to replace.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing point that CON's "pooled licensing fees" alternative reproduces the very cost-socialization CON criticized bonds for, combined with PRO's answer to the Q2 pressure question framing the bond as a win-win (deters bad engineering or funds cleanup regardless), was the most decisive exchange CON never fully rebutted.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side B won by cleanly exposing the contradiction in Side A's alternative. Side A argued that bonds are useless because no servicing market exists to spend them, yet proposed funding remediation through insurance and pooled fees, which would face the exact same barrier. Side B's argument that escrowed funds pull that very market into existence resolved this, and B correctly identified that A's pooled fees merely rebuild the externality by socializing the cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing correctly identifying that Side A's reliance on 'pooled licensing fees' simply socializes the cost of retirement onto competitors and the public, rebuilding the exact externality the prompt aims to solve.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): PRO won a competitive debate by keeping the decisive comparison live: when retirement plans fail, who pays? Its externality frame, tiering answer to regressivity, and plans-vs-money clash outlasted CON’s alternatives. CON’s best pressure—the “posts-into-a-vacuum” jurisdiction and servicing-path attack—was real and memorable, and insurance/standards/pooled fees were a coherent package, but those alternatives either socialize costs or still leave a funding hole after hardware fails. PRO answered the Pressure Questions more directly (flag-state obligor + design-stage capital lock-in) and crystallized “what follows failure” cleanly in the closing. Clipping hit both sides; the edge is PRO’s cleaner route to a decision, not a blowout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s live comparison that approved plans still fail and only a forfeited bond leaves working capital (vs CON’s insurance/standards leaving remediation unfunded or socialized)—especially after answering the vacuum/jurisdiction pressure with the flag-state permitting analogy.
