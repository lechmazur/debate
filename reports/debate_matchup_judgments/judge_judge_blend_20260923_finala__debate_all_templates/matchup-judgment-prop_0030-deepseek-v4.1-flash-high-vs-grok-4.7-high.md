### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued a sophisticated methodological debate about how to construct the counterfactual, but PRO's case rested on concrete, verifiable mechanisms (customs friction, lost passporting and professional recognition, investment uncertainty, labor shortages) that it sustained and sharpened across rounds, including a direct and effective rebuttal to CON's opening claim that Brexit barely touches services (passporting/recognition/data adequacy are precisely services-sector harms). CON's strongest contribution was the "freeze is asymmetric" critique — that holding domestic policy constant lets PRO ignore EU-membership costs while charging exit for friction — which is a clever original point, but CON never converted this into a concrete demonstration that Britain is economically stronger today; it mostly asserted that changed "legal defaults" (tariff independence, ending contributions, points-based hiring) are themselves results, without showing realized output gains. PRO's closing crystallized this asymmetry well: "power is not a result," and "the motion is present-tense." The Pressure Questions exchange was well-executed on both sides with proper A1/A2 answers, but PRO's answers more directly reinforced its own framing while exposing that CON's "in-force offsets" remain unproven in outcome terms. Overall PRO built the more concrete, harder-to-rebut path to victory, while CON's framing critique, though intellectually sharp, stayed theoretical.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 response to CON's "services are shallow in the single market" claim — pointing to lost passporting, professional recognition, and data adequacy as friction precisely in Britain's strong sector — combined with CON's Rebuttal 2 counter that this "freeze" methodology asymmetrically ignores tariff/contribution/migration liabilities Britain would still bear if it stayed, forms the decisive exchange; PRO's final answer that CON offers only unrealized "power" rather than a realized productivity mechanism tipped this clash in PRO's favor.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by controlling the counterfactual framework and keeping the debate present-tense. PRO identified concrete, operating frictions—new goods and services barriers, investment uncertainty, and labor shortages—that are traceable to Brexit. CON’s strongest reply, that EU membership imposed structural mismatches on a services economy and that autonomy itself is a gain, was blunted when PRO showed Brexit added services friction rather than removing it, and when PRO framed CON’s autonomy as an unrealized option that cannot outweigh realized drag. CON’s recurring “convenience is not strength” frame was memorable but never fully answered PRO’s methodological pressure: under the same domestic policy baseline, the exit wedge is visible friction without a visible offset.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 1 turn on services ([B2.1]–[B2.2]), which converted CON’s “Britain is a services economy” pivot into a liability by showing that Brexit added passporting, recognition, and data barriers exactly where Britain is strong.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A won by strictly enforcing the present-tense burden of the motion ("stronger today"). A effectively contrasted the realized, ongoing frictions of Brexit (trade barriers, labor shortages, investment chill) against the unrealized, hypothetical benefits of regulatory autonomy. While Side B made a strong structural case about the retired liabilities of EU membership and the mismatch of the single market for a services economy, A's closing crystallization—that options and legal defaults do not equal current economic output—decisively resolved the weighing clash in PRO's favor. Side A also handled the pressure questions more cleanly, neutralizing B's attempt to make the counterfactual comparison impossible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's handling of Side B's "freedom unused" and "change in legal default" arguments. A sharply pointed out that while unbanked deregulation might be a ministerial choice, the present-tense motion dictates that hypothetical future gains and mere legal options cannot outweigh present, realized economic drag ("The forms are here; the gains are not").

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides argued well, but A built a tighter, more decisive comparative case. A's core move—isolate the one variable that actually differs (border friction) once both sides conceded domestic weaknesses (housing, skills, investment) are common to either path—gave the debate a clean logical structure that survived pressure. A answered the Q&A round in proper format and gave a plausible magnitude comparison (small budget contributions vs. large trade-friction costs on the deepest market), directly engaging B's demand for a net calculation. B's reframing was rhetorically sharp ("headwind, not engine failure"; "one variable changed") and its pre-2016 baseline point (access already existed yet growth was weak) was a genuinely strong challenge, but A's response—that this shows access wasn't a boom machine without showing that removing an added friction fails to matter at the margin—adequately blunted it. B's closing ultimately rested on "may not be proof," effectively conceding it could not establish that membership costs equaled or exceeded the conceded Brexit headwind, leaving A's directional claim (headwind conceded, other factors equal, so no-Brexit is stronger) as the more resolved position going into the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 turn (A2.1–A2.6), which seized on B's own "headwind" concession and reframed the entire debate around isolating that single variable once domestic weaknesses are held constant—later reinforced in the A1/A2 pressure-answers comparing magnitudes of membership costs versus trade friction—was the most decisive exchange, since B never fully rebutted the "common factors cancel out" logic, only contested whether the remaining friction was large enough to matter.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by isolating the comparative question more cleanly: domestic stagnation exists on both paths, so the decisive difference is the self-imposed border friction on Britain’s largest market. Side A (CON) raised valid net-comparison and pre-2016 baseline objections, but never proved that membership costs and lost autonomy outweigh the conceded headwind; their counterarguments remained diffuse and speculative. Side B’s framing made the burden and the weighing clearer to a third-party reader.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 directly answering Side A’s pressure questions by showing membership costs (net contributions, common external tariff) are smaller than the conceded Brexit headwind, while isolating cross-border friction as the unique, persistent drag on a shared domestic baseline.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a clean, decisive comparative framework: "cancel what is common." By conceding that domestic drags (housing, skills, etc.) exist on both paths, Side A isolated Brexit friction as the decisive marginal difference. Side A effectively neutralized Side B's pre-2016 argument—that single market access didn't previously cause an investment boom—by pointing out that proving access isn't a magic cure does not mean imposing a permanent border tax on top of existing weaknesses leaves output unchanged. Side B struggled to prove that EU membership costs actually outweighed Brexit friction, relying instead on burden-shifting and demanding PRO prove a "boom," which was a strawman of PRO's actual marginal claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to the pre-2016 underinvestment argument. Side A clarified that proving single-market access wasn't a "boom machine" before 2016 does not prove that stacking a permanent border tax on those same domestic weaknesses leaves output unchanged today, effectively collapsing Side B's main defensive pillar.
