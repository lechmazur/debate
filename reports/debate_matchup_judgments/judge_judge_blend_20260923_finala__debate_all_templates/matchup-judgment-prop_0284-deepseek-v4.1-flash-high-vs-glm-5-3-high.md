### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but B built the more durable structural attack. B's key move—forcing A to choose between a "vague notice that changes nothing" and a "full disclosure regime that guts beneficial discrimination"—was answered by A only by introducing a new tiered apparatus (public notice + on-request explanation + audit access) not present in A's opening "simple notice" framing. B seized on this as a mid-debate remedy shift, and further exploited A's own concession ("some behavioral extraction dies... own that") to show that A's rule would strip access from exactly the low-income, undocumented buyers A claimed to protect. A never squarely rebutted this regressive-effects charge in the closing, instead restating the consent/asymmetry framing without engaging the "what dies" critique. B also lost some ground to a format violation (clipped opening), which is a real execution cost, but its core arguments survived and were reinforced in later rounds, while A's late remedy adjustment created a genuine vulnerability that B converted into the decisive closing frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1-B4.4), which caught A's shift from "simple notice" to a three-layer disclosure/audit regime and tied it to A's own concession that "behavioral extraction dies," turning A's efficiency defense of low-income drug pricing against A's own proposed remedy.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully exposing the structural flaws in Side A's remedy. B cornered A into choosing between a useless generic notice or a heavy regulatory apparatus, and then weaponized A's shift to a 'tiered regime' to show A was abandoning its original lightweight stance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 brilliantly dismantled A's answers to the pressure questions, using A's 'fix eligibility' line to prove that A's rule would destroy the very algorithmic access benefits B was defending.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO maintained a live route: material-term consent requires knowing your price was personalized; public discounts survive because eligibility is verifiable, covert willingness-to-pay extraction does not. CON's strongest access argument was blunted by tiered disclosure (public notice + private on-request explanation + audit), which answers the vague-vs-full dilemma and mitigates gaming, while CON's alternative left discovery to after-the-fact testers and subpoenas. On the central same-seat-same-moment clash, PRO made the choice clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's same-moment comparison (same seat/fare/class charged differently by device/history is not a different good) plus tiered-disclosure answer to A3 forced CON to defend secrecy-dependent pricing rather than public low-income tiers.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): A built a consistent, easy-to-apply distinction (visible/rule-based vs. hidden/individualized pricing) from opening through closing, and used it to turn nearly all of B's own examples (student fares, surge pricing, loyalty discounts) into confirmations of the PRO framework rather than counterexamples. Crucially, A's clarification that "disclosure" means disclosure to the buyer, not publication to competitors, directly defused B's strongest and most original objection—that a disclosure/ban default would kill quiet competitive discounting by exposing prices to rivals. B's pressure questions were sharp and forced a genuine concession (A4.1: "very little harm remains... for most transactions"), and B's closing attempt to redefine the harm as "inability to comparison-shop" rather than mere ignorance of personalization was a legitimate return to the original clash, but it arrived without A having a further chance to respond, and A had already partially pre-empted it with the "opt out, time purchase, or walk" mitigation argument. On balance A's case hung together more tightly across the whole debate and better survived cross-examination, while B's dilemma framing, though clever, was substantially answered rather than left standing. Two clipped A speeches are a real execution cost but did not remove load-bearing content, since A's key moves (the visible/hidden distinction, the buyer-only disclosure clarification, the backstop-ban structure) were all delivered before the cuts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer that the motion's disclosure prong means disclosure "to the buyer," not publication to rivals, which directly neutralized B's central "quiet discount/incumbent protection" objection built across Rebuttal 1, Pressure Questions, and Rebuttal 2.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically exposing a fatal contradiction between Side A's stated harm and its proposed remedy. Side A argued the harm of algorithmic pricing is the inability to comparison-shop against a public price, but eventually retreated to defining "disclosure" as merely telling the buyer the inputs or letting them see their own quote. Side B correctly pointed out this fails to restore the comparison baseline, meaning the rule either does nothing or bans pro-competitive discounting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing speech decisively highlighted Side A's shifting definition of disclosure, noting that if merely seeing your own quote counts as "disclosed," it completely abandons Side A's foundational complaint about the loss of a public comparison signal.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won the disclosure definition fight. PRO defined disclosure as telling the buyer the practice/inputs and later as the buyer knowing her own price, which CON showed is either toothless versus PRO's own comparison-shopping harm or trivially satisfied by any sale. PRO conceded "very little harm remains" after such disclosure and that the "quiet discount" gets caught, letting CON frame the motion as empty notice or a ban that kills pro-competitive entry/retention discounts that help price-sensitive buyers — leaving PRO's proxy/discrimination point as a narrow tail, not the usual case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4's A1/A2 - showing disclosure collapses to a label CON accepts and ban then must ban efficiency-enhancing secret discounting, forcing PRO's B4 bullet-bite.
