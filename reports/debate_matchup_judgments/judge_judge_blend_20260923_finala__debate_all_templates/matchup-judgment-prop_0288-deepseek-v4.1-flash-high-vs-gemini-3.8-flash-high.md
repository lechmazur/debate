B's honest A1/A2 concession that "no pre-suspension human judgment applies" in emergencies was a bold, format-compliant answer, but it effectively conceded the core proposition violation and left B relying entirely on "temporary and correctable" framing. A exploited this by pointing out that suspension harms (missed dialysis, eviction, stranded travelers) are not fully remediable by retroactive payment — a concrete asymmetry B never directly rebutted with comparable specificity. B's opening "circuit breaker" framing was vivid and its industrial-fraud scenario was a genuine pressure point, but across rebuttals B failed to adapt to A's refined middle-ground position, making its later speeches repetitive rather than progressive. A's closing crystallized the clash more cleanly ("evidence versus automated suspicion") than B's closing, which mostly recycled the earlier dilemma.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 catching the tension between A's original strict definition of "meaningful human review" (individualized file/context review) and A's later claim that an analyst can clear a flagged batch "in minutes" was the sharpest single exchange — but A's Rebuttal 2 answer (evidence-based triage distinguishing batch-level corroborating evidence from both rubber-stamping and individualized audits) neutralized it, and B never adequately re-engaged that synthesis afterward.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but CON identified and exploited the decisive structural ambiguity in the motion: does "without meaningful human review" require review strictly prior to any suspension, or can it be satisfied by a brief, bounded hold followed by prompt review? CON built a concrete alternative mechanism (short automatic hold, hard deadline, automatic reinstatement, back pay, hardship advances) that plausibly satisfies due-process concerns while still stopping fraud that "operates in minutes, not months." PRO never solved the practical problem this raises: when pressed in the Pressure Questions round, PRO's own answer (A4.1) conceded that its rule forbids even brief holds, without offering any working mechanism to stop money or a fraudulent visa from completing during the review window — its remedy of "flag for rapid inspection" doesn't explain how the transaction is actually paused. CON, by contrast, directly and honestly answered PRO's toughest question (conceding it cannot guarantee post-hold review will be timely) but pivoted persuasively to comparative reversibility and procedural safeguards, blunting the force of PRO's strongest asymmetry argument (that back pay cannot undo eviction or missed care) by showing fraud losses are often just as unrecoverable and harm third-party claimants. PRO's closing recycled its irreversibility point without re-engaging the fraud-in-minutes mechanics problem CON had exposed, leaving CON's practical case for a middle path unrebutted at the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1/2 sequence (B2.1–B2.9, reinforced by A1/A2 answers) turning PRO's "irreversible harm" framing back on itself by showing fraud losses are frequently unrecoverable too, combined with PRO's own admission (A4.1) that its rule bans even brief protective holds without a workable substitute for stopping active fraud.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO successfully defended the absolute nature of the motion by demonstrating that even temporary suspensions impose irreversible human harms—eviction, food insecurity, missed medical care—that back pay cannot remedy. CON's concession that back pay often fails to undo these harms [A4.4] collapsed their "reversible pause" framing and fatally undermined their comparative weighing. While CON mounted a practical challenge about fraud rings and fiscal losses, they never fully overcame PRO's core asymmetry: the state should absorb financial risk rather than force vulnerable individuals to absorb survival risk based on unverified algorithmic flags. PRO's sharper moral framing, more damaging rebuttals, and sustained pressure on the irreversibility of deprivation provided the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's first rebuttal [B2.1-B2.8], where they reframed CON's "temporary protective hold" as an immediate human crisis, established that post-suspension review does not satisfy the motion's requirement, and crystallized the decisive clash around who bears the risk of algorithmic error.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO kept the motion's bright line clear and won the risk-asymmetry clash. CON reframed suspension as a reversible hold but conceded in A4.4 that back pay cannot undo eviction/missed care, which PRO turned into a decisive moral cost in B5. CON's mitigation (hard clock, auto-reinstatement, hardship advances) was late and still admitted no guarantee of timely post-hold review, failing to answer B3.1. A's strong fraud-in-minutes point showed practical strain but did not neutralize PRO's core that lifelines should not be severed by an unverified statistical guess before any human checks the facts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q2/B5 leverage of A4.4 — using CON's own admission that back pay doesn't undo eviction to collapse the 'reversible pause beats irreversible payout' framing.
