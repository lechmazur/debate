### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON won a competitive debate by keeping the motion's dual burden live: "much more" plus an actual shift from visible defense. PRO built the stronger systemic case that cables are a force-multiplier and that ordinary rerouting is not wartime resilience, but it repeatedly soft-pedaled magnitude into "marginal" dollars and then named a frigate hull as the cut. CON turned that answer: the named platform is itself a cable-protection/sea-control asset, while route diversity, landing hardening, and repair contracts can be compelled by licensing without a procurement raid. Regulation-plus-deterrence therefore undercut the need for the motion as written more cleanly than PRO restored it. Clipped CON turns cost polish and completeness, but the live comparative path still favored CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2/Closing strike on PRO naming a frigate cut: escorts are the scarce public good that only defense buys, while most cable resilience can be licensed onto operators—so PRO funds the regulable piece by removing the irreplaceable protector.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO defined 'much more' as large relative to tiny cable baseline (one frigate deferred) and made resilience a force multiplier: modern forces depend on civilian ports, finance, cloud, allied nets satellites can't replace at scale. CON's strongest paradox - cutting the frigate that escorts repair ships - landed, but PRO answered gray-zone deniability and that escort without repair mission escorts nothing. CON relied on routine rerouting and regulation, was repeatedly clipped for length, and conceded mandates still compel real investment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Frigate-cut paradox: A - you fund protection by removing a protector, repair can't happen without sea control; B - escort with no repair capacity escorts nothing, one fewer hull with working comms is more useful than full fleet on brittle network.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built a stronger comparative case by framing national defense as an interconnected system rather than a mere platform count, effectively arguing that cable resilience acts as a force multiplier for the entire military and economy. Side A also handled the pressure questions exceptionally well, providing a concrete, plausible trade-off (deferring one frigate in a ten-ship program) that satisfied the "much more" burden for cable investment without dismantling the fleet. Side B delivered sharp, memorable rebuttals—particularly the insight that cutting a frigate means "removing a cable protector"—but leaned too heavily on commercial regulation to solve the redundancy issue. Side A successfully dismantled this by highlighting that private mandates cannot replace state-funded wartime coordination, intelligence-led monitoring, and strategic spares. Furthermore, Side B incurred repeated execution costs, with four separate turns clipped for exceeding the word limit, which disrupted their final crystallizations. Side A's holistic weighing, direct answers to pressure, and cleaner execution secure the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over the frigate trade-off: Side B's sharp Rebuttal 2 turn arguing that "PRO would pay for cable protection by removing a cable protector," followed by Side A's closing counter that "an escort with no prepared repair mission escorts nothing," which perfectly crystallized the interdependence of the assets and the necessity of the systemic view.
