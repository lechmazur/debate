### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but B landed the more damaging and unresolved pressure. B's core test—does the person who shows up match the impression created, and was the invitation to meet sincerely meant—is simple, workable, and squarely engages the motion's "deceptive unless disclosed" framing by arguing that undisclosed use is not inherently deceptive when outcomes match. A's case relies on a process-based theory of deception (undisclosed authorship/production method is itself the harm), but two of B's sharpest challenges exposed real cracks in that theory that A never fully closed: (1) the "pixel-identical" hypothetical, where an AI-denoised sat-for photo and a fully AI-generated likeness built from selfies can be visually indistinguishable and produce the exact same impression, yet A's rule brands only one deceptive; and (2) the human-ghostwriter parallel, where a friend supplying warm lines "vanishes at the table" just as an AI assistant would, yet A exempts the friend while demanding disclosure for the model. A's answers to both (in Rebuttal 2 and Closing) largely reasserted the distinction ("a model's product passed off as one," "a friend is a person in your life") without resolving why process should matter when the resulting impression is identical. B also sustained a values-based objection (stigmatizing dyslexic, non-native, and anxious daters) that A only partially answered by insisting disclosure isn't shaming, without engaging the practical social-signaling harm B raised. Pressure Questions were well-executed on both sides with proper A1/A2 formatting, but B's questions did more damage to the opposing case's internal consistency than A's did. B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly ("process replaces falsehood as the crime"), while A's closing largely restated prior claims rather than resolving the two outstanding contradictions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's [B4.3]/[B4.4] exchange—pointing out that a pixel-identical AI-denoised photo vs. AI-generated photo, and a human ghostwriter vs. an AI assistant, would be treated differently under A's rule despite creating identical impressions—was the most decisive clash, and A's closing response did not fully resolve it.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically exposing Side B's standard for deception as an arbitrary focus on process over material falsehood. By showing that Side B would permit a friend's hidden ghostwriting but punish AI, and permit heavy photo filters but punish pixel-identical AI generations, Side A successfully framed the proposition as technophobia rather than a coherent ethical standard.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive, particularly the points that a friend's wit "also vanishes at the table" and that punishing an AI photo that resembles the user while allowing heavy filters punishes "provenance, not dishonesty."
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by proving that undisclosed AI creates deception at the point of choice—swipes, trust, and emotional investment—before any meetup, and that clear disclosure is a minimal requirement for informed consent. Side B's material-deception standard could not overcome this pre-meetup harm, and its analogies to makeup or human friends collapsed under A's distinction between enhancing a real photograph and substituting a generated likeness, and between a person in one's life and a hidden model that vanishes offline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal, which drew the decisive "sat for" versus AI-generated likeness distinction and separated a human friend from a vanishing assistant, directly answering B's pressure questions and exposing that B was defending provenance rather than honesty.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): The decisive exchange was B's pressure question forcing A to admit whether it was defending the motion "as written" (which imposes a blanket disclosure presumption on all AI-generated photos and AI-written assistance) or a narrower carve-out rule excluding trivial fixes. A answered "as written" but then immediately reintroduced the "typo fix is not assistance in the relevant sense" exception — a distinction the actual motion text does not contain. B caught this inconsistency cleanly in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, repeatedly showing that A's workable version of the rule is not the proposition on the table, while B's alternative (fraud/material-likeness test) tracks a coherent, consistently defended line throughout. A's safety/consent argument (that bait works before the "revocable coffee") was emotionally resonant and never fully neutralized, but B's more rigorous mapping of argument to the actual wording of the motion, combined with the unanswered "distrust the software, not the person" critique and the marginalized-user cost argument, gave B the cleaner, more consistent path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question 2 ("Are you defending that rule, or a narrower one the proposition never states?") followed by B's Rebuttal 2 exposing A's answer as exactly that narrower, undefended rule — this exchange most damaged A's ability to claim victory on the motion as written.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully proving the motion is simultaneously too broad and too weak. A capitalized on B's attempts to exclude "typo fixes" and add "tiering," showing that B had to rewrite the proposition to defend it. A's argument that truly deceptive profiles should be taken down rather than "badged" neatly neutralized B's safety impacts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A1 response in Rebuttal 2, arguing that actual AI counterfeits are deception and should be taken down entirely, whereas a disclosure badge just "leaves the bait up," completely flipped Side B's safety and sunk-cost arguments.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing that the core harm—counterfeiting identity and character before a meeting—undermines consent and safety in a context built on trust. Side B’s overbreadth objection was sharp but relied on stretching the motion’s language to cover trivial edits, while A offered workable de minimis and tiered distinctions. A’s turning of the vulnerable-users argument and its memorable framing of the “when do they get to know” question proved more decisive than B’s technical critique. B’s concession that counterfeit faces and ghostwritten charm are deception left its alternative—meet-first verification—exposed as too late to protect users from sunk emotional and physical risk.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s rebuttal that “close enough after the lie worked is still bait” and “coffee is revocable only after you have already traveled, sat exposed, and risked safety on attraction built by fiction,” which dismantled Side B’s “meeting is the test” framework and shifted the debate to when consent must be informed.
