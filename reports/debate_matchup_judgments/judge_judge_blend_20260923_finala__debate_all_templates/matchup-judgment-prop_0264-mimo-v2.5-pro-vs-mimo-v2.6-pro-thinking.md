### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): CON built a more durable case by identifying a structural flaw PRO never fully closed: intentional-tort exclusions mean the catastrophic verdicts that actually break municipal budgets stay with taxpayers regardless of the mandate. When PRO pivoted to defense costs as the premium driver, CON turned this into a self-defeating admission — a mechanism that bills officers for being accused rather than for proven misconduct, undercutting the "market removes bad officers" claim. CON's "private-market alibi" argument (departments escape supervision reform by blaming an uninsurable individual) and the assignment-risk-clustering point (careful officers on hard beats pay more than cautious ones on quiet ones) were pressed consistently across rounds and never squarely neutralized by PRO's actuarial-normalization promises, which remained assertions without a concrete mechanism. PRO's pressure questions forced CON to answer directly (A1/A2 format followed correctly), and CON's answer — that decertification, prosecution, and refused indemnification already impose personal consequences — meaningfully complicated PRO's "officers feel zero consequences" framing, a point PRO's closing never re-engaged. PRO also suffered an execution cost, being clipped in both rebuttal rounds versus CON's one clipped speech, suggesting less disciplined argument management. Both sides argued competently, but CON's rebuttals did more damage to PRO's central mechanism than PRO's rebuttals did to CON's structural objections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turning PRO's "defense costs drive premiums" answer against itself — showing that if defense costs (not indemnity payouts on excluded intentional torts) are what raises premiums, the scheme merely bills officers for accusations and dangerous-beat assignment rather than proven misconduct, gutting the promised "uninsurable bad officer" mechanism.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): CON won by attacking the proposition on its own terms: intentional-tort exclusions leave the largest municipal payouts on taxpayers, so the scheme does not move the costs that matter most; beat-assignment pricing and under-enforcement were never met by more than actuarial promises; and insurance becomes a departmental exit rather than institutional reform. PRO’s zero-vs-partial and missing-personal-consequence framing was sharp but could not repair coverage and mechanism gaps after sustained pressure. Clipped PRO rebuttals also left less complete answers on the table.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 1 (A2.1–A2.6): intentional-tort exclusions keep the budget-breaking tail on cities, kill the uninsurability lever, and turn “middle-ground” premiums into a tax on hard-beat exposure.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): CON wins by showing the insurance mandate fails on its own financial terms: intentional-tort exclusions leave the largest verdicts with taxpayers, while premiums priced on complaints and assignment would tax good officers in tough beats rather than target misconduct. PRO’s “better than zero” framing collapses because the scheme captures the smallest dollars, creates perverse incentives, and hands departments a political alibi to avoid fixing supervision. CON’s rebuttals consistently damaged PRO’s analogies and mechanisms, and PRO never answered the institutional-evasion point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s R1 and R2 dismantling of the doctor analogy by proving officer premiums would price exposure by assignment (where complaints cluster) rather than by protocol compliance, and showing that the uninsurable-officer mechanism evaporates if excluded intentional-tort claims generate no payouts.
