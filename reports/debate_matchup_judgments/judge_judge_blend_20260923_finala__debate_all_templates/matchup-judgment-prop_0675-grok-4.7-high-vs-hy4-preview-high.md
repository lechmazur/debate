On balance, B made the debate easier to decide by isolating and pressing the one unresolved tension in A's structure.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and engaged the same core comparison throughout, with clean use of the pressure-question format. CON's distillation argument (frontier training can happen via gray-market/third-country compute, then be distilled onto legal chips) was a genuinely damaging attack that forced PRO to concede the finished system isn't grounded and pivot to a "fielding generations" framing. PRO's pivot held up reasonably well and its core asymmetry—commercial loss is convertible/migratable while training-cycle time is not—remained largely intact and was reasserted clearly in later rounds. CON's counter-move that lost sales are equally "compounding" and that continued restriction teaches allies to design around U.S. parts was clever but rested on a shakier inference: allies face no export restriction themselves, so their incentive to defect from the U.S. stack is less obviously symmetric to the adversary's forced indigenization, and CON never fully closed that logical gap despite B's pointed pressure question exposing it. PRO's answers to CON's toughest questions (A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2) were more internally consistent, distinguishing a state-driven production mandate from voluntary commercial migration, whereas CON's parallel answers relied more on asserted inevitability of full-stack catch-up without engaging the practical difficulty (e.g., leading-edge packaging/fabrication chokepoints) that PRO had flagged. On balance PRO's framing of "convertible dollars vs. non-recoverable time" gave a cleaner decision rule that survived the exchange better than CON's "self-expiring delay vs. permanent structural harm," yielding a clear but not dominant win for PRO (Side B).
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The sharpest exchange was CON's distillation attack (A1.2/A2.1: teacher models can be trained via gray-market or third-country compute and then distilled onto still-legal chips) against PRO's chokepoint claim, followed by PRO's B4.1 concession-and-pivot to "fielding generations" (arguing controls degrade successor generations rather than ground current systems). This exchange most clearly defined what each side was actually left defending after the clash.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): B narrowly won the comparative burden. A persuasively showed that controls impose real delays on successive model generations, but repeatedly asserted rather than established that lost demand would migrate and preserve U.S. scale. B more convincingly framed the industrial cost as structural: exclusion accelerates a rival stack, erodes U.S. defaults, and encourages partner hedging, while military effects can leak through stockpiles, domestic chips, rented capacity, and distillation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s closing answer to A’s “lost sale versus lost cycle” frame was decisive. B argued that the cost is not one sale but ecosystem independence, while the delayed cycle doubles as the rival’s investment period. A exposed that later, coarser refreshes are still genuine slowing, but did not show why that temporary advantage outweighs the claimed structural competitive loss.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully arguing that the military delay caused by chip restrictions is leaky (via distillation and inference) and self-expiring (as the rival is forced to build a domestic stack), whereas the industrial harm is structural and permanent (eroding the US software/hardware moat and losing the volume needed to fund future R&D). Side A's rhetoric and grasp of AI and semiconductor mechanics were sharper, effectively neutralizing Side B's "lost sale vs. lost cycle" framing by showing that the "lost cycle" is exactly the time the rival uses to achieve independence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of the "lost cycle" framing in Rebuttal 2 and the closing, arguing that the denied cycle is precisely the stretch the rival uses to stand up an independent stack, making the military delay self-expiring while the industrial harm (moat erosion and partner hedging) becomes permanent.
