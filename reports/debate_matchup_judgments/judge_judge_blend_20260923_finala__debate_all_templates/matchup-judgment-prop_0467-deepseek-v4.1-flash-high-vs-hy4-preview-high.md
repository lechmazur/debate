### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): The debate turned on B's central dilemma: the motion is either redundant (catastrophic cyberattacks already qualify as armed attacks under existing effects-based law) or dangerous (it extends a military option to non-lethal, gray-zone disruption). A's opening anchored the case on an effects-based reading tied to "under international law," a legitimate legal move since "armed attack" is itself a scale-and-effects term of art, not an evasive swap of the motion. Across rebuttals, A closed both horns: on redundancy, A argued that without explicit equivalence, states inconsistently apply the effects test to code versus kinetic weapons, creating a real-world safe harbor that mere doctrinal theory doesn't fix; on danger, A explicitly conceded in Rebuttal 2 and the pressure-question answers that non-lethal, reversible disruptions warrant no military response, directly neutralizing B's core escalation fear. B's attribution argument, while sharp, was partly self-undermined once B conceded that in genuinely lethal cases existing law already permits force under the same attribution standard—meaning the motion doesn't uniquely worsen attribution risk where it matters most. By the close, B was largely restating the dilemma ("redundant or a bluff") without landing fresh damage, while A had directly answered the two horns with a coherent crime/armed-attack line. Both sides executed the pressure-question format well and argued crisply, but A adapted more successfully to the live clash and left a cleaner path to a verdict.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.4), directly answering B's pressure questions by conceding no military response applies to non-lethal disruption while insisting sustained catastrophic effects warrant the armed-attack category, which defused B's "dangerous extension" horn most effectively.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): B wins on the central clash about what "equivalent under international law" means. A pressed a strong overbreadth/redundancy dilemma, but B credibly narrowed via the existing scale-and-effects threshold and answered A's pressure questions directly: temporary blackout = crime, collapse = sustained large-scale humanitarian harm; non-lethal hacks often mean no military response is proportionate. Critically, B reframed military response as including reversible military cyber operations, defusing A's bomb-vs-blackout and escalation claims. A concedes lethal cyberattacks are already armed attacks, which makes B's codification look like clarification rather than a dangerous new license, while attribution fog applies equally to current law.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 answer on proportionality - that proportional military response does not mean bombing, it can be a reversible defensive cyber operation by military forces, and that proportionality caps use - which neutralizes A's core escalation and non-equivalence argument.
