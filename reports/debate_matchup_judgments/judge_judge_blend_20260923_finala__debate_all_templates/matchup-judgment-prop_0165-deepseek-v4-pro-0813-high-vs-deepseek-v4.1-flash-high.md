### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): CON built a sharper, more sustained line of attack: the "narrow vs broad" dilemma (a minimal handshake standard won't cure the app/stored-balance friction PRO cites, while a standard broad enough to cure it necessarily reaches the competitive authentication/settlement layer PRO claims to leave untouched). This point was raised in Rebuttal 1, sharpened via Pressure Questions, and never fully neutralized by PRO, whose Rebuttal 2 and Closing mostly reasserted that the standard "fixes only the doorway" without directly rebutting CON's claim that new pricing/plug-and-charge/grid-service methods must cross that same doorway, giving whoever writes the standard control over market entry. CON's "scalpel not sledgehammer" alternative (condition openness on subsidies/rights-of-way, plus targeted outcome rules) gave a concrete, comparative policy that PRO's strongest counter—that most charging already uses public rights-of-way, so the distinction collapses—only partially addressed and CON never fully answered, but by that point CON's dilemma framing had already done the heavier argumentative work. PRO's opening (infrastructure analogies, public-money argument) was strong, but Rebuttal 1 was clipped for length, an execution cost, and later speeches leaned on restating the "minimal floor" claim rather than directly dismantling CON's boundary-control mechanism. Pressure Questions were used well by both sides, but CON's answers (A1/A2) engaged the dilemma head-on while PRO's answer to capture risk ("manage it via transparency and appeal") was comparatively generic. Overall CON made the debate easier to decide by keeping the central tension crisp through every round.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 dilemma ([B2.1]: "If the mandate covers only the plug-to-payment handshake, it won't deliver the seamless experience PRO promises... If it reaches those layers, PRO is freezing the competitive stack"), reinforced in Rebuttal 2 ([B4.2]-[B4.3]), was the decisive exchange; PRO never fully escaped it.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making interoperability a limited access floor rather than a government-designed charging ecosystem. Its distinction between the standardized doorway and competitive service layers substantially answered Side A’s innovation dilemma, while its network-wide confidence argument showed why subsidy-only conditions leave meaningful gaps. Side A offered a strong targeted-regulation alternative and credible capture concerns, but did not establish that an updateable payment-and-roaming interface would materially freeze innovation. B’s clipped first rebuttal was an execution cost, but not enough to reverse the substantive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s A1 response was decisive: standardizing authentication, ordinary payment, and roaming can remove mandatory apps and stored balances while leaving pricing, reservations, diagnostics, and grid services competitive. This directly weakened Side A’s repeated claim that the standard must be either ineffective or sweeping.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully proving that driver confidence is a "network property" that cannot be solved by Side B's piecemeal approach of only regulating subsidized chargers. Side A effectively defended the feasibility of standardizing the "doorway" (payment and roaming) without freezing the entire technology stack, neutralizing Side B's core dilemma.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's argument that targeted conditions fail because drivers cannot know in advance which chargers are compliant, effectively dismantling B's alternative.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently and followed the pressure-question format cleanly, but PRO built the more resilient case. PRO's key move—narrowing the mandate to the "public interface" (payment, authentication, roaming, connector compatibility) while leaving speed, chemistry, and business model open—defused most of CON's "premature technical bet" and "innovation freeze" attacks, and PRO kept returning to a sharp, memorable frame (gatekeeper vs. market, the driver at 5% battery with no substitute). CON's strongest material was the investment/anchor-contract argument (universal access removes the exclusivity that justifies building marginal chargers) and the point that payment/authentication ties into genuinely unsettled areas like fleet billing and V2G. But CON's answer to PRO's decisive pressure point—that privately funded chargers on public curbs/corridors remain chokepoints even when subsidy strings only cover subsidized hardware—was weak: CON fell back on a membership/fleet-card analogy that undercut the urgency of the "driver at 5%" scenario PRO kept emphasizing, and never closed the gap between "targeted conditions" and the unsubsidized private chokepoints PRO identified. PRO's answers to CON's pressure questions (A1/A2) were also more directly responsive and better integrated into the ongoing comparison, while CON's answers, though present and formatted correctly, mostly restated priors rather than resolving the chokepoint problem. The result is a competitive but clear win for PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's argument that privately funded chargers on public curbs, corridors, and grid connections remain chokepoints even when subsidy conditions only bind government-funded hardware (A2.5, sharpened in A4.3-A4.4) exposed the central gap in CON's "targeted conditions + competition law" alternative, and CON's reply (B4.1/B4.3, analogizing to membership fueling programs) never fully closed that gap.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by framing interoperability as a limited public-interface requirement and showing that Side A's targeted conditions would leave a two-tier patchwork of open subsidized sites and private chokepoints. Side A raised a credible standards-freeze concern, but its decisive investment claim remained speculative: it never established that open payment and access would prevent anchor contracts or eliminate profitable differentiation. B also explained how modular, revisable standards could preserve innovation while addressing immediate network lock-in.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest rebuttal was that site-specific subsidy conditions cannot solve corridor-wide network effects: drivers need predictable access at privately funded chokepoints too. Side A defended selective conditions but did not resolve the resulting patchwork or show why interoperability necessarily destroys the contracts that finance marginal stations.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by systematically dismantling Side B's alternative of targeted subsidy conditions and antitrust enforcement. A effectively argues that a patchwork of open and closed chargers defeats the purpose of a reliable public network, using the compelling and practical scenario of a stranded driver. While Side B makes strong points about the risks of premature standardization and a "slow committee," Side A successfully mitigates this by distinguishing between the public interface and the underlying hardware/speed innovation, ultimately proving that the harms of proprietary fragmentation outweigh the costs of standard-setting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal decisively neutralizes Side B's targeted subsidy alternative by pointing out that a driver at 5 percent charge cannot audit subsidy contracts, and that targeted strings merely create an unworkable two-tier system of open and locked stations.
