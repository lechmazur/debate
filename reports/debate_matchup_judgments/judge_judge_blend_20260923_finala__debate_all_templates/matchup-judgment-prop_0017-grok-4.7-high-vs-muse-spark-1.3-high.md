- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued crisply and adapted well, but A won the decisive exchange over hazard-vs-status. A conceded early that fuel, waste, and blockage can be cleared immediately regardless of vacancy, which preempted B's strongest "firetrap" imagery and let A frame the remaining dispute narrowly: is it justified to criminalize a person for merely remaining outdoors once the hazard is cleared, with no usable bed, storage, or path out? B's rebuttal in R2/Closing (rebuilding after warning is "a new choice") is clever but never fully answers A's pressure question about where the person is supposed to go if every bed is full — B's own A1 concedes the "lawful place" is just standing outdoors without a tent, which A seized on in Closing to show that "rebuilding" often just means having nowhere else to keep belongings or shelter from weather. B's "administrable bed standard" attack lands some damage on A's "usable" test (curfew, sobriety, sex-separation exceptions), and A's answer to that pressure question is reasonably precise without collapsing into an unworkable veto, but B never quite dismantles the core distinction A draws between clearing hazards and prosecuting mere presence. A also better answered B's pressure questions with clean A1/A2 formatting, while B's answers were folded into rebuttal argument without as sharp a callback. Both closings crystallize the clash well, but A's is slightly more responsive to the specific "rebuilding" argument, closing the loop on why cite-and-disperse doesn't end the hazard cycle. Overall a clear but competitive win for A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A2 answer in Rebuttal 2 (that hazard remediation—seizing fuel, clearing waste/needles—can happen "again if the hazard returns" without needing a criminal charge against the person left outdoors) effectively separated hazard-abatement from status-criminalization, undercutting B's overpass/firetrap framing and forcing B's Rebuttal 2 into the weaker "rebuilding is a new choice" argument that never fully answered where a person is supposed to go when shelters are full.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s decisive response was that Side A’s repeated-cleanup alternative restores a sidewalk only temporarily while forbidding sanctions against immediate re-encampment. This exposed the practical gap between permitting hazard removal and denying cities the power needed to keep the hazard from recurring.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and executed the Pressure Questions format properly. B's most creative move was the conduct/status distinction—claiming anti-encampment laws forbid "occupying" or "settling" ground with structures, not sleeping itself, so a person can always obey by resting without a tent even when shelters are full. This neatly answered A's core "impossible to obey" challenge in the moment. But A's closing exploited the unresolved gap in that move: if anti-camping ordinances are citywide, "moving the tent" only relocates the violation (grass, doorway, under a bridge), so B never specified where a person without shelter could lawfully rest overnight without violating the ordinance's ban on prolonged occupation. B's final speech repeated the conduct/status framing without closing that specific loophole, leaving A's "vanish without anywhere to vanish to" charge as the last word on the decisive clash. A's "genuinely available" standard, defined by objective criteria (open, reachable, no forced separation from partners/pets/medication), also survived B's "endless veto" attack reasonably well by anchoring to concrete, checkable conditions rather than subjective preference. On balance, A more successfully forced the debate onto its preferred terrain and left the final substantive gap in its opponent's account exposed and unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing line in [A5.2]-[A5.3], showing that B's "just move the tent" answer to the impossibility question collapses under a citywide ban (illegal on the grass, under the bridge, at the doorway too), which B's own closing failed to directly resolve.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by exploiting the proposition’s categorical “only when.” It distinguished resting outside from establishing an exclusionary encampment and argued that cities may prohibit the latter even while shelter capacity remains incomplete. Side A persuasively defended genuine alternatives and targeted safety rules, but never fully established why shelter, storage, and services must all be prerequisites to every anti-encampment law.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest turn came after Side A conceded that a ramp-blocking tent could be moved despite zero available beds. B used that concession to argue that anti-encampment rules can likewise regulate occupation rather than unavoidable sleep, directly weakening A’s impossibility premise.

CON never fully resolved the core burden of naming a lawful act of obedience when every bed is full; the distinction between resting and encamping remained abstract against PROs concrete impossibility argument. CONs veto critique was blunted by PROs narrowing to objective, administrable standards, and PROs distinction between targeted spatial rules and blanket camping bans held through the final exchange. CONs parallel duty to public order was real but insufficient to overcome the propositionss central moral chokepoint.
