### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by effectively dismantling Side A's core premise that setting rules and confirming a cart require the same cognitive effort. By drawing a sharp distinction between 'anticipating abstract failures' (hard) and 'recognizing concrete errors' (easy), Side B successfully proved that item-by-item confirmation adds essential judgment that rules cannot replicate. Side B also won the weighing debate by showing that an AI's automated mistake within a budget cap is much harder to reverse than a missed purchase opportunity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's final argument distinguishing between 'writing' (imagining abstract failures in advance) and 'recognizing' (spotting concrete errors at checkout) cleanly neutralized Side A's claim that a user who writes a thin rule will inevitably rubber-stamp a confirmation prompt.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by exposing a persistent gap in PRO's framework: rules can cap spending and whitelist merchants but cannot practicably enumerate every dimension of a satisfactory purchase. CON's concrete examples—surge fares under the cap, ten small packs instead of one large, nonrefundable basic fares, lookalike sellers—survived PRO's repeated "fix the rule" refrain because PRO never showed how a normal user could realistically pre-authorize every failure mode without turning shopping into contract drafting. CON crystallized this into a sharp cognitive distinction between generating perfect abstract rules and recognizing concrete errors at a glance, directly rebutting PRO's fatigue argument and showing that even a tired tap delivers more judgment than an omitted rule clause. CON also won the weighing by demonstrating that a wrong auto-booking imposes costs (money, delay, refund fights) that exceed the harm of a delayed prompt, and that after-the-fact remedies do not rebook a ruined night. PRO's analogy to corporate travel and autopay was effectively distinguished, and PRO's Rebuttal 2 suffered from a word-limit clip that left key pressures partially unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's distinction between abstract rule-writing and concrete error recognition (A4.1/A5.2): showing that spotting a triple-priced surge fare or ten small packs requires only recognition at the moment of purchase, whereas writing a rule that excludes every such possibility in advance is unrealistically burdensome. This directly undermined PRO's equivalence between missed rule clauses and unread prompts.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by introducing a decisive epistemic distinction in the late rounds: the difference between the abstract cognitive load of writing perfect rules and the concrete cognitive load of recognizing a bad purchase in a final cart. This directly dismantled Side B's core argument that a user who writes a thin rule will also blindly tap through a confirmation. Side A also effectively highlighted that an AI agent "grades its own homework" and cannot flag anomalies it wasn't explicitly programmed to catch, making the final human check a necessary backstop against technically permitted but practically wrong purchases. Side B argued well and had strong rhetoric, but failed to overcome A's concrete vs. abstract reframe in the closing, and suffered a minor execution cost from a clipped rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's distinction in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing between the abstract difficulty of writing comprehensive rules and the concrete ease of recognizing a flawed final cart, which shattered PRO's assumption that rule-writing fatigue equates to confirmation fatigue.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B successfully proved that budget and merchant limits only cap the size of a financial loss but fail to address the qualitative harms of incorrect, unchangeable, or compromised bookings. Side A's attempt to expand "merchant rules" into complex conditional logic was effectively checked by Side B, who also won the crucial mechanical point: bad pages are designed to look routine, meaning an agent will fail to escalate them.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal, where they dismantle Side A's "escalation" model by pointing out that malicious or reworded pages are specifically designed to look predictable, meaning the agent will silently book them without triggering the human check.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by showing that budget and merchant rules as written cannot capture the live material terms of purchases—refundability, substitutions, connections, reworded fares—and that relying on an agent to pattern-match offers against presets lets binding harms execute silently. Side B (PRO) had to keep expanding merchant rules beyond the proposition’s text and never fully answered the problem that dark patterns are designed to look routine, making the ambiguity-defaults-to-ask safety valve unreliable. CON’s distinction between containing error size and actually preventing or unwinding wrong purchases remained intact, while PRO’s claim of a concession was overstated and CON’s fatigue counterproposal offered a cleaner alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal showing that PRO’s safety valve of ambiguity defaulting to ask fails because deceptive pages are built to look routine, and that PRO’s defense required smuggling granular term restrictions into merchant rules far beyond the motion as written.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by rigorously holding Side B to the text of the proposition and exposing the logical gaps in PRO's model. Side A effectively demonstrated that "budget and merchant rules" cannot anticipate the nuances of a live rendered offer, such as nonrefundable fares, substituted items, or dark patterns. When Side B attempted to expand the rules to include specific terms or introduce exception pings for "material differences," Side A correctly identified this as either rewriting the motion or conceding the need for item-level confirmation. Side A's distinction between merely capping the financial size of an error and actually preventing the binding harm (lost clinic slots, sunk fares) was decisive and unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled Side B's "ambiguity defaults to ask" and expanded "merchant rules" by pointing out that deceptive pages are specifically designed to look routine and unambiguous to an unattended agent, meaning the agent will authorize the charge without pinging the user.
