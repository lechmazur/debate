### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side B won by cleanly separating earnings-based accrual from the later trigger for payment. Side A compellingly exposed verification problems, but largely treated those problems as reasons platform work cannot count at all rather than showing why tightly conditioned claims, audits, and prorated credits are insufficient. Side B also answered the cost and dilution objections comparatively, while Side A under-addressed social insurance beyond unemployment. Repeated clipping reduced Side B's margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange was the response that a logged-off afternoon may fail the payment trigger even while prior platform earnings count toward accrual. This directly weakened Side A's central moral-hazard example; Side A's reservation-wage reply created real pressure but did not prove that imperfect trigger adjudication justifies crediting none of the work.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully proving that platform work lacks an insurable separation event, making it fundamentally incompatible with unemployment insurance. Side B tried to sidestep this by separating eligibility accrual from benefit triggers, proposing a 'comparative offer' test. However, Side A decisively showed that this test merely adjudicates reservation wages rather than proving job losses. Side B also suffered from poor execution, having all four of its substantive speeches clipped for exceeding the length limit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which dismantled Side B's 'comparative-offer test' by pointing out that evaluating declined offers at worse fares just underwrites a reservation wage rather than proving a real layoff.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing that the proposition concerns standardized earnings records for accruing work history, not altering benefit triggers. This analytical split dissolved most of CON's moral-hazard and dilution objections, which assumed gig workers could claim benefits merely for logging off. PRO showed that transaction-level platform data could actually improve verification of involuntary loss compared with conventional attestations, and that collecting contributions without crediting records is unjust. CON's insistence that eligibility is a claim ticket never overcame PRO's demonstration that unemployment systems already separate monetary eligibility from separation eligibility, and CON failed to show why platform work could not be fitted into that existing architecture.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's separation of accrual from benefit triggers (A2.1–A2.3), which revealed that CON's central harms were attacks on a payment rule nobody proposed, and PRO's Q2 pressure question exposing the injustice of collecting premiums while refusing to credit the record.
