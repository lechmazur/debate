### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued this cleanly and with real philosophical substance, but PRO built the more resilient path. PRO's core move—grounding the moral equivalence between accepted withdrawal-of-treatment practice and assisted dying, then repeatedly forcing CON to explain why "adding a cause" is worse than "removing support" when both are patient-directed and death-causing—was never fully closed by CON, who kept restating the distinction as intuitive rather than resolving the underlying asymmetry. CON's strongest weapon, the irreversibility argument (a paused refusal vs. an irrecoverable dose), was genuinely sharp and forced PRO to answer with a cost-of-delay framing (forced suffering as itself a harm); PRO's answer was adequate but not fully dispositive, keeping this exchange close. However, CON's own answer to the pressure question about non-imminent degenerative patients exposed a real gap: continuous sedation, CON's proposed alternative, was conceded not to apply to long-decline, non-dying patients—precisely the population PRO's case centers on (e.g., ALS)—leaving CON's response to that suffering category as bare moral assertion ("must not be licensed") rather than a substantive answer. PRO's closing rhetorical device ("Paperwork can be refused. A locked bathroom cannot.") crystallized the stakes more memorably and consistently than CON's abstract restatement of the bright line. Both used the Q&A round well, but PRO's answers more directly neutralized the specific pressure raised.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 turning CON's "unreliable competence" argument back on ventilator-withdrawal cases, forcing CON into the irreversibility distinction—paired with CON's genuinely strong reversibility rebuttal in Rebuttal 1—was the debate's sharpest exchange, but CON's later concession that sedation doesn't extend to non-imminent degenerative decline was the more decisive moment, since it left PRO's core suffering population unanswered.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by exposing the gap between Side A’s terminal-dying framing and a motion that also covers potentially lengthy degenerative decline.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both questions directly, distinguished sedation by purpose and limited applicability, and showed why A’s starvation-versus-assisted-death comparison was a false binary, especially for patients in long degenerative decline.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by grounding the debate in the lived reality of the patient and effectively dismantling Side A's primary moral distinction between withdrawing care and prescribing a lethal dose. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): This came down to whether the omission/act line (A2.1, A4.7) could survive the "empty menu" pressure from B (B4.4, B5.4). B's strongest move was forcing A to admit that a patient who has already refused sedation and has no tube to decline is left with only suffocation, paralysis, stockpiling, or violent self-harm. A's answer—that "care that relieves without manufacturing a brief death" still exists for such a patient (A5.2)—is thin and somewhat question-begging, since B had specified this is exactly the patient for whom that care has already failed or been refused. However, A recovered ground by consistently returning to the systemic argument: that licensing killing changes the "chart" itself, making death something families, systems, and assessors credit as an option, and that the eligibility gates (terminal/degenerative disease, exclusion of treatable despair) cannot actually be applied independently of the very despair and fear that make the request possible (A4.6, A5.5). B never fully answered why clinical assessment of "treatable despair" is meaningfully independent from the patient's own report of unbearable suffering, which A pressed effectively in both rebuttals and the close. A's Pressure Question (A3.1) forced B into naming gates, and while B's answer (B4.1-B4.2) was substantive, A's follow-up correctly noted the assessor must still credit the patient's own account, reopening the vagueness problem. B's closing restated its bedside case well but didn't add a decisive new answer to the credibility-of-gates challenge. On balance, A did slightly more damage to B's core mechanism (the safeguards) than B did to A's core mechanism (the omission/act distinction), giving A a narrow win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over A3.1 (Q1) and B4.1-B4.2 (A1), followed by A4.6/A5.5 pressing that "excluding treatable despair" still requires crediting the patient's own report of suffering, was the pivotal clash — it tested whether safeguards can be operationalized independent of the very request they're meant to screen, and A's follow-up landed harder than B's defense.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by isolating the safeguarded residual case: a competent, terminally or degeneratively ill adult whose treatable despair has been excluded, whose palliation has failed, and who rejects prolonged sedation. Side B defended the act-omission distinction and raised credible concerns about pressure and diagnostic uncertainty, but never explained how prohibition protects that patient rather than merely preserving medical clean hands. Its repeated appeal to relieving care did not answer the stipulated case of refractory suffering.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange was its response to palliation: sedation only solves suffering if the patient accepts unconsciousness, while patients without life support have nothing equivalent to withdraw. Side B conceded refusal was permitted but answered chiefly that no lethal act was owed, leaving A's central harm comparison intact.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully trapping Side A on the scenario of a competent patient who refuses palliative sedation. While Side A relied heavily on the philosophical act/omission distinction (letting disease take its course vs. introducing a new cause of death), Side B effectively demonstrated that enforcing this distinction leaves the patient with no lawful option but to endure agony or attempt violent suicide. Side B's answers to the pressure questions were sharp, clearly defining the clinical "gates" independent of the patient's request, whereas Side A's responses felt somewhat evasive regarding the practical reality of the patient who rejects prolonged unconsciousness. Side B's closing crystallization—that "clean hands are not a protected patient"—was rhetorically decisive and exposed the callousness inherent in Side A's strict prohibition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they clearly articulated the independent clinical "gates" (disease class and psychiatric evaluation) and explained that forcing palliative sedation on a patient who refuses prolonged unconsciousness is simply "the ban renamed."
