### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): B won by separating legitimate safety and access standards from a resident veto based merely on preference. A compellingly exposed privatization and enforcement risks, but repeatedly treated flexibility as though it necessarily meant blocked access or ignored harm. B defended a regulated, fee-based, revocable model and showed that patios can violate old categorical rules while still satisfying present safety standards.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to A’s first pressure question was decisive: concrete violations warrant enforcement or removal, but preference for the old streetscape does not justify eliminating compliant patios. A never fully established why old bright-line rules should control when actual access and livability standards are met.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B won by anchoring the debate to the actual text of the motion and showing that the "even if" clause was doing the heavy lifting. B argued persuasively that pre-pandemic rules already permitted compliant sidewalk dining, so restoring them would not ban all patios but would only reject expanded encroachments. B thereby exposed that the "even if" was designed to override neighbors with real grievances about safety, noise, and captured public space, not merely to deflect empty-curb nostalgia. A offered a strong economic and civic vision but slightly mischaracterized B as wanting to remove already-compliant dining, and never fully resolved the tension that if regulation alone were sufficient, the override clause would be unnecessary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal (Turn 8), where B directly answered A's pressure questions by demonstrating that compliant patios could remain under old rules, clarified that the dispute was about extra flexibility rather than a blanket ban, and reframed the "even if" clause as a mechanism that silences legitimate neighbor grievances and authorizes capture in advance.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively controlling the interpretation of the motion. B effectively demonstrates that pre-pandemic rules already permitted compliant outdoor dining, meaning the "more flexible" clause specifically applies to the extra expansions (like roadway sheds and narrowed paths) that residents are objecting to. Side A spends the debate defending outdoor dining in general and framing resident objections as mere "nostalgia," but fails to overcome B's logical trap: if a patio meets old rules, the motion is unnecessary; if it requires the new flexibility, it causes the exact harms residents are reporting. B's rebuttals and closing crystallize this distinction perfectly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to Side A's Q1 (B4.1-B4.3), where B clarifies that restoring old rules doesn't ban compliant patios, but rather ends the "extra flexibility" (roadway sheds, pinched paths) that actually harms the block, completely dismantling A's strawman.
