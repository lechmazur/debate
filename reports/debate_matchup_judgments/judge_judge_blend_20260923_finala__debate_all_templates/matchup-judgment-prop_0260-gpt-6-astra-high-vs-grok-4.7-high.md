### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and followed the pressure-question format correctly, but B built the more decisive path. B turned A's own concession ("neither default proves consent," A2.1) into a recurring wedge, showing the opt-out default is a legal presumption rather than a moral upgrade. B also sustained a concrete mechanism critique that A never fully neutralized: that "robustness against evasion" creates an incentive asymmetry where missed trackers are countable and easily punished by testers while broken checkouts/pages are diffuse and hard to attribute, biasing enforcement toward overblocking. B further pressed a specific economic point—that most small-publisher traffic is one-time and unreturning, so a "remembered site-specific exception" mainly helps sites people already know (i.e., large login-based platforms), which doubles as a consolidation argument. A's answers to these points remained largely at the level of asserting that "transparent standards" and "testing both error types" would resolve the tension, without explaining why the incentive structure B described wouldn't dominate in practice, and without directly rebutting the one-time-visitor economics. A's principle ("burden should fall on those seeking access, not those seeking protection") was clear and consistently restated, but B's closing crystallized the clash effectively ("The opt-out stays on paper. The funding does not.") while A's closing mostly reasserted the framing without closing the concrete gaps B had opened.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 point [B2.1]-[B2.4], turning A's concession about consent into an indictment of the "opt-out" framing, combined with the incentive-asymmetry argument about testers punishing underblocking (countable) far more reliably than overblocking (diffuse harm to users), was the most damaging exchange, since A never fully closed that gap in later speeches.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's practical mechanisms. While Side B argued beautifully on the principle of consent and default friction, Side A proved that the proposed exception mechanism (users hunting in browser menus for drive-by sites) would destroy small publishers and consolidate power to platforms with logins. Side A also decisively won the technical clash on breakage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's argument that "missed trackers are countable, the failed checkout is not" perfectly neutralized Side B's claim that independent testing would prevent overblocking, proving a structural incentive for blockers to break sites.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): A won by sustaining a clearer burden-allocation framework and executing the debate format more cleanly. PRO established that neither default constitutes consent, but only opt-out makes permission the condition of access to a user’s device. CON landed a sharp substantive blow with the “one-time visitor” insight, but PRO partially absorbed it by conceding some revenue loss and pivoting to the principled choice of who should bear the friction. A critical execution gap favored PRO: in the Rebuttal 2 after Pressure Questions, PRO began with the required A1:/A2: labels, while CON skipped them and buried its answers in prose, incurring a real format penalty. PRO also crystallized the decisive clash more effectively in the closing, where CON largely repeated earlier lines.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The sharpest exchange concerned whether site-specific exceptions could preserve ad-funded publishing. B4’s “one-time visitor” rebuttal—arguing that most small-publisher traffic arrives once via search or links and never returns to remember an exception—materially damaged PRO’s claim that publishers could simply ask permission. However, PRO sustained its broader live route by reframing the issue as burden allocation: lawful commercial speech does not create an automatic right to occupy someone else’s device, making the opt-out a legitimate condition rather than a ban.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly within the rules, but B built the more damaging structural attack on the motion as written. B's opening move—splitting "tracking" from "advertising" and demanding PRO justify suppressing harmless, non-tracking ads—forced A to defend the full bundle rather than just the privacy harm. A tried to resolve this by claiming the third-party ad channel is technically inseparable from tracking, justifying default blocking of both; but A then promised users could restore ads while keeping tracking blocked via a "grant" mechanism, which implicitly concedes separability is possible after all. B's final rebuttal and closing directly exploited this tension ("Permission to display an ad does not give a browser a new ability to detect surveillance"), and A's closing response ("separation is a grant, not a property of an open pipe") gestured at an answer but never explained how the browser can enforce that separation if the channel truly can't be audited. B also strategically conceded default opt-out tracking protection as justified, which neutralized much of A's market-failure and enforceability argument for that half of the bundle while keeping the ad-suppression half unjustified. A's rhetoric was punchier, but B's rebuttal chain was more logically decisive and better resolved the central clash the debate turned on.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 / Closing sequence ("Permission to display an ad does not give a browser a new ability to detect surveillance") exposed a real contradiction in A's mechanism between claiming the ad/tracker channel is unverifiable (justifying default blocking) and later promising users can reopen ads while tracking stays blocked (implying it is verifiable/separable after all). A's closing response did not fully resolve this tension.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully narrowing the debate to the exact burden of the proposition (mandatory ad blocking) and setting a decisive logical trap. By fully conceding default tracking protection, Side A forced Side B to defend why ads themselves must be blocked. When Side B argued that users could later opt into ads without tracking, Side A exposed a fatal contradiction: if the browser can separate ads from tracking after a user opts in, it can do so by default without the ad mandate. Side B's closing attempt to resolve this ("separation is a grant") failed to explain how user permission suddenly gives the browser new technical auditing capabilities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.4) identifying the core technical contradiction: if ad and tracking pipes can be separated upon user opt-in, they can be separated by default; if they cannot, the opt-in is unsafe.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): CON won by exposing a decisive logical tension in PRO's case. PRO argued that third-party ad pipes are unverifiable and must be blocked by default, yet promised users could later opt into ads while keeping tracking blocked. CON showed that if that separation is technically achievable, the narrower tracking-only mandate suffices; if it is not, PRO's opt-out promise is unreliable. CON maintained that default system-level tracking protection captures the privacy benefit without the unjustified additional suppression of lawful advertising, and PRO never fully resolved why the law must bundle ad blocking with tracker blocking rather than simply close the tracking channel.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal and closing ([B4.4] through [B5.3]) exposed that PRO could not simultaneously claim third-party ad channels are inherently unverifiable and promise users could reopen those same channels later for ads without surveillance. This contradiction undermined the core justification for making ad suppression the legally mandated default.
