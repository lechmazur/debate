### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by exposing a structural flaw in Side B's bridge metaphor: making "expected manufacturing growth" part of the retirement threshold creates a moving finish line that turns a temporary bridge into a permanent treadmill, and by turning PRO's own export answer—contracting cleaner electricity—into evidence that clean build-out, not coal delay, is the real path to manufacturing competitiveness. CON also offered a concrete crowding-out mechanism (fixed capacity charges on idle coal draining utility budgets) that PRO never rebutted. While PRO's reliability and irreversibility arguments had weight, they failed to neutralize the treadmill problem or the export-turn, and their pollution and flexibility defenses were undermined by CON's evidence about old subcritical units. The clipped turns from CON are an execution cost, but the core argumentative path remained cleaner and more damaging.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over whether PRO's "expected manufacturing growth" retirement threshold creates an indefinite delay, where CON framed it as a treadmill and PRO could not show why rapid growth would not perpetually push the retirement date forward.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): CON won the sequencing clash. PRO's own rule — retire only when clean covers current + "expected manufacturing growth" [A4.1] — plus the motion's rapid-growth premise creates the treadmill CON identified: demand never stops arriving, so retirement never arrives. B answered A's Q2 with a concrete mechanism — fixed capacity charges for standby coal consume discom balance sheets needed for renewables/storage — while A never showed how declining-utilization backup avoids that crowding. The export argument also flipped: PRO conceded exporters need to contract cleaner electricity [A4.2], which is CON's model of powering tomorrow's factories with new clean, not old coal. That left PRO with an irreversibility claim that CON matched with carbon-border lockout and health costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1-B4.4 treadmill + fixed-charge mechanism: B shows PRO's threshold (today plus expected growth) never lets old coal retire under rapid growth, and money spent keeping old units available directly crowds out the transmission/storage factories need, undermining PRO's bridge claim.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side A built a decisive logical trap around the threshold for retirement, convincingly arguing that requiring clean power to cover "expected manufacturing growth" makes the finish line a moving target—a "treadmill" rather than a "bridge." Side A also brilliantly turned Side B's own concessions against it, using Side B's admission that exporters can survive by contracting clean power to prove that clean build-out, not coal retention, must be the priority. Side B's case was sensible but failed to escape Side A's strategic framing and struggled to answer the carbon border charge pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's "expected growth" threshold as a permanent delay mechanism and used Side B's own "cleaner electricity" concession to prove that clean build-out must be the priority for exporters.
