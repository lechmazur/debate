### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing demolition of significant buildings is an irreversible, disproportionate fix for shortages driven mainly by non-historic land restrictions. Its "ratchet" critique exposed that PRO's safeguards are structurally weak: the same actors setting housing targets also judge when retention is "unworkable," making the exception elastic and self-certifying. PRO's housing urgency was resonant but never overcame the fact that opening corridors and parking lots first is the reversible alternative, whereas a demolished street wall cannot be rebuilt.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's ratchet critique (A2 and A4): it showed PRO's "substantial net gains" and independent review are illusory limits because the housing target itself defines the "need" and "feasibility" that triggers demolition, so the exception expands to match political demand rather than discovering genuine conflicts.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): PRO maintained the more live path: historic districts can block valuable density, “build elsewhere” potential is not equivalent to delivered comparable homes, and safeguards (substantial net gains, retention study, readiness, cumulative loss) can limit loss. CON’s ratchet/elasticity critique stayed alive but was undercut by its own pressure answers – especially admitting it would still prohibit demolition even with no displacement, proven net gains, and no feasible retention – which turned its proportionate alternative into an absolute architectural veto. That concession, exploited in A’s closing, left PRO’s balancing test as the cleaner reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2 A1/A2 reframe – faster site does not win by default but delay/access are housing outcomes, and each demolition must still clear independent net-gain, retention, tenant, readiness and cumulative-loss review – directly blunted B’s ratchet and “open other land first” attacks.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully trapping Side A during the Pressure Questions round. Side B's Q2 forced Side A to admit they would prohibit demolition even in a scenario with substantial net housing gains, zero displacement, and no feasible retention. Side B effectively weaponized this concession in the closing to demonstrate that Side A grants architecture an absolute veto over human housing needs, regardless of safeguards. Furthermore, Side B provided stronger comparative weighing on the concept of irreversibility, successfully contrasting the permanent loss of buildings with the irreversible, compounding human costs of housing exclusion and overcrowding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they capitalized on Side A's rigid answer to Q2 to prove that the CON position relies on an uncompromising architectural veto rather than a balanced policy tradeoff.

### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): PRO successfully grounded the debate in the motion's explicit acceptance of architectural loss, showing that historic district rules routinely overreach to protect ordinary warehouses, vacant lots, and altered rows while freezing transit-rich neighborhoods. CON's alternatives remained abstract and were effectively framed as geographic scraps. While CON landed sharp punches on delivery risks and ensemble value, PRO's rebuttals better narrowed the clash and crystallized the comparative stakes: a conditional waiver system tied to net new homes versus a preservation presumption that CON admitted would hold even when it blocked the last viable path to needed density.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (B4) answers to CON's pressure questions, distinguishing individual landmarks from district-wide vetoes and exposing CON's threshold as an effectively permanent "architecture first, delivery never ripe" presumption.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): PRO made the decisive comparison stick: when the historic district itself is the transit-rich site, "build elsewhere" assumes away needed density. PRO answered B's delivery/displacement pressure with a conditional waiver (net additional + rehousing) and the breadth-of-veto principle distinguishing individual listing from district-wide freeze, which preserves a live path to housing without claiming all preservation is worthless. CON's irreplaceability and binding-commitment points were strong but left the permanence of exclusion unweighed and justified an indefinite preservation-first sequenced veto.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Exchange around A3.1 Q1 / B4.1 A1 – PRO forced CON to admit protections stand even when needed housing has no other route; PRO then flipped CON's own permanence claim to show years of exclusion are also irreversible, and CON's response that parks would also be sacrificed highlighted the cost CON was willing to accept.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by directly embracing the prompt's core tradeoff—losing some significant buildings for needed density—and effectively dismantling Side A's "build elsewhere" argument as relegating housing to transit-poor "scraps." Side A's demand for "binding delivery commitments" was well-countered by B as a sequenced veto that paralyzes development.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it answered Side A's pressure questions by clarifying that even individual landmarks may yield if they block needed density, fully embracing the prompt's "even at the cost" clause, and framing Side A's demand for exhaustive proof as a "sequenced veto" that ensures no housing is ever built.
