### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Both sides built a genuine, well-grounded clash: B anchored on the real-world Coogan-law analogy and a "conflict of interest" frame, while A countered with the joint-value/parental-stewardship frame and repeatedly exposed practical unworkability in B's proposed mechanisms. The decisive exchange came on B's revenue-threshold answer to A's Q1: A's "farm kid becomes an employee when crop prices rise" reductio landed hard and was never squarely rebutted, showing that B's threshold logic converts identical content into "work" purely because it succeeded — exactly the arbitrariness A warned about. On removal, B's pressure question did force A into the stark "no sweeping deletion veto" admission, which B's closing used effectively, but A had already pre-armed a response (exposure-plus-erasure: copies survive elsewhere while deletion destroys siblings' footage, parental authorship, and existing contracts), so the damage was contained rather than fatal. B also never resolved the deeper practical problem that in a "family-influencer business built around children," the child is most of the content, making "edit around them" removal nearly indistinguishable from full deletion — a gap A kept pressing. Beyond substance, B's opening, Rebuttal 1, and Rebuttal 2 were all clipped for exceeding word limits, a repeated execution failure the judging frame explicitly treats as a real cost, while A stayed within limits throughout and delivered tighter, more disciplined speeches. Combining slightly sharper unanswered rebuttals with cleaner execution gives A a clear, if competitive, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's "farm kid becomes an employee when crop prices rise" response to B's revenue-based threshold (A4.3), which exposed the arbitrariness of B's trigger and was never directly answered afterward, was the most decisive single exchange.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side B won by centering the unresolved conflict of interest: parents control filming and money while benefiting from continued use of the child's persona. B answered the threshold objection with a commercialization trigger and showed why imperfect removal from the primary channel remains meaningful. A raised real concerns about joint value, shared archives, contracts, and administrability, but its reliance on parental stewardship never adequately protected the child once the enterprise became large or the child reached adulthood. B's repeated clipping reduced its margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was its answer that rent and food supplied from channel revenue are not a guaranteed protected share but evidence of why independent protection is needed. It also effectively answered the claim that surviving copies make deletion worthless by comparing meaningful control over the primary channel against CON's lack of any clear exit right.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): PRO built the stronger path by anchoring its case in the already-accepted Coogan-law model, defining a workable revenue threshold to separate family memory from commercial work, and crystallizing CON's refusal to grant any enforceable deletion right. CON's parental-stewardship frame was rhetorically vivid but never squarely answered the core conflict-of-interest asymmetry: parents earn more the more intimate footage they publish. CON's admission that there should be "no sweeping deletion veto," combined with its inability to name any protective threshold it would accept, left PRO with the only live affirmative framework.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The deletion-rights exchange initiated by PRO's Pressure Question Q2: CON answered that there should be "no sweeping deletion veto," thereby defending a status quo of permanent involuntary exposure; PRO then crystallized this in Rebuttal 2 and Closing as a choice between imperfect removal and no removal at all, materially damaging CON's live route to victory.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued well, but CON (A) built the more durable case by isolating the motion's truly distinctive content. Once PRO conceded that the earnings fix is simply extending existing Coogan-style child-labor protections [B2.5, A4.1], the earnings front became a wash, and the debate turned on the removal right. There A's pressure question about whose account controls mixed sibling/parent/commercial footage and mirrored copies [A3.2] forced PRO into a "restrict, blur, de-monetize, or limit" menu [B4.2] that A correctly recharacterized in the final rebuttal and closing as an administrative tribunal rather than a genuine removal right—precisely the "censorship veto dressed as a right" warned about in the opening [A1.5-A1.6, A5.2]. PRO's closing never returned to defend the sibling/parent-account problem or rebut the tribunal characterization, instead re-running the labor-exploitation framing that was no longer contested. That left A's decisive framing unanswered at the close. A's case suffered execution costs from two clipped speeches, but the substantive weighing in each was largely completed before the cutoff, so the penalty is real but not decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing exploiting PRO's "restrict, blur, de-monetize, or limit" answer [B4.2] as proof of an unadministrable tribunal rather than a removal right, combined with PRO's failure to answer the sibling/parent whose-account-controls question in its own closing, was the turning point of the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): B won by correctly framing the motion as conditional regulation, not a blanket ban. A conceded that commercial child performers deserve trusts, hour limits, and enforcement, which substantially validated B's earnings case. B also answered the strongest removal objections with tailored measures such as blurring, delisting, and ending monetization; imperfect removal still meaningfully reduces exposure and profit. A raised a serious shared-family-speech concern, but its underinclusivity and mirror-copy objections demanded perfection rather than defeating targeted safeguards. A's two clipped rebuttals were an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first rebuttal was decisive: it showed that A's preferred extension of performer rules was itself a form of the stronger earnings protection proposed, while explaining why commercial scale creates distinct labor incentives and privacy harms.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by showing that once both sides accepted stronger earnings protections for child performers, the motion's remaining distinctive element—a right to later removal—could not survive scrutiny. PRO's removal right, when pressed on mixed family-commercial content, siblings in the frame, and mirrored clips, collapsed into a vague menu of "restrict, blur, de-monetize or limit" rather than genuine removal. CON crystallized this as an unworkable administrative veto over lawful family speech. PRO established a plausible harm story on scale and labor, but CON successfully isolated the live clash on the removal mechanism and demonstrated that the proposition as written added a censorious, unenforceable veto to reforms CON already supported. CON's sharper progression across rounds and cleaner crystallization in the closing gave it the edge despite clipped rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's dismantling of the removal right in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they showed PRO had retreated from "removal" to a bureaucratic "menu" of platform-tailored restrictions that still left unpaid content and family archives vulnerable while imposing one-party censorship on shared memories.
