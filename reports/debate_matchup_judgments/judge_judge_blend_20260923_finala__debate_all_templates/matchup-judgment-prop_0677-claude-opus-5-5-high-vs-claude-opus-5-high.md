### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that disclosure supplies the missing fact needed to begin investigating otherwise hidden automated decisions. Side A compellingly argued that generic notices may become boilerplate and exposed a circular enforcement problem for evasive employers, but it did not establish that a common, truthful notice is therefore useless. Its chilling-effect claim was also weakened by the low compliance burden and employers' continuing need for scalable screening. Side B more clearly compared the worlds: imperfect notice sometimes unlocks existing rights, while silence never does. Side A's repeated clipped turns were an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest response was that discovery being available does not help unless a worker first knows automation was involved. Side A's later answer—that universal notice creates noise and dishonest employers may send nothing—was sharp, but it showed imperfections shared by disclosure laws generally rather than defeating the value of notice for compliant cases.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): B won the decisive clash on whether this motion's thin, self-certified “method label” actually helps the wronged worker. After A accepted universal notice and employer-side first-instance certification, B showed the label is noise for compliant employers and silence for evasive ones, so it fails the very suspicion-to-discovery path A needed. A’s “rights expire through ignorance” framing and contradiction pressure were strong, but never escaped that internal gap in the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.7–B4.8: self-certifying “no meaningful influence” sends no notice, so the suspicion needed to reach the logs never forms—the exact gap A’s opening identified.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): CON wins on mechanism. PRO's case requires the sentence to create actionable suspicion, but CON forces a dilemma that survives: if keyword filters count, notice is universal boilerplate with no signal about bias [B4.1, A5.2]; if they don't, the nurse gets nothing. PRO embraces the first horn as a public good, abandoning individual contestability. Enforcement compounds it: employer self-certifies meaningfulness [B4.3], so the evasive employer who sends nothing never generates the suspicion needed to subpoena the logs CON says exist [A4.7-A4.8]. A both resolves B's alleged contradiction (redundant to workers, costly to employers) and keeps live alternatives: reasons, audits, discovery, aggregate reporting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4's dual-audience resolution plus enforcement gap - showing compliant employers send identical noise while evasive ones send nothing, so the notice lands where nothing went wrong and stays silent where it did, turning B's own log-existence claim into a trap.
