PRO’s “no branch hurts” argument (legalization either adds homes that lower rents or changes nothing) decisively blunted CON’s skepticism, and its rebuttal that displacement and speculation already occur under restrictive zoning undermined CON’s harmful-branch theory. CON’s failure to directly answer PRO’s pressure question about a net negative mechanism, combined with its inability to overcome PRO’s comparative framing, leaves PRO’s case standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 2 and Closing argument that legalization has no harmful branch—either it produces homes that lower rents or produces nothing—while every harm CON identified already exists under restrictive zoning. This neutralized CON’s theoretical objections and crystallized the decisive comparative question.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO kept the motion's comparative burden front and center - more affordable than today, not perfectly affordable - and built a compounding mechanism (20 years of additional units, lower land cost per home, filtering) that CON never fully neutralized. ### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by maintaining a strict comparative burden: adding 20 years of legal capacity makes housing more affordable than the constrained counterfactual, even if prices do not become absolutely cheap. Side B argued forcefully that labor bottlenecks, land capitalization, and induced demand would neutralize any gains, but Side A successfully rebutted this by explaining that labor markets adjust over two decades and that induced demand would require implausible one-to-one cancellation. Side A suffered execution penalties for repeated word-count clipping, but their underlying economic logic was decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (A4), which effectively neutralized Side B's labor-constraint argument by noting that prohibiting homes does not solve a labor shortage, and that construction capacity would expand over a 20-year horizon given legal certainty.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO successfully defended the core supply-and-demand logic that legalizing density would have produced more housing over two decades, which would have made high-demand American cities more affordable than they are today, even accounting for temporary construction bottlenecks and some demand response. CON raised real causal leaks—construction costs, endogenous demand, and land capitalization—but each was partially addressed by PRO’s total-cost framing, the physical reality of added units, and the argument that CON needed near-perfect demand cancellation to negate the counterfactual. PRO’s concession that weaker-demand cities would see little change did not undermine the motion because the affordability crisis is driven by constrained, growing metros where zoning binds. CON’s rhetorically sharp “permission is not affordability” frame ultimately asked the judge to treat attenuation as negation, which was less persuasive than PRO’s cumulative, comparative case for larger stock and weaker landlord bargaining power.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s “permission is not affordability” chain—especially the bungalow replacement example and the agglomeration argument that added capacity near jobs draws in more high earners, leaving cities bigger but not cheaper—was PRO’s toughest pressure, but PRO parried effectively by showing that the physical units still absorb demand that would otherwise bid up older stock, and that CON’s story required implausibly elastic one-for-one demand cancellation across decades of cumulative construction.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO kept a live supply-demand path while CON conceded key premises. CON admitted zoning blocked some viable projects and that banning homes does not lower rents, then relied on partial leaks—labor bottlenecks, expensive concrete, land capitalization, and induced demand—without showing they sum to zero affordability effect over twenty years. PRO answered each: prohibition guarantees non-construction, wood-frame duplexes/triplexes avoid tower costs, competition limits land windfalls, and demand exists whether or not new homes are built, so preventing them forces bidding on old stock. Extra thousands of homes across many growing metros, plus immediate vacancy chains and gradual filtering, means rents lower than the scarce counterfactual, even if magnitude varies by city.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Pressure Q2 and B4/A4 exchange: PRO forced CON to admit banning a home doesn't cut rents, exposing that CON needs near one-for-one induced demand to cancel supply benefits, which CON could not justify beyond "affluent arrivals."
