### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but A (CON) executed the exchange more decisively. A's recurring "two masters" point—that reservists face genuine conflicts of loyalty with their employers during simultaneous, multi-sector attacks—was never squarely resolved by B, whose best answer ("no mechanism guarantees availability... true of standing units too" [B4.1]) conceded symmetry that A had already distinguished (standing units have one employer, one mission, no competing claim). A's pressure question on sovereign command (A3.2) also forced a costly concession: B's answer that fusion/command "should be permanent—and civilian" [B4.3] handed A the opening to argue that B's own model requires a full-time backbone, undercutting the "reserve-first" thesis and reducing the dispute to a mostly cosmetic civilian-vs-military label rather than reserve-vs-standing substance. B's closing reframe ("the brain can be civilian; the shield must be reserve") is clever but arrives late and doesn't fully dissolve that tension. B's opening and rhetorical framing (2 a.m. hospital example, "who is already inside the terrain") were vivid and original, and B's case on legitimacy and access was never fully dismantled, keeping the debate competitive rather than lopsided. But on the decisive comparative question—whose model better survives a simultaneous, high-stakes crisis—A's dual-loyalty and permanence arguments went comparatively unanswered, giving CON the cleaner path to the judge's decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's use of the pressure-question answers (A4.1–A4.5) to show that B's own "permanent civilian fusion agency" concedes the need for full-time backbone capacity, paired with the unresolved "two masters" employer-conflict problem, was the most damaging and consequential exchange in the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully exposing a structural flaw in Side A's model: during a simultaneous, systemic crisis, reservists would be needed by their civilian employers, meaning a national call-up would simply move scarcity around rather than guarantee availability. Side A conceded this lack of a guarantee, allowing Side B to dominate the final weighing on national readiness and unified command.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal (B4.4) delivered the decisive blow by pointing out that a legal call-up during a systemic crisis does not create extra experts, but "merely decides which attacked employer must lose one."
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON won the decisive availability/access clash. PRO's embedded-talent and legitimacy case is strong, but B showed reserve status itself grants no automatic access — both models need reporting, sensors, and agreements — and that reserve-first assigns highest duty to workforce with dual loyalty. PRO's concession that "No mechanism guarantees availability" [A4.1] was weaponized effectively in B4 and B5, while B answered the visibility pressure. Civilian brain/shield reframe helped A, but B kept primacy on assured fusion and unified command.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R2 A1/A2 - that reporting/sensor interfaces give visibility without reserve primacy, and that statutory call-up just moves scarce experts from one failing employer to another, exposing PRO's concession on availability.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but A's line of attack proved more durable across the exchange. A's central move—that any "permanent civilian staff" capable of directing continuous sovereign functions (attribution, escalation, classified intelligence access) either collapses into a standing command in all but name or lacks the authority to actually run a crisis—was pressed consistently from Rebuttal 1 through the closing, and culminated in a sharp, voter-friendly dilemma. B raised a genuine counter to this (the "equivocation" charge in B4.4, that permanence/discipline doesn't equal militarization) but never fully cashed it out: B's closing dropped that thread rather than pressing it home, leaving A's dilemma as the last word on the table. B's case on expertise and surge capacity (distributed simultaneous response vs. one bottlenecked unit) remained largely unanswered and is a real strength, but A's repeated point about employer veto, conflict-of-interest timing, and the need for a unified sovereign decision-maker during crisis did enough damage to keep that advantage from being decisive. B's Rebuttal 1 being clipped for length is a minor but real execution cost. Both sides handled pressure questions with proper A1/A2 formatting, but A's answers more directly reinforced its core dilemma while B's answers, though clear, didn't fully neutralize the "standing command in disguise" charge. Overall, A gave the cleaner, more sustained reason to vote for its side.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's dilemma in Rebuttal 2/Closing ("a permanent civilian staff either is a standing command in all but name, or lacks authority to direct a crisis") was the most decisive exchange; B's equivocation counter in B4.4 was a strong answer but was not pressed through to the closing, letting A's framing stand as the final word on the central clash.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by cleanly turning Side B's arguments about civilian dependence and access. Side B's attempt to frame any permanent state coordinating body as 'military in all but name' was effectively neutralized by Side A's analogy to civilian emergency services.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive for pointing out that if Side B relies on embedded civilians for access, they inherit all the same employer conflicts and availability problems as Side A, but without the preparedness of a formal reserve.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): CON wins by holding PRO to the "rather than" burden. A shows reserves are useful for breadth and local knowledge, but B shows core functions—attribution, deterrence, prioritization, escalation control—require continuous sovereign command, and that PRO's necessary permanent civilian staff collapses into a standing command or lacks authority. Employer veto and client-conflict pressure, raised in B3 and unanswered by A4, leaves the reserve contingent on private employers precisely when needed most. Clipped R1 also cost PRO.
