### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by defining the motion as confidential identification by competent authorities rather than public exposure, then showing that Side A's preferred court orders and downstream due diligence still require someone to record or know the owner. Side A raised meaningful concerns about corrupt registries, data security, and false filings, but its decisive claim that banks would treat unverified filings as conclusive was asserted despite Side B explicitly preserving ordinary due diligence. Imperfect enforcement did not establish that lawful anonymity is preferable to a legal disclosure duty, liability for false filings, and an investigatory trail.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange was the empty-filing-cabinet response: court-sealed disclosure cannot uncover an owner whom nobody was ever required to identify. Side B then directly answered the false-certification objection by denying that registry filings replace bank due diligence, substantially weakening Side A's central harm comparison.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON)): PRO won by demonstrating that CON's alternative of "targeted, court-controlled access" functionally presupposes the universal record-keeping floor that the motion mandates, and by extracting a clean concession from CON (B4.2) that at least one jurisdiction should be allowed to host companies with no authority knowing the owner. PRO effectively turned CON's vulnerable-group examples and maintained that even imperfect enforcement and liable front-men beat a legal blank. CON's "false certification" critique was sharp but could not overcome the structural incoherence of defending an anonymity escape hatch against a universal ban proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure questions and subsequent rebuttals establishing that court-ordered disclosure is useless without an underlying record, forcing CON to admit that some total anonymity should remain lawful (B4.2) — an admission PRO ruthlessly crystallized into the decisive clash.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO successfully narrowed the debate to the core mechanism of the ban: prohibiting companies where no authority knows the owner. PRO forced CON into a fatal concession by demonstrating that CON's preferred court-controlled access inherently requires a registry to exist, and when pressed, CON admitted a narrow exception for total anonymity to protect dissidents. PRO brilliantly exploited this concession in the closing, showing that any legal loophole for total anonymity becomes the exact escape route for kleptocrats. PRO also effectively neutralized CON's false certification argument by explaining that downstream jurisdictions can prosecute false filings when assets arrive, making an imperfect paper floor vastly superior to a lawful blank.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2, where PRO answers the pressure questions by explaining that false filings become prosecutable evidence when dirty money lands in capable jurisdictions, and dismantles CON's abuse survivor example by showing that a judge can order the registry to disclose, proving CON's model relies on the very record PRO's ban mandates.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): B narrowly won by showing that targeted enforcement either leaves jurisdictions available for regulatory arbitrage or becomes functionally universal once applied everywhere. A powerfully established the danger of ownership records in predatory states, especially for domestic dissidents, but overstated that kleptocrats would lose nothing and never fully showed that transaction-level checks could match the coverage of a common beneficial-ownership rule. B's repeated clipping and incomplete answer to domestic persecution kept the result close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest answer was that targeted pressure merely moves the shell-company industry to the next jurisdiction, while applying the same correspondent-banking squeeze to every fallback jurisdiction effectively recreates B's universal baseline.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): CON persuasively showed that the word "universally" imposes unnecessary and dangerous obligations: it forces authoritarian regimes to compile verified ownership data on domestic dissidents and NGOs while delivering no added anti-money-laundering benefit, since every effective enforcement tool PRO offered—conditioning market access, bank-level due diligence, targeted real-estate checks—operates through risk-based targeting rather than a universal mandate. PRO never recovered from the concession that a company formed inside Russia is already within Russia's reach, and CON successfully absorbed PRO's enforcement arguments into its own targeted framework. B's rebuttals were sharper, more progressive, and better crystallized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's demonstration that risk-based standards let dissidents choose protective jurisdictions (e.g., Swiss banks shielded by Swiss courts), whereas a universal ban forces ownership data into every state's hands—including predatory ones—and that PRO's touted refusal mechanism is functionally the same risk-based discretion CON advocated.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 Pro (thinking) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively attacking the word "universally" in the motion. A demonstrated that a universal ban would force authoritarian regimes to maintain comprehensive registers of domestic beneficial ownership, stripping local dissidents and NGOs of their anonymity. Side B's reliance on "foreign situs" failed to address the plight of domestic actors, and B's concession in the closing that a company formed in Russia is already in Russia's reach effectively handed A the debate. A also successfully co-opted B's enforcement mechanisms as targeted alternatives that do not require a universal mandate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, specifically A1, where A clearly distinguished between the protections of a foreign court evaluating an MLAT request versus a predatory state having direct, unmediated access to a domestic register under a universal mandate.
