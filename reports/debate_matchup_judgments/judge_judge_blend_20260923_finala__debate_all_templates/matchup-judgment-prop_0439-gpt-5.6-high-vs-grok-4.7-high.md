### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON's rebuttals more precisely exploited tensions inherent in PRO's own proposed design. CON's recurring point—that need-based allocation and a bidding ban structurally exclude the impatient/ineligible buyers who actually sustain the black market, while screening produces "sorted but rejected" sellers who are now medically valued and still need cash—was a sharp, design-specific critique that PRO never fully neutralized, instead falling back on general assertions ("reduction doesn't require serving every buyer") without explaining why the marginal black-market customer or newly-marketable rejected seller would actually disappear. PRO's framing that the motion only requires "reduction, not perfection" was strategically strong and correctly calibrated the burden, but CON directly engaged that standard by arguing the design creates offsetting new leakage (price transparency aiding side-deals, workup-enhanced rejected sellers) rather than merely conceding imperfection. CON's closing crystallized this mismatch cleanly, while PRO's closing largely reasserted its supply-side story without closing the specific gaps CON had opened. Both sides met format requirements for Q&A and answers, but CON's answers (A1/A2) more directly dismantled PRO's mechanism, giving CON the more decisive rebuttal exchange overall.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to A1 (A4.1-A4.3), showing that need-based allocation gives the kidney to whoever ranks highest on medical need—not to the impatient buyer about to turn to a broker—directly undercut PRO's central "the switcher is the eligible shortage-driven patient" claim, and was never squarely resolved by PRO's closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A systematically dismantled Side B's mechanisms by maintaining strict comparative discipline. Side A proved that Prohibition inherently rejects all compensated sellers (neutralizing B's 'rejected seller' harm) and clearly explained how expanding system-wide supply reduces wait times and overall desperation, defeating B's narrow focus on queue-jumpers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3) was decisive in pointing out that Prohibition rejects all compensated donors anyway, effectively neutralizing B's main argument about the harms of rejected sellers.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won on the proposition's two-part test. The sole-public-purchaser model moved the debate from morality to comparative harm: under prohibition all paid transplants are underground with zero protection, while regulation shifts marginal transactions into screening, consent testing, and follow-up and shrinks the shortage that pushes eligible patients toward brokers. CON's core mismatch argument—that the legal kidney goes to a different patient than the would-be black-market buyer—never answered the queue-level effect and concedes more lawful kidneys. The life-years ledger also favored PRO once donor risk was weighed against dialysis burdens.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's B2/B4 answer on reduction vs abolition — that an added legal kidney removes a recipient from the queue, shortens waits system-wide, and denies brokers shortage-driven customers even if exact matching differs — which directly undercut A's claim that serving someone else is not a customer lost.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently and followed format requirements (Q1/Q2, A1/A2) correctly, and both openings suffered equal clipping costs. The decisive divergence emerged in the middle rounds: Side A's "false identity" move (A2.1) — that legalization would reach a population never touched by black-market brokers — forced Side B into an unresolved tension between claiming displacement (harm reduction) and claiming addition (life-year gain) for the same pool of transactions. B's rebuttals offered genuine regulatory design (private interviews, non-assignable payment, cooling-off) that answered the coercion-detection question reasonably, but never cleanly separated which transplants were displaced versus added, nor quantified crowd-out of altruistic donation or long-term donor harms beyond immediate surgical mortality. A exploited this gap consistently, adding a plausible external-validity critique (existing low-complication data comes from a screened, socially supported volunteer population, not commercially recruited sellers) that B did not squarely rebut. A's closing crystallized this into a clean, memorable logical challenge — that B was counting the same transplants as both a black-market harm avoided and a newly created life-year — which stood unanswered because A held the last speech. B's "judge against the ban, not utopia" framing was a fair and important point, but it did not fully discharge PRO's burden to show the two comparative claims in the motion, leaving A with the cleaner, more damaging comparative account by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's turn 4 "false identity" argument (A2.1–A2.2), later crystallized in the closing (A5.1–A5.2), which exposed that Side B's case implicitly required both displacement and addition without reconciling or quantifying either — a tension B never squarely resolved.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side A won by relentlessly maintaining the comparative baseline of the current ban's failures. Side B's argument regarding 'additionality' and 'crowd-out' was sharp, but Side A successfully countered that the massive existing waitlist makes it highly improbable that paid supply would fail to outpace displaced altruistic supply. Side A also decisively proved that shifting eligible participants to a regulated market inherently shrinks the black market, even if a residue of ineligible buyers remains.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 brilliantly dismantled the 'crowd-out' and 'expansion' arguments by clarifying that new paid sellers provide net life-years, while eligible black-market participants shift to the legal channel, leaving only a smaller 'residue' underground.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): B kept the comparative test alive: a licensed fixed-price channel with mandatory aftercare versus today’s broker + dialysis deaths. Even if residual black-market cases remain and some new pressured sellers appear, B showed plausible partial displacement and net additive transplants. A’s additionality/crowd-out and hidden-coercion critiques landed but stayed at speculative scale and did not establish that donor long-term costs or replacement effects cancel years gained or restore black-market size. Reduction does not require abolition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second-rebuttal point that a private interview and formally unassignable payment cannot detect a rehearsed threat or stop a creditor demanding cash after deposit, directly undercutting B’s claim that procedure solves coercion.
