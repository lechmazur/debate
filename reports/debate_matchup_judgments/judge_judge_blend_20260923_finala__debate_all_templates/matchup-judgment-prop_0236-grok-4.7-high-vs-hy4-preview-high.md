### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-adapted cases—A's "concealment is the offense, not the tool" thesis versus B's "assignments build skill, not product" framing. The pivotal exchange came in the pressure questions: A forced B to admit that whenever an essay is genuinely graded on unaided origination, the ban model must relocate that assessment into supervised, controlled space [B4.1], since home writing "cannot be certified" either way. A capitalized cleanly on this in closing, showing that B's mature position converges toward A's own structure (default permission with opt-outs where origination truly matters), while B's version demands a much larger disruption to ordinary homework practice under the same label of "ban." B's counters (theater/complacency, "cite and ship," the vivid "autopsy of formation" line) were sharp and kept the debate close, but never fully dislodged A's core efficiency claim that disclosure-based defaults are more enforceable and less dishonesty-inducing than a nominally strict rule that cannot actually be checked on take-home work. A's rebuttal 1 was clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost, but the surviving content and A's subsequent speeches recovered the thread and delivered the more decisive closing resolution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure question about whether the ban requires scrapping most take-home essays, and B's forced concession in Rebuttal 2 that assessed origination must move into supervised space, was the turning point—A then used that concession in closing to show its own framework already accounted for exactly that limit, sharpening the comparative case in its favor.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that unaided origination can still be required and supervised when it is the learning goal, while a broad ban adds an unverifiable prohibition to take-home work. Side A compellingly explained how cited outsourcing can undermine skill formation, but its eventual solution of relocating meaningful assessment into controlled settings largely conceded B's task-specific restriction model. B's clipped first rebuttal was an execution cost, but not enough to erase its stronger comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange forced Side A to admit that reliably grading unaided origination requires supervised assessment. B then showed that this safeguard is already compatible with default permission, leaving the broad ban to govern work that neither side believes can be authenticated at home.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a remarkably tight and high-quality debate by successfully cornering CON on the enforceability of take-home work. CON argues powerfully that default permission turns skill formation into "delegated production," but PRO forces CON to admit that home origination cannot be verified. When CON argues schools must relocate origination to supervised spaces and stop grading take-home essays as unaided, PRO cleanly captures this concession: CON's supervised solution is identical to PRO's classroom opt-out. This leaves CON defending a blind ban on remaining homework that it admits it cannot check. PRO's pragmatic argument that unenforceable bans merely teach concealment ultimately edges out CON's normative stance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 and Closing sequence, which points out that CON's concession to relocate origination into controlled spaces is functionally identical to PRO's classroom opt-out, leaving CON's ban to govern only the unchecked homework where it inevitably breeds concealment.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but B built a tighter, more coherent line of attack that survived the full exchange. B's opening framing—"covert use under a ban is a cheating problem; open substitution under permission is a credential problem"—identified the structural weakness in A's transparency mechanism early, and B's pressure question (Q2) forced A to concede in Rebuttal 2 that "any credential claiming independent mastery must include a supervised performance" (proctored writing, live proofs, oral exams). B correctly seized this as a decisive concession: if genuine certification requires a restriction-like checkpoint anyway, then the "permissible by default plus transparency" regime never actually does the certifying work A claims for it, and effectively reintroduces bans through the back door for anything that matters. A's closing tried to compartmentalize this ("targeted instruments" vs. "the ordinary middle") but never resolved why the ordinary, unchecked middle should be trusted to certify judgment if its own side agrees mastery requires a model-free checkpoint. A had strong opening material (equity critique of bans, citation-as-map-of-authorship, apprenticeship framing) and made reasonable counters (bans also can't verify, unexamined essays hide outside help too), but its central mechanism—process logs and occasional oral spot-checks—was shown by B to be gameable, unscalable, and ultimately non-load-bearing once A conceded the checkpoint requirement. B's rebuttals were sharper and more consistently exploited this fault line across rounds, giving it the cleaner, more resolved path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 response to A's answer on unaided performance ([B4.5]-[B4.6]): "That instrument belongs to the restriction, not to default permission... If the grade means anything only after an unaided checkpoint, transparency was never the certificate." This directly weaponized A's own concession from the pressure-question exchange and was never adequately neutralized in A's closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): B won a close debate by offering the better comparative policy: transparent AI-assisted work for ordinary assignments alongside targeted unaided checkpoints for independent mastery. A sharply exposed how logs can be fabricated, but repeatedly conflated permissible AI use with automatically crediting substitution and treated targeted restrictions as a concession to broad bans. A also never showed why an unenforced authorship rule produces more trustworthy evidence than disclosed process plus selective verification.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s unaided-performance challenge was decisive: independent mastery can be tested through proctored checkpoints without banning AI across ordinary coursework. This preserved the proposition while undercutting A’s claim that any such checkpoint conceded the motion.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully proving that process logs do not certify the student's ability to do the work, only their ability to supervise or stage AI. A also trapped B into conceding that real verification requires unaided (AI-restricted) performance, cleanly undercutting B's core mechanism that transparency itself verifies learning.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of the driving analogy in Rebuttal 1, noting that a learner driver is actually driving, whereas a transparency log merely records a student narrating a machine's driving.
