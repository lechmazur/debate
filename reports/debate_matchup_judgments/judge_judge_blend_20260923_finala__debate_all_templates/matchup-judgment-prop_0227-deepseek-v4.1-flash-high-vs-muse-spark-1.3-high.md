### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built a case around a concrete, unresolved emergency scenario and pressed PRO (B) into two damaging concessions that were never repaired: (1) no emergency exception exists, so "safe harbor" only immunizes reporting, not the actual payment decision, meaning executives who pay still go underground rather than to the state; and (2) PRO's own admission that support "cannot perform magic" against leak-only extortion, where no backup or forensic team can unpublish stolen data, meaning the ban strips victims of their only lever without actually eliminating the incentive to pay covertly. A's "lifeboat vs. draining the ocean" framing crystallized this: the ban's certain present harm is traded for a speculative aggregate benefit that B never showed survives evasion, shadow brokers, and leak extortion. B's case (business model/collective-action framing, shrinking the "richest payer pool") was coherent and well-organized, and its pressure questions were sharp, but its answers in Rebuttal 2 largely reasserted the aggregate theory ("if too slow, that's an argument for speed, not for keeping the ransom economy alive") rather than closing the mechanism gap A kept exploiting. Both sides executed the format well (labeled A1/A2, clean PQ rounds), but A's rebuttals did more actual damage to B's live route, while B's closing mostly restated its framing without neutralizing the safe-harbor and leak-extortion critiques.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.3), which used B's own concessions—no emergency exception and "cannot perform magic" on leak extortion—to argue that the ban sacrifices tonight's victim for speculative future gains while driving payments underground and destroying the intelligence trail, a point B's closing never fully answered.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): PRO wins because it offered the only coherent mechanism that directly shrinks the global ransomware revenue pool while pairing the ban with funded victim support. The edge is narrow because CON successfully exposed tension in PRO's safe-harbor design and leak-extortion fallback, but PRO's weighing—that policy must count the future attacks a ransom guarantees, not merely the single crisis it might resolve—remained the cleaner comparative standard after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over data-leak extortion and the "false rescue" of payment (B2.6, A4.3, B4.2), where PRO showed leak models still depend on revenue that a major-market ban would constrict, while CON landed the counter that desperation and irreversible shame would drive payments underground regardless.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully framing the debate around the collective-action trap of ransomware, arguing convincingly that individual rescues guarantee systemic ruin. Side A's systemic weighing ultimately proved more robust than B's localized, victim-centric framing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but CON (A) landed the more decisive structural blows. The pressure-question exchange was pivotal: A's Q1 forced B to articulate a "destination plus bargain/intent" legal test for catching disguised ransom payments, and A then exploited this in Rebuttal 2 and Closing by noting that if governments possess the tracing/beneficiary-identification tools needed to enforce such a test, those same tools could be used to sanction and disrupt cash-out points directly—making the ban's added value uncertain while its costs (criminalizing desperate victims) remain concrete. A's Q2 on timing also extracted a real concession from B ("no law can teleport responders"), and B's fix—narrow life-safety carve-outs and reporting immunity—inadvertently softened the "hard ban" premise that B's entire deterrence logic depended on (attackers must believe payment is truly impossible). B's rebuttals were substantive and rhetorically vivid (the "pity the first hospital, forget the next ten" framing, the piracy-analogy correction, the reporting-incentive flip), but they never fully resolved the redundancy problem or the exemption-erodes-deterrence tension that A kept pressing. A's closing crystallized this into a clean, memorable dilemma ("if tools work, ban unnecessary; if they falter, ban criminalizes the wounded") that was easier to use as a decision rule than B's closing, which mostly reasserted the starvation logic without resolving the carve-out contradiction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.2) turning B's own "destination plus bargain/intent" enforcement test back on itself—arguing that if governments can trace beneficiaries and intent well enough to police a ban, they could use those same tools to sanction and disrupt cash-out points without needing to criminalize victims—was the most decisive exchange, and A repeated it cleanly in Closing without B fully neutralizing it.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won by demonstrating that CON's alternative of funding resilience without prohibiting payments leaves the core extortion incentive intact, effectively subsidizing attackers while merely managing victim harm. CON's enforcement dilemma and victim-harm arguments were sharp, but PRO successfully answered the hospital deadline concern by emphasizing preventive support, showing that payment often fails anyway, and—crucially—identifying a collective-action problem that CON's non-criminalization approach cannot solve: without a ban, executives still face liability for refusing to pay and will continue fueling the ransomware economy. PRO's legal test for disguised payments and its framing of the policy as a finite transition cost versus perpetual extortion provided the cleaner comparative frame after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal [A4] directly answering CON's pressure questions with the "destination plus bargain" legal test and the preventive deterrence argument, then pivoting to crystallize that CON's plan amounts to a permanent subsidy for gangs—this exchange defined the decisive clash.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by providing concrete, plausible policy mechanisms that neutralized Side B's strongest practical attacks. When pressed on how to distinguish ransoms from legitimate consulting fees, A offered a grounded "destination + bargain" test analogous to sanctions enforcement. When pressed on the danger of prosecuting desperate victims, A clarified that the law targets executives and insurers, not caregivers, and includes life-safety carveouts. Side B's closing argument—that tracing payments to enforce a domestic ban is the same as tracing them to dismantle foreign attacker infrastructure—ignored the jurisdictional realities of cybercrime. Side A's causal story that a ban forces ex ante investment in resilience was cleaner and better defended.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they answered the pressure questions by delineating the "destination + bargain" legal test and clarifying that prohibition targets the business decision of executives and insurers rather than jailing caregivers under duress, effectively defusing Side B's most emotionally resonant attacks.
