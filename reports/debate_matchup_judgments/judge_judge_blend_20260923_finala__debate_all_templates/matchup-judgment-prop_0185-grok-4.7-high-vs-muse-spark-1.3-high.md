### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that structural balance depends on revisable estimates and that constitutional entrenchment turns forecasting errors into legal rigidity. Side A compellingly established deficit bias and explained how correction accounts, courts, and supermajority escape clauses could constrain it, but those additions strengthened B’s dilemma: if enforced strictly, the rule risks delayed procyclical cuts and emergency vetoes; if routinely paused, revised, or suspended, legislative discretion still does the essential work. B also retained the largely unanswered point that borrowing can finance long-lived investment rather than merely current consumption.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange targeted A’s correction account and emergency hatch: postponed repayment can still bite during a weak recovery, while permitting pauses and suspensions makes the system replicable through ordinary law. PRO never overcame the epistemic problem that structural balance is a contestable estimate, nor explained why ordinary statute could not replicate the "daylight" procedures without entrenching a gamed forecast above the legislature. CON's "discretion in constitutional costume" framing crystallized the decisive comparative: the rule priced correction, not error, and made the wrong year harder to leave.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 and Closing effectively neutralized PRO's notional-account and emergency-hatch defenses by showing that deferring correction merely spreads procyclical damage over time and that supermajority votes are still politics, not higher-law constraint.

Side A also successfully pressed Side B on the core vulnerability of the CON case: the inability of ordinary statutes to prevent boom-year borrowing. Side B relied heavily on the semantic argument that constitutional rules with exceptions are merely "discretion in constitutional costume," but failed to explain why a statutory version of these mechanisms would survive the exact electoral pressures that Side A identified. Side A's comparative weighing—a correctable rule against an incorrigible bias—was decisive and cleanly resolved the central clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they directly and cleanly answered the pressure questions about how the correction account functions during weak recoveries (pausing the correction while capping cumulative drift) and weaponized Side B's failure to name a single statutory mechanism that has ever stopped boom-year borrowing.
