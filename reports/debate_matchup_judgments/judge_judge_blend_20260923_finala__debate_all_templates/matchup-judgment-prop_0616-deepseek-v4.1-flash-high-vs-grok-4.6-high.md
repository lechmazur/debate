### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Both sides ran a coherent, well-structured case with no fabricated evidence and good adherence to format (both answered pressure questions with proper A1/A2 labels). A's core mechanism — that frontier equipment gates the "next increment" of compute needed for advanced military AI, and that the motion's conceded cost is simply the price of that delay — was clear and consistently applied. But B's opening introduced a sharper, more original mechanism (restriction triggers a state-priority "crash program" that converts a dependent customer into an autarkic rival, destroying the very allied scale that funds the tech lead) and sustained it through the debate. Critically, B's dilemma — "if substitution is hard, the cost is still immediate and the fence is leaky; if it's easy, the fence fails outright" — was never squarely dissolved by A, who repeatedly answered with the vague claim that "transfer of initiative is a hypothetical endpoint," without explaining why the years supposedly bought for the defender aren't simultaneously years the adversary spends successfully decoupling. B's "different clocks, same window" rebuttal directly exploited this gap and went unanswered in A's closing, which mostly repackaged the "replenishment race" framing rather than resolving the timing mismatch. That left B with the cleaner, more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's "different clocks, same window" argument in Rebuttal 2 (B4.5-B4.7), which showed that the years A claims are "bought" by restriction are the same years the adversary spends building industrial independence — collapsing A's temporal framing and going substantially unanswered in A's closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully proving that advanced nodes are uniquely necessary for military AI, specifically highlighting the performance-per-watt constraints on edge platforms. Side A inadvertently reinforced B's point by conceding that power is already a binding constraint, which made B's case for denying the power-efficient advanced nodes highly convincing. Furthermore, B effectively absorbed A's commercial impacts using the motion's text.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's argument in B4.1 that mature nodes impose brutal performance-per-watt penalties, making advanced nodes essential for edge inference on constrained platforms, neatly dismantled A's core premise that military AI can rely on older chips.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won the central comparison: day-one iteration on installed stock vs replenishment race for frontier training, sensor fusion and low-power edge. CON (A) never dislodged the claim that mature nodes impose power/bandwidth penalties and stockpiles deplete, while its "commercial cost = security cost" was blunted by the motion conceding major cost and by PRO's offset argument. CON's autarky-transfer framing was strong but PRO turned it into the delay mechanism itself - years spent duplicating lithography/etch is still delay behind the allied frontier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4 turn that a crash program is the delay mechanism, not a refutation, plus concrete stall missions - frontier multimodal training and low-power edge inference - which directly answered A's Q1 about which missions cannot iterate on old nodes.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases around a genuine chokepoint theory versus a leakage/sanction-paradox theory, but CON did the sharper comparative work. CON's "choose one" pressure in Rebuttal 1 exposed a real tension in PRO's framing (frontier-only chokepoint vs. broad "military AI" claims) that PRO never fully resolved, only narrowing its claim to frontier training without squarely rebutting the internal inconsistency. CON's pressure questions and subsequent rebuttal also successfully shifted the burden onto PRO to demonstrate that the ban produces a durable, not merely leaky, delay; PRO's closing largely reasserted its mechanism (tacit knowledge, upstream tools) rather than answering that shift, and slightly overclaimed CON's conditional concession about a five-year threshold. CON's "sanction paradox" and "subsidy with a security label" framing gave the judge a cleaner, more original lens for weighing certain industrial costs against uncertain strategic delay, and CON's closing crystallized this by noting PRO "restates the prize" rather than proving delivery. PRO's case remained strong and rhetorically polished (concrete EUV/etch/metrology grounding, "sell the factory" framing), but it never neutralized CON's core causal doubt about whether the ban reliably buys the delay it claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 point that PRO "cannot have it both ways: narrow enough to make tool bans decisive, broad enough to cover military AI" [B2.3], combined with the Rebuttal 2 burden-shift ("the burden is on PRO to price the trade, not on CON to say never" [B4.4]), which PRO's closing did not squarely answer.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing semiconductor manufacturing equipment as the ultimate, un-bypassable physical bottleneck. Side A created a strategic contradiction by arguing that the adversary could just use imported accelerators and cloud compute, while simultaneously proposing downstream controls on those exact things as a better alternative. Side B capitalized on this by pointing out that policing dual-use boards and cloud access is vastly more leaky than stopping massive EUV lines.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's argument that black markets move boards, not EUV lines, neutralizing Side A's claims about leakiness while exposing the weakness of Side A's proposed downstream alternatives.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): PRO won the chokepoint comparison. CON's best path — that military AI can keep training on imported/cloud accelerators and older-node inference — was met with B's sharp distinction between rented compute and a fab: you cannot mass-produce leading-edge training accelerators without lithography/etch/metrology, and EUV lines don't smuggle like boards. B answered A1/A2 directly on years-of-lag and captive demand, while A conceded a 5-year lag would justify cost, which B then anchored to tools' tacit-knowledge delay. CON's sanction-paradox and R&D-erosion claims were plausible but mitigated by concentration and offset arguments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1-B2.7 / B4.3-B4.4 — training vs battlefield chips and boards vs EUV lines — which directly neutralized CON's older-node and black-market leakage route.
