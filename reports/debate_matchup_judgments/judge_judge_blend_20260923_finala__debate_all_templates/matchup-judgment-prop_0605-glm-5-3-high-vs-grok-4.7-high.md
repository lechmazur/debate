### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that ending employer permission does not erase notice periods, labor law, time limits, or an employer's liability for obligations incurred during employment. Side A's case repeatedly assumed that portability automatically creates an unregulated vacuum and lets former employers shed existing debts, but never adequately established either claim. B also weighed credible exit as an immediate check on abuse, while A's enforcement model depended on the abuser remaining the worker's immigration gatekeeper. A offered a strong and original governance concern, but B made the cleaner comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's answer that the motion removes employer veto power rather than all contract and immigration regulation decisively undercut Side A's textual attack. Notice rules can protect staffing without giving employers control over legal status, and payroll liability can survive a worker's departure.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively weaponizing the unique demographic context of the Gulf states, proving that kafala is fundamentally about state capacity to manage temporary populations rather than mere labor dynamics. Side A's framing that the motion 'frees the sponsor from the worker' brilliantly flipped Side B's core argument about liability. Furthermore, Side B's closing relied on the false claim that Side A never answered the distinction between notice periods and employer permission, which Side A had thoroughly dismantled in A4.4.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.4) decisively answered both of Side B's pressure questions, explaining how the state leverages the sponsor's desire for future permits to collect debts, and sharply distinguishing notice periods (timing a departure) from permission (guaranteeing an approved employer for the stay).
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully turning Side A's strongest point—sponsor liability—into a fatal flaw, arguing that a liable party who also controls the worker's legal status and ability to complain is a judge with a conflict of interest. Side B's mechanism of "automatic" enforcement through exit rights was more convincing than Side A's reliance on state inspection of an abusive sponsor. While Side A made a strong structural case about managing temporary migration, their textual arguments about notice periods felt pedantic compared to B's holistic and pragmatic defense of the motion's direction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of the "sponsor liability" argument by framing the sponsor as an abuser with a conflict of interest, effectively neutralizing Side A's core mechanism for state enforcement and shifting the burden to the practical reality of exit rights.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by consistently distinguishing state regulation of migration from an employer’s private veto over job switching. Side A’s demographic case largely attacked an untethered residence right that the proposition did not require, while its bureaucracy and permit-trading objections never established why ordinary state verification necessitates sponsor control. Side B also showed that complaint-triggered relief comes too late because fear of visa loss suppresses complaints in the first place. Side A raised credible implementation concerns, but did not justify retaining the decisive source of coercive leverage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response to Side A’s targeted-remedy proposal was decisive: if the sponsor veto must be suspended once abuse is alleged, the veto is functioning as leverage rather than migration control. Side B then explained why post-complaint protection cannot cure the chilling effect created before a worker dares to complain.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by cleanly distinguishing between state administrative processing and the coercive leverage of a private employer veto. Side B effectively neutralized Side A's strongest alternative—a complaint-triggered suspension of the veto—by pointing out that relying on a complaint while the employer still holds the threat of deportation ensures most workers will never complain.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing dismantled Side A's "complaint-triggered suspension" by showing that the threat of the veto prevents the very complaint needed to trigger the protection.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by decisively isolating the employer's veto as the core mechanism of abuse and exposing the practical impossibility of Side A's "complaint-triggered" alternative, which requires a captive worker to trigger their own rescue. Side A's administrative burden argument regarding the "tether" was strong, but Side B's conceptual dismantling of the sponsor's "private key" and the contradiction in Side A's targeted reform was more persuasive on the motion's core principle.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's "complaint-triggered suspension" in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, pointing out that a remedy requiring the victim to complain while the accused still holds the power to collapse their visa is practically useless and concedes that the veto itself is the leverage, not a migration control tool.
