### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides ran tight, well-structured cases and executed the Pressure Questions/Answers format correctly. The decisive clash was CON's "escape hatch" attack: if quota obligations can be discharged through money and operations rather than actual hosting, the motion's core mechanism (moving people) is unnecessary. PRO neutralized this more successfully than CON exploited it, reframing obligatory, formula-calculated contributions as "quotas under another name" and forcing CON to concede that its own "obligatory targeted support," enforced via budget conditionality, is structurally similar—differing mainly in instrument (money vs. placement), not in the presence of binding allocation. CON's distinction (money is quantifiable/auditable, hosting forces placement into communities) is coherent but never fully escapes PRO's reframing. PRO's closing "one emergency room receiving every ambulance" analogy also lands harder against CON's efficiency-based redefinition of "burden" than CON's "fund the triage" rebuttal lands back, since CON never squarely answers why funded capacity resolves the physical concentration and political burden PRO keeps foregrounding. CON's case remains strong and disciplined, especially on secondary movement and backlash risk, but PRO's handling of the central tension and its more resonant closing image give it a narrow but clear edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's move in Rebuttal 2 (A1) turning CON's "obligatory targeted support" into "quotas under another name," combined with PRO's closing ambulance/emergency-room analogy, which CON's "fund the triage" response only partially answers.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by distinguishing mandatory solidarity from mandatory relocation. It directly answered how frontline burdens could be shared through enforceable funding, personnel, processing, and capacity while preserving its objections about non-compliance, secondary movement, and backlash. Side B persuasively established the unfairness of the status quo, but increasingly defined any formula-based obligation as a quota. Its allowance that shares could be satisfied entirely through money or operations weakened the claim that host-allocation quotas were necessary and left Side A's feasibility comparison largely intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A4.1-A4.4 response was decisive: it accepted enforceable obligations, explained why hosting is uniquely difficult, and showed that Side B's funding-and-operations escape hatch either makes compulsory relocation unnecessary or dilutes quotas into Side A's alternative. Side B's “quotas under another name” reply was sharp but relied too heavily on redefining the disputed mechanism.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON exposed a structural tension in PRO’s case between binding relocation and PRO’s own financial opt-out: if quotas can be satisfied through money and operations, the compulsory hosting mechanism is unnecessary; if hosting is mandatory, the system faces the non-compliance and backlash CON predicted. CON’s alternative—enforceable capacity support, common processing, and surge funding at the external border—offered a coherent path to relieving frontline states without the sovereignty fractures. PRO never fully resolved why an allocative formula for people is indispensable if an allocative formula for resources can suffice, and CON’s sharper weighing of this clash in the final speeches created the cleaner route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal (A4) turned PRO’s financial-contribution escape hatch into a dilemma: either the obligation is met by money and operations, making the hosting quota redundant, or it requires forced placement, which is politically explosive and unenforceable. This framed the remainder of the debate and was never fully answered.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON's central analytic move—distinguishing "immediate/dockside" burden (beds, healthcare, screening, throughput) from "eventual/allocated" burden—was the sharpest structural frame in the debate, and CON sustained it across every round. The decisive exchange came when PRO, answering CON's pressure question about refusal (A3.2), fell back on "pre-committed EU-funded reception capacity and relocation slots" (B4.2) as the remedy for a state that refuses its quota. CON immediately exploited this: that fallback is CON's own operational-solidarity model, meaning the thing actually solving the crisis is EU-funded capacity, not the mandatory allocation formula itself. PRO's closing tried to recover by arguing quotas supply the "lawful basis" for assigning responsibility, but this is a more abstract, less concrete answer than CON's repeated dilemma (flexible quotas aren't binding; binding quotas provoke the resistance that makes them hollow). PRO's case remained persuasive on principle (geography shouldn't determine duty, assurance beats discretion) but never cleanly rebutted the capacity-vs-placement distinction once CON turned PRO's own concession against it. Both sides handled the Q&A format correctly, but CON's answers were more concretely mechanistic (assessed contributions, structural fund conditionality, EU agency deployment) while PRO's answers leaned on repeated assertion of "assurance" without fully closing the gap CON identified.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3-A4.5), which seized on PRO's own answer to the refusal question (B4.2: EU-funded substitution) to argue that the actual remedy PRO offers is identical to CON's operational-solidarity model, making mandatory quotas the non-distinctive, and arguably superfluous, element of PRO's case.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B won by distinguishing unavoidable initial intake from the much larger, prolonged burden of housing, adjudication, and support. Side A repeatedly proved that quotas cannot eliminate day-one dockside work, but the proposition did not require that; it required preventing frontline states from managing most arrivals overall. Side A’s funding-and-throughput alternative remained vulnerable because it supplied no mandatory destination for successful claimants or people awaiting lengthy decisions. Side B’s enforcement fallback was imperfect, but its closing clearly explained why capacity alone leaves the arrival state as the default jurisdiction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s most decisive response was that the hardest burden is not registration at landing but the months or years afterward. This exposed Side A’s fixation on same-day capacity as incomplete and supported B’s closing point that, without mandatory placement, frontline states remain Europe’s waiting room even if better funded.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON won by demonstrating that quotas do not relieve the immediate dockside burden and by turning PRO's own enforcement fallback—pre-committed EU-funded reception and relocation slots—into support for CON's operational-solidarity model. This stripped the quota of its distinctive necessity and showed that mandatory allocation adds political conflict without guaranteeing the throughput that actually helps frontline states. PRO never fully recovered from this exchange or overcame CON's consistent weighing that the scarce resource is capacity, not a map of legal obligations.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (Turn 8), where it answered PRO's pressure questions and showed that PRO's own mechanism for handling quota refusal—EU-funded substitution and relocation—was functionally identical to the operational solidarity CON had proposed, making the quota layer redundant or counterproductive.
