This makes B's path to victory clearer and less internally strained by the end of the debate.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and engaged directly with pressure questions using proper A1/A2 format. A's core asymmetry argument—that ecological damage from conventional inputs is cumulative and hard to reverse while fiscal misallocation to regenerative payments can be corrected via regional cuts—survived the exchange reasonably intact, and A used the "chemicals had no exit clause" distinction to answer B's strongest structural point (that matching scale merely creates a second entrenched clientele). B's best move was seizing on A's early concession that only "how fast" regenerative systems match yields remains open, and hammering it as an admission that the motion is a production wager; B's "photocopy, scale first measure later" framing was memorable and its alternative (outcome-tied contracts) offered a genuine competing instrument rather than mere denial. But B never fully explained why smaller, non-parity funding would actually induce the systemic farmer transition A insists only equal-scale money can achieve, and B's critique that A's cut-trigger is "not a trigger" landed but didn't fully dislodge A's underlying irreversibility logic. B's Rebuttal 1 was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost that cost B some follow-through. On balance A's case held together slightly better through the later rounds and its closing crystallized the asymmetry more cleanly, giving it a narrow but clear edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The pivotal exchange was A's concession in [A2.1] that only the *speed* of yield-matching remains open, which B repeatedly weaponized as proof the motion is an unproven production wager, versus A's counter in [A4.6] that unlike chemical subsidies, regenerative parity comes with a built-in regional exit/cut mechanism—this exchange defined the debate's central fault line on risk symmetry.

Side B convincingly argues that matching the scale of an entrenched, unmeasured system simply duplicates the original policy error and creates a second unkillable political clientele, rather than unwinding the first.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 handling of the pressure questions, specifically pointing out that opposing the motion allows for shrinking the conventional budget rather than preserving it at full size, which cleanly neutralized Side A's main offensive.
