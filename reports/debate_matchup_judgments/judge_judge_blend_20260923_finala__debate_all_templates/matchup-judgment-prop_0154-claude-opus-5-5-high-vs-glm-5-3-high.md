### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): B won by converting A’s own concessions into a cleaner comparative: if labs can identify works well enough to pay, identification was never the barrier; voluntary collective licensing can solve pooling and transaction costs without abolishing the right to refuse or installing a tribunal price. A’s live path—check versus nothing for the long tail, plus cable/music-style mass-reuse precedents—stayed vivid and realistic, but B better answered the decisive burdens on unsettled law, opt-out, unclaimed royalties, and what “compulsory” uniquely adds. After the full exchange, B made the vote easier: keep consent and let markets/collectives price, rather than legalize taking at a regulated rate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 turning A4.1/A4.3 against PRO: labs’ existing manifests undercut the “impossible to identify” premise, while the no-opt-out admission isolates compulsion as pure loss of refusal rather than payment machinery.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): CON won the necessity and rights clash. PRO's core was transaction costs and unenforceable veto, but PRO conceded no opt-out [A4.3], escrow for unfindable owners [A4.2], and that labs already track training data [A4.1]. CON turned those into a decisive route: if labs can identify for distribution they can identify to ask permission, and collective licensing solves transaction costs without compulsion. Voluntary pooling preserves the illustrator's right to refuse and keeps litigation/disclosure leverage, while compulsory adds only a tribunal-set price and permanent surrender. PRO never answered why compulsion is needed over voluntary collective licensing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.4-B4.5 — B uses PRO's own admission that labs keep data cards/manifests to destroy the "impossible to identify" premise from A1.1, showing voluntary licensing is feasible and compulsion is not required.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built a more compelling and historically grounded case, effectively demonstrating that compulsory licensing is the standard copyright remedy for the exact market failure (high transaction costs and fragmented ownership) present in AI training. Side A successfully neutralized Side B's core "consent" argument by highlighting that the right to refuse is practically unenforceable for the long tail of creators, making a guaranteed payment vastly preferable to a theoretical veto. Side A's closing distinction between identification and transaction costs decisively answered Side B's final attack, while Side B's reliance on pending litigation felt like a weaker alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it directly answered the pressure questions, admitted the lack of an opt-out, and powerfully reframed the "right to refuse" as an unenforceable legal fiction for individual creators, pivoting to the tangible value of guaranteed compensation.
