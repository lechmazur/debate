### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B's rebuttal chain did more damage. A's "floor not throne" framing and conflict-of-interest point were strong, but B's repeated mechanism—that friction and consent prompts do not stop social engineering, and that a legally mandated "outside door" turns every trusted app into a phishing vector—went largely unanswered. In Rebuttal 2, A's proposed fixes (separate consent, cooling-off, platform-enforced KYC) were met by B's sharp point that this either fails against deception ("three taps or one") or amounts to conscripting the gatekeeper to guarantee rivals' safety while stripping its control, a structural contradiction A never resolved. B also more effectively weighed harms asymmetrically (lost distribution vs. drained savings), a comparison A's closing didn't rebut, instead reasserting its general framing. A's pressure questions exposed real gaps in B's accountability model, but B's answers (courts/antitrust as remedy, fraud objection surviving licensing) were direct and specific, satisfying the A1/A2 format cleanly, while A's answers to B's questions were more optimistic assertion than resolution of the core deception mechanism. The debate narrowed well, and B's closing crystallized the decisive clash more sharply.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 point [B4.3]-[B4.4], showing that A's proposed friction-based safeguards (separate consent, cooling-off) do not defeat social engineering and that mandating outside payment doors turns trusted apps into phishing vectors even for cautious users, was the most damaging exchange, since A's closing never directly neutralized this "billboard effect" argument.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): CON won a competitive debate by making the spillover and harm-asymmetry clash decisive: even with a default curated store, mandated outside doors and cashiers turn trusted apps into steered payment hallways, and a wrongful block is not symmetrical to mass irreversible fraud. PRO had a clean floor-vs-veto frame and solid A1/A2 answers, but never neutralized that structural externality or the single-chain refund accountability point as cleanly as CON damaged the “cautious user loses nothing” claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s B2.4/B4.3–B4.4 spillover reply: mandatory outside payments convert trusted icons into law-blessed “pay cheaper outside” scripts that still burn non-sideloaders, so baseline friction does not preserve the curated product.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by effectively leveraging the "baseline security standards" clause of the proposition to neutralize Side B's security and fraud concerns. Side A's distinction between OS-level security (which can apply to sideloaded apps and third-party payments) and store-level curation was highly persuasive, and their defense of opt-in defaults blunted Side B's vulnerable-user arguments. Side B's "exhausted user" and "asymmetric harm" points were strong and rhetorically memorable, but Side A's rebuttals were sharper, more technically grounded, and more directly addressed the mechanics of the proposed regulation without relying on the status quo's flawed perfection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled the "exhausted user" scenario by pointing out that social engineering already exists inside curated stores and that OS-level baselines (like separate system consent, cooling-off periods, and API-level fraud scoring) can mitigate external steering without requiring a distribution monopoly.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-executed cases with proper Q&A formatting, but B's security framing proved more resilient under pressure. B's central move—"permission is an attack surface"—directly targeted A's load-bearing claim that "permit is not compel" leaves cautious users unaffected, and B sharpened this in closing with "certificates can be revoked, but credentials stolen in the interval cannot be un-stolen," a line A never fully answered. A's rebuttal that centralized review already misses trojans was cleverly turned by B into an argument for strengthening rather than multiplying chokepoints ("by the time any one distributor acts, the payload is elsewhere"), and A's response (kill-switches, runtime permission gating) reasserted mechanisms without rebutting the multiplication-of-attack-surfaces point. On the economics side, A's "permission to beg" critique of B's targeted anti-steering-plus-browser-checkout alternative was a strong and memorable rebuttal exposing real friction in B's remedy, but B's alternative still plausibly captures most competitive benefit while avoiding the motion's structural breadth, and A never fully proved that narrower fixes couldn't achieve comparable discipline. Because B successfully forced A to defend the specific breadth of the motion (both sideloading and payments) rather than just the general problem of high fees, and because B's rebuttals landed more decisively on A's core defensive claim, B wins a close but clear debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's [B2.1]-[B2.4] turn on "permission is compulsion for security" and A's "permit is not compel" claim in [A2.1], resolved further by B's closing line about credentials being stolen before revocation can act—this exchange most damaged A's central defensive framing and was never fully neutralized.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): CON won by keeping the decisive clash on breadth versus necessity: mobile sensitivity, social-engineering attack surface, and post-install revocation gaps were never fully neutralized by PRO’s “permit is not compel” / layered-kill-switch answers. CON offered a live narrower path (anti-steering + browser checkout, transparency, enterprise distribution with liability) that captured competition concerns without accepting a universal OS mandate, and consistently forced PRO to justify why benefits require the motion’s full scope—which PRO did not cleanly do. PRO landed strong ownership and exit-right framing and exposed weakness in pure anti-steering, but security default-state weighing and accountable alternatives decided the vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 (B4.1–B4.5): converting “permit is not compel” into a sanctioned scam script and showing browser checkout + narrow rules can discipline rent without mandating install/payment pathways.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A built a more compelling case by successfully defending the technical feasibility of baseline security standards (sandboxing, OS-level kill-switches, friction) and effectively dismantling Side B's narrower alternative. Side A's framing of "permit is not compel" and "choice of floors" resonated strongly, while Side B's reliance on the curated store as a security panacea was undermined by concessions about existing app store malware. Side A also delivered a devastating critique of Side B's browser-checkout alternative, exposing it as an insufficient remedy for monopoly rent.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's "browser checkout" alternative in the later rounds and closing, framing it as mere "permission to beg" that fails to discipline the gatekeeper's in-app toll or provide a true exit right for developers.
