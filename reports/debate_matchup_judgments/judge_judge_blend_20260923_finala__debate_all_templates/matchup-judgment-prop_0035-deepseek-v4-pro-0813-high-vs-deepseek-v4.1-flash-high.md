### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, competitive cases, but PRO's underlying market-failure logic (owners bear costs, occupants and public health bear harms) was never squarely dismantled by CON, and PRO's closing reframing—prevention versus "documentation of injury"—exposed a real structural weakness in CON's alternative: reactive, hazard-confirmed enforcement necessarily waits for proof (illness, outbreak, confirmed mold) before acting, which especially disadvantages children and workers who cannot self-diagnose exposure. CON's strongest material—the "regulatory code splintering" critique and the equity concern that complex, hazard-specific triggers favor large, well-resourced institutions—landed real damage and was never fully neutralized; PRO's response ("short inspection checklist," phased funding) asserted rather than demonstrated administrability. But CON's own answers (A1/A2) effectively conceded a form of enforceable, government-triggered air-quality regulation (abatement orders, fines, closure for confirmed hazards), which blurred CON's negation of the motion as written and let PRO's closing framing dominate the final comparison. Pressure questions were well-executed on both sides with clean Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 structure, but PRO's answers gave a slightly more concrete (if imperfect) mechanism, while CON's answers, though sharp, implicitly affirmed a narrower version of the very policy the motion calls for.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turn, where it seized on PRO's own multi-metric answer (CO2 caps, per-person minimums, PM2.5 triggers, mold checks) and reframed it as proof that "one standard" inevitably splinters into an expanding regulatory code favoring large institutions, was the most damaging single exchange—though PRO's closing "prevention vs. documentation of harm" reframing was the most effective counter-punch in response.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing the debate around prevention versus reaction. By pressing Side A to define their alternative, Side B forced the concession that Side A's policy relies on acting after 'an outbreak pattern' or proven hazard, which Side B devastatingly characterized as a 'documentation-of-injury' policy. Side A argued well that Side B's standards rely on imperfect proxies, but Side B convincingly argued that a baseline proxy is better than waiting for occupants to get sick.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing crystallization, which used Side A's own Rebuttal 2 criteria (waiting for an 'outbreak pattern') to reframe Side A's alternative as a 'documentation-of-injury policy.'
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won a competitive debate by keeping the motion’s core comparison live: a pre-harm legal floor versus CON’s reactive duty after proven hazard. CON landed sharp instrumental critiques—proxy gaming, expanding code, and distributional burden—and forced A to list multi-metric triggers, but never solved the status-quo incentive gap A exposed: transparency and “sick building” orders still wait for mold, smoke thresholds, CO, or outbreaks. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases: PRO framed indoor air as a captive, invisible hazard requiring an enforceable floor and market-failure correction; CON framed the motion as a blunt instrument mismatched to heterogeneous buildings and reliant on a weak proxy (CO2). The pivotal exchange was the Pressure Questions round, where CON asked what happens to an underfunded building that cannot meet the standard. PRO's answer explicitly rejected fines, closure, and exemption, replacing them with an "abatement order" plus funding responsibility and time-limited variances — but never specified a final sanction if compliance still fails. CON seized on this in Rebuttal 2 and the closing, arguing PRO's "enforceable" regime had been hollowed into "consultation with a CO2 monitor attached," while CON's own targeted-hazard model retained concrete tools (occupancy restriction, escalation) tied to directly observed dangers rather than a contested proxy. PRO's counter — that CON's alternative also needs thresholds and duty-holders — is fair but doesn't fully repair the specific gap CON exposed: PRO's own remedy lacked a clear final tooth. CON's closing crystallized this asymmetry more sharply than PRO's closing rebutted it, giving CON the cleaner "why vote against" narrative, even though PRO's overall moral framing (captive occupants, invisible chronic harm, prevention vs. after-the-fact proof) remained forceful and mostly unrefuted in principle.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's closing indictment that PRO, having rejected fines, closure, and exemptions in answer to the pressure question, was left with no actual final sanction — turning "enforceable standard" into process without teeth — was the most decisive and unanswered blow of the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing the hazard of poor indoor air as chronic and invisible, exposing Side A's reliance on "observable hazards" as a policy that waits for harm to occur. Side B also effectively neutralized Side A's attacks on enforcement feasibility by pointing out that both sides' remedies rely on the same machinery (abatement orders and schedules), but Side B's approach acts preventatively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Closing masterfully crystallizes the debate's core clash by contrasting Side B's preventative floor with Side A's reactive approach that "has no answer for the stale classroom that has not yet produced a photograph."
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A won a competitive debate by keeping the live comparison on captive occupants and chronic, pre-visible poor ventilation—the harm CON’s after-the-fact, demonstrable-hazard regime never solved. A answered B’s pressure with concrete duty-holders and abatement mechanics, and fairly treated tiering/funding as design of “should set standards,” not a rewrite. B landed the sharper sanctions and proxy attacks and made enforcement look messy, but those process objections did not outweigh A’s cleaner prevention floor once both sides needed thresholds, inspectors, and orders anyway.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R1/closing on CON waiting for visible mold/exhaust vs. chronic stale classrooms; B’s R2/closing sanctions trap was the strongest counterpunch but did not fully neutralize that clash.
