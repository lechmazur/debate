### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that Side A's financing argument supports charging less for voluntary commitment, not eliminating an accessible monthly alternative. Side A persuasively explained fixed content costs and exposed B's weak claim that unused seats are pure surplus, but it repeatedly assumed B demanded the same low price with costless flexibility. That missed B's explicit stay-put discount model and left the motion's concerns about forced lock-in and opacity inadequately defended.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's transit-pass response was decisive: annual discounts work precisely because a more expensive monthly option remains available. This turned Side A's strongest analogy into support for voluntary price differentiation rather than compulsory lock-in.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by effectively turning Side B's economic arguments into proof of the proposition. When Side B argued that friction and lock-in are necessary to cross-subsidize expensive content, Side A successfully framed this as an admission of "hostage billing" and an unfair tax on travelers. Side A also cleanly resolved the transit pass analogy by pointing out that true commitment is voluntary and explicitly discounted, not enforced through a glued-shut window.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Closing effectively turned Side B's transit pass analogy against them by pointing out that an annual pass is cheaper specifically because the monthly ticket exists as a viable, walk-away option.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins by controlling the central framing and winning the decisive pressure exchange. CON mounted a credible economic defense—arguing that upfront content costs require stable year-round revenue and that frictionless exit would force steady subscribers to pay more—but PRO successfully trapped CON into admitting that the "stay-put discount" cannot survive truly costless exit. PRO crystallized this as proof that the current bundle price depends on trapping distracted users, not on genuine value. PRO's turnaround of CON's transit-pass analogy in the closing was particularly effective: it showed that annual discounts are trades consumers choose, not windows that must be glued shut. Throughout the debate, PRO kept the moral stakes sharp—flexibility as honest product versus lock-in as cable sneaking back in—while answering CON's content-financing fears by noting that most households would not micromanage, that paused seats cost almost nothing to serve, and that a bundle worth keeping survives a click. CON's financing argument remained live but was never able to overcome the burden of defending opacity and lock-in against a proposition explicitly hostile to cable-style rigidity. The margin reflects a clear but competitive win: CON's economics were substantive, but PRO built the cleaner path to victory by sharpening the clash around voluntariness versus extraction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure questions and Rebuttal 2 combination. Q1 exposed that CON's stay-put discount requires preventing costless exit to survive, essentially conceding the bundle depends on a trap. In Rebuttal 2, PRO then collapsed CON's "everyone pauses" and "most never touch" arguments by showing they were contradictory extremes, and reframed idle seats as surplus extraction rather than necessary financing—leaving CON's content-funding story intellectually live but rhetorically damaged.
