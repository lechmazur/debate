### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the more coherent, motion-faithful case. Its central move—"non-mandate is not neutrality but a political choice to preserve an incomplete ledger"—directly answered CON's recurring "the state's power to mandate is inherently dangerous" argument by showing that power is already being exercised for the opposite distortion, so redirection is not a new risk but a correction. CON (Side A) never squarely rebutted this reframe; it kept repeating the Japan/Spain/US examples across rounds without adapting them to meet PRO's point, and its own Japan example (foreign pressure to include, not exclude, atrocity content) arguably cuts against its own case, a tension CON never resolved. In the pressure round, PRO's questions forced CON to justify why redirecting existing curricular power is worse than leaving it in its current selective form, and CON's answer mostly reasserted "it's still state power" without engaging the asymmetry PRO raised. PRO's answers to CON's questions were more responsive and used the required A1/A2 format cleanly. CON's strongest point—that mandates risk collapsing into propaganda or diplomatic weaponization—was never fully neutralized by PRO's insistence that "fixed ground" prevents this, but PRO's overall framing was tighter to the actual wording of the motion (requiring study, not requiring a specific state-approved narrative), while CON repeatedly conflated the two. On balance, PRO answered more of the decisive pressure and left a clearer, more defensible path to the resolution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's answer to its own Q1 in Rebuttal 2 ("A1: Redirecting existing curriculum power... prevents another state-approved distortion because the current default already is one") was the most decisive exchange: it exposed that CON's "state power is equally dangerous either way" argument ignores that the status quo already weaponizes that same power for omission, a point CON never adequately answered in its final rebuttal or closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by collapsing Side B's central objection: since governments already control curricula, non-mandate is not neutrality but an active political choice to preserve omission and distortion. A successfully reframed the motion as redirecting existing state power toward honesty rather than expanding it, while B never bridged the gap between "trust educators" and the reality of state-controlled school systems. A also better handled the empirical clash, showing that divisions in Spain and Japan stem from the historical crimes themselves rather than from mandated study, and that partial US slavery education permits exceptionalism precisely because it lacks the firm requirement A defends.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1-2 sequence turning the state-power objection by showing non-mandate is itself a political choice for distortion, and that Spain/Japan friction reflects unresolved crimes rather than mandated study, decisively blunted Side B's core institutional and empirical objections.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won the decisive clashes: that existing coverage is optional/dilutable and shrinks under political comfort, and that state power to soft-pedal already exists so non-mandate is not neutrality. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): The decisive fault line was A's "who decides / who frames" objection: if curricula are already captured by power (as B insists), a mandate to teach "major atrocities" simply hands that same power a new, compulsory liturgy rather than fixing the capture problem. A pressed this cleanly through openings, rebuttals, and a sharp pressure question, and B's answer ("historical consensus, not political power, defines the list") never actually resolved who certifies and frames that consensus for classrooms—A's follow-up correctly noted this merely relocates rather than solves the problem. B's case leaned heavily on vivid but somewhat overspecified textbook-wording claims (e.g., precise phrases like "forced immigration") stated with more confidence than a closed-book debate warrants, and repeated the Germany/Japan contrast without ever squarely rebutting A's exceptionalism point about Germany (total defeat, unique atrocity scale) or engaging South Africa beyond one line. B did land one strong unanswered point late—that "ordinary engines" (scholarship, activism) have already had decades to fix U.S./Japanese whitewashing and failed—which A's closing did not directly rebut, leaving a live thread for B. But on balance A's structural critique was more fundamental to the mechanism of the proposal itself, was pressed more precisely, and was never fully neutralized, giving A the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure question ("If everything is already captured by power, who impartially picks and frames the obligatory list?") and B's underpowered "historical consensus" answer, which A then correctly exposed as relocating rather than solving the capture problem.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by demonstrating that the status quo consistently sanitizes national history through politically motivated omission, and that requiring the inclusion of consensus atrocities corrects this existing capture rather than introducing new bias. While Side B raises sharp theoretical objections about selection and capture, it never overcomes A's concrete evidence that open-society mechanisms have repeatedly failed to prevent sanitized textbooks, and its alternative relies on an optimistic view of organic progress that A's cross-national examples undermine. A's rebuttal turning the curriculum-capture argument and its closing crystallization of the status quo's empirical failures provide a cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal (A2.1-A2.2), which turns Side B's curriculum-capture argument by showing that state curricula are already captured by sanitized narratives, making the mandate a corrective rather than a new source of distortion.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) kept the decisive clash on demonstrated failure of discretionary inclusion and why a mandate corrects rather than creates bias, using Germany vs Japan and US euphemism examples.
