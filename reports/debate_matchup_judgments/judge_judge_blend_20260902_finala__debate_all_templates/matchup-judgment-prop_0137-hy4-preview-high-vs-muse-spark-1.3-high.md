### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B won by consistently distinguishing offering and advertising egg freezing from making it the flagship women’s benefit. Side A’s autonomy, portability, and budget arguments showed that freezing itself has value, but did not establish why it should lead an employer’s women-focused marketing when IVF, leave, and caregiving support are absent. Side A repeatedly framed non-flagship status as secrecy or a gag, even though Side B expressly allowed advertising it as supplementary fertility-preservation coverage. Side B also answered the timeline objection well: the required supports benefit both earlier parents and delayers, while preventing workplace pressures from making postponement the safest option.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s first pressure question exposed Side A’s false dilemma between flagship promotion and offering nothing. Side A replied that only spotlighting creates awareness, but never justified why ordinary, clear advertising would be ineffective or why egg freezing must be the leading symbol of women’s support without a broader family-support floor.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by maintaining a laser focus on the motion's core issue: the corporate marketing of the benefit, rather than the benefit itself. Side A brilliantly turns Side B's best practical argument—that frozen eggs are portable and can be taken to a better employer—into the ultimate proof that the first employer shouldn't market it as their flagship women's benefit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing turn on portability: by pointing out that an egg leaves the firm's duties "at the lab door," Side A proves that marketing it as a flagship benefit means the employer is claiming credit for a chance it will never help convert.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully defending the proposition's core premise: marketing egg freezing as a "flagship" benefit acts as a substitute signal that lets employers avoid costly structural supports like paid leave and flexibility. Side A brilliantly neutralized Side B's portability argument in the closing, pointing out that if the egg's primary value is its portability to a future employer, the current employer is claiming "flagship" credit for a private asset it never actually helps convert. Side A also cleanly resolved the clash over the "starting earlier" wording by framing the required supports as timeline-neutral tools that remove the structural penalties forcing women to delay. Side B argued well on budgets and the danger of holding biology hostage to an ideal HR roadmap, but ultimately failed to overcome Side A's sharp distinction between offering a quiet supplement and marketing a high-tech delay as the defining emblem of women's support.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing turn on portability. When Side B argued that frozen eggs are portable and give women the freedom to move to a better employer, Side A sharply countered that this proves the employer is claiming flagship credit for a chance it never helps convert, exposing the perk as a false emblem of the workplace's actual support for working mothers.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by maintaining the crucial distinction between providing and prominently marketing egg freezing. Side A repeatedly treated loss of flagship status as if the benefit must become secret or disappear, but the proposition requires neither. B plausibly showed that prominent delay-focused messaging, amid weak support for current parents, signals institutional preference and lets a comparatively cheap benefit substitute for making early family formation workable. A’s visibility and advocacy-leverage arguments were thoughtful, but they did not establish why medically relevant information requires flagship recruiting treatment rather than ordinary benefits communication.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer that a chemotherapy patient needs accessible coverage and benefits navigation, not necessarily a recruiting billboard, most directly undercut A’s strongest time-sensitive-visibility example. It preserved access while explaining why flagship promotion carries a different institutional message.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully exposing a mechanical flaw in Side A's case: removing the 'flagship' marketing of egg freezing does not actually force companies to improve early family leave, it just encourages them to hide the freezing benefit. Side B consistently demonstrated that under the proposition, the practical constraint on early motherhood (bad leave) remains unchanged, while a valuable biological option becomes invisible to those who might need it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal (B4.1 and B4.5) effectively neutralized Side A's 'steering' argument by pointing out that silencing the egg freezing billboard leaves the 29-year-old with the exact same patchy leave, but removes a visible option for others, turning Side A's 'protection' into mere subtraction.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A wins by drawing a sharp, decisive distinction between medical coverage (which remains accessible in the health portal) and institutional marketing (which signals corporate preference and steers behavior). This effectively neutralized Side B's primary argument that the proposition would hide time-sensitive options from women who need them. Side A's rhetoric was more memorable, successfully framing the debate around the difference between paper options and workable paths, and dismantling Side B's analogies and pressure points with clean, grounded logic.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's chemotherapy example, pointing out that a cancer patient needs a physician and benefits navigation at diagnosis, not a recruiting deck. This sharply dismantled Side B's argument that "flagship" marketing is necessary for medical visibility.
