### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins on the strength of two connected analytical moves that PRO never fully neutralized. First, the dilemma at [A4.5–A4.7]: either river rights are genuinely non-negotiable and therefore obstruct legitimate infrastructure (as the motion itself concedes they might), or they operate like regulatory balancing standards, making personhood redundant. PRO spent successive speeches insisting guardians work within balancing frameworks and won't veto necessary projects—which is exactly what made them vulnerable to this dilemma. Second, and most decisively, PRO's own closing conceded that "the point is not a different substantive standard; it is a different plaintiff." Side A caught this immediately at [A5.1] and showed it proved CON's alternative: statutory standing for Indigenous co-management boards or environmental commissioners achieves the "different plaintiff" mechanism without the rights framing that converts negotiation into litigation. That counter-proposal was concrete, responsive to PRO's core mechanism, and left standing at the close. PRO's strongest live argument—existing regulatory tools systemically fail because no actor has standing to enforce them before permits are granted—was genuine and never fully answered. Side B's pressure question Q1 hit this squarely and CON's answer ("strengthen the tools incrementally") was the weakest moment in CON's case. But PRO's own admission that personhood adds a plaintiff rather than a new substantive standard largely conceded the standing fix to CON. PRO's repeated reassurances that personhood would never seriously complicate infrastructure also created tension with the motion's own framing ("even if it complicates"), which CON exploited cleanly in the closing. Multiple PRO speeches were clipped mid-argument, which did leave some burdens incompletely developed, but the decisive clashes were resolved against PRO on the arguments visible in the transcript.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dilemma at [A4.5–A4.7], sustained into the closing: PRO had repeatedly argued guardians operate within balancing frameworks just like regulatory standards, then conceded in closing that personhood's distinctive contribution is "a different plaintiff, not a different substantive standard." Side A caught this and showed it validated CON's alternative—statutory standing for co-management bodies achieves the plaintiff function without the litigation-generating rights framing. This turned PRO's own concession into a reason to vote CON.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): A won by making the decisive tradeoff clearer: personhood either has real rights force, in which case it pushes infrastructure and community conflicts into costly, unpredictable litigation, or it is merely a balanced regulatory factor, in which case statutory standing, co-management, and enforceable consultation can capture the benefits with less doctrinal risk. B had a strong mechanism around giving the river an advocate and answered the veto charge reasonably, but its later “different plaintiff, not different standard” framing let A argue that personhood itself was unnecessary. A also did more to undermine the broad request trigger and the portability of Whanganui-style examples.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest exchange was the second rebuttal and closing turn: it converted B’s proportional-balancing answer into a redundancy dilemma and then argued that standing for Indigenous or local bodies could supply the “lawyer” without creating river rights.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by exposing a decisive contradiction in Side B's case: personhood either operates as a non-negotiable rights claim that displaces democratic tradeoffs into litigation, or it functions as a balancing standard indistinguishable from robust environmental regulation with statutory standing. Side A crystallized this tension in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, noting that the motion itself embraces infrastructure complication while PRO spent the debate denying any. Side A's alternative—co-management, cumulative-impact assessment, and statutory standing—emerged as a cleaner path. Side B's repeated word-limit clips and failure to resolve the rights-versus-standards distinction weakened their live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 ([A4.5]–[A4.8]), which turned Side B's "different plaintiff" framing into a contradiction: if guardians merely balance proportionally like regulators, personhood is redundant; if they enforce non-negotiable rights, they force disputes into courts. This rebuttal defined the decisive clash.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Its most durable contribution was the persistent-standing argument: legal personhood creates standing that survives defunding, political shifts, and regulatory capture, whereas CON's preferred alternative—"strengthen existing regulation"—is vulnerable to every political wind that presided over the ecological failures prompting communities to seek personhood in the first place. CON never squarely answered PRO's sharpest question (why are these communities requesting something different if democratic regulation works?), and "fix the regulation" as a fallback is thin when the premise of regulation's repeated failure was never seriously contested. CON's technical advantages were real: the benchmark problem (no clear standard for a guardian's holistic "river health" claim vs. the specific public-interest metrics regulators already apply) was precise and damaging, and the fragmentation concern (multiple communities, competing guardians along one watershed) was partially live after PRO's "convergence" response. But these landed as second-order problems, not case-killers. PRO's Whanganui model survived CON's bilateral-exclusion critique well enough ("precedent not template; downstream users keep existing rights"), and the claim that upstream protection typically serves downstream users was a genuine neutralization of CON's manufactured community-vs-public-interest conflict. CON's closing was clipped—a minor execution cost—but most key arguments had been lodged. Overall PRO framed the stakes more memorably, answered pressure more directly, and left CON's "fix the regulation" alternative looking weaker than its "litigation lottery" framing of personhood. The margin is real but modest: CON's governance-accountability critique and fragmentation concern remained partially live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 [B2.4] was the most effective single move: by showing that upstream legal protection from industrial pollution directly serves downstream cities and farms, it collapsed CON's central claim that one community's request conflicts with broader public interest. CON's response in Rebuttal 2 fell back to "river health is value-laden," which is true but did not restore the conflict PRO had neutralized. CON's sharpest counter-move was its Q2 pressure question—what happens when multiple communities along one watershed each request personhood?—which PRO answered with plausible but speculative "convergence," leaving the fragmentation concern partially live.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): B wins a close but clear debate by making personhood sound like persistent legal standing and structured co-governance rather than the unchecked veto A described. A landed real pressure on democratic legitimacy, downstream users, fragmentation, and infrastructure uncertainty, but leaned heavily on speculative paralysis and treated standing as an automatic trump card. B’s Whanganui example, “voice not throne” framing, and status quo failure/capture argument gave the cleaner affirmative path under the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive rebuttal was reframing A’s “unelected guardian veto” into a standing-and-governance issue: ecosystems currently lack any legal seat while corporations and developers already have one, and personhood can be designed through co-governance rather than replacing democratic decision-making.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO built a clearer comparative path to victory by framing legal personhood as a necessary correction to a captured, skewed system rather than an undemocratic veto. CON landed real blows on downstream exclusion, subjective guardian power, and fragmentation risks, but their claim that communities merely seek "privilege" was alienating, and their closing was clipped before it could crystallize the clash—leaving PRO's final question ("if democratic regulation works, why are communities asking for something else?") unanswered on the ballot.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 reframing of legal personhood as adding a "voice" rather than a "veto" to a conversation already dominated by corporate interests and captured agencies, which directly neutralized CON's democratic-deficit and infrastructure-paralysis objections and set the terms for the rest of the exchange.
