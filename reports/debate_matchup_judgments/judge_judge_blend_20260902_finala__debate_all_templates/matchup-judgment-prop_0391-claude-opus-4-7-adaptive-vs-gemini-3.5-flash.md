- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won by making the cleaner comparative case: lax moderation shifts costs onto targets, drives people out of voice and games, and actually kills the social culture CON says it protects. B had a real scale/false-positive concern, but overstated it and sometimes misdescribed A’s model as reviewing every joke or endorsing keyword bans.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s rebuttal to mute/block was decisive: personal tools make the target pay the cost, fail against first exposure and coordinated abuse, and undermine the very organic chat culture B claims to defend.

PRO successfully framed the debate as a cost-shifting problem—under CON's model, targets bear the burden of muting and leaving while harassers face no consequences—and showed that behavior-based, severity-weighted enforcement is operationally feasible by pointing to existing human-reviewed systems for cheating and ranked abuse. CON never squarely answered why harassment escalation is a "corporate fantasy" while cheat reviews scale, and CON's answer that players can simply "preemptively mute" conceded PRO's empirical point that unmoderated spaces become sterile. Despite PRO's Rebuttal 2 being clipped for length, its core route to victory survived the exchange intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's first rebuttal (A2) dismantling CON's equation of aggressive moderation with dumb automation, rebutting the "language is plastic" futility fallacy, and exposing the burden-shifting problem of mute/block tools.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A wins by successfully decoupling "aggressive moderation" from Side B's rigid "automated, context-blind" framing, pointing to existing hybrid enforcement and anti-cheat models as proof of operational feasibility. A's framing of the core tradeoff—who bears the cost, the victim or the harasser—was sharper and consistently applied throughout. Side B's reliance on preemptive muting and private servers effectively conceded that safety requires victims to opt out of the social experience, which undermined B's own claim to protect organic culture and spontaneity. This flipped Side B's core cultural defense and resolved the clash decisively.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by making the key comparison about aggressive enforcement versus calibrated, structural protection. Side A had a strong inclusion and dignity case, but often caricatured Side B as merely defending slurs or saying "just mute," while under-answering the false-positive, appeal, and alternative-tool objections. Side B was somewhat too aggressive in characterizing A's stopping rule, but still landed the stronger practical critique: more aggressive bans are not automatically more accurate or more protective, and a self-correcting moderation regime matters.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's most decisive rebuttal was that bans are also reactive, evadable, and error-prone, while structural tools like reputation routing, sticky blocks, and default-safe voice can protect the next match without the same collateral damage. Side A never fully neutralized that broader alternative and kept reducing it to "just mute."
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON)): CON won by demonstrating that PRO's aggressive moderation framework pre-commits to disbelieving user feedback and cannot self-correct, while offering structural alternatives like reputation matchmaking and filter-by-default that protect victims without the collateral damage of false positives. PRO failed to engage substantively with these structural tools and over-relied on a moral asymmetry that CON showed was achievable through less destructive means.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing exploited PRO's "never a valid stop signal" line to show that aggressive enforcement cannot self-correct, while PRO never squarely answered CON's structural alternatives like reputation matchmaking and default-safe voice chat.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully isolating the motion's "even when" clause as a critical accountability test and outperforming Side A on practical mechanism comparison. While A built a compelling moral case around duty of care and the harms of toxicity, B effectively demonstrated that "more aggressive" enforcement at scale inevitably produces disproportionate false positives and is easily evaded via alt accounts. B's introduction of structural, preventative tools (reputation matchmaking, default filters, device fingerprinting) directly neutralized A's claim that aggressive bans are necessary to stop serial harassers. Crucially, A's Rebuttal 2 admission that player pushback is "never a valid stop signal" handed B a decisive strategic victory, allowing B to frame PRO's model as an uncalibrated system that cannot self-correct or detect its own errors. B's closing crystallized this clash perfectly, making the comparative choice clear and fatally damaging A's route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing sequence, where B directly answers A's pressure questions with proactive structural alternatives, then weaponizes A's own "stopping rule" answer to prove that PRO's model pre-commits to ignoring user feedback, rendering it incapable of detecting false positives or self-correcting.
