### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): CON identified and sustained a genuine drafting problem at the heart of PRO's mechanism: the "automatic stabilizer exception" is either narrow (covering only formula-based flows like jobless benefits and withholding, in which case it cannot cover the discretionary crisis tools—CARES Act-style direct payments, business support, enhanced UI—that actually drove 2008/2020 responses, forcing paralysis-inducing constitutional amendments during collapses) or broad (covering revenue crashes across sales/corporate/capital taxes, in which case it depends on the contested cyclical/structural estimate that courts and officials cannot adjudicate in real time). PRO never resolved this fork. In Rebuttal 2, PRO's answer to CON's sharp pressure question about amendment timelines during a sudden collapse was to call the delay question "secondary" and label supermajority bargaining "a feature, not paralysis," which sidesteps rather than answers the concrete concern. PRO's attempt to redefine the exception as narrow, auditable "formula flows" in [A2.1]/[A4.2] made the mechanism more defensible against the "unenforceable estimate" charge but simultaneously conceded CON's other horn—that large discretionary crisis response would need amendment, undermining PRO's central promise of preserved recession responsiveness. CON's closing crystallized this cleanly ("too slow or too vague"), while PRO's closing largely reasserted the original framing (default vs. discretion) without directly closing the fork. CON's pressure questions were also better targeted and did more decisive work than PRO's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1/2 fork ([B2.1]-[B2.4], sharpened in [B4.3]-[B4.4] and the closing) forcing PRO to choose between a stabilizer exception too narrow to cover real crisis responses or too broad to be enforced without contested real-time estimation—a bind PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer ("timeline is secondary") never actually resolved.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B won by forcing Side A into an unresolvable dilemma regarding the scope of the automatic stabilizer exception. - **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON proved the stabilizer exception creates a fatal dilemma: it is either too narrow to cover necessary discretionary crisis response (forcing slow constitutional amendment) or too broad to avoid contested real-time estimation of cyclical versus structural deficits. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): This was a tightly matched, technically sophisticated debate, but PRO edged it on two fronts. First, PRO's pressure question about boom-time deficits forced CON into an answer ([B4.1]) that explained voters reward tax cuts/spending now and "cannot pin" the cost on a single vote — which directly contradicts CON's own opening claim that "voters punish runaway debt" ([B1.1]) and hands PRO the exact deficit-bias mechanism its case needed. PRO's closing correctly weaponized this as a "confession," and CON's closing never returned to rebut it, instead narrowing entirely to the textual "automatic stabilizer vs. emergency valve" objection. That textual point was CON's strongest hit — it legitimately pressured PRO to defend an emergency escape clause not explicitly named in the motion's text — but PRO offered a plausible (if not fully elaborated) reply that threshold-triggered, pre-authorized valves are a form of automatic mechanism rather than discretionary expansion, and CON's closing, by dropping other live threads (regional downturns, judicialization risk) to focus solely on this one point, left PRO's late "confession" framing unanswered. On balance, PRO built a more cumulative, unified throughline from mechanism to rebuttal to closing, while CON's case, though sharp in isolated exchanges, ended with a narrower closing that ceded the last major clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's answer to CON's own Q1 in Rebuttal 2 ([B4.1]), where CON's explanation of why voters miss boom-time deficits inadvertently validated PRO's deficit-bias thesis, and PRO's closing conversion of that answer into a "confession" that CON's closing never rebutted.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A wins by setting a brilliant strategic trap in the Pressure Questions that forces Side B to concede the core premise of the debate. Side B admits in B4.1 that under legislative discretion, politicians cut taxes during booms and voters reward them while passing the bill to the future, perfectly validating Side A's 'deficit bias' argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A5.1 brilliantly crystallizes the debate by pointing out that B4.1 is not a rebuttal, but a confession of Side A's entire case against legislative discretion.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO built a coherent political-economy case that CON effectively conceded in [A4.1], explaining why booms produce structural deficits under discretion. While CON landed sharp procedural hits on textual fidelity and epistemic uncertainty, PRO’s defense that emergency valves are ex ante constitutional specification—not a silent expansion—was credible, and the substantive deficit-bias mechanism remained live after the exchange. PRO’s closing crystallized the decisive clash more cleanly, using CON’s own explanation of boom-time borrowing to show why an anchor is necessary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Q1 in the pressure round (why structural deficits persist in booms) forced CON to describe the concentrated-benefit/diffuse-cost dynamic in [A4.1], which PRO seized in the closing as a confession that electoral accountability is too blunt to stop the bias—turning CON’s answer into PRO’s decisive evidence.
