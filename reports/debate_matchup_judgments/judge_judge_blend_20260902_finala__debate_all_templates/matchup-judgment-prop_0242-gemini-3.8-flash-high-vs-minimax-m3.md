### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): PRO won a close debate by keeping the live burden on comparative "more," not mere sequence. CON's strongest live routes—high-turnout older voters, elite/institutional capture, and cable-first conditioning—were real and well argued, especially on Trump free airtime and cable as grievance content engine. PRO still made the cleaner decision path: unbounded participatory architecture, qualitative post-2008 mobilization (disinfo pipelines, coordinated offline action), and the global pattern without American cable. PRO answered CON's peer-democracy pressure directly and flipped CON's fusion/pipe move into an architecture win rather than a content-origin stalemate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1/A2 on peer democracies and "angry viewers vs mobilized actors," then closing flip of CON's Hannity-as-plumbing claim into proof that cable content only became era-defining once SM unbounded it.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won by maintaining a clearer comparative frame: social media's unbounded architecture—algorithmic amplification, peer distribution, and infinite scale—transformed polarization from passive media consumption into active, viral mobilization. PRO effectively turned CON's "fusion" argument by showing that cable content only reached politically transformative scale once routed through social media's infrastructure. Most decisively, PRO's rebuttal to CON's international pressure question deflated the cable-necessary thesis with concrete counterexamples (Brazil, Philippines, India) where social media drove polarization without American-style partisan cable. CON's institutional and demographic arguments remained competitive, but it never fully recovered from the global pattern evidence or explained why distribution was merely "plumbing" if it multiplied reach, added algorithmic targeting, and replaced producer gatekeeping with peer validation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's answer to CON's first pressure question (B3.1), where PRO cited Brazil, India, and the Philippines to demonstrate social-media-driven polarization in democracies without American cable news, dismantling CON's U.S.-exceptionalism argument and proving the mechanism travels independently.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B wins a clear, competitive victory by successfully arguing that social media's architecture didn't just distribute cable's content, but fundamentally transformed its scale, persuasive power, and the nature of political mobilization. While Side A made a strong case for cable's historical role in institutional capture and radicalizing older demographics, Side B effectively co-opted these points—noting seniors' heavy social media use and the democratization of elite capture without gatekeepers. Side B delivered a more decisive closing synthesis that dismantled Side A's "engine vs. pipe" distinction, proving that the medium itself reshaped the democratic terrain.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing response to Side A's claim that viral cable clips on social media are just "digital plumbing." Side B sharply pointed out that algorithmic distribution, micro-fragmentation, and peer-sharing transform the signal's persuasive power and scale, turning a bounded broadcast into an unbounded, mobilizing force.
