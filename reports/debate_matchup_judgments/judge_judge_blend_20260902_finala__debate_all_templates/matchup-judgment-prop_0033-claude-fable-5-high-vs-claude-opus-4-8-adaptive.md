### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.3** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by exposing CON's contradiction on efficacy, maintaining a defensible line between circumstance and private profile targeting, and showing disclosure remedies fail accountability. Side A answered pressure questions cleanly and crystallized the clash without evasion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4) directly answering both pressure questions then using CON's own truck/potion framing to show prohibition costs nothing while fixing the conceded harm.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by maintaining a coherent three-pillar framework (autonomy, accountability, exploitation) and successfully neutralizing CON’s strongest line-drawing objection. A extracts key concessions that the harms are real, shows that disclosure libraries cannot close the accountability gap for individualized hidden messages, and demonstrates that under CON’s own “reach vs. manipulation” framing, prohibiting the “potion” costs the “truck” nothing. While CON mounts a sharp critique about statutory vagueness and displacement to PACs, A offers a plausible statutory boundary modeled on existing sensitive-data categories and defends regulating the actor that democratically seeks power. CON’s attempt to collapse issue-salience targeting into psychological profiling is rhetorically powerful but does not fully bridge the intuitive and functional distinction between targeting a shared political concern and reverse-engineering an individual’s hidden emotional vulnerability. After the full exchange, PRO’s clean prohibition remains the more compelling remedy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 and Pressure Questions exposing the contradiction that micro-targeting cannot be simultaneously “vendor puffery” and “indispensable reach,” crystallized by the question: “If that lever genuinely doesn’t work, what exactly does prohibiting it take away from your school-board candidate’s truck?”
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully collapsing PRO's proposed statutory line between ordinary audience targeting and prohibited psychological profiling. CON's "truck vs potion" frame and late-round fork (a broad ban chills core political outreach; a narrowed ban permits the conceded harm) proved decisive. PRO built a compelling normative case on autonomy and accountability, but never fully resolved CON's practical challenge: modern campaign modeling inherently blends circumstance and emotional inference, making the prohibition either unenforceably vague or functionally empty. CON's alternative remedies (privacy law, ad libraries) and displacement argument (banning campaigns pushes tools to less accountable PACs) remained standing, giving the cleaner comparative path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turns PRO's own "truck vs potion" distinction against them, arguing that issue-salience scoring inherently infers emotional states ("cares about crime" equals "fears crime"). This creates a fatal fork: enforce the motion as written and every outreach model becomes litigable, chilling small campaigns; adopt PRO's narrowed reading and the exploitation stays legal. PRO's closing reiterates the distinction but never operationally resolves how regulators would audit the modeling pipeline to separate the two, leaving CON's practical critique decisive.
