### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A consistently anchored the debate in the word "offer," maintaining throughout that the motion creates an opt-in user tool rather than a default block or censorship mandate. That framing was established in the opening, survived every round, and was never squarely neutralized. Side A's pressure questions exposed the core contradiction in CON's case: if existing controls already do exactly this, requiring them cannot simultaneously be a dangerous new censorship architecture. The decisive blow came in Rebuttal 2, where Side B's own answers (B4.1, B4.2) conceded that the motion has "no mandate for default activation," places control "in the hands of the device user," and "does not create new censorship authority" but "just fixes a usability gap." Side A's closing correctly quoted those concessions back, leaving CON's case resting almost entirely on speculative worst-case implementation abuses. Side A's answer to the abuse concern was also live and grounded: abusive jurisdictions can already exploit vague moderation and age-rating regimes without this narrow opt-in tool, so blocking the policy offers no marginal protection to marginalized users. Side B's LGBTQ+ framing was vivid but overextended; it required ignoring that the motion only requires offering a toggle, not imposing default blocks. The functional-criteria answer from Side A was plausible and reasonable. CON's internal inconsistency, compounded by the Rebuttal 2 concessions, left no clean route to victory by the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's two pressure questions were the most decisive exchange. Q1 correctly identified that CON had never shown where the motion mandates default blocking or prohibits adults from turning the setting off. Q2 perfectly exposed the logical contradiction: if existing controls already do this "exactly," a required simple version cannot also be an unprecedented censorship threat. CON's Rebuttal 2 attempted to answer both but ended up conceding the opt-in, non-default nature of the tool and acknowledging it merely "fixes a usability gap"—validating PRO's core case and leaving CON with only speculative abuse arguments.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins a dominant victory after Side B commits a catastrophic execution error in Rebuttal 2. Side B answered the Pressure Questions from the PRO perspective, explicitly conceding that the mandate does not create new censorship authority and merely fixes a usability gap. Side A easily capitalized on this in the closing to sweep the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Closing, which efficiently points out that Side B literally conceded CON's core censorship and redundancy arguments in its own Rebuttal 2.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins because CON's answers to PRO's pressure questions effectively conceded that the motion is purely opt-in and merely fixes a usability gap, directly undermining CON's own opening censorship framing. PRO capitalized on this in closing to show CON's remaining harms are speculative implementation abuses not caused by the mandate, while PRO's comparative—a simple family safety tool versus fragmented settings—remained the cleaner, live path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure questions in Turn 5 that forced CON to admit the motion does not mandate default blocking and merely fixes a usability gap, which PRO then weaponized in closing to collapse CON's censorship narrative.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): PRO built a cleaner positive case: a narrow, parent-activated toggle on shared family devices where two of three targeted categories (gambling, pornographic) already correspond to existing app-store infrastructure, current controls are demonstrably inadequate, and adult users on personal devices bear zero burden. The seatbelt analogy—imperfect protection still beats none—held up reasonably well throughout, and CON never developed a concrete alternative, offering only the underspecified "simplify existing tools." CON's rebuttals were genuinely strong, particularly the asymmetric over-labeling incentive (stores over-include to avoid regulatory risk; parents cannot unblock hidden apps they never see) and the "wrong-layer" argument that harms flow through browsers, embedded web views, and already-installed apps rather than new store downloads. The "high-risk social apps" definitional problem was CON's sharpest sustained line, and PRO's answer—that existing age-rating frameworks already tag algorithmic feeds and unmoderated public posting—did not fully resolve the difference between broad guidance categories and a government-backed blocking mandate. PRO also overclaimed when asserting the toggle works "across all access pathways," and CON correctly identified this. Despite those real hits, CON's live route to victory was incomplete: the two cleaner categories (gambling, pornographic) were never seriously contested as vague, the toggle still provides a meaningful first barrier for those harms, and CON's implied alternative was never fleshed out. PRO's narrower positive case survives the exchange with reduced but sufficient force, giving it the edge in a competitive but decided contest.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's asymmetric-incentive rebuttal in Rebuttal 2 [B4.1] was the most damaging exchange: PRO's "parents can freely unblock mislabeled apps" response was cleanly turned by CON's observation that parents cannot unblock apps they never discover were swept in, and that the same time-and-tech-literacy deficit PRO cited to justify the toggle also defeats the one-tap-unblock safeguard. PRO never directly answered this contradiction.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's primary safeguard. Side A relied heavily on the premise that parents could simply unblock mistakenly classified apps to mitigate the harms of over-classification. Side B effectively countered that an app hidden from the storefront loses discoverability, meaning parents cannot unblock what they never see, and minors on shared devices cannot safely ask parents to unblock sensitive support spaces.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 decisively neutralized Side A's "one-tap unblocking" defense by explaining the discoverability paradox and the privacy barrier for minors seeking sensitive communities on shared devices.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON built the stronger path to victory by showing that the proposition conscripts app stores into making vague, discretionary classifications of lawful speech—especially “high-risk social apps”—under government pressure, while the resulting storefront toggle targets the wrong layer of harm. PRO’s defense that existing app-store labels settle the issue collapsed when their own definition of “high-risk social” relied on subjective policy criteria like algorithmic feeds and “known links” to mental-health harms, confirming the category is not pre-defined. CON’s asymmetric-incentive analysis directly answered PRO’s claim that parent activation and one-tap unblocking eliminate overclassification risk: stores bear cost for under-inclusion but not for invisible over-inclusion, so borderline lawful apps lose discoverability without parents ever knowing. PRO’s “perfect solution fallacy” framing missed the mark because CON’s objection was not that the toggle is imperfect, but that it is structurally mismatched to the primary harm pathways—browsers, links, embedded web views, in-app spending mechanics, and already-installed apps on shared devices—making benefits marginal while the speech-classification cost is structural. PRO never squarely neutralized this mismatch or the burden that “high-risk social” places on platforms to police lawful communication spaces.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 (A4) directly answered PRO’s A1 and A2 while exposing the decisive tension in PRO’s case: the asymmetric incentive for stores to overclassify borderline apps because regulatory and reputational risk punishes under-inclusion while over-inclusion is invisible, and the conflation of an app-store installation toggle with blocking actual use pathways such as browsers, links, and already-installed apps.
