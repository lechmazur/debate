### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won by holding Side A to the proposition’s explicit requirement of full root access. A persuasively defended regulated diagnostic parity, certification, and signed service tools, but its reliance on enforced safety envelopes and manufacturer-signed toolchains described bounded access rather than root privilege. B’s safety and cryptographic-risk case therefore remained live, although some claims about root overriding every physical safeguard were overstated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal crystallized the decisive dilemma: either A defended genuine superuser access with its safety risks, or accepted signed binaries and enforced boundaries that fell short of the motion. A never fully escaped that textual conflict.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): CON won by locking the clash to the motion's actual wording—"full diagnostic and root access"—and treating root as superuser privilege over BMS safety envelopes. After pressure and Rebuttal 2, CON made the cleaner path to victory on the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2 dilemma (A4.3–A4.5): PRO's A1/A2 concessions to signed toolchains and envelope enforcement collapse true root access, so PRO must either defend indefensible superuser BMS control or smuggle in ordinary diagnostics.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A built a highly persuasive case by grounding the abstract IT concept of "root access" in the physical and operational realities of automotive engineering. When Side B attempted a semantic trap—arguing that true root access means unconstrained superuser privileges that bypass all safety envelopes, and thus PRO conceded by accepting signed toolchains—Side A brilliantly parried by pointing out that even the manufacturer's own engineers operate within hardware interlocks and base-level safety firmware. Side A's closing argument effectively dismantled Side B's dictionary maximalism, proving that Side B's absolute definition would mean no one, not even the OEM, could service the cars. Side B relied too heavily on repetitive fearmongering about chemical fires and the semantic trap, failing to adequately address Side A's core point that the current dealership monopoly is based on franchise contracts, not unique safety capabilities. Side A's answers to the pressure questions were direct, practical, and logically sound, securing a solid victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Turn 7 and Closing response to Side B's semantic trap regarding "root access," where Side A proved that Side B's absolute definition of root would mean even the manufacturer couldn't service the cars, thereby redefining the term to mean practical parity rather than unconstrained god-mode.
