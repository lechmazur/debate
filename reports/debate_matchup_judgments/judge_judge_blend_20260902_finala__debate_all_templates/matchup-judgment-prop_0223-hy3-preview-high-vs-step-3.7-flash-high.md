### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B wins because it made the cleaner comparative case: coverage of more crash causes is not the same as effective fatality reduction, and scaling traditional stops creates serious inherent externalities of bias, escalation, and violence. Side A’s 33% argument was the strongest CON route, but it relied too heavily on simplistic arithmetic and under-proven claims that targeted stops would broadly and safely address all other fatality causes. On externalities, Side A’s license-suspension argument was relevant but overstated and met by Side B’s point that fines and debt are design-contingent while in-person policing risks are intrinsic. Side B’s evidence was somewhat overconfident, but its mechanisms and weighing were stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s rebuttal that “coverage is not effectiveness” was decisive: it neutralized Side A’s repeated claim that police stops win merely because they can theoretically address more crash causes, while tying that broader scope to higher bias and escalation costs.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A wins on decisive scope comparison (ASE covers only 1/3 of fatalities) and clearer weighing of fixable vs inherent externalities after both sides answered pressure questions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 opening directly answering both pressure questions on bias guardrails and warnings, then contrasting total coverage vs narrow corridors.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A wins because it exposed a structural coverage gap that Side B never squarely answered: ASE only targets speeding, which PRO conceded accounts for roughly one-third of traffic deaths, whereas targeted traditional stops address impaired driving, distracted driving, and faulty equipment that cause the rest. PRO’s attempt to reframe the motion as “per unit of harm” did not overcome the plain implication that a tool blind to two-thirds of fatality causes cannot deliver larger total safety gains. CON also showed that ASE’s regressive fines trigger mandatory state license suspensions in ways program-level caps cannot fully avoid, while traditional stops can issue on-the-spot warnings and waivers. PRO’s externality case on police violence remained live, but CON credibly narrowed its scope by restricting stops to high-risk violations with guardrails, making the aggregate harm smaller than PRO claimed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s repeated demonstration that ASE addresses only one-third of fatality causes while targeted traditional stops address all crash risk factors, and PRO’s failure to rebut that arithmetic without pivoting to a “per unit of harm” frame that evaded the motion’s wording.
