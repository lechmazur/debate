### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): PRO's central move — reframing the motion as a local, opt-out "switch" rather than a platform-wide ban — proved decisive because it directly defused CON's two strongest attacks (paternalism and the "public signal is destroyed" argument). CON's own answer to Pressure Question 1 conceded the local-hide framing ("For one viewer, PRO is right"), and PRO's closing capitalized sharply on that concession. PRO also landed a memorable, unanswered line ("income that depends on hunting people who would immediately turn the hunt off was never community support") that CON never squarely rebutted, instead reasserting that personalization is valuable without engaging the legitimacy question. CON's strongest surviving point — that a personalized upsell disabled for one user is, by definition, never delivered to that relationship — was real but PRO's response (the user who wants it can leave it on; this is autonomy, not suppression) was consistent with the motion's plain text about user-controlled tools, whereas CON's "prior restraint" framing sat awkwardly against a motion explicitly about giving users more control. Both sides executed the format correctly (proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2), but PRO's rebuttals were tighter, more responsive line-by-line, and rhetorically crisper, giving it a clear, comparative edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Turn 7-8 exchange on Pressure Question 1: CON's A4.1 concession that hiding is local for one viewer, followed by PRO's closing use of that concession ("CON already gave away the case") to collapse CON's public-square/paternalism framing into a narrower and weaker claim about compulsory enrollment.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by consistently distinguishing user-controlled opt-outs from bans. Side A established a plausible cost to creator income, but never justified why creators should retain access to personalized solicitation after a viewer declines it. B also showed that generic asks, tip jars, recognition, and rankings for willing viewers remain available, while existing alternatives such as leaving or ignoring do not provide equivalent control.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer that hiding a ranking changes only the opting-out user’s screen decisively weakened A’s public-signal argument. B then drew a workable distinction between ordinary appeals that remain available and personalized conversion mechanisms that users may disable, whereas A largely treated any effective opt-out as improper suppression.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): PRO won the characterization fight: it's a local view switch, not a ban. Once CON conceded in A4.1 that hiding on my screen doesn't delete the ranking for others, its public-signal and creator-livelihood routes collapsed. PRO flipped paternalism — the switch stops platforms from forcing the conversion layer — and its "income that depends on people who'd immediately mute it was pressure" weighing answered small-creator harm better than CON's persuasion-is-not-harm line.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Q1 about what actually disappears if hide is local, plus B4.1 A1 and CON's A4.1 concession that one viewer's hide leaves the ladder intact for creator and opt-in viewers. That exchange neutralized CON's networked-signal and room-intact claims central to livelihood and community-health arguments.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but CON landed the two most damaging exchanges. First, CON's "doctored room" attack on the leaderboard-hiding proposal exposed a real weakness in PRO's "personal filter, like hiding likes" analogy: hiding one's own metric differs meaningfully from hiding other people's public acts of support while still occupying the shared space. PRO's closing response (that attention is not an entitlement) was reasonable but arrived late and didn't fully dissolve the asymmetry CON identified. Second, CON's pressure question forced PRO to name a concrete surviving upsell example ("thanks for six months... badge unlocks the replay"), and CON's rebuttal then caught that example using the very spend/duration data PRO's own definition targeted — a sharp, well-timed contradiction that PRO could only partially patch by distinguishing "access" from "purchase demand." CON's rhetoric (stage lights, doctored room, "a relationship that cannot ask is not safer, it is fake") was also more vivid and memorable, sharpening the stakes for a reader. PRO's case remained principled and internally consistent on vulnerable-user protection and proportionality, and its rebuttals were competent, but it never fully neutralized CON's two core objections, leaving CON with the cleaner, more damaging path through the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 response to PRO's concrete upsell example ("thanks for six months" still uses spend/duration data PRO's own definition targets) was the most decisive single exchange, exposing an unresolved contradiction in PRO's administrability claim.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): B won by showing that optional, user-specific filters preserve creator sales and willing participation while giving vulnerable users an internal boundary short of leaving the community. A exposed real definitional ambiguity around personalized upsells, but its broader case depended on the unpersuasive premise that public recognition entitles creators and donors to every viewer's attention.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response that hiding a leaderboard creates a personal filter rather than rewriting the room decisively answered A's strongest framing. A's sharp dilemma about whether subscriber-history messages survive limited B's margin, but did not defeat the wider case for stronger user-controlled tools.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): PRO won the central clash on proportionality. B showed a hidden leaderboard is a personal filter like hiding likes that leaves public recognition intact for opt-in users, and that basic access offers remain while watch-history/location/rank-targeted upsells can be disabled. CON never answered Q2 with a concrete surviving offer and overstated communal injury, leaving its exit-not-boundary and fake-room claims without a live harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Rebuttal 2 exchange on whether one user's hide injures others: B4.1-B4.3 answered A3.1/A3.2 directly and reframed as personal filter vs compelled exposure, while A4.1-A4.3 conceded the filter removes public facts but failed to show why one opt-out damages willing fans.
