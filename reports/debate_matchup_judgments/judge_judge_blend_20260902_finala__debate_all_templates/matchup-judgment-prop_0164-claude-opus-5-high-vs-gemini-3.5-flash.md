### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won by establishing replication as a systematically underproduced public good and showing why discretionary, novelty-weighted funding does not solve that problem. Side B made a credible heterogeneity and opportunity-cost objection, but repeatedly treated the proposal as an identical quota imposed on every field or grant. It never adequately answered how important public findings get independently verified before costly downstream reliance. A's two clipped rebuttals were execution costs, and its claim that cheap fields should simply fund more checks left some waste concerns unresolved, but its central comparative case remained substantially stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange targeted B's proposed validation mechanism. B relied on private internal checks and competitive peer-reviewed grants; A showed that private validation does not reliably return knowledge to the public and that discretionary peer review largely recreates the status quo whose novelty incentives caused the underfunding. This directly reinforced the need for a reserved share.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B built a more coherent and sustained case showing that discretionary funding has systematically failed to produce replication because novelty-weighted incentives under-supply it. Side A never resolved the central epistemic problem that conceptual replication is ambiguous when it fails, and Side B devastatingly showed in closing that Side A's proposed alternative—relying on competitive peer-review and open science—is merely a description of the status quo that created the shortfall. Side A also failed to give a credible mechanism for how a lab verifies a specific landmark finding before committing years to it, and walked into a tension by calling replication simultaneously too cheap and too expensive. While Side B incurred real execution costs from two clipped speeches, the visible content won the decisive clashes, and Side B's closing crystallized the stakes far more sharply.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing identification that Side A's answer to the mechanism question—"competitive, peer-reviewed grants"—was not a rival proposal but a redescription of the exact status quo that had produced the replication shortfall, paired with the point that private pharma already validates publicly funded claims but keeps the answers so the public never learns the findings were hollow.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won the central market-failure clash. B's alternative of open mandates + competitive "valuable" replication was turned into an admission of the status quo that caused the crisis, and B never answered A's pressure Q2 about how a lab knows a specific landmark is real before betting 5 years on it. A's cheap-leverage and portfolio answers defused B's heterogeneity/waste objection better than B defused the diagnostic ambiguity of conceptual replication. Despite clipping costs, A gave the cleaner voting path: 90 sortable findings > 100 unsortable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 + Closing sequence on B4.2: showing B's "community deems valuable via competitive grants" plus industry internal validation is exactly the status quo and fails the pre-commitment test, with the turn that private validation privatizes the answer public money paid for.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by focusing on the fixed-share mechanism rather than disputing the value of replication. Its comparison showed that targeted replication and preventive safeguards preserve judgment, whereas a rigid set-aside can fund the marginal proposal because of its category rather than its value. Side A persuasively identified institutional bias and the unique benefits of independent testing, keeping the debate competitive, but never fully resolved diminishing returns, category padding, or why a fixed quota was superior to targeted mandates. B's clipped rebuttal was a minor execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A answered the weak-proposal problem by routing funds to replications in other fields; Side B sharply turned this concession by noting that the money still could not leave the protected category, so the quota ultimately overrides cross-category judgment at the margin.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): CON demonstrated that the fixed share only bites at the margin—when replication proposals are not worth funding—thereby overriding judgment rather than assisting it. PRO conceded that funds would be routed within the replication category to avoid returning them, confirming CON's fear that money would be attached to a genre rather than to value. CON also collapsed PRO's deterrence claim by showing that the "reputational and financial" penalties PRO cited already exist and have manifestly failed to prevent the replication crisis, leaving the ring-fenced budget with no credible teeth. Meanwhile, CON's alternative—point-of-production safeguards and targeted, judgment-based commissioning of high-stakes replications—remained intact and comparatively superior.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 showing that PRO's own "natural targeting" concession means the fixed share does no work while judgment does all of it, and that routing the pot across disciplines merely levies a tax on fields where replication is conceptually weak.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): B won on the marginal-effect comparison: B showed a fixed share only matters when it forces funding of a replication not worth funding, otherwise judgment does the work. A conceded the key point in A4.1 that weak proposals get routed to other replication proposals and money never leaves the category, validating B's genre-bias and cross-subsidy attack, and failed to answer the no-penalty audit critique.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B5 marginal-bite exchange – when replication is worth doing judgment already funds it; the quota only bites to fund the last, low-value replication because it is a replication.
