### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): B won a competitive debate by exposing two unresolved prerequisites: reliably defining low-employment programs and showing that caps would actually create shortage-field capacity. A plausibly argued that aggregated funds are fungible, but repeatedly assumed oversized, predatory programs and treated complex shortages as primarily funding problems. A also never adequately answered B's point that employment snapshots miss varied and long-term careers. B therefore made the policy's risks clearer: narrower access is certain, while added capacity and better employment matching remain uncertain.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's measurement rebuttal was most decisive. A dismissed successful non-vocational career paths as survivorship bias, but did not answer the structural objection that lagging employment metrics can misclassify programs and cannot reliably predict long-term labor demand.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B won by exposing that PRO's central mechanism—redirecting savings from capped low-employment programs to shortage fields—rests on a false fiscal premise. B showed that clinical placements require hospital partnerships and supervising nurses, while engineering expansion needs capital budgets and industry cost-sharing; cutting cheap humanities seminars does not convert into those specialized, non-fungible resources. A never solved this accounting problem, merely re-asserting that public funds are fungible. B also landed the stronger equity point: capping accessible on-ramps removes working-class access without guaranteeing admission to high-barrier shortage fields, making the policy attrition by design rather than mobility.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's sustained argument that humanities budgets are structurally non-convertible into clinical supervisory capacity and engineering labs, proving PRO's funding-redirection model is a false trade-off rather than a workable budgetary solution.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by making the conversion mechanism the decisive clash. PRO needed to show capping cheap lecture programs creates clinical placements and labs, but A repeatedly demonstrated different resource pools - hospital partnerships/supervisory nurses, capital budgets, bonding, industry partnerships - and offered direct debt remedies. B asserted fungibility and predatory-enrollment framing but never answered the mechanism question, leaving its finite-budget and protection claims without a live trade-off.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1/2 point that moving money from sociology/philosophy doesn't conjure supervising nurses or lab benches - low-cost and capital-intensive programs draw on different resources - which collapsed PRO's core fungibility claim and was never squarely refuted.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): B won by separating the existence of persistent shortages from whether the proposed caps would solve them. A plausibly distinguished demographic needs from cyclical demand, but never adequately showed that removing enrollment and revenue from other programs would create clinical placements, faculty, or suitably qualified students. B also exposed A’s computer-science example as evidence of forecasting risk and made the stronger fiscal and equity comparisons. A’s clipped closing and repeated characterization of B as opposing all alignment further weakened its finish.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive response was that even a genuine, decades-long nursing shortage does not establish the usefulness of capping unrelated programs: external training bottlenecks remain, while low-overhead enrollment revenue is lost. A’s answer that legislatures could redirect appropriations did not resolve either problem.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): CON won by showing that capping low-employment humanities destroys the net-tuition cross-subsidy that funds expensive STEM and clinical programs, creating a fiscal deficit without resolving the external bottlenecks (hospital placements, statutory supervision ratios, faculty shortages) that actually constrain nursing and engineering expansion. PRO’s demographic distinction between structural shortages and cyclical bubbles was plausible but never overcame CON’s concrete mechanism argument, and PRO’s clipped closing left the final crystallization unfinished.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s rebuttal that capping low-overhead humanities programs eliminates the surplus tuition that subsidizes expensive shortage fields while failing to create a single additional clinical placement or hospital bed, severing the link between enrollment caps and actual workforce relief.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): CON won the core clash on remedy effectiveness. Its accounting point that low-overhead humanities lectures generate net tuition subsidizing high-cost STEM/clinical programs and that nursing expansion is blocked by external hospital capacity/statutory ratios was never disproven; PRO only moralized that the model is "broken." CON's pressure on forecasting landed when PRO cited CS as a shortage then re-labeled tech layoffs as "unmanaged overexpansion," conceding whiplash risk. PRO's demographic standard in B4 was late and did not explain how caps create clinical slots. Clipped B5 closing added execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Exchange A2.2-A2.5 / B4.2: CON showed capping low-cost programs destroys surplus revenue without creating a single clinical placement, and used PRO's own CS pivot from shortage to glut to prove central planners cannot distinguish structural needs from cyclical bubbles. PRO's reply in A1/A2 reasserted demographics but left fiscal deficit and external-constraint points intact.
