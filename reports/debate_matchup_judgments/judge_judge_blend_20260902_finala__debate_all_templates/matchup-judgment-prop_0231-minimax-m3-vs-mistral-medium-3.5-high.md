### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger cumulative case across the full exchange. Three decisions stand out. First, the counterfactual on Vancouver: CON repeatedly argued that deaths rose while Insite operated, but PRO's response — hundreds reversed inside, fentanyl poisoning as the confounding variable, zero as the relevant baseline — was never squarely answered. CON never engaged with "what would the death toll look like without the site?" That silence matters. Second, the treatment-entry-rate clash resolved against CON. CON's signature "broken bridge" argument depended on under-10% treatment entry being damning. PRO's Q1 asked for the comparator rate in CON's preferred alternatives. CON's A1 essentially conceded that arrest and forced detox see "near-zero entry and high relapse" — which demolished the comparative force of the 10% figure. A bridge 90% don't cross is still better than a ditch. Third, the "next ten minutes" framing (PRO's Q2) was the most decisive pressure in the debate. CON's answer — mobile naloxone outreach — was a reasonable idea but was introduced in Rebuttal 2, not the opening, making it an undeveloped late addition rather than a grounded alternative. PRO's closing response that mobile response "takes time, takes a decision, takes a user who is still conscious" was not fully refuted. CON's closing gambit — judge by the city, not by the room — is PRO's strongest live challenge, and PRO did partially answer it with the counterfactual argument, though CON's final framing was cleaner on this point. The clipped Rebuttal 1 is a modest execution cost for CON. Overall PRO held the decisive burden — keeping someone alive through the immediate crisis — and CON never mounted a satisfying answer to that floor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question 2 ("what does your plan do in the next ten minutes?") and the follow-through in Rebuttal 2. It exposed that CON's preferred alternatives — treatment beds, enforcement, mobile outreach — all presuppose a living user with time to make choices. CON's belated mobile-outreach answer in Rebuttal 2 was too undeveloped to neutralize it, and PRO's response that a mobile unit still "takes a decision, takes a user who is still conscious" closed the loop cleanly. This exchange defined the decisive margin in the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): A won by making the cleaner marginal and counterfactual case: supervised sites save overdosing users who are otherwise alone, create health contact, and can reduce visible street use when properly scaled. B’s city-wide challenge was relevant, but it leaned too heavily on before-and-after death trends and displacement claims without answering A’s point that one under-scaled site cannot be judged as the whole policy. B also slid toward an “eliminate disorder” or “solve addiction” burden, which exceeded the motion’s reduce-and-permit standard. A’s answers on fentanyl, scale, and immediate rescue left the strongest live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s rebuttal to the Vancouver point was decisive: rising deaths during fentanyl contamination did not prove SCS failure, and on-site overdose reversals were a direct counterfactual reason to think deaths would be higher without them.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): PRO won by establishing an irreducible floor—immediate overdose reversal onsite—that CON’s mobile-outreach alternative could not match, and by reframing CON’s own examples of rising city-wide deaths and localized displacement as evidence of under-scaled supply rather than model failure. CON’s clipped first rebuttal and inability to show why a distributed network would not absorb displacement left its city-wide critique unpersuasive, while PRO’s sharper rebuttals and crystallization of the sidewalk-versus-room stakes gave the cleaner reason to permit the sites.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 1 recast Vancouver’s overdose surge, Sydney’s localized displacement, and low treatment entry as symptoms of insufficient scale and unmet demand, converting CON’s own evidence into support for the motion.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO opened with a richer evidence base and a clear on-site mechanism, establishing the stronger initial position. But CON progressively dismantled it across three key dimensions. First, the tautology point on "zero on-site fatalities" (A2.1) was analytically sharp and never adequately answered—PRO's Rebuttal 2 simply reasserted that city-wide Swiss reductions "track" SCS expansion without engaging the confounding critique. Second, CON's mismatch argument—that the motion covers Latin American crack, coca paste, and methamphetamine markets for which injection-oriented evidence is structurally irrelevant—was a genuine live burden that PRO failed to meet; asserting European inhalation rooms exist is not the same as showing those retrofitted rooms can absorb open-plaza crack scenes at São Paulo's scale. Third, PRO's Rebuttal 2 was only 155 words, leaving opportunity cost, the scale gap, and the confounding objection largely unanswered. CON's closing crystallized the "adaptability claimed is not adaptability demonstrated" line and the one-size-fits-all framing cleanly. PRO's closing recycled prior points without engaging the live pressure. The decisive path to victory belonged to CON: after the full exchange, more of CON's arguments remained standing while PRO's strongest evidence—Vancouver and on-site zero fatalities—had been materially damaged without adequate repair.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.1–A2.2 sequence in Rebuttal 1: the "zero on-site fatalities" tautology (staff with naloxone prevent deaths in a room they staff) combined with the confounding critique of Vancouver data (Insite cannot be isolated from concurrent naloxone distribution and treatment expansion). This directly attacked PRO's anchor evidence, exposed a real methodological gap, and was never squarely answered in any subsequent PRO speech.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A established a plausible direct mechanism for on-site overdose prevention and some disorder reduction, but leaned heavily on overbroad causal claims from limited examples. Side B made the more decisive comparison: on-site reversals do not by themselves prove city-wide mortality reduction, and the affirmative evidence fit European injection contexts far better than varied Latin American crack, coca paste, and methamphetamine markets. Side A's answers on adaptability and controlled population-level effects were repetitive and did not fully meet the pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange was challenging "zero on-site fatalities" as insufficient proof of city-wide impact, then pressing Side A for controlled evidence and for proof that SCS can absorb large Latin American open-air drug scenes; Side A's replies mostly restated mechanism and European inhalation-room examples without resolving those burdens.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON demonstrated that the proposition's evidence comes exclusively from European and North American injection markets, while Latin American crises involve different substances and scales the model has not shown it can handle. CON also exposed that on-site overdose reversals do not prove city-wide mortality reductions and that disorder data suffer from confounding and honeypot effects. PRO never adequately isolated SCS effects from concurrent policies or proved adaptability to Latin American contexts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal that on-site fatality prevention is tautological evidence for city-wide mortality reduction, combined with showing that Frankfurt's disorder reductions were measured within facility zones while adjacent blocks experienced concentrated nuisance.
