PRO's substantive pillars (power asymmetry, reasonable-accommodation standard, public interest) held up across rounds and were consistently defended. CON's strongest moments—the roofs/boilers distinction (building-serving vs. tenant-serving), the point that off-site charging means refusal is not a denial of mobility, and the "law must fit hard cases, not easy pilots" frame—were genuine and damaged PRO's case, but CON never fully dismantled PRO's core procedural proposal. PRO's most decisive move came in Rebuttal 2: the dilemma argument that if CON concedes "reasonableness," it has accepted PRO's position, and if it insists on an absolute override, it is arguing a straw man. CON's counter—if the right is merely procedural, PRO's climate/equity case is oversold; if strong enough to matter, CON's cost and litigation objections bite—was competent but came late and was slightly less crisp. PRO's closing crystallized the dilemma cleanly without introducing new evidence. CON's closing was tight and substantive but couldn't fully neutralize the procedural/accommodation-standard framework PRO defended throughout. On grounding, both sides used plausible general knowledge; PRO's "Portland pilot 5% of retrofit budget" figure was moderately specific but presented tentatively rather than as verified data, while CON's broader claims were well-anchored. Overall PRO maintained cleaner argument continuity and landed the sharper comparative framing in the critical later rounds.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger cumulative case. Its central framing—that the proposition shifts the default from arbitrary delay to reasoned decision-making, while preserving every real landlord defense—held up across the whole debate and was never squarely neutralized. The "right to impose" characterization CON used was effectively exposed as a strawman: if safety, cost, capacity, and parking remain valid grounds for denial, the only outcome the right actually changes is the inertia/blank-veto scenario, which CON never defended on the merits. PRO's handling of the pressure questions was notably more honest and direct than CON's: A1 acknowledged the threshold is a reasonableness test subject to challenge (rather than denying courts could review it), and A2 acknowledged residual landlord risk while explaining how the framework manages rather than erases it. CON's A2 answer—explicitly endorsing "wait or move" as the market alternative—was a damaging concession PRO correctly weaponized in both Rebuttal 2 and the closing. CON's strongest live points (vague standards invite litigation; insurance exposure doesn't transfer even with tenant payment; all-tenant cost absorption) were real and not fully resolved, but CON kept applying them too broadly, treating any legal review of landlord delay as intolerable coercion rather than defending the specific outcome of indefinite blocking of feasible, tenant-funded requests. PRO's closing crystallized the decisive clash cleanly: reviewable reasonableness versus arbitrary blockage, with CON having explicitly chosen the latter. The two clipped turns (PRO's opening and Rebuttal 2) represent a minor execution cost, but the visible arguments were sufficient to sustain PRO's lead.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was PRO's pressure question Q2 and CON's explicit A2 response. PRO asked whether CON's market alternative meant renters should "simply wait indefinitely or move." CON confirmed in Rebuttal 2 A2 that yes, "wait for the building to upgrade voluntarily or move" is the answer. PRO then used that concession in Rebuttal 2 and the closing to show CON was defending permission for indefinite exclusion rather than any principled limit on the proposed right. That moment shifted the comparison from abstract cost-balancing to a concrete distributional choice, and CON never recovered a winning principle afterward.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing the proposition as a shift from arbitrary vetoes to a reasonableness standard, rather than a blank check to force installations. Side A relied heavily on the claim that all tenants would subsidize the insurance/litigation risks, but Side B effectively dismantled this by pointing out that added insurance and indemnity could be paid by the requesting tenant. Side A also bit the bullet on telling renters to "wait or move," which Side B successfully leveraged as proof of indefinite exclusion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal directly answered Side A's pressure on insurance by pointing out that tenant funding can include added insurance and indemnity, neutralizing Side A's core "hidden subsidy" impact.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins by successfully framing the motion as a modest shift from arbitrary veto to conditional, reviewable reasonableness. PRO effectively neutralized CON's cost and liability concerns by detailing tenant-funded installation, code compliance, and indemnity, while weighing speculative administrative burden against certain exclusion. CON's reliance on a wait-or-move market alternative proved to be a major strategic vulnerability that PRO exploited across multiple rounds. Though CON raised valid points about lingering insurance exposure and litigation risk, it never fully answered how standard reasonableness tests and indemnity clauses mitigate those concerns, nor did it offer a practical path for renters in existing stock. PRO's clearer weighing, direct answers to pressure, and sharper crystallization secured the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 directly answering CON's pressure on liability and expense thresholds. By explaining that a reasonableness test tied to building facts and indemnity/code compliance manages risk without granting an unreviewable veto, PRO turned CON's right-to-impose frame into a defense of arbitrary blockage, then cleanly weighed speculative paperwork against certain exclusion.
