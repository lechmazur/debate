### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): PRO's decisive move was separating immediate content removal from the subsequent account suspension decision. This reframe—established in Rebuttal 1 and sharpened through the pressure questions exchange—directly neutralized CON's strongest safety objection: if harmful posts can be taken down at once (as platforms already do for CSAM and direct threats), then a mandatory procedural window before permanent account exclusion does not create the multi-day gap during which disinformation spreads or harassment escalates. CON's best counter—that gray-area cases require genuine deliberation and can't be fairly adjudicated in a compressed window—actually cut against CON's own position, since the need for deliberation is precisely why bare swift bans are problematic. PRO also answered the A1/A2 pressure question labels directly and honestly, while CON's Rebuttal 2 was clipped before completion, leaving its weighing argument incomplete. On small-platform burden, CON's dismissal of shared appeal services was mostly assertion; PRO's narrower framing (standardized notice only for the suspension decision, not a full pre-action hearing) remained plausible and unanswered. CON's alternatives—market incentives, voluntary post-suspension appeals, community panels—were introduced late and without development, whereas PRO's core moral claim (private infrastructure exercising public-square power without minimal procedural accountability) remained live throughout. The margin is clear but not dominant because CON ran a genuinely competitive case and the gray-area adjudication point is a real limitation PRO only partially answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 answer in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive exchange: by conceding that content removal can happen immediately while insisting due process attaches only to the subsequent permanent account suspension, PRO cleanly separated the safety and evasion objections from the procedural mandate. CON's response—that gray-area cases require time for meaningful review—inadvertently reinforced PRO's point that rushed, unaccountable suspension decisions are the problem the rule is designed to fix.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): B wins on the strength of two sustained arguments that A never fully neutralized. First, B's content-removal/account-suspension distinction—platforms can instantly remove harmful material while applying due process only to the irreversible account ban—was introduced in B2.2-B2.3, pressure-tested in B's Q1 via the "temporary posting restriction" middle ground, and defended in B4.2 with the tiered-process answer. A's response in A4.1 (fresh accounts, migration) is a real point but does not squarely rebut the specific mechanism B offered; it describes a different problem entirely. Second, B's switching-costs argument against market exit—that non-transferable audiences, customer bases, and support networks make competition an insufficient substitute—was never genuinely answered. A's best reply was that users maintain multi-platform presences, which speaks to a different kind of user than the ones B identified as most harmed. A's strongest surviving argument is the regulatory-overreach concern: once documented decisions exist, regulators can audit them and blur procedure into substance. This is a real pressure point, and B's response (the rule only governs applying existing policies, so overreach would be a separate unlawful act) is partially circular. Still, this one theoretical concern does not outweigh B's concrete infrastructure-harm framing and the coherent middle-ground design for complying with the rule. Execution costs also matter: A consistently ran well under the word cap in every substantive speech (opening 230/340, rebuttals 254/340 and 183/340, closing 138/220), leaving live burdens underdeveloped and B's pressure questions only superficially answered. B was clipped twice, an execution cost, but the visible content in those speeches remained strong enough to hold the key lines. On balance, B built and defended a cleaner path to the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was B's content-removal/account-suspension distinction (B2.2-B2.3), carried through B's Q1 pressure question and defended in B4.2. B established that due process applies only to the irreversible account ban, not to immediate content removal, then offered a concrete mechanism—temporary posting restrictions during appeal—to defeat A's timing-harm claim. A's response in A4.1 shifted to a fresh-account-migration concern rather than engaging the specific mechanism, leaving B's middle-ground design standing. This exchange resolved the central practical objection to the proposal in B's favor.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the central harm clearer: erroneous, opaque bans can seriously damage livelihoods, organizing, and community access, while basic notice, reasons, and appeal offer a direct remedy. Side A raised real concerns about delay, administrative burden, and regulatory creep, but relied heavily on speculative overreach and a weak market-exit alternative that Side B effectively attacked through network effects and switching costs. Side B’s narrowing between immediate harmful-content removal and final account bans was the key comparative answer, though slightly weakened by overclaiming trivial costs and some clipped turns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest rebuttal was that due process need not stop platforms from removing harmful content immediately; it only checks the more consequential account-level ban, undercutting Side A’s main “slower harm removal” route.

The decisive move was distinguishing instant content removal from permanent account suspension, showing that platforms could still delete CSAM and harassment immediately while only requiring due process for the irreversible act of banning a user. When pressed, PRO proposed freezing posting privileges during appeals, a concrete compromise that CON never adequately answered—CON's responses that abusers would simply migrate to fresh accounts or external links were unpersuasive because those evasions are possible under the status quo too. CON's market-exit alternative remained abstract and unsupported against PRO's concrete examples of audience lock-in and non-transferable communities. CON's procedural-to-substantive slippery slope and volume-burden arguments were plausible but speculative, and they lost ground by failing to engage the freeze-during-appeal mechanism in the closing. PRO's clipped speeches were an execution cost, but the arguments that survived were enough to maintain a live route to victory that CON did not squarely neutralize.
