### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): CON built a tighter, more sustained throughline: the "ledger" the motion assumes is not neutral (whose costs and benefits get counted, and by whom), caps on the most regulable, taxable, trackable form of tourism likely push volume into harder-to-manage substitutes (cars, vans, scattered day trips), and the housing-pressure criterion doesn't map cleanly onto cruise/coach day-trippers who don't rent apartments. CON also landed a clever turn on PRO's own "manageable vehicle" concession, arguing that PRO is choosing to cap exactly the traffic that is easiest to regulate while leaving harder-to-control substitutes untouched. PRO's principled framing (the motion's own threshold, "no industry entitled to expand past negative net contribution") was clear and its permit/lottery answer to allocation was reasonably concrete, but its housing response was a hedge ("one factor among several") that CON exploited effectively, and its substitution-effect answer (pair caps with parking/pedestrian controls) implicitly conceded caps alone don't solve the problem. PRO's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 were both flagged as clipped for exceeding word limits, a real, repeated execution cost that cut off its closing synthesis twice and reflects negatively on delivery discipline, while CON stayed within limits and used the Pressure Questions and Answer format cleanly throughout. On balance CON's case was more consistently damaging to PRO's live route to victory and better resolved the key clashes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's turn on the "manageable vehicle" concession (B2.4/B4.2-B4.4): pointing out that PRO's own admission that ships and coaches are easier to time, tax, and inspect than scattered cars means capping them just disperses volume into less regulable traffic, directly undercutting PRO's central mechanism for why caps reduce net harm.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully exposing that Side A's rejection of a "net harm" ledger leaves the city with no limiting principle against unlimited overload. Side A argued brilliantly that the ledger would be politically rigged and that capping efficient mass transport causes worse car traffic, but Side B's counter—that unmanaged crowds already exclude people and that ignoring harms values residents at zero—was decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which reframed Side A's "imperfect measurement" argument as a permanent license for unlimited overload, definitively answering Side A's strongest political economy critique.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully anchoring the debate to the motion's explicit threshold. While Side B offered excellent rhetoric and strong specific points (such as cruise passengers sleeping on board and the risk of substitution), their core argument relied heavily on the impossibility of neutral measurement and the risk of political bias in weighing harms. Side A decisively countered this by pointing out that refusing to measure resident harms simply values them at zero, and that Side B offered no limiting principle for what to do when the motion's condition is actually met. Side A's framing that Side B's position converts uncertainty into a "permanent license for overload" was the decisive weighing mechanism. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Both sides argued with unusual precision, but B built the more durable structural attack: that the motion caps by transport *mode* when the actual harm is occupancy/density at a specific place and time. B's taxi-vs-bus-passenger example in Rebuttal 2/Closing was never answered by A and exposed that a mode cap both excludes harmless arrivals and admits identical harmful ones. B also exploited A's Rebuttal 2 concession that the cap functions only as a "backstop" after targeted tools fail, arguing this narrows PRO's position beyond what the motion's plain trigger ("when pressures outweigh gains") actually states—a sharp catch of a shifting goalpost that A's closing did not resolve. B's reversal on housing (day-trippers who "sleep aboard" create less bed-demand than the overnight tourists PRO prefers) was a clean, plausible mechanism A never fully neutralized; A's counter (souvenir turnover drives conversion) was asserted rather than firmly established. A's case remained strong on the synchronized-surge mechanism and the "rations openly vs. rations by eviction" framing, and its dashboard answer to Q1 was a reasonable attempt at operationalizing "outweigh," but B correctly noted it measures without resolving incommensurable units, and A's closing repeated the surge dilemma without directly meeting B's occupancy/reservation counter-mechanism. Both answered pressure questions in proper A1/A2 format. On balance, B's rebuttals did more damage to A's live route than vice versa, giving B a clear but not dominant win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 point that a reservation/occupancy system can ration the crowded lane itself regardless of arrival mode, illustrated by the taxi-vs-bus passenger comparison, most directly undercut PRO's core mechanism and went unanswered in A's closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a decisive victory by separating the harms of overcrowding from the specific transport modes (cruise ships and buses) targeted by the motion. Side A systematically proved that direct interventions like street occupancy limits, emissions rules, and rental bans solve the harms without arbitrarily banning specific arrival methods.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal and closing perfectly dismantled Side B's "what happens when the slots are full" argument by pointing out that a full city justifies capping street occupancy for everyone, not broadly banning specific transport modes whose passengers might otherwise disperse.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B wins by analytically dismantling the proposition's specific mechanism. While Side A eloquently framed the harms of overtourism and the need for a volume ceiling, Side B successfully proved that capping specific transport modes (ships and buses) is both over-inclusive and under-inclusive compared to capping the actual harms (street occupancy, emissions). Side B's point that cruise passengers sleep aboard and thus do not drive short-term housing conversions effectively neutralized Side A's housing argument, and their alternative of capping street-level occupancy rather than transport modes provided a cleaner, more logical policy response to the motion's conditions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they decisively distinguished between capping a transport mode and capping actual street occupancy, proving that a mode cap excludes harmless dispersed arrivals while failing to stop identical crowding from other transport modes.
