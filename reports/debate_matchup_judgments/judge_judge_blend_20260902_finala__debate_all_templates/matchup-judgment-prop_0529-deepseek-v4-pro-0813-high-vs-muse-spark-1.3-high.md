### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): The decisive clash was whether coordinated public compute creates genuine independent capacity or merely adds bureaucratic overhead on top of the same private infrastructure. CON (A) built a sustained throughline—speed mismatch, "sublease with a committee landlord," loss of vendor-switching exit options, and the claim that disclosure/audit legislation can achieve accountability without building a duplicate public cloud. PRO (B) landed good early points (conflict of interest, telescope pooling analogy, peer-review vs. corporate selector) but conceded in Rebuttal 2 that the reserve "need not own every private model" and instead relies on procurement leverage—precisely the opening CON needed to argue the entire compute-coordination apparatus is unnecessary if disclosure conditions can be imposed directly while researchers rent wherever capacity is cheapest. PRO's closing attempt to rescue the case ("access rights are not access capacity") asserts that pooled capacity is more independent without explaining why reserve-rented hardware differs from grant-rented hardware, leaving A's "sublease" critique unanswered. A's pressure questions and rebuttals stayed tightly focused on this fault line and progressively sharpened it, while B's answers, though competent, did not fully close the gap opened by its own concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 response to B's concession that "the reserve need not own every private model"—arguing this means the proposed mechanism collapses into ordinary disclosure regulation, which doesn't require public compute coordination at all—was the most damaging and unanswered exchange in the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): A won by distinguishing formal audit permission from the independent capacity needed to exercise it. B raised credible concerns about procurement lag, political allocation, and access to closed models, but repeatedly treated the public reserve as replacing all other options even though A explicitly defended it as a countervailing option alongside private firms. That weakened B's central claim that coordination eliminates exit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B argued that disclosure rules could provide access without public compute; A answered that disclosure alone leaves researchers dependent on the audited firm's infrastructure for reproduction, retraining, and inspection. This directly preserved the proposition's unique benefit.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by severing the audit goal from the public-compute mechanism. It showed that conditioning access and compelling disclosure can be done via regulation and portable grants without building a centralized, lag-prone infrastructure pool that would likely rely on the same private hardware anyway. Side B (PRO) never established that frontier-scale public compute is necessary for the independent research it values, especially after conceding that closed weights remain inaccessible without separate compulsion. CON’s preservation of exit options, vendor pluralism, and sharper weighing of the politicization risks created a cleaner route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second-rebuttal severance: legislation and procurement rules can demand audit checkpoints directly, letting researchers spend grants wherever capacity is cheapest and newest, which undermined PRO’s claim that building coordinated public compute is necessary to gain leverage over closed firms.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): The debate collapsed into a single decisive fault line: is the frontier-research bottleneck proprietary permission (weights, logs, API access) or compute ownership? CON (A) built and sustained this "permission over ownership" frame from the opening through closing, and used it to land two key blows: (1) it forced PRO to narrow the motion's claim from full frontier-scale replication to merely "large but not firm-scale" evaluation/fine-tuning access, which CON then exploited to argue that national funding or regulated credits satisfy that narrower need without requiring cross-border public compute coordination; (2) it correctly separated "coordination on disclosure/regulatory leverage" from "coordination on compute infrastructure," pointing out that PRO's strongest late argument (joint bargaining power to compel model deposit) argues for coordinated regulation, not specifically for the motion's proposed compute-pooling mechanism. PRO (B) answered capably with the "empty room"/"tenant in the landlord's cloud" framing, arguing legal rights are hollow without auditor-controlled capacity, and raised a real equity point about smaller countries being unable to sustain compute alone — but never cleanly rebutted CON's ordering critique or the conflation charge, instead reasserting the same metaphor in closing. Both sides used the pressure round and A1/A2 format properly, so no execution penalty either way. CON's throughline was tighter and more precisely tied to the motion's specific wording, giving it the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 move exploiting B's narrowing ("large but not firm-scale" allocations) to argue the remedy is access rights rather than a coordinated public buildout, sharpened further in Rebuttal 2 by distinguishing coordinated regulatory leverage from the motion's specific compute-coordination ask.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): A won a competitive debate by showing that legal disclosure and independent compute are complementary rather than substitutes. B persuasively established that public GPUs cannot overcome withheld models, but never neutralized A’s broader case for open-model research, replication, noncommercial work, and reliable capacity outside firm control. B also conceded regulator-controlled or targeted independent compute, weakening its opposition to the proposition. A’s case for international coordination over national provision was somewhat underdeveloped, keeping the margin modest.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange concerned B’s “empty room” argument. B showed disclosure must be legally compelled first, but A answered that disclosure alone still leaves auditors dependent on firm-controlled infrastructure and that public capacity remains necessary for meaningful reruns. B’s concession that verification needs regulator-controlled or other independent compute ultimately supported A’s central independence claim.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing that PRO's plan addresses the wrong bottleneck. The decisive clash turned on whether independent frontier research requires publicly owned compute or enforceable legal access to models. CON showed that without legal compulsion forcing firms to disclose weights, logs, and APIs, public clusters are expensive empty rooms. Once that access is compelled, targeted independent compute or certified auditors can verify without the political delays and coordination costs of a multinational buildout. CON sharpened this ordering error through direct answers to pressure questions and a closing that crystallized the chokepoint as proprietary permission, not accelerator ownership. PRO's tenant-in-the-landlord's-cloud metaphor remained resonant but could not overcome CON's demonstration that compute ownership adds no leverage where the real barrier is legal disclosure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.3), where CON turned PRO's pressure question back on them by arguing that public compute gets the order backwards: disclosure must be legally compelled first, and only then does independent compute matter. This framed the debate around the true chokepoint and neutralized PRO's core safety argument.
