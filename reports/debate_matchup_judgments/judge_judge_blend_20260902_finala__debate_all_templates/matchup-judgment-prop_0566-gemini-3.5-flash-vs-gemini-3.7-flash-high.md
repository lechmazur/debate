### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by combining a clear fairness principle with the crucial distinction between ordinary legacy applicants and genuinely transformative donors. Side A never adequately established that hereditary admissions preferences are necessary for alumni loyalty or philanthropy, instead repeatedly asserting that limits would cause financial shortfalls and wealthy replacement admits. It also treated the motion’s sharp limitation largely as total abolition. Side B’s claims that every displaced seat would reach a first-generation student were overstated, but its broader case did not depend on that guarantee.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s first pressure question exposed the central gap: most legacy beneficiaries are not transformative donors. Side A answered with general claims about intergenerational community but never explained why that community requires an admissions advantage for each alumnus’s child, leaving its core financial justification unsupported.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): B won by keeping the decisive zero-sum fairness clash live and forcing A to defend ordinary hereditary boosts, not just mega-donor financing. A's resource/community tradeoff was real and coherent, especially on replacement seats and non-outlier schools, but never fully neutralized B's separation of typical legacies from transformative philanthropy or the examples of schools that limited preferences without collapsing. B answered pressure more cleanly and gave the sharper reason to prefer the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first rebuttal split ordinary legacy admits from mega-donors and used Johns Hopkins plus non-dynastic alumni cultures to collapse A's Robin Hood necessity claim.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by effectively dismantling the moral and practical defenses of legacy admissions. While Side A made a strong, pragmatic case about the financial realities of private university endowments and correctly identified the unique, billionaire-funded circumstances of Johns Hopkins, Side B successfully neutralized the "Robin Hood" defense. Side B highlighted that the vast majority of legacy admits are not transformative mega-donors, but merely affluent applicants receiving an unearned structural boost. Side B's rhetorical framing of the issue as a choice between earned opportunity and hereditary entitlement was highly persuasive. Side A's argument that eliminated legacy seats simply go to other wealthy non-legacy students was a strong tactical point, but it ultimately conceded the zero-sum exclusion of lower-income strivers without proving that dynastic preferences were a necessary or justifiable alternative to fund the institution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where they systematically dismantled Side A's financial defense by distinguishing between transformative mega-donors and the vast majority of legacy admits who simply receive an unearned boost without providing equivalent financial support, effectively neutralizing the "Robin Hood" justification.
