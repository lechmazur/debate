### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Both sides built genuinely strong, well-structured cases and the Hawaiian immersion example became the central battleground, with B landing a real hit by reframing it as a "domestication" story rather than proof of PRO's model. B's pressure questions on legitimacy of state ranking and the "public money, community hands" incoherence exposed a real structural weak point that A never fully resolved (who defines "the community" or "authentic" language remained live). However, A's Q2 on who funds twenty years of paid transmission forced B into offering "community endowments" as the answer, which A then exploited decisively in closing: endowments require surplus, and the communities with the strongest restitution claims are precisely those without surplus, meaning private capital systematically bypasses the neediest cases. B's closing addressed the political-owner distinction but never rebutted this wealth-correlation point, leaving A's final weighing argument standing largely unanswered. A also neutralized much of B's "illegitimate ranking" framing by noting that zero public funding is itself a ranking by default. On balance, A's closing did more to resolve the decisive clash in its favor, while B left its strongest late-round vulnerability unaddressed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing turn on B's "community endowment" answer to Pressure Q2 — arguing that endowments require surplus while endangerment tracks dispossession, so private funding structurally misses the neediest communities — landed as the most decisive, largely unanswered blow of the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A consistently dominated the structural analysis of funding, successfully arguing that only the state can provide the necessary time horizon for preservation. Side A expertly neutralized Side B's strongest argument (that public funding means state control via FOIA) by introducing the highly plausible analogy of publicly funded legal aid and sealed museum collections. Furthermore, Side A's closing decisively dismantled Side B's late introduction of 'private endowments' by pointing out that endowments require surplus wealth, which dispossessed communities systematically lack.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing brilliantly dealt with Side B's alternatives. A's Rebuttal 2 used legal aid and sealed museum collections to disprove the necessity of FOIA intrusion, and A's Closing exposed the wealth prerequisite in B's 'endowment' model.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): PRO held the cleaner path on irreversibility and the structural funding gap: private/charity mechanisms already exist and still leave languages dying, while only patient public capital reliably pays for multi-decade transmission among the dispossessed. CON's control and "domestication" attacks were sharp and original, but designable (vested trusts, audit vs content) and never neutralized the scale/need asymmetry; CON's own endowment concession was flipped into source critique rather than case defeat.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON (B) sustained a sharper, more disciplined line of attack that PRO (A) never fully neutralized. B's central metric — a standing fund can salary dozens of elders but cannot payroll the "ten thousand daily choices" parents make at the kitchen table — became the organizing weighing device of the debate, and A's closing never directly answered it, instead reasserting that paid time enables love without explaining how payroll reaches unpaid home transmission. B also landed two structural catches that A only partially defused: the tension between "community governance" (A1.5) and the fund preserving memory because it "teaches citizenship" belonging to the nation (A1.6), and the point that "procedural" certification of legitimate councils/dialects (A4.3) still requires someone to adjudicate authenticity, just under a euphemism. B's Ireland counter-example was used efficiently across rounds to undercut A's reliance on Hawaiian, and B exposed that A's own defense of Hawaiian ("elder paid to sit and speak to toddlers," funded through legislated immersion) is functionally school funding, forcing A into conceding in its own closing that "schooling is standing public money" — a move B preemptively answered by noting schools/reparations/archival law are rights owed independent of this specific ministry-style fund. A's case had strong opening architecture (public goods framing, reparative debt, distrust of parachute researchers) and some good rhetorical counters (the "bootprint after lifting the boot" line), but its Rebuttal 1 was cut off mid-argument (a real execution cost), and its answers in Rebuttal 2/Closing largely restated earlier points rather than resolving B's sharpest pressure. B's pressure questions were also more incisive, targeting the ownership contradiction and the finite-budget certification problem, and B's subsequent answers to A's questions (schooling-in-disguise, math-vs-language asymmetry) were tighter than A's answers to B's questions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 point that "the elder's twenty years are paid the way every teacher's are: by a school... That answer concedes my point: their fund is schooling in disguise" (B4.1), combined with the recurring "hearth stays cold" metric (B4.6), which A's closing never directly rebutted.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): PRO won by demonstrating that CON's alternatives either succumb to precarity (volunteer fatigue, moldy cassettes) or quietly concede the resolution (funding minority schools with public money). CON's attempt to define public funding for immersion schools as separate from the motion's "standing funds" was effectively cornered and dismantled by PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to the math analogy (B4.2) was brilliantly argued, pointing out that professionalizing a fragile practice relocates it away from the home. However, PRO's strategic identification of CON's "schools as rights" stance as a concession secured the larger debate.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): CON built the cleaner path by keeping the kitchen-table transmission barrier decisive, treating Hawaiian as schooling (not the motion) and Ireland as the relevant long-run counter, then packaging justice as rights/repatriation/anti-discrimination without a purpose-locked fund that still gatekeeps. PRO's public-goods and continuity case stayed live but was narrowed into "fund schools as schools" by the close, which CON already accepted without the motion; ownership and canonization pressure also cut PRO harder than precarity cut CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON R1 on Hawaiian as state schooling versus Ireland's century of funded institutional Irish with shrinking hearths (A2.2–A2.3), later locked by the math/professionalization answer (A4.2).
