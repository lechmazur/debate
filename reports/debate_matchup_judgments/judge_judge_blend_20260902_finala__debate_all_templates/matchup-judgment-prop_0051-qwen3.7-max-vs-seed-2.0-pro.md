### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): PRO (Side B) held a narrow but real edge across the full exchange. Its core regulatory-arbitrage framing — that domestic retailers face strict product-liability while cross-border mega-platforms evade the same obligations through jurisdictional engineering — was introduced early, developed consistently, and never squarely rebutted by CON. CON pivoted almost entirely to consumer affordability, which is a real concern but leaves the normative asymmetry argument largely standing. PRO's "bottleneck" mechanism — that platforms have sufficient economic leverage to force supplier compliance once accountability attaches to the storefront — was plausible and causal, and CON's "regulatory theatre" counter was weaker by comparison; CON asserted it wouldn't work without addressing the economic leverage logic. PRO also effectively answered both pressure questions: the corporate-lobbying question was turned back to prove regulatory arbitrage; the child-without-a-coat question was answered by identifying it as a social-safety-net failure rather than a justification for toxic imports. CON's strongest sustained point — "you are pretending a hidden stock of safe affordable clothing is waiting, and it isn't" — was genuine pressure and PRO's secondhand-market alternative never scaled convincingly. That gap prevents a larger PRO margin. CON's rhetorical framing ("sitting in a child's backpack right now") was the debate's most vivid line, but the underlying argument about alternative availability, while strong, coexisted with an unaddressed regulatory-arbitrage problem and an underperforming "regulatory theatre" claim. On balance PRO controlled the normative core of the motion more consistently and answered pressure more honestly, earning a clear but competitive win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Q1 about corporate lobbying was the most decisive single exchange: rather than being embarrassed by the lobbying fact, PRO reframed it as direct confirmation that the current cross-border model survives on regulatory arbitrage rather than genuine efficiency, which turned CON's own question into support for the PRO case and left CON's A1 without a clean follow-up.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won by keeping the motion centered on marketplace responsibility for safety and transparency, and by making the trade-off look like internalizing hidden harms rather than simply banning cheap clothing. Side B had a compelling affordability frame, but leaned on overbroad claims that poor families would have no alternatives and that the policy would do nothing at all, without adequately answering A's bottleneck argument that marketplaces control access, promotion, logistics, and supplier incentives. B also used some unsupported, sweeping claims about EU rules and corporate lobbying. A's responses were cleaner, especially on regulatory arbitrage and product-safety harms to low-income consumers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's first rebuttal was decisive: it reframed B's poverty argument by arguing that low-income consumers are also the most exposed to toxic goods, rejected the small-seller defense by focusing liability on mega-platforms, and answered the foreign-government point with the marketplace bottleneck theory.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A wins by sustaining a coherent framework around regulatory arbitrage: cross-border mega-platforms function as de facto retailers and should face the same product-safety and labor-transparency obligations as domestic stores, rather than externalizing true costs onto workers and consumers. Side B effectively pressed the regressive consumer impact and the risk of entrenching incumbents, but Side A materially blunted these attacks by showing that toxic goods disproportionately harm the very low-income families CON claims to protect, by turning the domestic lobbying observation into proof of unfair competition (A4.1), and by answering the "coat" dilemma with concrete, non-toxic alternatives. CON's argument that the policy would not directly improve overseas factory conditions retained some force, yet never fully answered why product-safety checks should not apply to marketplace gatekeepers, leaving PRO's core comparative advantage alive at the end of the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 turn on domestic corporate lobbying (A4.1), where PRO converted CON's own "entrenching incumbents" point into evidence that foreign mega-platforms currently survive only on regulatory arbitrage, thereby validating the need for the proposition rather than undermining it.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): PRO built a cleaner and more durable case across the full exchange. The level-playing-field argument—that no physical retailer escapes basic safety obligations, so no logical reason a digital marketplace should—was introduced early and never squarely neutralized. PRO's most decisive move came in Rebuttal 2's A1: rather than defending a full audit army, PRO pivoted to "retain and disclose supplier chain records they already collect," directly cutting the core of CON's compliance-burden argument. CON's counter—that this still creates insurmountable liability forcing de-listing of micro-sellers—was technically interesting but was asserted more than demonstrated and arrived late. PRO also landed the debate's central enforcement asymmetry: CON conceded that customs screens less than 1% of small cross-border parcels, then offered "intelligence-led sampling at logistics hubs" as the fix—a speculative alternative introduced largely in Rebuttal 2, too late and underdeveloped to displace PRO's enforcement-point-of-sale argument. CON's monopolization claim had real structural logic and was never fully answered by PRO, but PRO offered a plausible counter (externalizing costs lets large platforms undercut honest sellers), and the claim never crystallized into a decisive advantage. PRO's pressure questions were sharper and directly targeted CON's live weaknesses; CON's questions largely repeated arguments already on the table. The closing "lifeline that maims the person holding it is not a lifeline" effectively crystallized the stakes without introducing new material. CON's closing was more repetitive. On balance, PRO answered the most important pressure, closed the enforcement gap more convincingly, and gave the cleaner reason to vote for the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2: the pivot from "audit every Tier-4 factory" to "retain and disclose supplier chain records they already collect" was the most decisive single move in the debate. It directly answered CON's central compliance-burden attack, reframed the obligation as basic record-keeping rather than a global audit mandate, and shifted the burden back to CON to show why even that narrower requirement was impossible—which CON never did cleanly.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): B wins a close but clear debate by making the feasibility and cost clash more decisive. A had a strong moral frame about hidden harms, equal treatment with physical retailers, and marketplace accountability, but later narrowed the policy to record-keeping while still claiming it would prevent lead, fires, and exploitation. B effectively pressed that stronger platform liability for opaque supply chains would cause delisting, consolidation, and higher prices, and offered a more concrete alternative enforcement architecture in response to A's pressure. A's closing overstated that B had no alternative and did not sufficiently answer the monopoly/compliance burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal was most decisive: it answered A's questions with targeted customs sampling and seller blacklisting, then framed A's "basic record-keeping" move as either too weak to solve the harms or still burdensome enough to force platforms to de-risk by excluding micro-sellers.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): A won by narrowing its mechanism to basic record-keeping at the point of sale, which provided a more tangible enforcement path than B's underdeveloped customs-intelligence alternative and blunted B's monopoly attack. A's reframing of unsafe cheap goods as a hidden regressive cost, sharper direct answers to pressure questions, and clearer moral crystallization left the cleaner live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2, which recast the policy as requiring platforms to retain supplier records they already collect rather than personally auditing millions of Tier-4 subcontractors, directly defused B's feasibility pressure and undermined the monopoly concern.
