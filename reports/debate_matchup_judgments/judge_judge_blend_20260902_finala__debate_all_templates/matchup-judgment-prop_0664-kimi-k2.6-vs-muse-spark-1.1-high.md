### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases (deterrence-by-denial vs. deterrence-by-punishment logic), but A (CON) executed the exchange more effectively. B's closing, by contrast, simply re-asserted the contradiction claim without engaging A's answer to it, leaving A's resolution unrebutted at the end of the debate. B also suffered a real execution cost: Rebuttal 2 was cut off mid-thought by the word limit, losing potential force at a critical juncture. A consistently turned B's own claims against it (e.g., framing "must strike Japan and the Philippines simultaneously" as escalation-multiplication rather than stability) and reframed economic deterrence's speed persuasively (regime survival calculus in minutes vs. invasion in days). This left A with the cleaner, more complete path through the clash by the final speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1), answering B's pressure question about the "fragile yet triggers preventive war" contradiction by distinguishing tactical vulnerability from strategic intolerability, was the pivotal exchange—B's closing failed to challenge it, leaving it as A's decisive, unanswered point.

A also directly answered B's alleged contradiction: a battery can be tactically vulnerable while the permanent alliance architecture remains strategically intolerable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A4.1 was decisive. It answered B's strongest pressure question by separating tactical survivability from political lock-in, preserving the preventive-war mechanism and undercutting B's repeated claim that A's case was internally contradictory.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by keeping the decisive clash on overall conflict risk, not just win probability if war starts. B repeatedly showed forward bases create closing-window preventive incentives and use-it-or-lose-it multi-front triggers that elevate accident and preemption pathways more than pure denial deters, while cleanly neutralizing A's "fragile yet intolerable" contradiction by distinguishing tactical saturation from permanent political lock-in. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued coherently, but B built a tighter, more resilient causal story around timing: economic deterrence punishes after a fait accompli succeeds, while forward denial prevents the fait accompli from succeeding at all. B's pressure question exposed a real tension in A's case — that the same forward assets are simultaneously an "existential noose" and easily "mappable trucks" offering only hours of protection — and A's answer (calling this "two sides of the same coin" producing a use-it-or-lose-it incentive) never fully dissolved that tension. B's answer to A's own pressure question (that preemptively striking dispersed forward assets would require attacking Japanese and Philippine territory, thereby guaranteeing the very regional war Beijing wants to avoid) was the most decisive single move in the debate: it converted A's tripwire/preemption theory into an argument for why preemption is self-defeating, and A's closing never directly engaged this point, instead re-asserting the "use-it-or-lose-it countdown" without addressing why that countdown would trigger a war Beijing cannot win. A's economic-automaticity claims (instant market seizure, capital flight, insurance voiding "at the first salvo") were repeated across speeches but remained largely assertion rather than mechanism, while B's counter that sanctions require political will that will be divided mid-crisis stayed unrebutted in substance. Rhetorically both sides closed with clean parallel lines, but B's throughline (denial-before vs. punishment-after) made the choice easier to decide, which is the deciding factor under a close, competent contest.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to A's Pressure Q1 (A1 in Turn 7): striking forward-deployed assets first would require attacking sovereign Japanese and Philippine territory, turning "preemption" into the very region-wide war Beijing's Taiwan-only strategy is designed to avoid — a point A's closing never directly rebutted.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by tying deterrence to Beijing’s prospective theory of rapid military victory and explaining why physical denial acts before a fait accompli, while economic disruption—even if immediate—may not prevent the initial gamble. Side A offered a coherent security-dilemma case, but relied too heavily on asserted Chinese perceptions and automatic economic collapse without showing that these effects outweigh the deterrent value of forcing Beijing into a broader, harder war.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its answer to preemption: attacking dispersed systems across Japanese, Philippine, and U.S. positions would itself require the region-wide war Beijing seeks to avoid. This turned Side A’s tripwire objection into an additional deterrent cost and directly supported the proposition’s comparative claim.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): A won by framing the core metric as Beijing's theory of a quick fait accompli and showing forward denial breaks that theory before any economic pain can land, while cleanly resolving B's security-dilemma and automatic-market claims. B's perception and tripwire arguments stayed live but never overcame A's consistent weighing that post-invasion accommodation voids pure economic deterrence; A's direct A1/A2 answers to pressure and tighter comparative closings made the proposition easier to accept.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A4.1–A4.2 answers to B's pressure questions: preemption would launch a wider war (not a free shot) and stockpiling proves Xi banks on surviving costs after a fast win rather than proving economic deterrence works alone.
