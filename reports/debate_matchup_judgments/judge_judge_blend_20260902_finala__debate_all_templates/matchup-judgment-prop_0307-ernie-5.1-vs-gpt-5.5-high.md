### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Both sides opened competently. PRO had the cleaner structural arc—no clipped speeches, consistent framing, and a recurring "when possible already handles hard cases" defense. CON had two clipped speeches, which is a real execution cost. Despite those costs, CON won the decisive dialectical exchange: its pressure question forced PRO to define "possible" as a funded, auditable, 24-hour professional supervision plan. CON then converted that answer brilliantly—if that is the standard, the home default either costs as much as institutional care (killing the cost advantage) or it tolerates gaps filled by family labor (restoring every safety risk CON raised). PRO's closing response—that the default compels public financing—is an interesting systemic argument but remains speculative and does not directly answer who bears the risk while the system transitions. CON's closing crystallization ("a default is not a staffing roster") was sharper and more actionable. PRO never escaped the bind that their safety-compatible definition of "possible" and their original cheap-home framing cannot coexist. PRO's relocation-stress-syndrome mortality argument, while real, was correctly neutralized by CON's confounding observation (institutions get the sickest patients). CON wins on the quality of argumentative development across rounds, particularly in turning PRO's own answers into CON's evidence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 response to PRO's A1 answer: PRO had just defined "possible" as a funded, 24-hour professional plan—CON immediately turned this by showing PRO had conceded CON's own standard. If home must match residential reliability to qualify, then home is not the organizing default; it is an option selected only after proving capacity. This single move dismantled PRO's cost framing (24-hour home coverage isn't cheap) and their autonomy framing (it's essentially scattered institutional care) simultaneously, and PRO's closing never recovered from it.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins because it directly answered the pressure questions with the required A1/A2 structure, used the "when possible" clause to force a funded reliable standard, and showed how the default shifts incentives and avoids relocation harms. Side B suffered execution costs from clipped turns, weaker direct answers to pressure, and less effective weighing on the default.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4) directly answered B's pressure questions and crystallized why the default favors home when funded standards apply.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing a fatal tension in Side A's case: to make home care safe, A had to define "possible" as requiring funded 24/7 professional supervision, rapid-response crews, and sensor networks, essentially recreating institutional-level care in scattered residences and undermining the claimed cost savings. B's second rebuttal turned A's pressure answers against A, showing that the home default either relies on dangerous hidden family labor or becomes prohibitively expensive. B's closing crystallized the issue decisively: a default is not a staffing roster, and shifting the organizational presumption to home before reliable capacity exists places risk on vulnerable elders and overstretched families. While A's relocation-stress argument remained live, B successfully neutralized the cost and safety advantages by showing they depend on an idealized home model that collapses under real-world accountability and staffing constraints.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal, where B turned A's answers to pressure questions by demonstrating that A's "funded, auditable, 24-hour professional plan" standard was actually an institutional reliability standard being recreated at home, thereby undermining A's cost-savings and practicality claims.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won on the three decisive clashes. First, PRO correctly anchored the debate in the motion's modest "when possible" qualifier throughout, while CON kept arguing against a "home care always" position that PRO never defended. Second, PRO's most damaging single move came in Rebuttal 2: "What punishes low-income elders is CON's default: because a ramp or aide is missing, the person is removed from home instead of the support gap being fixed." CON had no adequate answer to this inversion of the equity argument. Third, PRO's systemic point that defaults determine where assessments, funding, and staffing flow was never squarely rebutted by CON. CON's "world that doesn't exist" argument—using current home-care inadequacy to justify institutional default—was directly turned by PRO's closing ("that turns the current shortage into an argument for preserving the shortage"), and CON had no answer in the exchange. CON made real pressure with the equity and feasibility arguments, and the pressure questions were sharp, but PRO answered them honestly (notably declining to invent outcome statistics), maintained the proportionality framing throughout, and offered the cleaner reason to vote on the motion as written. CON's references to unspecified studies and exact claims ("higher post-fall mortality") were only marginally more precise than PRO's general background knowledge, with no evident verification advantage. Both sides had clipped turns, roughly symmetrical, so no decisive execution penalty for either. PRO built a stronger live route to victory and defended it more effectively across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's equity pressure was decisive: rather than accepting that low-income seniors are better served by institutional default, PRO reframed the entire equity clash by arguing that institutional default is precisely what removes people from their homes when modest supports are missing—and that the default itself determines whether public funds flow to fix the support gap or to subsidize relocation. CON's response in its own Rebuttal 2 called this "an institutional default wearing a home-care mask," but never engaged with the systemic resource-flow point, leaving PRO's comparative analysis standing.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): PRO defended the motion's "when possible" qualifier more consistently across rounds, directly answered pressure questions with A1/A2 structure, and maintained a clear proportionality comparison that CON's equity/safety rebuttals did not neutralize. CON narrowed the debate effectively on resources but relied more on unsupported assertions about data and outcomes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1/A2 responses to CON's Q1/Q2 on infrastructure and fall data, which clarified the standard without evasion or new claims.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by defending the motion as written: a systems-level default toward supported home care when it is safely possible, not a mandate to abandon elders. Side A established that institutional default systematically over-treats modest needs and preserves resource gaps, whereas organizing around home care creates pressure to fund aides, modifications, and respite. Side B never squarely answered Side A&#39;s limiting-principle pressure or explained why needs like meals, bathing, or transport justify total relocation. Side B&#39;s strongest points relied on conflating &quot;supported home care&quot; with unsupported solitary aging and on invoking unverifiable &quot;studies&quot; while demanding impossible data from Side A. Side A&#39;s proportionality framing and honest refusal to invent statistics demonstrated cleaner argumentative discipline and better engagement with the actual proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A in Rebuttal 2 distinguishing that CON was comparing unsupported solitary homes to idealized institutions, whereas the motion requires comparing supported home care to unnecessary institutionalization for elders whose needs do not require total relocation.
