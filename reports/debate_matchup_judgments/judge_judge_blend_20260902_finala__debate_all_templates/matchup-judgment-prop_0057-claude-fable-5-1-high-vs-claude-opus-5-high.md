### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by keeping the decade-out ledger as the decisive standard. A handled the immediate “despite wider war” clause well—bounded retaliation, proxy weakness, and the evaporating-unspent-threat frame were real—and the window argument partly answered B’s contradiction charge. But A never neutralized B’s live core: imperfect monitoring still forced costly parallel cheating, the strikes spent visibility, and A’s candid inability to locate the 60% stockpile undercut the claimed setback and future strikeability. B’s “threat consumed / crater for eyes” crystallizations stayed cleaner and unanswered through the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3–B4.5: inspections make a hidden hall require a full second program; turning A’s stockpile non-answer into proof the strike spent the asset later detection/deterrence need.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): CON won the central trade. PRO argued managed escalation risk and a long setback, but conceded it cannot locate the 60% stockpile and that monitoring never found hidden material. CON turned that into its decisive accounting point: counted material forced any covert dash to be a second, signature-rich program; breaking accounting lets Tehran divert into a small, hideable hall for the easy 60-to-90 step. Combined with PRO's own claim that Iran sat at 60% without dashing because facilities were known and reachable, CON showed the unspent threat was deterring while preserving sight, and the strike spent sight for rubble.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1-B2.4 and B4.3: detectable-by-whom attack plus the technical reversal that Natanz-to-60 is the hard industrial part and 60-to-90 is a room, and that losing counted material removes the need for a parallel program.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B built a more cohesive and technically grounded case, effectively arguing that the strikes traded crucial transparency for a mere temporary delay. B's rhetorical framing ("threat executed is a threat consumed," "cannot credibly threaten an address you cannot name") was exceptionally sharp and decisively weaponized Side A's concessions about the stockpile's location. Side A argued competently but struggled to overcome B's structural critique of losing inspection accounting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it dismantled Side A's detection claims by explaining how inspection accounting forces a parallel covert program, and then used Side A's own admission about not knowing the stockpile's location to prove the deterrent was broken.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): A won a competitive debate by keeping a clearer expected-value route live: bounded, absorbed retaliation versus permanent nuclear blackmail, then crystallizing concrete irreversible setbacks against consent-dependent verification. A answered pressure more directly, forced B’s cameras/warehouse dual claim into internal tension, and closed with a clean comparative vote. B landed sharp technical punches on HEU survival, feedstock notification, and lost eyes, plus a strong JCPOA history correction, but two clipped turns and a fuzzier final magnitude/risk-bounding story left the decision slightly less decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s A4.5–A4.6 contradiction squeeze on B’s warehouse-sprint vs. inspectors-as-honesty ledger, answered by B’s feedstock/notification salvage but still tilting the weighing toward A’s concrete-vs-revocable close.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): CON won the core trade. PRO's window and bounded retaliation story was plausible, but conceded the HEU survived and was moved and could not quantify increased distance to a bomb beyond direction. CON showed with ordinary knowledge that 60% was already 95% of enrichment work, that the last step fits a small undeclared cascade, and that IAEA seals on feedstock were the alarm that made a veto executable. Striking turned a revocable but detectable stockpile into an unlocated one, swapping eroded but real transparency for rubble, and making wider-war risk recurring not bounded. PRO's answers on belief-based deterrence and noisy weaponization did not restore a concrete detection mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2/B4 on feedstock: cameras guarded the HEU itself, so a broken seal gave days of warning for a known-target strike; the strikes did Iran's diversion for it, moving several hundred kg off-books without Tehran breaking a seal, collapsing PRO's "paperweight" and "standing veto" claims.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side A built a highly cohesive case that effectively turned Side B's strongest arguments into liabilities. A's distinction between revocable, consent-dependent inspection regimes and irreversible physical destruction of industrial capacity was decisive. A also demonstrated excellent epistemic discipline by refusing to invent a precise timeline for the delay, focusing instead on strategic direction. Side B made strong points about the survival of the HEU stockpile and the loss of IAEA "eyes," but struggled to defend the physical barrier of inspectors against A's "warehouse dash" trap, and suffered minor execution costs from clipped turns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where A exposed the contradiction in B's case regarding the "warehouse dash" and the utility of inspectors, while honestly answering the pressure questions without faking precision on the exact months of delay.
