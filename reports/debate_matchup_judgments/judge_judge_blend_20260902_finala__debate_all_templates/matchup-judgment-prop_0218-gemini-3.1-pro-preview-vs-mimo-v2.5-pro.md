### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins on case completeness, execution, and the decisive battery-throttling response. Two of CON's speeches were clipped before completion—real execution costs that left PRO's price-competition argument almost entirely uncontested and CON's closing rebuttal truncated. The price-reduction benefit is the most certain and direct consequence of the motion, and CON essentially ceded it. CON's strongest contributions were the managed-custody reframe (diagnostic tools are the attack pathway, not just schematics) and the car-analogy demolition (cars do not store biometric credentials), both genuinely sharp. But PRO answered the software-obsolescence challenge with a specific, plausible mechanism—battery degradation causes CPU throttling, which consumers perceive as generational obsolescence, so a cheap battery replacement restores apparent performance before software support actually ends. That mechanism is grounded in a well-known real-world episode and directly narrows CON's most damaging remaining argument. CON's reply that "other software obsolescence still exists" is true but weakens the comparative impact rather than refuting the mechanism. On security, CON's managed-custody argument is the debate's most sophisticated single point, but PRO correctly noted that hardware exploits still require physical possession of the specific target device, keeping the risk speculative rather than systemic. Comparatively, PRO maintained the cleaner burden: guaranteed, daily price gouging and universally degrading batteries against physically gated, architecturally contingent exploit scenarios. That weighing, combined with no execution penalties and consistent round-by-round adaptation, gives PRO the clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's battery-throttling response to CON's software-obsolescence argument (B4.3, answered at A3.2). CON raised the strong point that devices are discarded because software ecosystems move on, not because of fixable hardware. PRO countered with the specific mechanism that aging lithium-ion batteries reduce voltage supply and force OS-level CPU throttling, so consumers experience hardware degradation as "the phone is slow and outdated" before software support actually ends. This is a real, well-documented phenomenon, and it directly bridges hardware repairability to what had appeared to be a purely software disposal driver—materially shrinking CON's most important live argument.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): A wins because the affirmative gave the cleaner comparative story: repair competition plausibly lowers repair costs and extends device life at broad scale, while B’s security and safety risks, though intelligently developed, remained less quantified and less clearly likely to outweigh those benefits. B’s software-obsolescence and managed-chain-of-custody arguments were strong, but B under-contested the price-reduction route and did not sufficiently answer A’s status-quo safety turn that genuine parts and manuals can reduce current gray-market repair risks. B also had two clipped turns, which slightly hurt execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was reframing “security by obscurity” as a managed-access problem for diagnostic and firmware tools; however, A’s broader weighing that these risks are targeted and speculative compared with widespread repair-cost and e-waste benefits carried the final comparison.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON wins by successfully limiting the scope of PRO’s claimed benefits and providing a more realistic model of security risks within the five-year horizon. PRO’s case hinged on the certainty of e-waste reduction and cost savings, but CON’s introduction of software obsolescence (OS drops, app requirements, ended security patches) effectively undercut the magnitude of those benefits, showing that hardware repairability alone won’t stop mass disposal. On security, PRO relied on an idealistic “defective architecture” standard, while CON convincingly argued that real-world security depends on managed access and chain-of-custody for diagnostic tools—guardrails that national mandates would strip away faster than existing architecture can be redesigned. CON also leveraged the five-year timeframe more strategically, framing it as a window of exposure before regulatory or technical guardrails can adapt. PRO was clear and consistent but ultimately overstated benefit certainty and never fully resolved the software obsolescence or managed-access clashes. Minor clipping on CON’s turns did not obscure the core argumentation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over diagnostic tools and security architecture was decisive. PRO dismissed risks by claiming any tool-based vulnerability proves a “defective” device. CON countered with the managed-access model and a “house key” analogy, explaining that real-world security assumes controlled custody of maintenance interfaces, and that legislating unrestricted access commoditizes attack pathways faster than the five-year window allows for architectural redesign. This directly neutralized PRO’s security dismissal and grounded CON’s risk claim in practical, structural reality.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): PRO built the cleaner comparative case and defended it more effectively under pressure. The decisive advantage came in the pressure question exchange: PRO demanded that CON name a single jurisdiction where repair laws produced security breaches or actual retail price increases. CON's responses—pointing to New York's explicit exemption of parts pairing and distinguishing state laws from national mandates—were not without substance, but they partially undermined CON's own case. If existing laws can be and were crafted to preserve security mechanisms, that supports PRO's argument that risks are manageable, not guaranteed. CON used the NY exemption to claim "lawmakers acknowledged the risk," but the more natural reading is that careful law design can preserve security—which is PRO's position. PRO's authentication-vs-authorization distinction in Rebuttal 2 was the most technically substantive move of the debate: it correctly identified that device security verification and manufacturer gatekeeping are separable functions, and CON never squarely answered it, mostly repeating the biometric scanner scenario without engaging the distinction. CON's strongest live arguments—software obsolescence limits e-waste benefit in a five-year window, and national mandates would go further than existing regional ones—are real, and they prevented PRO from dominating. The software obsolescence point especially deserved more sustained rebuttal from PRO. But PRO's counter that enormous volumes of hardware are discarded for purely fixable hardware failures (cracked screens, dead batteries on phones with perfectly capable processors) captured the bulk of the e-waste argument. CON's case consistently predicted structural, guaranteed harms that have not materialized in any comparable market, while PRO pointed to documented competitive repair markets, EU repairability trends, and a decade of independent technician work without safety collapse. The clipping of three PRO speeches is a minor execution cost, but the key arguments were delivered before each clip. CON's case was competent throughout but ultimately rested on projections it could not ground in any real-world comparator, and that asymmetry was exposed clearly by the pressure questions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure question asking CON to name a jurisdiction where repair laws produced actual security breaches or price increases was the pivotal exchange. CON's response—citing NY's explicit exemption of parts pairing and the state-vs-national distinction—had real content but inadvertently supported PRO's case: if laws CAN be designed to preserve security mechanisms, the risk is manageable rather than structurally guaranteed. PRO then pressed the authentication-vs-authorization distinction in Rebuttal 2 to close the loop, and CON never directly answered whether device-level function verification is separable from manufacturer-exclusive authorization lockout.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate. A gave the more plausible model of right-to-repair as parts, tools, documentation, and competition with security exceptions, while B often relied on a maximalist version where national laws necessarily ban all meaningful component verification and force modular redesign. B had a strong security theme, especially biometrics and stolen-parts markets, but A answered it with the authentication versus manufacturer lockout distinction and pressed the lack of real-world harms or price spikes. On e-waste and prices, A’s cracked screens, dead batteries, repair-market competition, and lifespan-extension story was more concrete than B’s more speculative AI obsolescence and baseline-cost projections. A’s clipped turns were an execution cost, but not enough to overcome the stronger comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s most decisive rebuttal was distinguishing legitimate component verification from proprietary authorization lockouts, which directly undercut B’s claim that right-to-repair automatically creates backdoors.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): CON wins by successfully neutralizing PRO's core empirical defense. When PRO pointed to existing state and EU laws as proof that security risks are speculative, CON sharply noted those laws explicitly exempted the very parts-pairing mechanisms at issue, making the comparison invalid. CON also leveraged the five-year timeline effectively, arguing software and AI obsolescence will drive device turnover more than hardware failure, and grounded security risks in concrete mechanisms like chop markets for stolen phones and biometric interception. PRO argued the repair monopoly angle competently and drew a useful distinction between verification and authorization lockouts, but could not fully overcome CON's contextualization of the legislative landscape, the timeline burden, and the clipped execution costs in multiple later rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's pressure questions and closing, pointing out that existing state laws explicitly exempted parts pairing due to security concerns. This directly undercut PRO's central claim that real-world evidence proves risks are speculative and forced PRO onto the defensive in the final stretches.
