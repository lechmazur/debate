### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a clear but competitive debate. The decisive clash ran on three axes: the "closed loop" defense, the enforcement mechanism question, and the trading-card overinclusivity worry. On the first, CON correctly argued that secondary markets don't redefine a product's legal category, but PRO's rejoinder—that the neurological injury occurs during the compulsive chase, not at cash-out—remained live throughout and was never squarely neutralized. On enforcement, PRO's most effective move came in its A2 response: gambling classification supplies strict liability and duty-of-care obligations that transform "age verification" from a cosmetic checkbox into an enforceable bar, whereas CON's spending caps depend on voluntary corporate implementation. CON kept characterizing gambling regulation as AML audits and payout verification, which misrepresented the duty-of-care dimension PRO kept foregrounding; that mismatch cost CON credibility on the core policy question. On the trading-card analogy—CON's strongest structural attack—PRO offered a genuine substantive reply in Rebuttal 2: friction, immersive one-click design, real-time near-miss architecture, and infinite microtransaction scalability distinguish digital loot boxes from physical packs in ways the law already uses (carnival games vs. casinos). CON's "overinclusivity" charge had real bite on social media algorithms, but PRO didn't need to fully answer that framing because the debate was about loot boxes specifically. CON's "sledgehammer vs. scalpel" slogan was rhetorically durable and the legal-precision case was well-organized throughout, but the precision alternative (spending caps) never overcame the enforcement gap PRO identified. Both sides complied with format requirements; the A1/A2 labels appeared correctly in both Rebuttal 2 speeches. PRO's rhetorical imagery ("slot machines wearing video game costumes," "you cannot extract casino revenues while evading casino responsibilities") was consistently stronger and reinforced the substantive case rather than substituting for it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 response to CON's Q2 pressure question: when CON asked why PRO didn't simply advocate direct age-gating rather than full gambling classification, PRO answered that gambling classification IS the enforcement mechanism that makes age-gating meaningful—strict liability and duty-of-care convert a perfunctory birth-year checkbox into a genuine barrier. This directly neutralized CON's recurring "regulatory misfit" framing by showing that the non-AML elements of gambling law are precisely targeted at minor protection, not financial-extraction compliance.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): This was a genuinely competitive debate decided by two key clashes. First, on the blanket-ban question: CON consistently argued that classifying loot boxes as gambling imports existing statutory prohibition of minors from participation—a legally grounded point. PRO answered (A2) that regulation "as gambling" means applying the protective architecture (age gates, spending caps, odds disclosure) rather than wholesale statutory import, and pointed to real-world jurisdictions that have done this without total game bans. CON's rebuttal that gambling frameworks are "not à la carte" was strong but slightly overconfident, since the motion's text—"regulated as gambling"—is reasonably read as applying the oversight framework rather than verbatim criminal codes. PRO's interpretation held. Second, on cash-out and monetary value: CON's legal distinction is the stronger technical position, but PRO's gray-market argument and social-capital reframe were responsive enough to prevent CON from claiming a clean win there. The most damaging dynamic for CON was that their preferred alternative—mandatory refunds, spending notifications, parental controls—converges suspiciously closely with what PRO is actually proposing, undermining the sharpness of the opposition. PRO maintained a consistent, harm-centered narrative throughout, while CON's "proves too much" arguments (TikTok, sneakers, Happy Meals) were clever but occasionally deflected rather than directly answered the child-vulnerability harm story that PRO kept returning to. Neither side delivered a blowout; PRO edges CON by maintaining a tighter line between argument and motion, while CON's statutory-prohibition argument, though strong, was somewhat overstated and partially neutralized by PRO's jurisdictional response.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive rebuttal exchange was PRO's A2 response to CON's pressure question about the blanket-ban charge. CON had consistently argued that gambling classification automatically triggers criminal exclusion of minors under existing statutes and challenged PRO to reconcile "spending caps" with a regime built on exclusion. PRO answered that regulating as gambling means importing the protective architecture—age verification, hard caps, mandatory odds disclosure, bans on manipulative animations—applied to digital storefronts, not rewriting criminal codes to permit underage wagering. This was direct, format-compliant, and sufficient to blunt CON's most consistent attack without conceding the motion. CON's counter (you can't cherry-pick safeguards from a prohibition regime) was their strongest remaining shot, but PRO's closing held the line adequately, leaving CON's blanket-ban argument partially neutralized rather than decisive.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B won a competitive debate by keeping the focus on the mechanism and minor-specific harm: real-money payment, randomized rewards, frictionless repeat purchasing, and exploitation of developing impulse control. Side A’s legal-boundary and overbreadth arguments were strong, especially on cash-out value and the risk that gambling classification becomes prohibition, but they leaned too heavily on current statutory categories in a normative policy debate. B made the cleaner case that gambling-style safeguards fit the harm and that the underlying games need not be banned, while A’s alternative consumer protections felt less developed against B’s point that parental tools are inadequate against deliberately engineered compulsion loops.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was B’s answer to the trading-card and parental-control objections: digital loot boxes are instant, infinitely repeatable, and algorithmically optimized in a way physical packs are not, so ordinary parental oversight is an inadequate substitute for gambling-style safeguards.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side A wins by systematically dismantling Side B's criteria for gambling and trapping Side B in a legal contradiction. Side A effectively uses trading card and social media analogies to show that neither 'random chance' nor 'frictionless psychological loops' uniquely define gambling. Furthermore, Side A successfully argues that regulating loot boxes 'as gambling' would trigger blanket bans on minors under existing statutes, exposing Side B's proposed 'safeguards without prohibition' as an impossible legal standard.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal decisively exposed the contradiction in Side B's remedy, pointing out that statutory gambling frameworks are not 'à la carte' and would inherently ban sales to minors rather than just impose spending caps.
