### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won by showing that disclosure creates the record needed for workers, regulators, unions, and tribunals to identify and challenge algorithmic decisions, while remaining compatible with stronger substantive protections. Side A offered an original and rhetorically sharp warning about regulatory crowdout and employer-authored compliance, but its decisive claims—that disclosure would become a legal shield, alter discrimination standards, and displace later regulation—were largely asserted rather than established. Side A also compared disclosure against preferred substantive reforms as though only one could pass, despite conceding that the motion prevents neither. The clipped first rebuttal was an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its answer to the “one bite at the apple” argument: human review, collection limits, and cause standards do not become less available after disclosure, while enforcement is substantially harder when regulators do not know that a system exists or what records to request. Side A’s sequencing response was clever but too speculative to reverse that comparison.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): PRO won a close, high-quality clash by keeping the decision rule on the motion as written: invisibility versus mandatory disclosure. CON’s strongest moves—notice-as-shield, certificate-of-regularity, Goodhart selection for crude metrics, and sequencing/who-holds-the-pen—were sharp and original, but they never defended secrecy on the merits and often substituted a preferred alternative statute for the up-down on disclosure. PRO repeatedly forced the live comparison that every enforcement and tribunal path still needs a named system and record, answered pressure cleanly on fraud exemptions and subpoena/registry value, and crystallized that silence cannot be audited. CON’s clipped R1 and political-economy crowding-out claim were real but less decisive than PRO’s floor argument once both sides had traded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s R2 answers (esp. existence vs threshold exemption + “knowing what to subpoena”) against CON’s claim that substance replaces disclosure.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): PRO kept the decisive clash on enforcement: you cannot contest, correct, or audit a system you may not be told exists. CON's strongest moves - notice as employer shield, Goodhart selection to cruder metrics, and one-bite regulatory crowding - are plausible but rely on speculative legislative economy and filing-as-standard-of-care. PRO answered the fraud-threshold pressure by separating existence/data-categories from tripwires, and answered the entry-powers pressure with "knowing what to subpoena" and a proactive registry vs retrospective individual litigation. CON's R1 clip and its concession that the motion does not legally block additional reforms left its harm story indirect, while PRO's lights-on-first precondition remained live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.2 - PRO's answer that entry powers locate the door but not what's behind it, and that disclosure builds the registry needed for proactive, systemic audit rather than waiting for one worker to afford suit, directly undercuts CON's burden-shifting alternative.
