- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): PRO established a cleaner principle and did more damage to CON's core defenses. CON never overcame the moral arbitrariness of charging users for protection against platform-created risks. Though competitive, PRO's rebuttals narrowed the clash more effectively and left a live route to victory on risk allocation and perverse incentives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's exposure of the scammer/victim wealth asymmetry, showing that a paywall filters out cash-constrained victims more reliably than it stops determined bad actors, while severity-based triage provides a better sorting mechanism than ability to pay.

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B won by maintaining a highly cohesive narrative about perverse incentives and safety as a network good. - **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built the stronger case by establishing safety as a network good undermined by paywalls that create perverse incentives for danger-as-conversion, directly answering pressure questions on triage and coercion while showing spillover effects to all users; Side A's layered model was weakened by overbreadth critiques, clipped execution on key rebuttals, and insufficient rebuttal of incentive distortions despite solid distinctions on baseline vs. intensive services.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 directly addresses A's pressure questions by reframing priority as severity-based routing (not paywalled) and optional verification without exclusion pressure, exposing the layered model's failure to prevent free-tier hunting grounds.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by keeping the debate anchored to the proposition’s specific features—identity verification, block tools, and scam-response—and by showing that paywalling them creates perverse incentives and network-wide vulnerability. A’s severity-based triage answer absorbed B’s scarcity pressure while denying that emergency harm mitigation should be a concierge service. B’s overbreadth argument stretched the motion to include boutique human services, which read as a reframe rather than a direct defense of reserving the listed core protections. B also conceded that urgent scam triage should follow severity, not wealth, which blunted the defense of paid safety tiers. Combined with B’s repeated word-limit clipping and A’s sharper progression across rounds, A built the cleaner live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal reframing “priority” as severity-based rather than wallet-based, directly answering B’s pressure while showing that rationing emergency scam response by payment is morally and functionally indefensible.
