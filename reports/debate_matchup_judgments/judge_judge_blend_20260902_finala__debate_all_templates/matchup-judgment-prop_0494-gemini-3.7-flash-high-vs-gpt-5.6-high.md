### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by demonstrating that the proposed reforms mutually reinforce each other and by successfully undermining Side A's two core inevitabilities. B showed that anonymity is already imperfect in specialized fields, that layered protections plus post-decision publication provide comparable or better safeguards than the status quo, and that fixed compensation with editorial quality controls does not inherently incentivize speed over rigor. B also offered plausible alternative funding mechanisms that weakened A's cost-inflation argument. A repeatedly treated B's fixed-fee model as piecework gig labor and relied on an idealized view of current anonymity that B effectively challenged. B's rebuttals were cleaner, more comparative, and left fewer live burdens unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response to the anonymity/retaliation concern in Rebuttal 2 [B4.1], which argued that covert retaliation is possible under both systems, that post-decision publication with conflict screening provides contestable evidence, and that the relevant standard is net quality rather than zero risk—directly neutralizing A's central claim that confidentiality is indispensable.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won by turning CON's two inevitabilities into manageable design choices: fixed, verdict-independent fees conditional on substance, and post-decision publication that lets the community audit quality. That directly undercuts CON's gig-economy churn claim and counters the hierarchy worry by noting imperfect anonymity now plus attribution giving junior scholars visible credit and contestable records. CON repeated hierarchy and cost-pass-through fears without answering how public visibility makes low-effort reviews reputationally costly or why unpaid rushed reviews preserve candor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second-rebuttal move that payment does not reward volume because editors can refuse inadequate reports and publication itself audits quality, making churn reputationally expensive rather than profitable.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully defending the synergy of the proposition's two reforms and providing superior weighing. While Side A delivered a highly persuasive and rhetorically polished attack focusing on the chilling effect of public reviews and the perverse incentives of payment, Side B effectively neutralized the core "compound failure" argument. Side B astutely pointed out that if reviewers submit superficial "rubber-stamp" reviews to avoid retaliation or maximize hourly returns, the public nature of the reports makes this laziness visible to the community, thereby damaging the reviewer's reputation and defeating the purpose of the rubber stamp. Side B also centered the reader's ability to inspect the scientific process, offering a more comprehensive view of how peer review functions for the broader community, whereas Side A focused almost exclusively on the reviewer's experience. Side B's mitigation of the retaliation risk through post-decision publication and timestamped records was more robust than Side A's reliance on the "uncertain plausible deniability" of anonymity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to Side A's "compound failure" argument. Side A argued that combining payment and public reports would incentivize "paid, performative rubber-stamping" to avoid retaliation while maximizing hourly rates. Side B cleanly resolved this by pointing out that because the reports are public, a lazy or overly polite rubber-stamp review would be immediately visible to readers and authors, making the reviewer's lack of rigor reputationally costly and thus neutralizing the incentive to churn out superficial work.
