### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): CON (A) built a tighter, more resilient case by arguing that safety filtering is implemented as ranking within the recommender itself, so "blocking recommendation features" either strips protection (falling back to raw search/chronological feeds) or, if bounded to preserve safety floors, isn't actually the power the proposition promises. This "dangerous or empty" dichotomy was set up in the opening, sharpened via the LGBTQ/mental-health pressure question, and decisively exploited in Rebuttal 2 when PRO's own answer (platforms must keep crisis resources regardless of parental block) let CON argue PRO's "more power" collapses into the status quo. PRO (B) had a coherent structural-conflict-of-interest frame and correctly noted that unused existing controls suggest wrong-kind-of-power rather than enough power, but never cleanly escaped the dichotomy in closing, instead retreating to an analogy (bounded rights are still real rights) that didn't directly rebut the "platform keeps the power" charge. PRO also suffered a real execution cost: both its Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 were clipped for exceeding word limits, meaning key final arguments were cut off before reaching the judge, while CON's speeches were fully delivered and cleanly structured throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turning PRO's answer to Pressure Question A2 (platforms must retain crisis-resource content even against a parental block) into the "dangerous or empty" trap — arguing that if the platform still gatekeeps safety-relevant content, parents haven't actually gained the power the proposition demands, while a genuine full block reverts to unsafe raw search/chronological feeds.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully uncoupling engagement-maximizing recommendation features from baseline safety filtering, neutralizing Side B's core technical argument. Side A also brilliantly defended against Side B's censorship trap by pointing out that legal guardrails exist for all parental rights without rendering them meaningless.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's final response that legal floors in areas like healthcare do not make parental authority meaningless, perfectly neutralizing Side B's claim that a safety baseline would negate the proposition.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): B wins by converting the core clash into a clean dilemma the proposition cannot escape: actual blocking of recommendation features removes the ranking layer that implements safety demotions/boosts (leaving raw search/peer trends), while keeping that layer means parents get no meaningful extra power beyond today's restricted-mode floors. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): CON built the more decisive comparative frame: defaults protect every child regardless of parental engagement, while toggles only protect children whose parents already have the time and literacy to use them — precisely the equity gap PRO's model reproduces. The pivotal exchange came when CON's Pressure Q1 forced PRO to admit its "one durable switch" actually meant different parents choosing different ages, which CON then exposed in Rebuttal 2 as reconstructing the very complexity dashboard PRO claimed to avoid. PRO's strongest counter — that a regulatory default still needs a parent-held enforcement key because platforms will silently reset or bypass it — was clever but never resolved the ambiguity of what the "default" state of its own switch is for a disengaged parent, leaving CON's "default exposure is the dominant failure mode" claim largely unrebutted. PRO's Apple App Tracking Transparency analogy was a good, grounded answer to the "platforms won't cooperate" pressure, but CON's reply that mandated recommendation controls are far more feature- and age-graduated than a binary tracking toggle was a fair, unresolved rejoinder. CON's case suffered an execution cost from a clipped closing that cut off its final line of argument, but the substantive rebuttal wins earlier in the debate were strong enough to outweigh that cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Q1 ("single default or granular multi-feature control?") combined with its Rebuttal 2 callout that PRO's answer — different parents picking different ages via the "same simple switch" — actually conceded the complexity/configuration problem CON had been pressing since the opening.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): PRO won by effectively neutralizing CON's "complexity tax" argument. PRO consistently framed the motion's "more power" as a simplified, OS-level lock rather than a proliferation of confusing in-app toggles. Furthermore, PRO's pressure on the bypass issue forced CON to concede that regulatory floors suffer the same "friends' phones" vulnerability, blunting CON's attack. Finally, PRO successfully argued that parents, not regulators, possess the necessary context to make age-specific calibrations.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2, which used CON's own logic to show that safe-by-default standards fail the exact same "friends' phones" bypass test CON used to attack parental controls.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): A kept the motion as written and built the cleaner comparative path: current controls are illusory and inequitable, a durable OS-level block-or-delay delivers real power with less work, and parental ownership coexists with floors rather than being replaced by them. B's equity and false-confidence critique landed early but was progressively weakened by A's reverse (today's scavenger hunt is the real tax) and by the shared-bypass problem that leaves non-engaged families unprotected either way; B's late collapse of "one durable action" into endless dashboards and the clipped closing left the non-engaged-child route less decisive than claimed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.6) turning B's multi-device bypass against the manufacturer-standard alternative while still delivering the home-device lock for the working parent B invoked.
