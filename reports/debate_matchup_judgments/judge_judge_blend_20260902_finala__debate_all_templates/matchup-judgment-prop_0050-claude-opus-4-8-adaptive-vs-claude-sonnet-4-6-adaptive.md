### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (PRO)): A wins because it made the decisive clash clearer: if the goal is polluter-pays climate policy, escalation should track emissions, distance, and cabin class rather than raw personal flight frequency. B had a plausible fairness and behavior case, but never fully justified why the same emissions should be taxed more just because they are spread across more boarding events. A also kept the registry and avoidance objections live, while B's answer relied on imperfect self-reporting and existing passenger data without fully solving cross-carrier aggregation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange was the response to B's claim that the ratchet adds escalating marginal cost: A argued that this either double-counts harm already priced by emissions or punishes frequency rather than pollution, undercutting B's own polluter-pays framing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON)): Side B systematically dismantled the proposition's core mechanism by proving that flight count is a flawed proxy for emissions, effectively using the '30 short hops vs. 5 long hauls' comparison. Side A's attempt to salvage the metric by weighting flights only allowed Side B to prove that emissions pricing was doing the real work, while Side B cleanly won the administrative clash by contrasting complex individual registries with simple taxation at the source.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2 sequence, where they trapped Side A into conceding that flights must be weighted by distance, and then used that concession to prove the 'person-counter' ratchet was an unnecessary double-counting mechanism.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON won because PRO never justified why a progressive levy should track boarding events rather than cumulative emissions. CON's counterexample—thirty short hops versus five long-hauls with identical emissions—exposed that the ratchet punishes frequency, not harm, and PRO's closing still attacked a flat per-unit straw man rather than CON's live cumulative-emissions alternative. CON also landed that the per-person registry creates unique avoidance problems that source-level emissions taxes avoid. PRO's behavioral-threshold argument remained intuitively appealing but could not survive the double-counting and mis-targeting charges once CON sharpened the comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 introduction of a cumulative-emissions charge that escalates without counting heads (A4.4), which directly answered PRO's claim that only a per-person ratchet could create escalating marginal cost, and which PRO never addressed in the closing.
