### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO's equity baseline argument persisted across the full exchange: the current optional system already leaves underfunded districts offering zero practical skills, while core mandates force resource allocation the same way math and English mandates do. CON's most creative counter—that sham courses consuming instructional hours are worse than zero—was genuinely sharp but rested on two contested assumptions CON never fully proved: (1) that practical skill mandates inevitably produce compliance theater when academic mandates don't, and (2) that the displaced academic time is more valuable than any practical baseline for students who otherwise receive nothing. CON's "third path" of optional/community-based programs was never fleshed out enough to explain how low-income students without engaged parents or enrichment resources would actually access these skills voluntarily. CON had the better rebuttal execution overall—the A2.4 infrastructure dilemma ("if serious enough to matter, needs infrastructure; if trivial enough to fit anywhere, too trivial to be core") was the sharpest single exchange in the debate and was never satisfactorily answered—and the zero-sum time catch, when PRO admitted "reallocated time" in B4.3, was a genuine gotcha. Despite this execution edge, PRO's underlying position survived more intact: "something imperfect is better than nothing" is more intuitive and less assumption-dependent than CON's "sham is worse than zero," and the analogy to existing mandated subjects provided a working counter CON never cleanly neutralized. PRO wins narrowly on the strength of the equity path's durability, not on rebuttal quality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.4 infrastructure dilemma was the sharpest individual exchange: if the mandate is serious enough to matter it requires serious infrastructure most schools lack, and if it is trivial enough to fit any classroom it is too trivial to deserve core status. PRO never squarely answered this dilemma, only restating that basic tasks need no labs—which CON rightly countered was too trivial to warrant displacing academic hours. However, PRO's B2.2 equity flip—that the current optional system is already the two-tier failure CON warns mandates would produce—did the most sustained work across the debate by consistently putting CON on the defensive about what concrete alternative exists for low-income students.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the debate anchored to the motion’s equity and universality claims: optional status leaves many students with no access to cooking, repair, or finance, while core status at least creates an enforceable baseline. Side A had sharper rhetoric and a real opportunity-cost objection, but leaned too heavily on assumed sham implementation and overstated that practical instruction must either be trivial or catastrophically resource-intensive. B’s case that these skills are common, concrete adult needs and can reinforce academics survived better than A’s claim that abstraction plus optional community programs is enough.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was answering the equity pressure: A effectively accepted that underfunded schools may currently offer zero access, then argued sham courses are worse than none. B made the cleaner comparative point that a mandated minimum is preferable to leaving marginalized students with no life-skills instruction at all.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively establishing that school time is zero-sum, successfully framing Side B's proposal as an unfunded mandate that would lead to "compliance theater." Side A's responses to the pressure questions deftly turned Side B's premises against them, proving that a sham practical course is worse than no course because it cannibalizes the academic time necessary for building transferable cognitive skills.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A1 and A2 in Rebuttal 2 perfectly dismantled Side B's pressure, explaining that a "sham course" is worse than zero because it consumes zero-sum instructional hours without building competence.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO)): PRO (Side B) ran the stronger overall case across the full exchange. Its equity argument — that optional status in resource-constrained environments predictably means these courses disappear first in the districts that need them most — was never adequately neutralized. CON's counter-proposal of "targeted, high-quality electives" relies on optimistic assumptions about underfunded districts that CON's own funding-scarcity argument undermines: if these districts cannot staff existing mandates, there is no reason to trust they will voluntarily offer robust optional life skills courses. PRO's Pressure Question 1 exposed this contradiction sharply, and CON's A1 response was somewhat circular. PRO's "shallow beats absent" framing in Rebuttal 2 was the clearest comparative stake of the debate, and CON's only answer was to assert the existence of targeted electives — which is precisely what PRO questioned. CON's contextual variation argument had real force, and PRO's integration-avoids-crowding claim was somewhat optimistic; CON rightly noted that core status requires standalone assessment benchmarks. But CON never cleanly resolved the central question: why should we trust that the districts serving the most vulnerable students will, under optional status, actually provide these courses? CON's closing crystallized a genuine point about mandatory shallow content versus targeted quality, but the underlying bet on voluntary provision in underfunded systems remained shaky throughout. PRO's closing was tighter and more persuasive, making the stakes cleaner. Neither side used fake precision or invented citations, so epistemic discipline was roughly even.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question Q1 was the most decisive exchange: by pointing out that if affluent students already master these skills at home, optional status permits them to skip electives while leaving vulnerable students unprotected — the very privilege gap CON claimed to avoid — it forced CON into a circular answer that optional programs let districts "target" resources without explaining why underfunded districts would actually exercise that option. PRO's "shallow beats absent" response in Rebuttal 2 then closed the loop memorably, leaving CON with no clean rebuttal beyond asserting that better-targeted electives would emerge under a system PRO had already shown tends to drop electives first.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO)): Side B won by making the equity and universality clash clearer: optional life-skills education leaves access dependent on family background and district luck, while core status creates at least a baseline floor. Side A had a real resource/crowding argument, but leaned too heavily on overstatements that these skills are too contextual or irrelevant, and its alternative of targeted optional programs was less guaranteed than B’s universal floor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s answer to the funding and equity pressure—integrating life skills into existing subjects and arguing that “shallow beats absent” as a floor for underfunded districts—was the decisive exchange, because it directly blunted A’s crowding-out claim while preserving B’s privilege/lottery argument.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO)): Side B won by successfully exposing a contradiction in Side A's case regarding underfunded schools. While Side A landed strong hits on the zero-sum nature of instructional time and contextual variation, Side B demonstrated that Side A's alternative—relying on 'targeted, high-quality electives'—is implausible in the very underfunded districts Side A claims to defend. Side B's framing that 'shallow beats absent' and that electives vanish under scarcity provided the cleaner comparative path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing observation that Side A's 'theory of perfect targeting collapses under the weight of real-world scarcity' decisively dismantled Side A's core equity argument.
