### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A ran a more architecturally complete case: the public safety visibility argument (shadow populations cannot cooperate with police) went entirely unrebutted across all five rounds; the economic and labor-market arguments were never squarely answered; and the "no realistic queue to jump" point—many covered individuals never had a viable legal path in the first place—fatally undercut Side B's most emotionally resonant example. Side A also used the IRCA precedent legitimately to answer the "fixed caps" claim, and Side B's counter (IRCA raised the baseline and lengthened waits) was asserted with unwarranted confidence rather than developed carefully. Side A's repeated framing of the false binary ("fix the queue OR help the resident") was never convincingly dissolved by Side B, whose own alternative ("fix the queue") was exposed as a vague aspiration rather than a competing policy. Side B argued with real consistency and emotional power—the Manila nurse narrative and "obedience is for suckers" line were memorable—but the core fairness argument depended on a fixed-caps claim the CON could not substantiate and a rule-of-law principle that the PRO neutralized by pointing to the lawlessness of the existing shadow system. Side B's best move was the IRCA concede-and-pivot (conceding no direct displacement but arguing systemic baseline inflation), which partially limited damage but did not answer why keeping ten million people stateless serves the nurse better. Two Side A speeches were clipped for length, a real format cost, but the visible content was strong enough to sustain the margin. Side B's epistemic overreach (claiming past regularizations reliably produced larger inflows without qualifying that claim) cost credibility where it mattered most.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's [A2.3] argument that many long-term residents have no queue to jump at all—because the legal queue for their category was never realistically accessible—directly undercut Side B's sustained Manila nurse framing. Side B continued to invoke the nurse as the universal comparison case but never grappled with the population whose situation differs structurally from hers. That asymmetry left Side B's fairness narrative resting on a comparison that did not actually cover most of the affected group.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A wins by effectively dismantling Side B's zero-sum 'queue-jumping' argument and maintaining focus on the practical benefits of accountability. - **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side B wins due to tighter execution and a more resilient framing of the core tradeoff. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A maintained a cleaner and more consistent path through the debate. The core framing — that the real choice is between a permanent shadow class and a structured earned-status program, not between regularization and a hypothetical sustained enforcement regime — held up well across rounds. Crucially, Side A's A1 answer in Rebuttal 2 was the debate's most honest and effective argumentative move: rather than inventing an example, PRO conceded none existed and reframed the absence as evidence of structural impossibility in democratic contexts, not mere political cowardice. That pivot — "human communities will not tolerate it" — was responsive, well-grounded in political reality, and directly answered CON's pressure without evasion. CON's strongest material was the IRCA anchor (three million legalized, population rebuilt to eleven million), which is solid general knowledge and was maintained throughout. The "not tried vs. cannot work" distinction was also a real argumentative move. However, CON had two speeches clipped for exceeding the word limit, which is a genuine execution cost — those arguments were incomplete as received. CON's enforcement-as-viable alternative never fully resolved the core problem PRO identified: in the democratic societies the proposition addresses, long-term removal at scale has no sustained precedent, and CON's counter-examples (Singapore, Japan, Australia) operate in governance and migration contexts that weaken the comparison. Side B's wages argument (status without bargaining power leaves wages flat) was good, but PRO's narrower concession — removing employer leverage raises the floor even if not all wages — was sufficient to blunt it. The deciding factor is that Side A answered the motion's actual stakes more directly and closed with cleaner crystallization of why the proposition should pass in the world that exists, not the ideal one.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A1 in Rebuttal 2 — directly conceding no example of sustained enforcement exists, then arguing this absence reflects structural political and moral impossibility in democratic societies rather than untested potential. Rather than evading CON's pressure question, PRO leaned into it and reframed the very absence of evidence as its own proof. This moved the debate from a factual dispute to a structural one, where PRO held the stronger ground.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B won by decisively breaking Side A's core binary framework. While Side A powerfully argued that enforcement is functionally impossible due to human and political realities, Side B effectively countered by distinguishing between structural impossibility and lack of political will, citing concrete examples like Singapore and Australia. Side B also cleanly won the clash on the 'magnet effect' by using the IRCA example to directly answer Side A's pressure question about exponential growth, leaving Side A's pragmatic case severely damaged.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was exceptional, directly dismantling Side A's pressure questions with devastatingly effective background examples (Singapore/Australia/Japan for enforcement, IRCA for the magnet effect).
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO wins by successfully grounding the debate in political and moral feasibility. While CON offered a structurally competent case around rule-of-law, magnet effects, and the IRCA precedent, PRO consistently framed CON's "consistent enforcement" alternative as a democratic impossibility rather than a mere funding shortfall. CON's own admissions that enforcement is "electorally painful" and repeatedly abandoned ultimately validated PRO's core premise: governments cannot and will not mass-deport long-term rooted residents. PRO handled pressure questions directly, maintained tighter format discipline (avoiding the length clips that cost CON), and crystallized the clash around a realistic binary (regularization vs. permanent shadow class) that proved more persuasive than CON's hypothetical third path. CON's IRCA example was strong but insufficient to overcome the feasibility deficit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2) decisively turned CON's pressure on enforcement feasibility. By arguing that decades of abandoned enforcement reflect a structural democratic constraint rather than mere political cowardice or underfunding, PRO neutralized CON's entire alternative pathway and forced the debate back to the tangible harms of the status quo versus the actionable fix of regularization.
