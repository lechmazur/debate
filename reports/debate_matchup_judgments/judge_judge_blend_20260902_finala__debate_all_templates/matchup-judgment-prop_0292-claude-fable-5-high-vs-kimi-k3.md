### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by exposing a fatal contradiction in Side A's case. Side A's opening argued that a new right of action is necessary because defamation law fails when people know the image is fake. However, to escape Side B's pressure question about satire, Side A argued their statute only reaches fakes a reasonable viewer would mistake for genuine. Side B brilliantly capitalized on this, pointing out that Side A just redefined defamation and completely collapsed their own justification for needing a new speech-narrowing law.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.4 perfectly catching A's contradiction between their opening (the harm doesn't require belief) and their A1 answer (the harm is deception/mistaken for genuine).
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won the narrowing tradeoff. PRO ended up limiting its statute to deceptive, realistic fakes a reasonable viewer could mistake for real – which B showed is defamation/false-light territory that already provides private suit, subpoena, damages with built-in speech protections (fault, public-figure, reasonable-reader). That made the motion's payload clause – create an explicit new tort even if it narrows protections, exported to all countries – gratuitous. B also answered A3.1 with regulator-enforced platform duties, while A3.2/ B3 Q2 on Moscow/Ankara exposed that PRO's safeguards are optional and its abuse answer ("regimes have sharper weapons anyway") conceded cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3-B4.5 / B5.1: B turns PRO's A4.1 reasonable-viewer test into defamation and shows updated existing torts give victims the lawsuit without the mandated narrowing, leaving the motion with all cost and no unique benefit.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively exposing the analytical flaw in Side A's defense of the statute. By forcing Side A to narrow their proposed cause of action to "fabrications a reasonable viewer could mistake for genuine" in order to save political satire, Side B effectively proved that the remedy Side A actually defends is already covered by existing defamation and false light doctrines. Because these existing torts can be updated to cover deepfakes without narrowing speech protections, Side B successfully argued that the motion's core trade-off is purely costly and unnecessary. Side B also provided a much stronger answer to the global abuse argument, noting that the motion mandates the narrowing of speech while Side A's proposed safeguards are merely optional. Side A's case was well-constructed, but Side B's strategic trap in the pressure questions and clean comparative weighing in the later rounds secured a clear victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they capitalized on Side A's "reasonable viewer" concession to prove that the desired remedy is just defamation in disguise and requires no narrowing of speech protections, rendering the motion's explicit trade-off gratuitous.
