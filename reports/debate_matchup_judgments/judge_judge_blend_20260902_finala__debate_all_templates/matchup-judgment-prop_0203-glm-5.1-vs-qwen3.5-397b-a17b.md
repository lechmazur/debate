### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON)): Side A won on the strength of a sustained logical trap that Side B never cleanly escaped. PRO's Q1 pressure question exposed a genuine contradiction: CON simultaneously argued that substitute capital adequately funds the rival and that only our commercial presence gives us meaningful visibility. In Rebuttal 2 and the closing, PRO hammered this relentlessly and clearly. CON's answer in B4 tried to resolve the tension by distinguishing "global liquidity funds them" from "only our firms inform us," but this response is self-defeating: if the rival is funded regardless, the visibility we purchase is of a process we cannot stop, while intelligence agencies already provide monitoring without requiring us to capitalize the rival's build-out. PRO's second core distinction—selling products versus investing in rival indigenous capacity—was never adequately refuted. CON's rebuttal claimed investment locks in ecosystem compatibility and thus downstream sales, which is plausible but speculative, and PRO rightly pointed out firms can maintain global product revenues without funding competitor capacity directly. PRO also made an effective causation reversal on decoupling (China's self-sufficiency push predated screening debates), which CON never addressed squarely. CON's best arguments—innovation velocity from larger revenue pools and standard-setting through commercial presence—had genuine force but were undercut by PRO's maintained distinction between sales-based revenue (preserved under screening) and rival-funding investment (blocked). CON's closing repeated these points rather than sharpening the clash. Side A's closing was clipped, a real execution cost, but enough of the crystallization was visible, and the critical work had already been done in Rebuttal 2. Overall, PRO built a coherent, internally consistent case and exposed a live contradiction in CON's that was never resolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure question and the A4 follow-through crystallized the decisive clash: CON cannot simultaneously claim substitute capital is "strategically sufficient" for the rival and that our commercial presence is "uniquely informative" for us. If the rival is funded regardless, our investor seat is a paid subscription to watch our competitor grow—not a lever of influence. CON's attempt to resolve this by separating "funding them" from "informing us" still conceded the rival gets the capital, which undermines the entire visibility case.

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): PRO wins by cleanly framing outbound investment screening as closing a logical loophole in existing safeguards, distinguishing capital deployment from product sales to neutralize CON's economic harm claims, and exposing a decisive tension in CON's case between the sufficiency of substitute capital and the strategic value of commercial presence. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): PRO built the stronger through-line because civil-military fusion—the claim that civilian AI and chip investment is structurally indistinguishable from military support in authoritarian states—was introduced concretely and never squarely neutralized. CON's intelligence and leverage counterargument was partially undermined by PRO's observation [A2.3–A2.4] that dual-use R&D is walled off from foreign shareholders regardless of equity stakes, meaning commercial observation yields signals about public-facing activity rather than the sensitive programs screening aims to disrupt. CON's most effective move was exploiting PRO's "marginal delay" concession [A2.7] and turning "expertise, networks, validation" against PRO [B2.6]—a genuine tension that ran throughout the debate. But PRO's recovery ("marginal delays are decisive in technology cycles where fabrication spans years") is plausible, and the risk-asymmetry framing [A4.10]—security failure is existential, leverage failure is financial—remained a live, largely unanswered comparative throughout closing. CON's response (intelligence failure also carries strategic cost) is true but less vivid and doesn't rebut the asymmetry as cleanly. The rerouting argument [B1.2] is CON's other strong unanswered point, but PRO's analogy to other security protocols—imperfect enforcement still reduces volume and raises costs—is reasonable and broadly accepted practice in export control regimes. On balance, PRO's civil-military fusion claim and risk-asymmetry framing outweigh CON's intelligence-visibility case, which was weakened by the fusion rebuttal. Margin is real but competitive; CON ran a technically superior rebuttal campaign.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive rebuttal exchange was CON turning PRO's own "expertise, networks, validation" concession into a leverage argument [B2.6], exposing internal tension in PRO's case: if private capital is uniquely valuable, it also creates dependencies the rival can't easily replace—making the firm more pliable to democratic pressure. CON's route to victory remained more intact after the full exchange.
