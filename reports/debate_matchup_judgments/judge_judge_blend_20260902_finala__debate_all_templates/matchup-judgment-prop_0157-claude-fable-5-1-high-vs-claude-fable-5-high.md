### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won a close, high-quality debate by answering the central feasibility objection and preserving the comparative need for universal coverage. B's legislated contract alternative was strong, but remained narrower and left vaccines outside qualifying funding or procurement agreements uncovered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange was its answer that the law binds the firm rather than individual engineers, with compliance measured by validated production. This materially weakened B's claim that compulsion can produce only paperwork; B's later litigation objection showed delay and enforcement difficulty, but not impossibility or superiority to leaving unwilling firms free to refuse indefinitely.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won because it established that a legal mandate with a fixed window is the only mechanism that guarantees technology transfer from unwilling firms, and it rebutted Side B's impossibility claim by defining compliance through validated production rather than conscripted individuals. Side B's alternative of contract-up-front clauses was coherent but only bound firms that accepted public funding, leaving a fatal gap for players that the motion explicitly covers, and it never squarely answered Side A's pressure on what legally compels governments to deploy non-IP tools. While Side B landed sharp conceptual blows on consent versus compulsion and tacit knowledge, Side A's empirical anchor of 2021 discretion failures and its cleaner comparative framing provided the stronger live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (A4.1–A4.2), which answered Side B's pressure question by reframing the mandate as a performance obligation tied to regulator-validated batch production rather than employee conscription, and which exposed Side B's implicit concession that contractual obligations could compel transfer.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively dismantling the mechanical feasibility of the proposition. While Side B argued forcefully from moral urgency and the historical failures of voluntary sharing, Side A proved that all successful historical tech transfers were voluntary, and that compelling tacit knowledge transfer ex-post leads to malicious compliance and delayed litigation over batch failures. Side A also cleanly resolved Side B's closing trap by distinguishing ex-ante contractual consent from ex-post statutory compulsion, securing a clear and robust path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which exposed that Side B's "results-based" compliance metric for tacit knowledge transfer would simply turn the fixed time window into a countdown to litigation over failed batches, rendering the mandate practically unworkable during an emergency.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won a competitive debate by showing that technology was only one bottleneck and that inputs, orders, engineering cooperation, and export restrictions often remained decisive. More importantly, B defeated A's claim that negotiated transfer conceded away the innovation objection: a voluntary, priced contract for a chosen partner is economically different from a universal statutory trigger. A made a strong case that law prevents recurring discretion failures, but overstated how quickly transfer would create doses and never fully answered B's consent, pricing, and investment distinction. B's clipped rebuttal incurred an execution cost but preserved its core path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was its answer to A's claimed contract concession. By distinguishing a negotiated sale from an authorization-triggered compulsory transfer, B restored the innovation-cost argument and undercut the centerpiece of A's closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side A won by narrowing the clash to the reliability of the instrument. After Side B conceded that governments could write compelled, compensated transfer into purchase contracts, Side A showed that the only remaining dispute was whether the obligation should be discretionary or guaranteed by law. Side A leveraged the real-world pattern of voluntary failures—Moderna refusing the WHO hub, the absent Warp Speed clause, the shelved Kenya plant—to prove discretion repeatedly failed, while Side B could cite only the single Serum Institute success. Side B’s “binder-not-knowledge” and malicious-compliance objections were plausible but never fully grounded why a compensated statutory obligation would chill investors more than a contractually compelled one, and Side B’s Rebuttal 2 was clipped for exceeding the word limit, leaving key counter-arguments unfinished.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 1 turning Side B’s “the barrier was know-how, not patents” objection into direct support for the motion’s scope over manufacturing technology, and the subsequent collapse of Side B’s innovation-chilling argument once Side B conceded that compelled transfer could be written into contracts.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO)): Side A built a more empirically grounded case, successfully demonstrating that the actual bottlenecks in vaccine distribution were supply chain inputs, lack of purchase orders, and export bans, rather than just IP or know-how. Side A effectively used the Aspen and Serum Institute examples to prove that even when technology was transferred, production failed without guaranteed orders. Side A also laid a brilliant trap regarding the timeline, forcing Side B to concede a 12-month window that matched voluntary cooperative transfers, thereby neutralizing the speed advantage of the mandate. Side B argued well but ultimately conceded that pairing the mandate with procurement was necessary, allowing Side A to prove that the procurement, not the mandate, was the actual policy doing the work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it dismantled Side B's 12-month timeline concession and used the Serum Institute example to prove that transferred technology still failed due to export bans and lack of orders, rendering the mandate redundant.
