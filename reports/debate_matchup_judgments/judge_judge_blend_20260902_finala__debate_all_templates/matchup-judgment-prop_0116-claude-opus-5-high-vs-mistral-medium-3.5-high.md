### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): CON won by demonstrating that the cheapest, fastest adaptation to default liability would be screening out risky applicants rather than improving programs or cutting tuition. PRO never recovered from CON's "cheapest compliance route" framing or the devastating line that the metric "reads family wealth and calls it curriculum." PRO's own pressure question admitting universities could deny risky students backfired when CON showed this converts institutional accountability into applicant discrimination. PRO's alternative funding sources and reliance on program-level aggregation failed to answer CON's core causal challenge, while CON's prospective-vs-retrospective distinction showed existing federal tools were superior without the harmful side effects.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's answer to PRO's pressure question in Rebuttal 2, exposing that PRO's admission that universities can deny risky applicants proves CON's cream-skimming case rather than answering it, combined with the framing that program-level aggregation means the penalty finds whichever school enrolled students without family cushions.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): CON won the incentive and fairness clash. PRO claimed schools would improve services; CON showed declining risky applicants is free, immediate, and flips sign from revenue to liability. CON's wealth-cushion example proved defaults proxy family shock absorbers, not curriculum, and PRO's answer that aggregation is at program level conceded the point. PRO never justified retrospective liability over the prospective alternative of cutting off loans, and its funding answer of waste-cutting was vague. Pressure questions exposed this directly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure Q1 about two identical $25k borrowers diverging on parental cushion plus B2's sign-flip / cheapest-adaptation argument — PRO's A1 answered by re-asserting program-level measurement, which CON in B4 reframed as "the metric reads family wealth and calls it curriculum."
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B built a significantly stronger and more realistic causal story regarding institutional incentives. While Side A relied on the optimistic assumption that universities would respond to liability by improving career services and cutting administrative bloat, Side B convincingly argued that the path of least resistance is cream-skimming—rejecting poorer or riskier applicants. Side B's pressure questions were devastating, particularly the "two students" hypothetical, which exposed that default rates often measure family wealth rather than educational quality. Side A's attempt to answer this in Rebuttal 2 by pointing to program-level aggregation walked directly into Side B's trap, allowing Side B to deliver the decisive blow: "The metric reads family wealth and calls it curriculum." Furthermore, Side B successfully contrasted prospective federal tools (cutting off loans before harm occurs) with Side A's retrospective penalty system.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side A's answer to the "two students" pressure question, demonstrating that aggregating defaults at the program level simply penalizes institutions that enroll students lacking family financial cushions.
