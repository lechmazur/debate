### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but CON built a more sustained and damaging structural critique: that hiding rankings and disabling "highly personalized" upsells is underinclusive (the same status race and intimacy simulation relocates to creator shoutouts, chat spam, and tier/streak/goal nudges) and that "highly personalized" is a soft, gameable boundary. This point was introduced in Rebuttal 1, sharpened by pressure questions, and driven home decisively in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where CON turned PRO's own question ("would you need to censor shoutouts?") into proof the toggle can't reach the actual mechanism. PRO's distinction between "organic speech" and "scalable mined intimacy" was asserted but never fully defended against CON's counter that tier/streak/goal cues are equally surveillance-fed. PRO also weakened its own vulnerable-user narrative by conceding the tools are opt-in, which CON exploited (the teen who most needs protection is least likely to self-diagnose and enable it) — PRO's rebuttal that platforms "could" default-on the feature was speculative and outside the motion's actual text. CON's alternative framing (real injury is drained wallets, addressed by caps, not by hiding a dashboard) gave a cleaner, harder-to-dodge metric that PRO never fully neutralized. The debate ended with CON's underinclusiveness/soft-target argument still live and unanswered in substance, giving CON the clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO's pressure question (A1: "No, CON does not require censoring shoutouts—that is exactly why the motion fails") was the pivotal exchange: it flipped PRO's own question into confirmation that the toggle cannot reach the actual mechanism of parasocial pressure, and paired with the tier/streak "soft target" point, it left PRO's core mechanism claim structurally undermined for the rest of the debate.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO kept the debate on the proposition's own yardstick – limiting parasocial monetization, not just wallet drain. Hiding quantified public rankings and disabling surveillance-built upsells directly removes the scalable, platform-engineered intimacy loop described in A1.4/A2.1, while remaining user-selected so badges and charity ladders survive for opt-in fans. CON's strongest pressure – relocation to creator voice [B2.1] and tier/streak evasion [B2.2] – shows incompleteness but not failure, and its overbreadth claim collapses once the tool is framed as per-user hide rather than platform-wide ban. The opt-in vulnerability [B3.2] was answered by surfacing/prompting [A4.3]. Caps are non-exclusive and preserve the manipulative simulation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's relocation argument that hiding the widget moves status to creator voice and community norms [B2.1] / A1 in [B4.1] – exposing the motion as attacking mirror not engine and forcing PRO to distinguish scalable interface from organic speech.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively proving that the motion's specific tools are easily circumvented by creators (via live shoutouts) and platforms (via tier/streak nudges), rendering them cosmetic. Side A's alternative of financial caps directly addressed the core harm of drained wallets, whereas Side B struggled to defend the practical effectiveness of hiding a leaderboard when the parasocial pressure simply relocates to organic speech or opaque metrics.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2), which trapped Side B by showing that refusing to censor creator shoutouts guarantees the parasocial pressure survives, and that excluding tier/streak nudges allows platforms to easily evade the "highly personalized" restriction.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases without fake precision, but A adapted more sharply across rounds. A's opt-out framing neutralized the paternalism attack early, and its gambling/loss-limit analogy gave a workable answer to "how much harm must be shown" that B never cleanly rebutted (B's answer to that pressure question amounted to "the line is never crossed," which restates rather than resolves). A also gave a concrete, administrable criterion for personalized vs. generic prompts (use of behavioral data) when pressed, while B's strongest original contribution—the leaderboard-as-collective-good externality argument—was never fully disambiguated (does "hide" mean hiding your own view or removing your name from others' view?), leaving room for A's closing reframe: that if the game's value depends on unwilling spenders staying visible, that is itself the harm needing a remedy. B's closing mostly reasserted the externality point rather than directly defusing that flip, so A ended the debate with the cleaner, more resolved comparative frame, even though B's core objection remained a live and respectable minority argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question ("why does hiding decay the leaderboard unless the game depends on exposing unwilling spenders?") and its callback in Closing 1 turning B's externality argument into "proof the tools are needed" was the most decisive exchange; B's answer (B4.1) reasserted the collective-ritual claim but never resolved the underlying tension A exposed.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO kept the motion as opt-out, not ban, and won the freedom test – hiding personal rank and disabling data-driven upsells expands individual control while leaving generic support intact. A answered Q2 with an administrable data-use line, and turned CON's core collective-externality argument in Q1/A4/A5: if the game decays when unwilling spenders hide, that proves exposure was non-consensual. CON never showed systemic harmlessness beyond assertion that exit suffices, and its answer to when marketing becomes harm was non-responsive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Q1 that if hiding your own rank kills the board the game depends on exposing the reluctant, reinforced in A4-A5 to reframe B2.1/B4.1 decay as proof of need, not cost.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by successfully framing the proposition as a basic opt-out "monetary self-defense" tool, akin to screen-time limits or casino loss limits. This framing effectively neutralized CON's demand for proof of "systemic, unavoidable harm," as PRO correctly noted that opt-out toggles do not require epidemic-level damage to be justified. While CON introduced a strong, original argument about the "negative externality" of decaying the collective leaderboard experience, PRO bit the bullet and argued persuasively that a collective game relying on forced exposure and stripped privacy is inherently exploitative. PRO's responses to pressure questions were direct and administrable, whereas CON struggled to defend its artificially high burden of proof for a mere user-toggle.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response to CON's "decay" argument in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, turning CON's claim that the tools ruin the leaderboard into proof that the leaderboard's value illegitimately depends on trapping vulnerable spenders who cannot opt out.
