### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.3** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side A won because its economic rebuttals and answers to pressure questions established that price limits create market exit risks while targeted subsidies enable progressive, visible sharing of costs; Side B's solidarity framing was not neutralized on regressivity or adverse selection but proved weaker after comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal 2 answers on subsidy visibility versus price-cap fragility and the hidden flat-tax effect of cross-subsidies directly damaged B's core "front-end limits" claim.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): CON won by drawing a decisive analytical distinction between risk classification and affordability, showing that legally limiting actuarial fairness does not eliminate costs but instead shifts them regressively onto low-income healthy policyholders through inflated premiums. CON successfully exposed that PRO’s model embeds a hidden flat tax inside the insurance pool rather than sharing burdens progressively through taxation. PRO’s moral frame of pricing vulnerability remained rhetorically potent, but they never squarely answered the distributional damage within the pool or the concrete risk of insurer market exit, leaving CON’s causal and comparative story the cleaner route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s regressivity turn in Rebuttal 2 and Closing: demonstrating that compressing premiums forces young, low-income healthy workers to subsidize wealthier high-risk buyers, converting PRO’s solidarity claim into a regressive cross-subsidy that fails on PRO’s own values.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by cleanly separating risk classification from affordability and demonstrating that its preferred tools (mandates and targeted subsidies) avoid the unique structural fragility of premium caps: insurer market exit. A's Rebuttal 2 introduction of the regressivity problem—that compressing premiums acts as a hidden flat tax on low-income, low-risk buyers—directly undercuts B's solidarity framing and remains largely unanswered. B's front-end solidarity narrative and line-drawing defense were rhetorically cohesive, but B never fully resolved the practical weighing on market stability or the distributional unfairness of cross-subsidies within the risk pool. Despite minor clipping costs on both sides, A provided the sharper comparative analysis of policy failure modes and a cleaner reason to prefer back-end progressive support over front-end price distortion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 comparison of failure modes: a shortfall in subsidies remains visible, quantifiable, and still delivers partial coverage, whereas a misaligned premium cap triggers silent insurer withdrawal and leaves vulnerable buyers with no carrier at all. This directly neutralized B's claim that premium regulation is less fragile and decisively shifted the practical weighing.
