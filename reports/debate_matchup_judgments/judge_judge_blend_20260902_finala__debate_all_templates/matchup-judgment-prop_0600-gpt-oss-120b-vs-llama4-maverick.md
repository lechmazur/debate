### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Llama 4 Maverick (CON)): Side A (PRO) won by consistently engaging with CON's core objections and making sharper comparative cases across the exchange. Three moments were decisive. First, PRO's "valley of death" framing gave the protectionist tools a principled time-limit rationale, and the claim that Brazil reduced its tariff by half after industry maturation was the most specific and direct answer to CON's entrenchment objection—CON never adequately refuted it. Second, PRO's reframe in Rebuttal 2 that nearshoring-focused industrial policy is itself the mechanism to close infrastructure gaps (rather than a policy blind to those gaps) was clever and went unanswered; CON simply repeated the infrastructure-gap objection as if PRO had not addressed it. Third, PRO's Q2 to CON—demanding a concrete alternative mechanism—proved decisive: CON's A2 explicitly conceded it was "not obligated to present a comprehensive alternative," which is a serious execution failure in a debate about prioritization, where showing a superior option is the natural burden for the opposing side. CON's recurring strengths (entrenchment risk, speculative market figures, limited domestic linkages) were plausible but underspecified and repetitively stated rather than deepened across rounds. CON's closing recycled earlier points using misformatted reference IDs and added no new weighing. PRO's clipping was a minor cost but the visible content consistently did more argumentative work. The margin is clear but not dominant because CON's entrenchment argument retained some force and several of PRO's numbers were suspiciously precise without grounding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Q2: instead of defending against the infrastructure-gap criticism, PRO reframed it—arguing that a nearshoring-focused industrial policy is precisely the vehicle for closing those gaps, using Colombia's logistics-hub upgrade as a live illustration. CON then repeated the infrastructure objection in its closing as though the reframe had never happened, which crystallized that PRO had the stronger answer on this pivotal clash point.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Llama 4 Maverick (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): Side B won by more directly answering pressure questions and demonstrating that protectionist tools could function as time-limited bridges generating self-sustaining fiscal returns, whereas Side A repeatedly failed to engage the sunset-clause defense and offered only vague, non-specific alternatives when pressed for concrete mechanisms to overcome Latin America's structural weaknesses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 turning Latin America's infrastructure and regulatory deficits into justifications for nearshoring-focused industrial policy rather than insurmountable barriers, while directly rebutting the Brazil ethanol temporariness claim with specific timeline and tariff-reduction details.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = Llama 4 Maverick (CON)): Side A constructed a tighter causal chain linking nearshoring, calibrated protectionism, and fiscal multipliers, directly engaging the motion's explicit trade-offs. A successfully turned B's infrastructure critique by arguing the policy itself funds logistics and regulatory upgrades, and defended the temporariness of tariffs with sunset mechanisms and revenue-recoupment timelines. B raised legitimate political-economy concerns about lobbying and policy entrenchment, but leaned on repetitive free-trade generalities and never adequately answered how open competition alone would capture a time-sensitive nearshoring window. Despite A's clipped turns imposing a minor execution cost, the visible substance was sharper, more responsive to pressure, and offered a cleaner comparative path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer to the infrastructure/regulatory pressure question, which turned B's structural critique into an offensive point by arguing that nearshoring-focused industrial policy explicitly earmarks fiscal resources for logistics corridors and customs modernization, thereby neutralizing B's claim that regional deficits make the strategy unworkable.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Llama 4 Maverick (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side B (CON) built the stronger cumulative case across all five rounds. Its opening deployed three concrete mechanisms—fiscal crowding-out, retaliatory blowback, and the technology-transfer illusion—each illustrated with a distinct regional example (Mexico's maquilas, Argentina's 2000s tariff hike, Brazil's auto subsidies). Side A (PRO) never dislodged any of these: "careful policy design" recurred across every round as the answer to fiscal and enforcement problems without being spelled out in operationally convincing terms. CON's pressure questions (Q1 on fiscal displacement, Q2 on enforceable domestic-R&D instruments) hit real gaps, and PRO's A1/A2 answers offered fiscal reallocation and performance-based incentives without addressing how those instruments escape the rent-seeking and enforcement pathologies CON had already named. CON's own answers to PRO's questions were more effective: it correctly separated the retaliation mechanism from supply-chain depth (a non-trivial logical clarification), and it reframed Mexico and Chile as pre-advantage exceptions rather than replicable templates. CON's closing crystallised the structural-drain argument cleanly, while PRO's closing largely recapped earlier points. The repeated clipping of CON's speeches is a minor execution cost, but it affected mostly end-of-speech material and did not obscure the core argumentation. Overall, CON's arguments were more grounded, its rebuttals more damaging, and its weighing sharper.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's Q1 in Rebuttal 2 [B4.1]: PRO tried to argue that since multinationals already avoid building local supply chains, the retaliation claim is undermined. CON cleanly separated the two mechanisms—retaliation is triggered by the *policy act* (tariffs, local-content rules), not by the depth of firm integration—and then turned the very absence of local supply chains back against PRO, showing that the protectionist wall yields neither upgrading nor safety from trade blows. This was the sharpest logical move in the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = Llama 4 Maverick (PRO)): CON built a coherent, mechanism-driven case showing that nearshoring industrial policy imposes structural fiscal drains and provokes trade retaliation while failing to generate genuine diversification. PRO never overcame the concrete historical pattern—Argentina’s collapse, Brazil’s subsidy failures, Mexico’s shallow assembly integration—or gave a credible account of how “careful policy design” would prevent rent-seeking and displacement of health and education spending. CON’s rebuttals directly defanged PRO’s examples, turned PRO’s best pressure question into a reinforcement of the retaliation risk, and crystallized the clash in the closing as a losing fiscal equation. PRO’s responses remained vague and repeatedly appealed to unspecified “targeted” fixes without engaging CON’s causal specifics.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s answer to PRO’s pressure question about the apparent contradiction between shallow supply-chain integration and retaliation risk (A4.1), where CON explained that protectionist policy acts themselves trigger retaliation regardless of firm embeddedness, and that shallow integration makes the cost-benefit ratio of such protectionism especially unfavorable.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Llama 4 Maverick (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side B wins by effectively leveraging the proposition’s explicit concessions on fiscal burdens and protectionism. While Side A repeatedly relied on “careful policy design” as a catch-all solution, Side B dismantled this by detailing structural constraints: state capacity limits, rent-seeking risks, shallow MNC tech transfer, and the inevitability of trade retaliation regardless of supply chain depth. Side B’s weighing of fiscal crowding-out against low-value job creation was consistently sharper and better grounded in regional examples. Side A’s responses were competent but generic, failing to materially offset B’s macroeconomic and implementation critiques.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 1 and 2 effectively neutralized Side A’s spillover and “nuanced protectionism” claims by explaining why multinationals avoid local supply chains and why retaliation is triggered by the policy act itself, not integration depth. This closed off Side A’s primary route to victory.
