### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by steadily widening and then exploiting the core gap in PRO's regime: transparency and debris-control obligations describe who is extracting and how, but they do not allocate priority when two operators target the same scarce site, and they bind only signatories. PRO never closed that gap. The decisive turn came in Side A's Rebuttal 2, where A4.3 conceded that "other states can grant their own licenses to the same resource class." Side B correctly treated this as a damaging admission—overlapping national titles, competing safety zones, and no universally accepted umpire is not legal order; it is legalized collision. The phrase "disclosure is not allocation, and debris control is not adjudication" [B4.2] crystallized the distinction cleanly and was never answered. Side A's reliance on the Artemis Accords as the dispute-settlement forum was repeatedly weakened by the non-signatory problem, and the maritime-law analogy—while apt—never explained why early occupation at uniquely scarce polar sites is reversible in the way sea lanes were not. Side B's reversibility comparison (slower profits vs. entrenched first-mover rights as a property regime) survived the full exchange intact. Side A's opening and first rebuttal were strong, keeping the debate competitive, but the pressure-question round and Rebuttal 2 shifted momentum clearly toward CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 [B4.2–B4.4]: after PRO admitted rivals could issue their own licenses to the same resource class, Side B turned that concession into proof that PRO's regime produces overlapping titles plus no common umpire—"legalized collision, not legal order." The companion line "disclosure is not allocation, and debris control is not adjudication" was the sharpest single formulation in the debate and was never adequately answered.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully proving that the proposed conditions (transparency and debris control) cannot resolve the core issues of site priority and exclusion zones. Side B effectively used Side A's admission that multiple states could issue licenses for the same resource to demonstrate that Side A's model leads to "legalized collision" rather than certainty, and weighed the irreversible harm of first-mover monopolies against the temporary cost of delayed investment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal capitalizing on Side A's concession about overlapping national licenses, branding it "legalized collision."
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by isolating the structural flaw in PRO's mechanism: transparency and debris control do not solve allocation or adjudication. CON's distinction between disclosure and allocation directly neutralized PRO's core regulatory claim, while CON's weighing of reversible delay versus irreversible, bankable precedents gave a clean comparative metric. PRO's reliance on voluntary accords and diplomatic objections failed to answer how overlapping national licenses would be resolved without a binding forum, a vulnerability CON exploited sharply in rebuttals and closing. CON also handled pressure questions directly and maintained tighter epistemic discipline throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 line "Disclosure is not allocation, and debris control is not adjudication" perfectly crystallized why PRO's transparency regime cannot prevent de facto appropriation or resolve competing safety zones. Paired with turning PRO's "overlapping licenses" admission into proof of "legalized collision," this dismantled PRO's path to certainty and secured the win on the irreversibility weighing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): PRO wins on three decisive grounds. First, CON's heaviest legal weapon—the OST/Moon Agreement prohibition—was materially weakened when PRO correctly noted that major spacefaring states are not parties to the Moon Agreement; CON never recovered from this, continuing to invoke it as if universal. Second, PRO's fish-in-the-ocean framing of extracted-vs-territorial resources held up; CON's samples-vs-commercial-scale distinction is coherent but does not close the logical gap PRO opened, and the "defeasible commercial permission" answer in Rebuttal 2 was reasonable and unrebutted. Third, and most decisively, CON conceded in B4.3 that state-backed firms "can launch, extract, and sell regardless of any de-facto prohibition because they control launch licenses, financing, and downstream markets." That admission directly demolished CON's "gray zone levels the playing field" argument—the centerpiece of their case—and PRO correctly identified and exploited it in the closing. CON's entrenchment-of-early-rights concern is real and was never fully dissolved by PRO, but it cut both ways: PRO's reframe that ungoverned informal practice hardens faster than explicit, limited permissions was equally plausible and CON did not answer it squarely. CON's closing recycled the OST argument without addressing the fatal B4.3 concession, leaving the round to PRO on the most important live clash. Both rebuttals were clipped, neutralizing that structural factor across both sides.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing pivot on CON's B4.3 concession was the most decisive exchange. CON had admitted that state-backed firms could extract and sell in the gray zone anyway; PRO immediately reframed this as fatal to CON's entire equality argument—if powerful actors proceed regardless, withholding legal recognition strips oversight leverage without preventing the race. CON's closing ignored this concession entirely, cementing PRO's advantage on the central comparative question.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A wins decisively due to a catastrophic unforced error by Side B in Rebuttal 2. Side B inexplicably contradicted its own opening and argued Side A's case, stating that the gray zone removes legal tools, harms compliant firms, and entrenches the strongest actors. Side A capitalized on this fatal concession immediately in the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Closing catching Side B's concession in A2, cleanly resolving the pragmatic clash over whether a gray zone or conditional legality better constrains bad actors.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by establishing a more practical and resilient framework for pre-treaty governance. PRO's core comparison—governable, transparent activity backed by Earth-side leverage (insurance, licensing, financing) versus an ungovernable gray zone that only empowers state-backed first-movers—proved decisive. CON's reliance on a strict reading of the Outer Space Treaty was abstract and failed to offer a realistic mechanism to stop powerful actors from extracting regardless of legal status. Crucially, CON suffered a major execution error in Rebuttal 2 by stating that the gray zone "actually entrenches the advantage of the strongest actors," directly validating PRO's central equity claim and undermining CON's own case. PRO capitalized on this concession cleanly in the closing. PRO's answers to pressure questions were also more direct, offering concrete, defeasible licensing mechanisms and market-enforcement hooks rather than relying on the illusory protection of legal uncertainty.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which dismantled CON's "gray zone levels the playing field" claim by detailing Earth-side market/legal leverage and sharply exploiting CON's accidental concession that uncertainty actually entrenches powerful actors.
