### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): A won by making the decisive comparison about timing and institutional capacity: frontier developers can evaluate model-level dangers before irreversible release, while downstream users cannot. B established a real competition and threshold-drift concern, but repeatedly asserted that licensing yields zero safety without defeating A's point that leakage makes the pre-release gate more valuable, not worthless. A also handled the required questions more directly and weighed reversible delay against irreversible diffusion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response to the leakage objection was decisive: counterfeit or illicit circulation does not make lawful premarket controls pointless, and risk-based regulation cannot control leaked models either. This exposed B's argument as an imperfection objection without a superior comparative alternative.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by locking the decisive clash on burden allocation and irreversibility: who must prove dangerous capabilities before diffusion, and why post-hoc fines plus deployer paperwork leave catastrophic risks unowned. CON's moat/capture and mutability points landed hard and stayed live, but PRO answered them more cleanly (licensing as ongoing operator conditions, not frozen artefacts; threshold already in the AI Act; leakage as normal condition that does not kill first-release gates) and flipped the SME opacity problem into a stronger indictment of CON's regime. Closing crystallization favored PRO's "permission precedes deployment" frame over CON's cartel frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO R2 on leakage (counterfeits/encrypted munitions still licensed; control the first-release gate) plus the flip that under CON nobody owns pathogen-synthesis risk.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): B won the decisive asymmetry clash: both face leakage, opacity, threshold rot, but licensing puts burden on weight-holders before irreversible diffusion versus on SMEs after. A conceded that under risk-based the logistics SME only checks its own use case [A4.3], leaving B's pathogen uplift question assigned to no capable actor — which B crystallized in closing. A's moat/capture story explained the alleged contradiction but never answered comparative compliance tax or systemic-responsibility gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R2 A1/A2 answered hard leakage/revocation pressure by citing counterfeit drugs/encryption/firearms and flipping to first-release leverage and invisible refusal power (drug candidates never marketed), then collapsing A's case into pointing at flaws licensing shares but handles better.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by isolating the proposition’s actual addition: a pre-deployment veto beyond existing testing and enforcement duties. Side A never supplied a meaningful refusal standard and only partially answered the open-weights dilemma. Its closing then mischaracterized B’s alternative as unvetted release followed by lawsuits, despite B consistently defending mandatory pre-deployment checks, market withdrawal powers, and strict liability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it used A’s concession that no bright line exists to argue that licensing adds arbitrary discretion or a ceremonial stamp, then turned A’s airline analogy with the 737 MAX example and contrasted regulatory approval with strict developer liability.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON won by narrowing the live clash to what licensing uniquely adds: a discretionary veto without a usable refusal standard, plus a responsibility-shifting stamp. PRO’s check-first framing was memorable but repeatedly failed against CON’s point that the AI Act already requires pre-deployment checks, and that PRO conceded both “no bright line” and weak control of open weights. CON’s alternative (enforceable duties + suspension + strict liability) undercut the strawman that CON wanted unvetted release, and later rounds crystallized the cleaner comparative: bind firms to outcomes rather than co-sign foresight. Clipped early CON turns were a real cost, but not enough once the standard/open-weights/liability routes were pressed home.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 linking PRO’s dual concessions (no refusal standard + open-weights limits) to the 737 MAX stamp-as-shield and strict-liability ownership of unforeseen harm.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON wins because it collapses the difference: the AI Act already requires pre-deployment evals, red-teaming, and market withdrawal. PRO never supplies a refusal standard and in B4 concedes "no perfect bright line." CON turns that concession into liability shield and discretionary negotiation, backed by the 737 MAX example where certification shielded the maker, and presses the open-weights dilemma which B4 fails to resolve. PRO's "check vs clean-up" framing is shown to be false since CON defends mandatory checks plus strict liability. Execution cost for A's clipped openings does not outweigh that core strategic control.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 flipping the airline analogy and pairing B4.1 no-bright-line concession with B4.3 open-weights concession to show licensing licenses what it cannot evaluate and creates a state co-signature for the developer's shrug.
