B's best counter-move was the controlled-assessment answer in Rebuttal 2 A1 (in-class writing, oral defenses, proctored exams), which was genuinely strong and gave B a real path to victory; B could have forged a winning line by pressing that these environments verify independent competency regardless of outside AI access. But A's closing dismissed that answer with rhetorical gestures ("delusion," "nostalgic fallacy") rather than substantive engagement, and yet B also failed to sharpen the comparative—it never explained why those controlled assessments, combined with selective AI introduction later, would outperform A's transparency-plus-citation model on real-world readiness. A's inversion (permission → guided scrutiny of outputs; prohibition → black-box reliance) therefore remained the most durable comparative claim on the table. B's transparency-logs-don't-prove-origination argument is strong and makes A's framework genuinely costly, but A reframed the goal as grading editorial judgment rather than origination, and B didn't fully close that loop. B's clipped closing is a minor execution cost. On balance, A's pragmatic case aligned better with the motion's actual terms and survived the full exchange more cleanly.

B's response in closing ("permission validates a shortcut and calls it innovation") was rhetorically sharp but did not address the empirical premise that students are already using AI unguided, making prohibition the actual accelerant of dependency. This inversion stood as the debate's most decisive unrebutted comparative claim.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON)): This was a genuinely competitive debate with strong material on both sides. CON wins on balance for three interconnected reasons. First, CON's foundational distinction between AI and mechanical tools was established early (B1.1), maintained throughout with the exoskeleton analogy and the algebra-before-calculators framing (B4.4), and was never fully defeated by PRO. PRO's "evolved struggle" reframe is rhetorically appealing but sidesteps CON's sharpest point: that students who lack the foundational experience of drafting arguments independently cannot meaningfully evaluate or critique AI output (B4.3, B3.2). PRO's A2 response ("you can't learn to distinguish hallucinations by pretending tools don't exist") is fair but actually concedes that AI interaction develops the skill rather than showing that the skill can be built this way from scratch—which is exactly what CON contests. Second, CON's pressure question Q1 (transparency theater) was strong and PRO acknowledged the mechanism's flaws while arguing the ban alternative is worse—a partial concession. Third, CON's in-class assessment pivot (B4.1) was the most decisive single response in the debate: rather than merely defending the abstract value of bans, it offered a concrete, constructive alternative that directly answered PRO's clearest pressure point about enforcement. PRO correctly labeled this a "retreat," but CON's rebuttal—that it ensures verified foundational competence before AI collaboration—was coherent and wasn't squarely defeated. PRO's strongest sustained argument (bans create covert use and hypocrisy) is real and partially live, but CON's in-class pivot neutralized its most urgent form. The cognitive development argument, more philosophically grounded and consistently prosecuted, gives CON the cleaner path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A1 in Rebuttal 2 (B4.1): the in-class writing/blue-book/oral defense pivot. Rather than just defending the philosophical value of bans, CON directly answered PRO's strongest pressure—"how do you enforce a ban without intrusive investigation?"—with a concrete structural alternative. This changed the comparison from "ban vs. transparency mandate" to "venue-controlled assessment vs. take-home AI permissibility," forcing PRO to argue that in-class work is a retreat rather than that bans are unenforceable. The pivot was responsive, not evasive, and partially neutralized PRO's clearest live argument.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (PRO)): Side A narrowly wins by making the central educational harm clearer: generative AI does not merely assist with mechanics but can replace the formative struggle of drafting, organizing, and developing independent judgment. Side B had a strong practicality case about bans being hard to enforce and AI literacy being necessary, but it too often assumed students already possess the literacy needed to validate AI outputs. Side A’s later answer that schools can use in-class writing and oral defenses gave a plausible enforcement alternative and helped blunt B’s strongest point. B was more comparative at times, but A’s “you cannot critique what you do not understand” framing better answered the proposition’s default-permissibility burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s answer to the AI-literacy claim was most decisive: if students have not first developed independent writing and reasoning skills, then process transparency and AI curation do not ensure learning, because students lack the foundation needed to judge the tool’s output.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON)): CON won by defending a coherent foundation-first pedagogy against PRO's integration model. B successfully distinguished AI from mechanical tools, established that writing is the primary mode of thinking, and provided a concrete enforcement alternative (in-class assessments) that broke PRO's 'unenforceable ban' narrative. While PRO offered a realistic vision of modern tool use, they never fully answered how students can critically evaluate AI outputs without first mastering independent synthesis, and their 'curation is thinking' framing remained less intuitive than CON's 'writing is thinking' core. B's closing crystallized the stakes cleanly: education must cultivate the thinker before the tool-manager.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2, which answered A's enforcement pressure by proposing in-class writing and oral defenses as authentic verification, and deployed the algebra-before-calculator analogy to show that foundational struggle must precede tool use.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON)): Side B wins by successfully neutralizing PRO's core practical claim that bans are unenforceable and administratively burdensome. When pressed, B offered a concrete alternative (in-class writing, oral defenses) that shifts the assessment venue rather than policing digital logs, effectively answering the enforcement pressure. B also built a stronger pedagogical sequencing argument: foundational cognitive friction must precede tool use, making default permissibility a cart-before-the-horse approach that risks technical dependency. PRO's case was rhetorically polished and pragmatically framed around realism, but never fully resolved how students develop the baseline literacy to critically evaluate AI outputs without first mastering unassisted drafting. B's exoskeleton analogy and clear weighing of cognitive development over output optimization provided the cleaner, more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 directly answered PRO's pressure on ban enforcement by pivoting to in-class assessments and oral defenses, removing the need for forensic log-policing while preserving academic integrity. This practically defused PRO's strongest argument and allowed B to crystallize the sequencing clash: verify foundational mastery first, then allow tool augmentation.
