- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): B won by keeping the motion’s distinct injury live—necessary unpaid care still causes a pension penalty—and by forcing A into an unresolved contradiction: credits are too modest to protect yet large enough to trap secondary earners.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Q2/A-side pressure on the modest-credit vs labor-trap contradiction, then B4 crystallizing that both claims cannot drive CON’s case.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO successfully defended that unpaid care should not erase pension rights by showing CON's alternative policies leave unavoidable care gaps unaddressed. PRO's risk-pooling reframe and residual-care argument survived the exchange as live routes to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7) where it directly answered CON's pressure questions, demonstrated that some verification borderline cases are unavoidable under any targeted program, and showed that CON's standard would exclude every caregiver from recognition merely because some arrangements are hard to verify.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A built a much stronger and more logically consistent case. A successfully framed pension credits as a core function of social insurance (pooling risks that disrupt earning capacity) and decisively dismantled Side B's primary "labor-market trap" argument by exposing the contradiction in claiming the credits are both too small to provide retirement security and large enough to incentivize workforce exit. Side B's alternative proposals (infrastructure and minimum pensions) were effectively neutralized by A's argument that they address different harms and do not rectify the specific loss of contributory entitlements.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's exposure of Side B's logical contradiction regarding the size of the credits—asking how they could be simultaneously too modest to protect caregivers yet generous enough to drive them out of the workforce—was the turning point of the debate, which Side B failed to adequately resolve.
