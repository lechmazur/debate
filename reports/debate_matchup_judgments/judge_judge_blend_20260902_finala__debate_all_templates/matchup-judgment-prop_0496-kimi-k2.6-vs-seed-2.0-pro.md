### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): A built a coherent structural case (classified contracts, jurisdictional fog, casualty opacity, courts-martial vs. legal gray zone) and executed sharp, targeted rebuttals that engaged B's specific claims point by point. Crucially, A's pressure question exposed that B's central supporting anecdote (an "identical" unnamed special-forces raid) was unverifiable invented detail, and B's answer in Rebuttal 2 quietly abandoned the specific claim, retreating to an unsupported "every independent defence observer confirms" assertion — a grounding failure the judging criteria explicitly penalize. B's counterfactual framing (banning contractors just pushes violence into darker, less visible channels) was a genuinely strong comparative move and its closing landed a memorable rhetorical point about A's "concession" that some violence would migrate elsewhere. But B never adequately answered A's distinction that uniformed/special forces remain formally bound by military law and courts-martial even when underused, whereas contractors are structurally outside that system — a distinction A repeatedly leveraged. On balance, A's case was more consistently grounded and its rebuttals did more real damage to B's central mechanism than B's rebuttals did to A's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question exposing the unverifiable "identical raid eighteen months earlier" anecdote, and B's subsequent retreat to a vague, uncited "every independent defence observer confirms" claim rather than defending or owning the specific example.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B won by establishing that contractors institutionalize a scalable, profit-driven escape from military law, public command, casualty visibility, and electoral responsibility. Side A offered a sharp displacement argument and memorable window metaphor, but treated migration to covert force as inevitable without adequately supporting it. Its decisive comparison relied on an unnamed raid, brittle claims about consequences after Baghdad, and vague appeals to every independent defence observer. B exposed those grounding problems and reasonably weighed that eliminating one normalized evasion channel still improves accountability even if covert action remains a separate problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s pressure on A’s unnamed special-forces comparison was decisive. A responded by calling secrecy the operating norm and invoking unspecified observers, but never validated the purported parallel incident or proved that banned contractor operations would systematically migrate rather than be deterred. This weakened the factual foundation of A’s entire substitution case.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side A won by successfully grounding the debate in political reality rather than idealistic principle. Side A's core argument—that banning PMCs will not stop states from using force but will merely push them toward inherently less visible and less accountable covert special forces and proxies—was highly persuasive. Side A effectively used the historical reality of the Baghdad (Blackwater) contractor scandal to prove that PMC abuses do generate massive public backlash, congressional hearings, and consequences, contrasting this with the total opacity of classified special forces operations. Side B relied heavily on the principled stance that democratic states should simply "stand down" if they cannot use uniformed troops, which Side A easily dismissed as naive. When Side B conceded that operations might migrate to covert channels but dismissed it as a "separate problem," Side A capitalized on this to show that PRO's ban actively worsens the very accountability they claim to champion. Side A's pragmatic framing and sharp comparative weighing secured a clear victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal, which contrasted the massive public fallout, lost contracts, and ministerial testimonies following the Baghdad contractor incident with the complete public silence surrounding a comparable uniformed special forces raid, decisively breaking PRO's claim that contractors are a uniquely unaccountable black box.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): CON built the more durable structural attack: the My Lai/Abu Ghraib parity point undercut PRO's claim that contractor impunity is uniquely "by design" rather than a symptom of systemic impunity that also afflicts uniformed forces, and the displacement argument (banning contractors pushes states toward militias, proxies, and air campaigns with even less oversight) was never substantively neutralized — PRO's closing only rhetorically dismissed it ("that is surrender") rather than showing the ban actually improves accountability outcomes. PRO's "failure vs. design" distinction was a reasonable conceptual move but remained mostly asserted, and PRO's own pressure-question answer (governments prefer contractors for "lower political temperature") arguably conceded CON's convenience-not-severance framing. CON's closing crystallized the double-bind more cleanly than PRO resolved it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's repeated My Lai/Abu Ghraib parity point in Rebuttal 1 and 2, forcing PRO to rely on an unproven "failure vs. design" distinction that was never independently substantiated, combined with the unanswered displacement argument (proxies/militias/airstrikes) that PRO only rhetorically waved away rather than refuted on outcomes.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by showing that accountability depends on legal and command architecture rather than employment status, making regulation and universal deployment approval a credible alternative to a categorical ban. Side B sharply distinguished democratic failure from deliberate outsourcing, but never established why the elected government that hires, directs, and regulates contractors cannot remain publicly answerable. Its universal claims about absent political consequences were also under-supported.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s answer to the corporate-veil argument was decisive: the contracting state can impose military command, rules of engagement, domestic prosecution, and parliamentary approval. Side B repeatedly called contractor impunity “the design” but did not neutralize this regulatory alternative, leaving the necessity of a ban unproven.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side B won by successfully distinguishing between the incidental failures of military justice and the structural design of private contractors to evade democratic oversight. Side B's rebuttal to the displacement argument (we don't legalize corruption just because secret bribes happen) was highly effective, while Side A relied too heavily on "regular troops do it too" without fully overcoming the normative force of the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to Side A's comparison to uniformed troops, crystallizing that impunity for soldiers is a "failure of democracy" whereas impunity for contractors is "the design," effectively neutralizing Side A's main comparative argument.
