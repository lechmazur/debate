### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO won by narrowing the debate to the only live issue that mattered: the gap between when a system can compute a fee and when it is shown. CON’s strongest line—variability and exactness at consent—was real, but never fully explained a legitimate purpose for silence once inputs already exist short of sunk-cost extraction. PRO’s continuous pressure on that delay, the sunk-cost mechanism, competition distortion, and the triviality of fixed menu/print disclosures outweighed CON’s redesign and approximation warnings, which PRO largely contained by separating small fixed-charge sellers from platforms that already calculate fees in real time. CON spent too much energy accusing PRO of rewriting the motion rather than defending the delay itself, so PRO left the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s later capture of CON’s “verification” defense: if a fee is verified enough to bill, it is verified enough to display, collapsing CON’s claimed accuracy gap.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO won by demonstrating that the delay between when platforms calculate mandatory fees and when they display them serves no legitimate purpose other than exploiting sunk costs. CON's defenses—transaction complexity, small-business burden, and verification gaps—were systematically dismantled: fixed charges require only printed notices, dynamic platforms already compute fees earlier in the flow, and a fee verified enough to bill is verified enough to disclose. CON never offered a persuasive justification for withholding the exact fee during the screens between input and checkout, leaving PRO's exploitation narrative as the only live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which collapsed CON's "verification gap" by showing that if a fee is provisional it cannot be charged, and if it can be charged it can be shown—proving the delay is strategic rather than necessary.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won the timing clash that the debate narrowed to. Itemization and exact totals are preserved under PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4/B5 gap argument - if a fee is verified enough to charge on final screen it is verified enough to show one step earlier; CON's own "inside the checkout flow" admission proves the delay is strategic, not accuracy-driven.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): B won by boxing A into a live dilemma: under A's own "once calculable" standard the opening browsing-comparison injury is largely unreached, so the residual remedy is a one-screen cosmetic shift with the same sunk costs, while mandated earlier disclosure risks padded/bundled quotes that undercut the honest-competitor logic A itself advanced. A's payment-vs-review distinction and anti-padding replies never fully neutralized that ledger; B's weighing (toothless or harmful) made the decision clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R1: A's refined "once calculable" rule fails the $12-vs-$14 browsing harm and reduces to redecorating checkout one tap earlier while padding still corrupts comparisons.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): CON won by showing that PRO's own "once calculable" standard abandons the core browsing-comparison harm PRO opened with. For delivery apps and many ticketing fees, mandatory charges only become calculable after the cart is built and the address entered—precisely when the sunk-cost hook PRO complained about has already occurred. CON also demonstrated that shifting disclosure from the payment screen to the review screen changes almost nothing: the same inputs are entered, the same exit is one tap away, and the comparison shopper has already committed to a platform. PRO conceded that padded estimates are acceptable and that cross-restaurant comparison is not fixed, leaving CON's "toothless or harmful" frame decisive at the end of the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's sustained critique that PRO's calculability standard cannot reach the browsing shopper, reducing the rule to moving a total one tap earlier while undermining the market-competition benefits PRO promised.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A (CON) won the central weighing clash. It showed PRO's opening harm - browsing $12 vs $14 comparison [B1.2] - is not reached by PRO's own narrowed rule of "once calculable" [B2.1], reducing the benefit to moving a number from payment to review with identical cart, address, and account sunk costs. PRO never answered why that one-tap shift materially changes consent given high cart abandonment and no money moved, while A's padding/cross-subsidy mechanism survived. PRO retained the ticketing example but lost the decisive comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1-A2.3 exposing that B rewrote the motion to 'once calculable' and that this makes the remedy either toothless (same sunk costs, 30 seconds earlier) or harmful, directly severing B1.2's promised browsing benefit.
