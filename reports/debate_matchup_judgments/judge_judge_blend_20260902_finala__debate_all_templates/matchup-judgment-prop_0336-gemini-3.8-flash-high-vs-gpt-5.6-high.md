### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases without fake sourcing, but PRO's throughline was more resilient. PRO's central asymmetry — that an abuser or state needs to unmask a target only once for irreversible physical harm, while a vulnerable user must stay protected perpetually — was pressed consistently from opening through closing and never fully neutralized. CON's main strategy was to defend an idealized "safeguarded" real-name design (public pseudonyms, data minimization, danger-based exemptions), but PRO's repeated catch-22 rebuttal (unvetted exemptions invite abuse; vetted exemptions force vulnerable users to register their danger with a corporate database that states can subpoena or breach) exposed a real structural tension CON never resolved, only softened with "some evasion remains." CON's strongest counter — that platforms already hold identifying metadata regardless of real-name policy, so pseudonymity was never true secrecy — was answered adequately by PRO's distinction between ambiguous metadata and authenticated, court-ready proof. CON's closing correctly noted PRO relies on worst-case framing, but that meta-complaint didn't concretely rebalance the stakes PRO had already established. Both handled the Pressure Questions round competently with proper A1/A2 formatting, but PRO's answers did more direct work reinforcing its core mechanism, while CON's answers mostly reasserted its idealized design without closing the exemption loophole. PRO's rhetorical framing was also sharper and more memorable, aiding clarity of the decisive comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's recurring "exemption catch-22" argument (A2.1, sharpened in A4.1 answering Q1) against CON's safeguarded-design defense (B1.1, B4.1) was the pivotal exchange: CON conceded no architecture perfectly separates endangered from malicious claimants, and that unresolved tension anchored PRO's case through the closing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by forcing the actual comparative burden: expected harm under safeguarded private verification with public pseudonyms and danger waivers versus pervasive disposable-account abuse that also silences and endangers the same vulnerable groups. PRO’s catch-22/honeypot and irreversibility points landed hard but never completed the net-harm weighing or neutralized the claim that anonymous stalking/brigading itself produces physical and exit harms; CON consistently answered pressure without collapsing into “all safeguards guaranteed to fail.”
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s expected-total-harm and non-unique-irreversibility reply (A4.2/A5.1) against PRO’s one-failure asymmetry.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B wins by showing that Side A never completes the required comparative weighing, instead treating tail-risk identification failures as automatically decisive while dismissing accountability benefits as merely marginal. B successfully distinguishes moderate real-name designs—backend verification with public pseudonyms and danger-based exemptions—from reckless public passport mandates, and blunts the honeypot concern by noting platforms already retain extensive metadata. B’s framework of expected total harm, prevalence, and safeguarded defaults creates the cleaner path to victory on the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s catch-22 on exemptions (A3.1) versus Side B’s response (B4.1) that exceptions need not store vulnerability dossiers, paired with A’s sharp distinction between ambiguous metadata and court-ready authenticated identity (A4.3) and B’s counter that verification need not mean permanent centralized archives (B4.3).

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A built and sustained a single decisive frame — that conduct-based tools and identity-verification are both evadable, but exposure of legal identity is irreversible while account-level harassment can be repeatedly remedied — and used it to answer every major B thrust. A also exploited a real tension in B's case: B's opening claim that "no serious whistleblower" relies on mainstream platforms conflicts with the motion's own subject matter and was never repaired, and B's later concession that authoritarian regimes "already trace dissidents through telecom data" undercut B's own justification for removing pseudonymity, a point A converted into the memorable "preassembled record" line that B's closing never directly rebutted. B's rebuttals were substantive (sockpuppet economy, friction costs, harm to harassment victims) but leaned on overstated framing ("PRO's fatal concessions") that mischaracterized A's more careful comparative claims, and B's closing largely reasserted prior points rather than resolving the irreversibility-vs-evadability asymmetry A had put at the center of the debate. Both sides respected the evidence discipline and answered pressure questions in proper A1/A2 format, but A's answers more squarely defused B's questions (acknowledging imperfection symmetrically) while B's answers didn't fully neutralize A's core asymmetry.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3–A4.6), especially the "preassembled record" line and the closing distinction between recoverable moderation failures and irreversible identity exposure, which B's final closing did not directly answer.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the motion’s actual subjects and asymmetric weighing live: irreversible exposure/chilling for dissidents, whistleblowers, and vulnerable speakers versus imperfect, resource-skewed deterrence of abuse. B cleanly separated persistent pseudonymity from disposable anonymity, forced the government-compulsion and state-troll burdens, and crystallized that miss-and-block is not symmetrical with unarrest/unfire/unout. Side A’s disposable-account and swarm-harassment case was forceful and raised real costs of impunity, but it overclaimed collapse-of-industrial-abuse, treated metadata tracking as if it erased pre-linked identity risk, and repeatedly shifted toward general public-order benefits while downgrading the proposition’s named groups.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s post-pressure answers (B4) plus closing weighing: verification is also evadable and sorts against compliant vulnerable speakers, while exposure is irreversible and block-again is not a symmetry.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a decisive asymmetry: failure of conduct moderation is recoverable, while identity exposure is irreversible. It showed real-name policies screen out vulnerable users more reliably than they stop resourceful abusers or state actors, and it effectively deflated CON's claim that metadata surveillance makes platform identity disclosure harmless to dissidents.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 weighing that a returning harasser can be blocked again, but an exposed dissident cannot be unarrested, unfired, or un-outed, decisively crystallized the irreversibility gap.
