### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): The decisive clash was operational: can "explicit, time-limited opt-in" actually function given continuous, multi-app, background-generated location data? CON pressed this mechanism question relentlessly, most sharply by distinguishing episodic cookie consent (PRO's GDPR analogy) from always-on location streams. PRO's answers to the pointed pressure questions on this (B4.1-B4.3) were largely evasive—asserting markets would "adapt" and mirroring "how sensitive categories already operate" without specifying how continuous generation is reconciled with time-limited renewal. CON, by contrast, answered PRO's pressure questions concretely (re-identification liability, audits, purpose limits, private rights of action, and a first-party-vs-secondary-market competition story), giving a workable alternative that addresses the same harms PRO cites without the feasibility problem. PRO's moral framing ("real-time diary," "handing out copies of every key") was rhetorically strong and raised a real ex-ante/ex-post timing concern, but it never squarely rebutted CON's proposed preventive tools (bans on known-harmful sales, audits) as a live substitute. Because the motion's core mechanism (opt-in feasibility) was directly interrogated and CON's answers held up better under pressure while PRO's grew vaguer, CON built the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2's rebuttal in Turn 4 distinguishing episodic cookie consent from continuous location streams, later reinforced when B's Pressure-Question answers (B4.1-B4.3) conceded markets must "adapt" without specifying a workable mechanism—effectively validating CON's collapse/fatigue critique.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that nonconsensual sale creates an upstream privacy and safety injury that liability after misuse cannot fully repair. Side B offered concrete alternatives and a strong platform-concentration objection, but never established that time-limited consent requires constant prompts or that free maps, deliveries, and public analytics depend on brokers selling individual-level location without consent. Its market-collapse framing was therefore overstated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A most effectively answered the workability objection by distinguishing consented, purpose-limited or aggregated analytics from unrestricted sale. Side B continued to assume that time limits mean constant re-prompting, without proving that dichotomy.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): CON won the feasibility / workability clash that became decisive. PRO's GDPR response never solved the continuous vs episodic distinction, and in R2 PRO conceded the scalable secondary market must abandon raw streams, validating CON's collapse prediction. CON meanwhile answered PRO's enforcement-gap pressure with concrete tools – re-identification liability, de-id audits, purpose limits, private right of action – leaving PRO with powerful rights rhetoric about shelters and clinics but a damaged claim that the rule is narrowly targeted and spares useful analytics.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R1 attack on GDPR cookie-banner analogy as episodic vs continuous background location streams, plus R2 specification of re-ident liability and audit fines to close PRO's enforcement-gap claim.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but PRO (B) more successfully defended the central mechanism of the proposition. B consistently distinguished non-consensual broker resale of precise individual trails from first-party consented data and minimized/aggregated data, which directly neutralized CON's strongest recurring claim that a ban would sever emergency-dispatch, disease-modeling, and traffic-safety pipelines. CON's pressure question on enforcement under admitted opacity was sharp, but B answered it with plausible mechanisms (audits, discovery, whistleblowers converting existing lawful-seeming conduct into violations) and turned it into a double-standard charge. Critically, in Rebuttal 2 CON conceded "if opacity defeats the ban's enforceability, it defeats soft tools identically" — a concession that undercuts CON's own signature unenforceability argument and leaves CON relying mainly on the blending/overcompliance claim, which B had already substantially answered. CON's rebuttals were sharp early (the "bright line that cannot be seen" contradiction was a good catch) but the case narrowed and weakened after that self-inflicted concession, while B's framing (default commercial property of physical presence vs. restored agency) stayed consistent and got sharper through the rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question 2 ("if the ecosystem is too opaque for a sale ban, what detection power lets soft tools police the same invisible transfers?") forced CON into conceding that opacity burdens both regimes equally, which neutralized CON's core enforceability differentiation and was never fully recovered from.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by separating non-consensual sales of precise individual trails from first-party services and de-identified aggregates. Side B repeatedly asserted that the ban would sever emergency and modeling pipelines, but never established why those benefits require the prohibited transactions. A also exposed that opacity burdens B's softer regulations just as much as a ban, while explaining how audits, discovery, and whistleblowers can create deterrence despite imperfect detection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to enforceability was decisive: both approaches confront opaque intermediaries, but only prohibition makes discovered non-consensual sales unlawful. Side B's demand for near-perfect detection applied selectively to the ban and did not rescue its preferred transparency and penalty regime.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): PRO kept the clash narrow and decisive: the proposition bans sale of precise individual trails without explicit time-limited opt-in, not aggregated de-identified intelligence or first-party uses. That defused CON's public-safety blackout claim. On enforceability, PRO turned CON's opacity argument into a double standard - soft tools face the same detection problem but leave resale lawful - and supplied plausible mechanisms (audits, discovery, whistleblowers, penalties). CON never resolved that contradiction and relied on speculative over-compliance. Consent-fatigue was answered by comparing it to status-quo dark-pattern fatigue. PRO gave the cleaner comparative reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.2: PRO answers detection with audit/discovery deterrence and argues first-party + minimized aggregates continue, exposing CON's A1/A2 contradiction that opacity defeats ban but somehow enables soft tools, collapsing both enforcement and public-goods attacks.
