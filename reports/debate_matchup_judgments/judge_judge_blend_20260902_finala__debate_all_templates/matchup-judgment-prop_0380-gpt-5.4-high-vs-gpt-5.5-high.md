### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently throughout a genuinely contested exchange, with matching execution costs (Side A clipped in R1, Side B clipped in R2). The decisive margin goes to Side A for a cleaner and more consistently maintained central framing: CON's case was always a theory of full adulthood in general, not of voting capacity in particular, and CON never successfully escaped that characterization. PRO's "democracies already stagger rights because different acts require different capacities" argument was posed early and never adequately answered—CON kept restating civic-status arguments rather than showing why the franchise specifically must attach at 18 rather than 16. CON's sharpest original move—distinguishing structural/legal dependence from contingent adult dependence (B2.2)—was strong and only partially met, and CON's pressure question about excluding 15-year-olds landed real damage on PRO's line-drawing, which PRO answered weakly ("capacity generally present by then" is near-circular). However, CON's own closing anchor—"stable vs. unstable standard"—is itself vulnerable, because CON's 18-threshold is defended by a status argument that is equally arbitrary from a voting-specific perspective. PRO's closing crystallization ("a real electoral incapacity" vs. "symbolism of the adult boundary") was the cleaner comparative frame for the motion as written. The burden structure favored PRO throughout, and CON never translated its legitimate structural-dependence insight into a genuine voting-specific incapacity. A narrow but real PRO victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question Q1 (B3.1)—demanding a principled line that includes every 16-year-old but excludes every 15-year-old—was the sharpest challenge in the debate. PRO's answer (A4.1) was the weakest moment for Side A, relying on "capacity generally present by then" and the concreteness of civic obligations, which CON correctly exploited in B4.4–B4.5 as a rough cognitive guess rather than a principled standard. Yet CON's own 18-threshold faced the mirror challenge from PRO's A3.1—what voting-specific capacity appears at 18?—and CON's answer (B4.1: exit from parental control, binding obligations, adult consequences) was better characterized as a status argument than a voting-specific one. PRO's consistent pressing on this distinction ultimately did more decisive work than CON's line-drawing attack.

PRO's response appealed to a rough empirical guess about when capacity is generally present, which CON capitalized on to show that competence alone cannot ground a universal franchise without sliding downward or becoming the very individualized test democracy rejects.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): PRO built the stronger path to victory through three connected moves. First, the dependence dilemma (A2.4–A2.5 and Q1): if structured dependence disqualifies sixteen-year-olds, why do financially, institutionally, and religiously dependent adults keep the franchise? CON's answer—that legal minority is age-wide and entrenched rather than contingent—is a real distinction, but PRO correctly noted in its closing that democracies respond to dependence by protecting political voice (secret ballot, anti-coercion norms) rather than erasing it. That framing was never squarely answered. Second, PRO's Q2 pressure question forced CON to identify what capacity appears at eighteen and is categorically absent at sixteen. CON's answer—"adult civic independence"—was partly circular: it defines the threshold as crossing out of minority status without specifying what civic act that enables. PRO labeled this loop effectively throughout, including in the closing's "graduation certificate" framing. Third, PRO's closing error comparison was the most decisive crystallization in the debate: CON imposes a certain harm (categorical disenfranchisement of millions with basic political capacity who will live longest under the results), while PRO risks only adding capable citizens to an equal ballot. CON never directly answered that asymmetry. CON's strongest moves—distinguishing legal minority from contingent adult dependence, the "proves too much" attack on PRO's elastic standard, and "coherence and caution beat almost-adult"—landed genuine hits and kept the debate competitive, but they left the core burden unanswered: no concrete democratic harm from inclusion was ever identified. PRO also made better use of the pressure-questions round, asking sharper questions that generated more productive pressure on the live clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's dependence dilemma in Rebuttal 1 (A2.4–A2.5): "If dependence disqualifies sixteen-year-olds categorically, CON owes an explanation for why dependent adults keep full political power while politically aware minors get none." CON answered by distinguishing legal minority (age-wide, entrenched) from contingent adult dependence, which was a real distinction—but PRO's closing correctly noted that the democratic response to dependence in both cases is the same protective mechanism (secret ballot, anti-coercion), not disenfranchisement. CON never resolved that asymmetry.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a remarkably close and high-level debate by decisively winning the clash on "structural dependence." While Side B made a strong case that many adults are also dependent, Side A successfully drew a distinction between the contingent, varied dependence of adults and the legally entrenched, age-wide dependence of minors, effectively neutralizing Side B's counter.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's distinction that adult dependence is varied and contingent, while minor dependence is legally entrenched and age-wide, perfectly answered Side B's pressure question.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): CON defended a coherent principle—full sovereign franchise should attach to the transition out of categorical legal minority—while showing that PRO’s standard of basic understanding plus being affected had no stable floor and that PRO’s school-based argument was administrative convenience rather than democratic membership. CON’s distinction between contingent adult dependence and the age-wide, legally entrenched minority status of sixteen-year-olds blunted PRO’s strongest objection and made the decisive clash clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s distinction between contingent adult dependence and the structured, age-wide legal minority of sixteen-year-olds, which preserved the coherence of tying the franchise to adult civic independence and blunted PRO’s claim that coercion concerns should equally disqualify dependent adults.
