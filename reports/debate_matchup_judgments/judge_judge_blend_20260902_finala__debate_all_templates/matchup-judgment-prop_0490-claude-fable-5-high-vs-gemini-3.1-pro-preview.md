- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): B won by making the debate about the marginal value of worldwide publication over structured expert access plus paper ballots and audits. A gave a solid transparency case and useful replies on deterministic builds and trust, but never fully answered B’s challenge to identify what public release uniquely prevents. B’s patch-window, attack-cost, and disruption-not-outcome framing more cleanly outweighed A’s trust claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered A’s pressure by arguing defenders are not blind under structured review, then separated paper’s protection of outcomes from software-enabled disruption, undercutting A’s claimed contradiction.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by successfully reframing the debate around the motion's distinctive demand: mandatory worldwide publication versus the status quo of structured expert access plus paper-ballot audits. CON sustained a tight burden-of-proof argument showing that open-source publication's marginal benefit was unproven—most devastatingly by pointing out that PRO never named a single concrete attack prevented by public release that controlled review could not catch. Meanwhile, CON's marginal-cost argument (published code serves as a verified blueprint for adversaries during lengthy recertification windows) remained live and largely unanswered. PRO's democratic-trust framing was rhetorically powerful but could not overcome the narrow technical gap in their case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange began with CON's Pressure Question demanding a concrete attack uniquely prevented by worldwide publication (A3.1). PRO's Rebuttal 2 offered only vague categories of "logic bombs and supply-chain attacks" (B4.1), which CON immediately exposed as unsupported assertion rather than mechanism (A4.5). This failure to identify a unique, tangible benefit of the motion's distinctive mandate—combined with CON's sustained demonstration that deterministic builds and forensic audits already work under structured access—left PRO's security case resting on generic "many eyes" theory that Heartbleed had already undermined.

B leverages A's concession on paper ballots to reframe the stakes, then delivers a decisive blow on the election software patching timeline: recertification takes months, meaning public disclosure creates a long, frozen vulnerability window that adversaries can exploit while defenders wait. A's attempt to call a contradiction between paper security and software vulnerability is cleanly resolved by B distinguishing outcome security from operational disruption and public panic. B's comparative weighing, grounded in election administration realities, provides the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 dismantling A's contradiction charge and pressing the unanswered Q1. B cleanly separates outcome security (paper) from disruption risk (frozen tabulation/public panic), explains why the recertification window makes public disclosure uniquely dangerous, and notes that A's proposed mitigations work identically under structured access.
