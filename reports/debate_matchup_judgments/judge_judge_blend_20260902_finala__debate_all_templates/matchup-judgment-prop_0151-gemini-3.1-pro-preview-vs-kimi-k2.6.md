### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): PRO (Side A) built the cleaner path to victory across the full exchange. Three factors decide it. First, PRO turned CON's own "psychologically brutal" concession into a persistent liability that CON never adequately resolved: CON's position reduces to accepting admitted severe harm because the alternative trusts platforms, but PRO correctly noted that CON's own preferred remedy—narrow enforcement of threats and doxxing—also trusts platforms, so the principled distinction collapsed. Second, PRO's pivot to user-controlled toggles (retroactive quote-disabling as a universal setting) was a genuinely responsive move to CON's platform-capture argument; CON's counter in B4.4 ("seatbelt in luxury cars") assumed delayed discovery rather than answering the proposal on its merits. Third, CON's strongest live argument—that a predator call-out and a cruel dogpile generate identical data signatures, so content-neutral friction is impossible—was real but overproved: the same logic would condemn existing narrow enforcement of doxxing and threats, since a threat against an abuser looks like a threat against an innocent person too. CON never closed that loop. On the other side, CON's opening was clipped (execution cost), the closing contained mislabeled reference IDs [C4.1, C4.4] that correspond to no visible content, and the "delay is deletion" line, while rhetorically strong, was never grounded in a concrete account of why a rate-limited viral moment cannot reconstitute itself once the spike passes. PRO's case had its own gaps—volume detection still struggles with the identical-signature problem—but the overall architecture was more coherent and PRO consistently used the exchange to narrow the dispute rather than widen it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive rebuttal exchange was A's use of CON's "psychologically brutal" concession in both A4.6 and A5.3 against CON's position that volume itself must not be treated as a system failure. CON conceded the harm was severe but insisted we must accept it to preserve accountability; PRO crystallized that this standard requires sacrificing independent creators to an engineered stampede to protect the exact corporate engagement machinery CON claimed to distrust. CON's response in B4.1 tried to redirect to platform incentives but never answered why accepting "psychologically brutal" mob volume is morally preferable to imperfect friction—leaving PRO's framing standing at the close.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B wins as it more convincingly showed friction tools like volume-based limits and user toggles can slow dogpiles without deleting valid criticism, directly answered pressure on neutrality and access by clarifying content-neutral mechanics and universal design, and weighed the psychological toll of scale against CON's romanticized accountability while noting megastars' insulation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 answers exposed that circuit breakers use velocity not content and retroactive toggles empower ordinary users, neutralizing CON's corporate-capture and distinction burdens while sharpening the comparison to delay vs. stampede tradeoffs.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully problematizing the feasibility and incentives behind PRO's proposed friction. While PRO offered a compelling moral case against engineered stampedes and argued for content-neutral velocity brakes, CON effectively demonstrated that algorithmic spike detection cannot distinguish legitimate call-outs from harassment without baked-in corporate risk calculus. CON's structural critique—that platforms profiting from outrage will calibrate brakes to protect advertisers and the powerful, not marginalized voices—undermined PRO's reliance on platform implementation. CON also defended the attention-economy reality that quote-posting is often the only way small accounts bypass algorithmic suppression, successfully turning PRO's delay argument. PRO's closing attack on delay was sharp, but CON's consistent pressure on the indistinguishability of viral criticism vs. dogpiles and the misalignment of platform incentives provided the cleaner path to victory. A minor execution cost applies for the clipped opening, but it did not materially damage the core case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's content-neutral velocity brakes [A4.3], arguing that a viral campaign exposing a predator looks identical to a harassment dogpile in the data, and any circuit breaker inevitably substitutes corporate risk-calculus for community judgment. This directly neutralized PRO's main technical solution and shifted the debate to platform incentives, where CON held the decisive advantage.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO built the cleaner comparative case across the full exchange. Three advantages survived the exchange. First, PRO successfully inverted CON's power-dynamic frame: the small creator without a PR team is far more often the target of an organized fanbase dogpile than a whistleblower exposing a mega-creator. CON never dislodged this. Second, PRO's core distinction between the clip itself (untouched) and the mass-coordination machinery (slowed) was never answered squarely. CON conflated the two throughout, which repeatedly weakened the accountability argument. Third, PRO exploited a real burden gap: the motion only requires platforms to "do more," not to design a perfect system, so CON's strongest point—the kill switch, where loyalists could flood a whistleblower's post to trigger automated demotion—is an argument that could be used against any moderation intervention, not just this one. CON never acknowledged that the status quo already harms powerless critics through the same dynamics; PRO did (A4.3), and that asymmetry mattered. CON's kill switch argument (B2.5/B4.2) was the most creative and damaging point in the debate and PRO's answer in A4.2 was partially inconsistent—earlier claiming the algorithm need not judge truth, then implying it can distinguish documentation from hostile flooding—which is a real execution cost. But CON's overall position ultimately counsels no algorithmic intervention whatsoever, an extreme stance the motion does not require PRO to defeat entirely. PRO's framing that platforms already throttle engagement for advertising and copyright (A1.5/A2.3) remained live as a direct analogy CON never neutralized, and the closing crystallization was tighter and more decisive than CON's, which mostly recycled earlier points.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was PRO's Q1 in the pressure round (A3.1) exposing that CON simultaneously claimed massive fanbases "do not need quote-posting to harass" and that these same features are indispensable "equalizers" for the marginalized. CON's A1 in rebuttal 2 attempted to resolve this by distinguishing sheer-numbers dominance from piercing an echo chamber, which was partially persuasive, but it implicitly conceded PRO's structural point that entrenched fanbases can dominate with or without quote-posting—meaning the feature's net effect favors the powerful more than CON's opening suggested.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side A (CON) built the stronger overall case by consistently advancing and defending the exploitability flaw in PRO's friction mechanisms (hostile-reply demotion as a kill switch), which remained live after direct pressure questions and was not squarely neutralized; B's distinction between documentation and mobbing did not address the content-agnostic trigger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal 2 answer on hostile-reply flooding as a platform-sanctioned kill switch for whistleblowers, which exposed an unresolved vulnerability in B's proposed velocity controls.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by decisively exposing a fatal structural flaw in PRO's mechanism and winning the empirical clash over how online accountability actually functions. PRO's proposed remedy—algorithmically throttling posts with overwhelmingly hostile replies—creates a reverse-engineering vulnerability: loyalist armies could intentionally flood a whistleblower's post with hostility to trigger the "speed bump," burying the exposé. PRO's response (claiming algorithms can distinguish "documentation" from "context-stripping" without judging truth) was hand-wavy and contradicted their own insistence on avoiding content arbitration. Additionally, PRO repeatedly leaned on the claim that "credible evidence travels on its own merits," which CON effectively dismantled as naive about the modern attention economy. CON's framing that virality is the "currency of accountability" and that algorithmic friction inherently protects entrenched creators was sharper, better grounded, and consistently defended. PRO's repeated attempts to paint CON's position as contradictory (elite weapon vs. grassroots shield) misread CON's actual comparative claim and grew repetitive. CON adapted better to pressure, landed cleaner structural critiques, and provided a clearer comparative reason to reject the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's identification of the "kill switch" vulnerability (A2.5/A4.2) was the most decisive exchange. By showing how PRO's hostility-based throttling mechanism could be weaponized by bad actors to bury whistleblowers, CON forced PRO into an unsolvable trap: either admit the algorithm must judge content truth (violating PRO's own framework) or accept that the mechanism will inevitably silence the vulnerable it claims to protect. PRO's response in B4.2 failed to resolve this, effectively conceding the structural flaw.
