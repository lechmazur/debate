### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built and sustained the more damaging structural attack: that PRO's position is internally contradictory — if offsets are physically incapable of neutralizing permanent emissions, permitting even a "small share" licenses the very fraud PRO condemns, but if offsets can work (as PRO concedes for hard-to-abate sectors), then the correct regulatory lever is quality/additionality/permanence standards, not an arbitrary quantity ratio that "no climate science has ever derived." CON pressed this via a sharp pressure question ("what makes a small share scientifically valid rather than politically arbitrary?") and PRO's answer (A1: reserving offsets for residual hard-to-abate emissions) never actually explained why a fixed percentage cap, rather than a needs-based or quality-based test, is the right mechanism — PRO instead fell back on asserting that voluntary standards have failed, which answers a different question. CON's closing crystallized this gap cleanly ("atmospheric physics has never produced a percentage"), and PRO's closing restated the physics/moral-hazard framing without closing that hole. PRO's case was rhetorically strong and grounded in plausible physical/ecological reasoning (permanence, land-use limits, lock-in), but several of its causal claims (e.g., "infrastructure lock-in," "arithmetic collapses") were flagged by CON as unsupported assertion and were mostly reasserted rather than defended with new mechanism in rebuttal 2. Both sides complied with Q/A formatting, but CON's questions extracted more real concession-adjacent tension than PRO's did.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Question Q1 in Turn 6 ("If temporary offsets can never physically neutralize permanent fossil emissions... why does PRO permit them for any share at all?") forced PRO into an answer that conceded offsets are legitimate for residual hard-to-abate emissions without ever justifying why a fixed percentage cap, rather than quality-based governance, is the correct mechanism — a tension CON exploited through the rest of the debate and PRO never resolved.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built the cleaner path to victory by forcing PRO onto an internal contradiction it never resolved: if offsets cannot physically neutralize permanent fossil emissions, a “small share” merely rations fraud; if some offsets are legitimate residuals, quality/additionality/permanence—not an undervable legal ratio—is the governing lever. A’s price-signal and climate-finance answers also undercut PRO’s lock-in and outsourcing story more effectively than PRO’s integrity framing answered the arbitrariness charge. PRO kept a strong atmospheric and market-integrity case live, but never derived a scientific basis for the specific limit after conceding residual use, so the decisive clash favored A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s sustained contradiction press (esp. A2.1 / A4.3–A5.2): PRO’s own permanence critique collapses the “small share” carve-out into either arbitrary fraud-rationing or a quality problem a quantity cap cannot solve.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): CON successfully exposed a central tension in PRO's case: if offsets are inherently non-permanent and fraudulent, allowing even a "small share" merely rations the fraud; if they are legitimate, a crude quantity cap arbitrarily punishes high-quality tons without improving quality. PRO never derived a scientific basis for where the "small share" line falls, leaving the core mechanism of the proposition looking politically arbitrary. While PRO landed strong points about aggregate ecological limits and moral hazard, CON's "quality not quantity" framing, the rationing-fraud dilemma, and the demonstration that a cap would strangle climate finance to the developing world created a cleaner path to victory. CON's rebuttals did more material damage to PRO's live routes than vice versa.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's first rebuttal (B2.1) introduced the decisive "rationing fraud" dilemma: if offsets are as ecologically fraudulent as PRO claims, capping them does not fix the problem; it merely rations it. This framed the rest of the debate and forced PRO into an unresolved contradiction that CON exploited through the Pressure Questions and second rebuttal.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Both sides opened strong, but the decisive clash was B's funding-versus-counting distinction: the proposition restricts what a company may *claim* as net-zero, not what it may *fund*. A never fully dismantled this distinction, repeatedly asserting that stripping "recognition" would cause capital to vanish, without engaging why real climate finance would require the net-zero label specifically rather than other incentives (government procurement, reputational credit for verified funding, etc.). B's pressure questions were sharper and more clearly exposed tensions in A's case (the branding-dynamic question and the leakage-still-happens-with-offsets question), and B's answers in Rebuttal 2 directly addressed them with the funding/counting frame. A's leakage and DAC-funding arguments were reasonable but largely repeated across rounds rather than evolving, and A's closing attempt to recast B's "claims should track reality" point as a confession of pure "corporate purity" bordered on an evasive reframe rather than a genuine rebuttal. B's closing more cleanly crystallized the actual clash (decarbonization-first ordering vs. unrestricted counting) and left the debate easier to resolve in its favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question Q2 (offshoring/leakage happens regardless of offset regime) combined with B's Rebuttal 2 answer distinguishing "funding" from "counting" offsets was the most decisive exchange, since A's response largely restated its relief-valve claim without resolving why unlimited offset counting specifically prevents leakage.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the decisive clash clean: net-zero claims should mean decarbonization first, with offsets as a residual complement, not a substitute. The funding-versus-counting distinction repeatedly undercut Side A’s capital-flight and DAC-starvation scenarios, and B’s answers to pressure were direct and format-compliant. Side A’s atmospheric-physics and leakage points were real, but often overstated the motion as a ban on investment rather than a limit on what may be counted, so A never fully neutralized B’s branding/integrity route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 1 (esp. B2.1–B2.2/B2.5): pledges that collapse once cheap offsets are limited were branding, not climate action; capital can still fund sinks without counting as the firm’s own reductions.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A established that unlimited offsets undermine the integrity of net-zero claims due to persistent additionality and permanence failures, and that limiting offsets forces real value-chain decarbonization while preserving a small share for hard-to-abate sectors. Side B raised credible concerns about capital flight and technology scaling but never overcame A's decisive distinction between funding offsets and counting them toward net-zero, nor did B refute A's core point that if offset demand depends entirely on greenwashing, its loss is not a climate cost. A's rebuttals were sharper and more adaptive, progressively narrowing the clash to the definitional integrity of net-zero, which B treated as irrelevant "corporate purity" without disarming A's causal story.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's sustained attack on offset integrity and the funding-versus-counting distinction, especially in Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2, which neutralized Side B's capital-flight scenario and exposed its conflation of commercial incentives with climate outcomes.
