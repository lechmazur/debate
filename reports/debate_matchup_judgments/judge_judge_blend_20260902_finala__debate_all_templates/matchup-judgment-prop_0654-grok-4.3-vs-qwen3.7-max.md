### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON built the stronger cumulative case by maintaining a single decisive pressure—the word "all"—consistently across every round while landing two clean turns that PRO never recovered from. The "lost the plot" reframe of PRO's forensics argument (A2.6) was sharp and rhetorically effective. More decisively, CON's A4.5 caught PRO in an internal inconsistency: PRO's own concession that low-risk structures can use "batched or event-triggered uploads" contradicts the continuous-monitoring claim that anchored PRO's opening, reducing the mandate to an expensive alarm system that still requires a physical follow-up visit. CON also introduced the "fails silently vs. legally mandated paper trail" distinction (A4.1), which PRO's closing never addressed. PRO's genuine live argument—that early detection converts reactive repairs into planned interventions—was real and was never fully neutralized by CON, which is why the margin is not larger. But PRO compounded structural weaknesses: the opening was 150 words short of cap, leaving the core case underdeveloped and vulnerable to CON's lifecycle-cost attack; the closing (143 of 220 words) failed to answer CON's silent-failure point or reassert the safety gain. PRO's Q1 pressure on inspection reliability was the strongest moment for that side, and CON's answer—legal mandate, strict liability, paper trail—was adequate if not decisive. Overall, CON ran a more disciplined, adaptive debate; PRO ran a thinner, less responsive one.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.5: after PRO conceded that low-risk structures could use "event-triggered uploads," CON exposed the internal contradiction—if uploads are event-triggered rather than continuous, PRO's signature claim of unbroken monitoring collapses into an expensive alarm system that still requires a physical inspector to verify, meaning the mandate effectively mandates both the sensor cost and the inspection cost simultaneously. This was the most damaging single exchange because it used PRO's own words to undermine PRO's core efficiency argument.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side A won by making the universality of the mandate decisive. B gave a plausible safety and efficiency case for sensors on important new infrastructure, but repeatedly relied on asserted low marginal costs and frictionless operations. A more convincingly showed that “all” includes low-risk and remote assets where lifecycle maintenance, telemetry, staffing, false positives, and sensor failure can overwhelm the benefit. B’s answers narrowed the monitoring promise rather than resolving the cost and operational burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest exchange was turning B’s “batched or event-triggered uploads” answer: A argued this undercut B’s original continuous-monitoring advantage while still imposing sensor-network costs and requiring manual verification.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): CON won by exposing the fatal overbreadth of requiring sensors on "all" new assets. They demonstrated that a blanket mandate imposes crippling lifecycle costs—telemetry, cybersecurity, replacement, and specialist labor—on trivial rural structures, while PRO could not squarely justify universal application. CON also turned PRO's concession that low-risk assets could use batched uploads into proof that the mandate forces payment for both a degraded sensor system and continued manual inspections, undermining the core efficiency claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal was decisive: it showed that legally mandated inspections leave a paper trail while sensors fail silently, and it exploited PRO's batched-upload concession to collapse the continuous-monitoring rationale, proving the "all" mandate creates redundant unfunded liabilities rather than net savings.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): PRO won on the decisive, unanswered clash: irreversibility of construction. The "concrete cures" argument — that embedding sensors must happen before the pour or never at all efficiently — was introduced early and carried through the closing, and CON never squarely neutralized it. CON's strongest sustained argument (low-hazard assets diluting resources) was partially answered when PRO noted the mandate covers only new construction (a separate budget stream from retrofits) and that grid nodes cascade. PRO's responses to pressure questions were sharper: the edge-computing / automated-alert rebuttal materially damaged CON's "data overload" claim, and CON's counter (verification overhead still exists) was too thin. CON also never answered PRO's pressure question B3.2 cleanly — arguing agencies "raise inspection frequency when early warning signs appear" is circular if sensors are the mechanism that reveals those signs earlier than visual inspection. CON's recurring-cost argument was real but left underpowered because it never attached a credible number or comparative weight against a catastrophic failure event. PRO's analogies (aviation dashboard, flashlight in a dark room) reinforced rather than substituted for the argumentative work. CON's case was competent and the low-hazard resource-diversion point remained a live concern, but CON's rebuttals mostly reiterated the opening's themes rather than escalating pressure on PRO's live routes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B2.5 false-dichotomy rebuttal — sensors do triage, inspectors apply contextual judgment where flagged — directly answered CON's flexibility concern and reframed the whole debate. It turned CON's strongest non-cost argument into a reason to support the mandate, and CON never recovered a clean response to it.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side B won by making the safety and timing clash clearer: periodic inspections leave real temporal and internal-detection gaps, while sensors can triage human inspection rather than replace it. Side A’s best argument was overbreadth and recurring cost, especially for low-risk assets, but B answered with the marginal new-construction window, redundancy, automated alerts, climate/load uncertainty, and the irreversibility of building without sensors. A’s replies on updated inspections and visual trends did not fully solve the core blind-spot problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was reframing A’s “human flexibility” objection as a false dichotomy: sensors do not eliminate inspectors, they direct them to anomalies that periodic manual checks may miss.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): A won by establishing that embedded sensors during new construction represent an irreversible decision point—once concrete cures, the opportunity is lost forever—while B's "flexibility" argument collapsed because forgoing sensors permanently locks infrastructure into reactive manual inspection. A effectively neutralized B's cost and data-overload concerns with modern edge-computing and triage analogies, and B never recovered from the temporal-gap and irreversibility framing. A's progression and closing crystallization were superior.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal to B's claim that failed sensors require major structural work, turning it by explaining redundant overlapping arrays and noting that a degraded sensor simply reverts that node to the manual-inspection status quo without compromising structural integrity, which defanged CON's strongest technical objection and set up the decisive irreversibility frame in the closing.
