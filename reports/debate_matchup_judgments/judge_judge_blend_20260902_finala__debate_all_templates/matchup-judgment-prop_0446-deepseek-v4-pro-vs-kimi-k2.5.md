### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Its decisive move was consistently turning CON's own warnings back: if states defect during crises, that is precisely why voluntary pledges have already failed and why binding obligations with trade-linked costs—not diffuse climate shame—are needed to change the calculus. The distinction between unconditional transfers (the $100bn pledge) and treaty-conditioned finance linked to carbon border adjustment revenues answered CON's chronic-underfunding attack more specifically than CON acknowledged. CON's strongest moment was "flexible pathways localize failure; a common timeline synchronizes it," a genuinely sharp comparative line, but the underlying alternative—carbon pricing plus technology sharing plus grid-readiness milestones—is substantially the current status quo, which PRO correctly identified as already delivering decades of delay. CON's sovereignty argument was powerful in isolation but internally strained: it conceded some treaties work (ozone, trade) while claiming coal is uniquely unsurvivable, without adequately explaining why trade penalties on a coal-using state facing blackouts are categorically weaker than trade penalties in any other treaty context. PRO's answers to the pressure questions (the common timeline as the "spine" that gives differentiation urgency; escrow from carbon border adjustment revenues) were more concrete than CON's rejoinders. Execution costs weigh on both sides: PRO was clipped four times, losing unknown tail arguments; CON invoked non-existent reference IDs [C3.2, C4.2] in its closing. These roughly offset, leaving PRO ahead on substantive argument strength by a clear but competitive margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive rebuttal exchange was PRO's response to CON's sovereignty-and-blackouts argument: PRO turned the warning around, noting that grid fragility and funding gaps materialize precisely under the voluntary status quo because no one invests until the crunch arrives, whereas a binding deadline with trade-linked enforcement unlocks the storage and transmission investment that prevents the crisis CON fears. CON never fully answered why trade penalties for non-compliance would be politically survivable for other treaty types but uniquely impossible for coal.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won by maintaining a relentless focus on the physical and political realities of energy security, effectively arguing that sovereign states will always choose "electrons over obligations" during a crisis. Side B also suffered from being clipped at the end of every speech due to length.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in Rebuttal 1 that legal deadlines simply evaporate during energy crises (using Germany's coal restarts as proof), demonstrating that "binding" timelines are an illusion when faced with the threat of blackouts.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B won by successfully turning Side A's core objections—sovereignty constraints, energy security risks, and chronic climate finance failures—into indictments of the flexible, voluntary status quo that Side A defended. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger cumulative case across the debate. Its core framing—thirty years of voluntary pledges have delivered rising emissions, not stability—was never squarely answered by CON, whose counter-argument ("Paris is better") implicitly concedes that voluntary frameworks also fall short. PRO's investment-certainty argument (binding signal redirects capital away from stranded coal assets) remained live throughout. The most decisive exchange came in B4.3, where PRO exposed a real internal tension in CON's position: CON simultaneously dismissed differentiated support as "ink on paper" while warning that premature shutdowns would destabilize grids—yet it is precisely the finance mechanism CON dismissed that would prevent those shutdowns. CON's answer in A4.1 was coherent (if finance fails and the deadline stands, closures happen without replacement capacity), but it required accepting a maximally pessimistic and self-defeating view of the treaty's own compliance dynamics. PRO's rebuttal that developing-nation pacing adjusts if support falters, while the binding terminal date still eliminates permanent regulatory havens, was adequate if not airtight. CON's enforcement paradox argument (treaty market-access sanctions = trade wars) had genuine bite, but PRO's distinction between reciprocal ex-ante conditionality and unilateral tariff imposition gave a reasonable structural answer, and CON's own alternative (carbon border adjustments) suffered from the same trade-war objection CON leveled at the treaty. The clipping of CON's first rebuttal was a real execution cost. CON's closing "fatal dilemma" framing was sharp, but it recycled arguments PRO had already partially answered rather than crystallizing a clean new path to victory. PRO's closing maintained the cleaner comparative case: voluntary chaos guarantees neither finance nor coordination, while even an imperfect binding framework anchors investment certainty and eliminates the race to the bottom. Both sides used grounded, plausible reasoning without fake precision. PRO edges out on argument strength, rebuttal directness, and overall framing clarity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B4.3 pivot: CON cannot simultaneously warn that the treaty forces destabilizing premature shutdowns on developing economies and dismiss the differentiated-finance mechanism as mere "ink on paper," since it is precisely that finance that prevents the blackouts CON fears. This heads-I-win-tails-you-lose charge was the sharpest single moment in the debate and CON's response, while internally consistent, required accepting a view of treaty dynamics so pessimistic it undermined CON's own enforcement arguments.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO)): Side A won by effectively grounding their arguments in the political economy of climate diplomacy, consistently showing why a binding agreement would fracture. Side A decisively won the clash over 'differentiated support' by pointing out that treaties cannot manufacture domestic budget authority, turning the binding deadline into a trap that forces blackouts or trade wars.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 expertly dismantled Side B's attempt to point out a contradiction, explaining that blackouts are the direct result of a rigid terminal deadline colliding with the historical reality of unfulfilled climate finance.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully framing the binding agreement as a political-economy trap. While Side B presented a coherent theoretical model for solving the collective action problem, Side A’s rebuttals effectively exposed a fatal dilemma: unreliable climate finance combined with rigid deadlines guarantees either grid destabilization or treaty-violating backlash, while multilateral enforcement mechanisms risk escalatory trade wars that unilateral measures avoid. Side A’s direct, mechanically grounded answers to pressure questions and sharper weighing of political feasibility over institutional design gave it the cleaner path to victory.
