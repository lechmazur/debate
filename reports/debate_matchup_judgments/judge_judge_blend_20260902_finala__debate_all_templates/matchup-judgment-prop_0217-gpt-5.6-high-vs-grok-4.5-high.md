### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and executed the pressure-question format cleanly, but PRO (B) handled the decisive clash points more effectively. CON's (A) central "wealthy exit" and "certainty" arguments were repeated across rounds but were never fully insulated from PRO's key counter: that CON's model of restrictions implicitly assumes near-perfect compliance and enforcement while holding pricing to a standard of zero imperfection. PRO explicitly named and pressed this asymmetry in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, and CON never squarely answered it, instead reasserting that restrictions "close the exit for everyone at once" without engaging the compliance/enforcement realism PRO raised. PRO also did more comparative work on administrative complexity, turning CON's "eligibility gauntlet" charge back by noting restrictions need the same hardship judgments plus intrusive monitoring of use-by-use legitimacy. CON's equity distinction between "rare/temporary" exemptions and "continuous means-testing" was asserted but not well substantiated. CON's strongest and most consistent point—the timing/exit problem where payment doesn't immediately reduce draw—was answered adequately (if not perfectly) by PRO's escalating-trigger and backup-cap mechanism, converting it into a shared uncertainty rather than a unique flaw of pricing. B's opening being clipped is a minor execution cost, but it did not prevent B from delivering the core case and outperforming in the later, decisive rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.5): "CON's own false standard... treats any wealthy user who keeps consuming as fatal to pricing, while assuming every restriction is obeyed perfectly" — this exposed an unaddressed inconsistency in CON's argument that persisted through Closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully defending the efficiency and adaptability of pricing while effectively neutralizing Side B's strongest attacks regarding certainty and equity. Side A consistently pressed the unaddressed harms of blunt bans (e.g., destroying valuable business output while missing indoor leaks) and smartly leveraged the word 'mainly' in the prompt to retain targeted caps as a backstop, resolving Side B's concerns about reservoirs being drained by the wealthy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (A4) systematically dismantled Side B's 'eligibility gauntlet' and 'wealthy exit' arguments by pointing out that both systems require administrative exemptions, but restrictions require ongoing intrusive spot enforcement and assume perfect compliance.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully framing the core tradeoffs as real-time physical certainty versus lagging market signals, and universal civic duty versus intrusive means-testing. Side A's rebuttal that tiered pricing requires a continuous "eligibility gauntlet" to protect large and medical households from steep baseline prices was highly effective; Side B merely asserted it was less intrusive than enforcing restrictions without neutralizing the administrative burden. Side A also capitalized on the "wealthy exit" by pointing out that if heavy users simply pay the higher tiers, the physical water is still consumed in real-time, undermining PRO's claim of guaranteed conservation. Side B's clipped opening was a minor execution cost, but A's superior clash resolution on equity and real-time efficacy secured the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A2), which sharply distinguished between rare, temporary hardship exemptions under universal restrictions and the continuous, intrusive means-testing required to establish baseline allowances under tiered pricing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won on the strength of a distinction that PRO never fully neutralized: universal restrictions can be conduct-based (banning specific discretionary uses like daytime irrigation or pool refilling) rather than volume-based, which sidesteps the family-size/equity problem PRO relied on as its central attack. CON's "payment is permission; restriction is a limit" framing sharpened the core stakes and exposed that pricing only predicts conservation after the fact, while restrictions can directly withdraw legal entitlement to low-priority uses. PRO's rebuttals (city averages, "observed drought responses," escalating tiers) leaned on plausible but unverified specifics and never squarely answered the conduct-vs-volume reframe, instead repeating that exemptions "mirror" restriction exemptions without engaging the substance of that distinction. PRO's opening was also clipped for length, costing it a clean concluding thesis. CON's closing crystallized the clash more cleanly than PRO's, which largely repeated earlier framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 point (A2.2-A2.3) that restrictions can classify the use itself (banning categories like ornamental irrigation) rather than needing to classify occupancy or need, contrasted with pricing's blindness to purpose — a distinction PRO never adequately rebutted in later rounds.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively dismantling Side A's claims about administrative simplicity and reliability. Side B successfully argued that conduct-based restrictions avoid the need to verify family size, whereas tiered pricing requires classifying every household's occupancy to avoid punishing large families. Side B also effectively neutralized the 'trucking' argument by pointing out that private trucking doesn't drain municipal water.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Turn 8, where Side B distinguished volumetric bureaucracy from conduct bans and neutralized the trucking argument by noting it spares the municipal reservoir.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B won the central comparative clash. A argued prices target waste and self-enforce via meter, but B showed volume != purpose, that a generous lifeline either misclassifies legitimate high-need households or requires the same individualized verification it claims to avoid, and that revenue is not conservation. B's framing that payment is permission while a ban withdraws municipal entitlement - so trucking does not drain the reservoir under restrictions but does under pricing - gave the decisive edge on reliability and fairness for recurring shortages.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's purpose-vs-volume and permission-vs-limit response across R1 and R2, especially that conduct bans don't need occupancy data while tiered pricing must classify every account, and that raising tiers after the fact cannot restore released water.
