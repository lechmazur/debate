### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by separating non-revocable ownership from unauthorized duplication. Its bounded, DRM-mediated transfer model preserved scarcity while protecting paid copies after shutdown. Side B repeatedly asserted that ownership creates infinite copying but never established why piracy or DRM cracking is uniquely caused by title rather than already possible under licenses. It also invoked perpetual hosting costs against A's proposal for locally retained offline copies, leaving its central economic objection underexplained.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange was its answer that one-time, verified transfers can preserve one-copy scarcity under the same technical controls platforms already use. Side B responded with DRM cracking and private copying, but did not show that those risks arise specifically from ownership rights rather than from digital files generally.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's reliance on DRM as a substitute for licenses. A successfully argued that without the live check of a revocable license, "permanent offline copies" inevitably lead to cracked DRM, endless free supply, and market collapse. B's demand for both post-shutdown offline permanence and platform-authorized resale created a practical contradiction that A effectively exploited.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing exposed the flaw in B's answers, noting that "platform-authorized" resale and transfer keys fail to prevent infinite free replication once files are permanently offline and the platform loses its live reach.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully decoupling DRM (copy prevention) from revocability (remote deletion). Side A convincingly argued that platforms can maintain scarcity through transfer keys and DRM without retaining the unilateral right to erase a consumer's purchased library. Side B's concession that consumers simply lose their purchases when servers shut down, justified only by abstract economic efficiency, was a significant rhetorical and substantive weakness that Side A effectively exploited.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first and second rebuttals, where it sharply distinguished between technical copy-prevention (DRM) and the platform's extra power to revoke access, dismantling Side B's core premise that ownership inherently destroys digital scarcity.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by separating ownership of a durable copy from any obligation to provide perpetual hosting, patches, or cloud services. It also answered the copying objection by distinguishing lawful transfer from piracy. Side B identified real digital-resale and compatibility problems, but repeatedly assumed that ownership necessarily permits unlimited duplication and that revocation is the only possible incentive for support. Those assumptions were not established, especially across books, music, films, and games alike. A therefore retained the clearer consumer-protection case while accommodating optional paid services.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest response was that meaningful ownership need not impose perpetual platform costs: consumers can keep an offline copy while paying separately for future cloud access or updates. This directly undercut Side B’s main cost argument and exposed its reliance on conflating ownership with guaranteed lifetime support.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully dismantling Side A's two main pillars (infinite piracy and abandoned updates). Side B provided plausible mechanisms for digital friction (transfer protocols, watermarking) and effectively pointed out that the status quo already suffers from the delisted catalogs Side A warned about.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal directly answered the threat of infinite duplication by introducing transfer protocols and watermarks, effectively neutralizing Side A's primary impact.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A successfully decoupled digital ownership from perpetual server maintenance, neutralizing Side B's primary economic argument. Side A also effectively used the status quo's flaws (delisted games, purged libraries) to demonstrate that Side B's feared market contractions are already happening under revocable licenses, making Side A's comparative case much stronger and more grounded in current consumer realities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in Rebuttal 2 pointing out that digital piracy already tests Side B's predictions about frictionless copying without collapsing the market, yet revocable licenses still result in delisted and bricked products, effectively collapsing Side B's slippery slope argument.
