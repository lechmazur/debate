### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B won by successfully dismantling Side A's core Pigouvian analogy. While Side A had excellent rhetoric and a strong framing around localized harms, Side B logically proved that unlike pollution (where emissions and output are separable), labor displacement is the actual engine of the productivity gain. This forced Side A into an unresolvable contradiction where steering behavior inherently means penalizing efficiency.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 argument that the carbon tax analogy fails because emissions are separable from output, whereas substitution and augmentation are not, destroying Side A's "equally productive but cleaner" framework.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing a structural tension in PRO's case: the tax must either deter efficient automation or fail as a corrective price. PRO's scaling trigger and Pigouvian defenses were adaptive but never overcame the perverse incentive problem or showed augmentation is comparably efficient to substitution. CON's alternative of broad taxation plus transfers remained cleaner and less distortionary after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2 exposure that PRO's tax cannot simultaneously steer firms toward augmentation and leave the automation surplus intact, sharpened in A4.4 by showing the scaling trigger penalizes more efficient mixed-automation projects.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by exposing a fatal contradiction in Side B's Pigouvian mechanism: if the tax is high enough to steer behavior, it deters efficient automation (since automation itself is the productivity gain, unlike pollution); if it is too low, it fails to steer. Side A also effectively dismantles the pollution analogy by showing that emissions and output are separable, whereas substitution and augmentation often occur in the same project, making a clean harm metric impossible without creating perverse incentives to hoard suboptimal labor. Side B's local-vs-national revenue point was rhetorically strong in the closing, but Side A's reliance on broad federal taxation to capture national gains was a cleaner economic answer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2 dismantling the Pigouvian contradiction and the pollution analogy, specifically proving that substitution and augmentation are not separable like carbon emissions, rendering the scaling trigger a penalty on efficient automation rather than a clean price on harm.
