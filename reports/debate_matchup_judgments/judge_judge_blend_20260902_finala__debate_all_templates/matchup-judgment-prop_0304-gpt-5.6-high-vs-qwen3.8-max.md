### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently within the closed-book constraints, but PRO built the more decisive comparative frame. PRO's core move—separating institutional-level distribution (who mostly gets seats and prestige) from individual-level transformation (what happens to the rare admitted outsider)—directly targeted the motion's "more than" comparison, and its pressure question forced CON to justify why marginal/counterfactual gains to a minority should outweigh aggregate benefits flowing to the already-advantaged majority. CON's answer (a counterfactual "substitution vs. addition" metric) was a reasonable reply but was never quantified or weighed against PRO's aggregate framing beyond repeated assertion, and PRO's closing correctly flagged this as "asserted, not demonstrated" without a strong CON rebuttal to that specific charge. CON's strongest original contribution—the political-consequences argument that labeling universities as "inheritance" institutions could alienate cooperation needed for reform—was met adequately by PRO (conditions on funding work better than flattering presumptions), leaving that exchange roughly a wash rather than a CON win. PRO also showed more escalation across rounds (unit-of-analysis critique, laundering mechanism, redundancy-of-privilege point in closing) while CON's rebuttals largely recycled the same substitution/additive distinction. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions format correctly with labeled A1/A2 answers, so no execution penalty there, though B's clipped opening is a minor cost. Overall PRO made the debate easier to decide by keeping the clash tied tightly to the motion's comparative language.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question ("why count gains to low-income entrants without weighing the more numerous already-advantaged entrants?") and CON's counterfactual-metric answer, followed by PRO's closing charge that the asymmetry was "asserted, not demonstrated," was the pivotal exchange that exposed CON's unresolved weighting problem.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side A won by successfully keeping the debate focused on the institution's systemic function rather than individual success stories. Side A's argument that elite universities "launder" inherited privilege into legitimate merit decisively neutralized Side B's claim that the credentials are merely "additive" for the wealthy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal explaining that a system of inheritance can be redundant, and that the university's distinct role is converting unequal preparation into an authoritative verdict of merit, brilliantly dismantled Side B's marginal-utility metric.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON won by reframing the empirical test from raw seat counts to marginal counterfactual impact on intergenerational closure. The additive-versus-substitutive distinction (A4.1) showed that for the already-advantaged the credential often ratifies status achievable through other means, while for low-income students it uniquely substitutes for inherited wealth and networks. This blunted PRO’s laundering argument. CON also outpaced PRO on the policy-framing clash, explaining with concrete political-economy logic (A2.1, A4.3) that treating elite universities as presumptively illegitimate risks alienating the very institutions whose cooperation is needed to broaden access. PRO’s clipped opening left its policy prescription unfinished, and its answer to the weighing challenge in B4.1 remained vague, failing to fully justify why institutional reproduction outweighs the demonstrated mobility effects.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 answer A1 (A4.1), which introduced the additive/substitutive distinction to rebut PRO’s institutional-weighing challenge. By arguing that elite credentials ratify existing advantage for the wealthy but create irreplaceable access for the poor, CON turned the seat-count objection into evidence that the gateway is valuable precisely because it is scarce, and shifted the burden back to PRO to show that demoting these institutions would actually reduce class closure.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): The debate turned on whether the correct test is a counterfactual comparison (marginal causal effect of admission) or an aggregate "what function does the institution certify" test. Side A (CON) consistently pressed the counterfactual framing from the opening and used it to expose a genuine weakness in Side B's case: PRO's "certification/laundering" mechanism, even if true for legacy and donor-linked admits, was never shown to outweigh the substitution effect for disadvantaged admits in net terms. PRO's strongest move—the "captured road" analogy and the policy-burden reversal argument—was clever, but CON directly turned it in Rebuttal 2 and Closing by noting the analogy concedes the road (the university) enables movement and that capture (unequal access) is the actual target, not the institution's function itself. CON's answer to Q2 in Pressure Questions (broadening access concedes value as mobility infrastructure) was also never fully neutralized by PRO's response, which reasserted the inheritance-presumption framing rhetorically but didn't answer why the same reform agenda wouldn't equally follow from CON's "underperforming mobility engine" framing. Both sides had strong closings, but CON's final speech more precisely named the logical gap in PRO's certification metaphor (retention vs. production) and kept returning to the decisive comparative question the motion demands ("more than promotes mobility"), which PRO's aggregate-certification framing asserted more than proved. PRO's case was rhetorically vivid and consistent, but relied on repeated assertions that certification network effects dominate net outcomes without directly rebutting CON's marginal-effect asymmetry argument beyond restating it. This gives CON a clear, if not overwhelming, edge on argument quality and rebuttal precision, despite a word-limit clipping in Turn 2 that cost it some execution credit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turn on the "captured road" analogy (A4.6), which directly flipped PRO's central metaphor into evidence for reform rather than presumption of inheritance, most decisively reframed the comparative question in CON's favor.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B won by establishing a rigorous causal framework (marginal impact versus headcounts) that successfully neutralized Side A's core claims. Side B convincingly argued that elite universities duplicate advantage for the wealthy while providing novel, substitute opportunity for the disadvantaged, meaning their net causal effect is mobility. Side B also effectively turned Side A's "captured road" analogy to prove that the universities fundamentally act as mobility infrastructure that simply needs reforming.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 effectively turning A's "captured road" analogy into proof that universities are mobility mechanisms, while also separating A's "meritocratic gloss" from material causal function.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON won by forcing the comparative burden required by "more than" and showing PRO never met it. CON's counterfactual framework—asking what opportunities students would have without elite universities—demonstrated that these institutions often duplicate existing advantages for the wealthy while substituting for missing family capital for the disadvantaged. CON successfully distinguished certifying privilege from causing entrenchment ("retention is not production"), neutralizing PRO's evocative "laundering inheritance into merit" claim. CON also showed that PRO's preferred reforms are fully compatible with treating elite universities as underperforming mobility infrastructure rather than presumptive inheritance vehicles, making PRO's policy framing unnecessary and potentially harmful. PRO's certification and legitimation arguments were rhetorically sharp but remained underspecified in ways that allowed CON to treat them as narrative gloss rather than dominant material function.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (A4), which answered PRO's pressure questions by arguing that retention of class position is not production of hierarchy, that marginal effects are asymmetric in favor of mobility, and that PRO's own "captured road" analogy implicitly conceded the university's core mobility function.
