### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Both sides ran coherent, well-structured cases, but CON (Side A) accumulated a narrow but real advantage across the later rounds. CON's decisive move was the A1 answer in Rebuttal 2: distinguishing a cross-examinable human complaint from a certified machine log that enters proceedings with scientific authority adjudicators cannot effectively contest. This directly answered PRO's pressure question while identifying a practical asymmetry PRO never resolved. CON also consistently pressed the "device control" asymmetry—accused tenants do not choose, pay for, or calibrate the monitor—which PRO attempted to neutralize only by asserting that "affordable certified meters" are accessible to any tenant. That response was underdeveloped and ungrounded; it was asserted rather than argued. PRO's strongest live route—that the current he-said/she-said system already disadvantages the powerless—was real and genuinely pressured CON, but CON's answer (subjective testimony is at least contestable; a certified number is treated as dispositive in overburdened courts) was the more empirically grounded practical claim. PRO's privacy rebuttal was actually its strongest individual exchange: measuring sound entering your own home from next door is a hard thing to characterize as invasive surveillance, and CON's "mapping domestic patterns" counter was comparatively thin. But the power-asymmetry and burden-shift threads ran more consistently through CON's case, and CON's closing crystallized the clash more cleanly with memorable framing ("commodification of proof," "when justice requires a gadget"). PRO's closing was competent but slightly more repetitive. Neither side introduced fake citations or brittle precision, both formats were correct, and both pressure-question rounds were sharp and on-point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A1 in Rebuttal 2 (A4.1): drawing a sharp distinction between cross-examinable human testimony and a certified log that enters the record with unchallengeable scientific authority. This directly answered PRO's Q1—"how is objective data worse than a subjective accusation?"—by identifying the qualitative difference in contestability, not just the presence or absence of data. PRO never found an adequate response to the practical reality that overburdened housing adjudicators treat a timestamped graph as dispositive rather than supplementary.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): A won narrowly but clearly because it kept the motion framed as admitting certified noise data as an evidentiary baseline, not making it dispositive. B’s strongest claim was that certified devices would be controlled by wealthier accusers and overtrusted by housing adjudicators, but A effectively pressed that the current he-said/she-said system is also power-skewed and often leaves both victims and falsely accused tenants without objective recourse. B’s privacy argument was rhetorically strong but somewhat overstated against meters in the complainant’s own home that do not record speech. A had some overclaims about objectivity and affordability, but its core comparative case better fit the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s answer that decibel logs prove sound rather than nuisance was the key rebuttal, because it neutralized B’s claim that measurement necessarily erases context while preserving A’s central benefit of objective proof.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by establishing a clearer path to victory around practical control of evidence, un-cross-examinable data in overburdened courts, and burden shift to low-income tenants; Side B's ideal objectivity claims were left vulnerable to A's direct rebuttals on economics and contestability, with A's Pressure Questions effectively exposing assumptions that B only partially resolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 directly answering the Pressure Questions by stressing device control and lack of cross-examination, which crystallized the decisive power-asymmetry clash over B's responses.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): B (PRO) built and maintained the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Its "facts vs. friction" and "status quo favors power" frames were established early and never seriously dislodged. The decisive exchange was B's Rebuttal 2 treatment of A's insulation pressure question: rather than defending against it, B executed a genuine judo move—arguing that logs showing normal living sounds at violation levels prove building failure and shift accountability to the landlord, not the tenant. A's response (A4.5) was that corporate landlords will use that same data to evict rather than remediate, which is partially plausible but does not answer the tenant's use of the same data as a defense. A also never fully neutralized B's cost-comparison argument (device vs. attorney hour) or B's consistent rebuttal that certified evidence does not equal verdict—adjudicators retain contextual discretion. A's strongest points—the retroactivity "defense requires a time machine" argument and the anchoring concern about overworked housing courts—were real and vivid, and B's answers to them were not perfect. But B's voluntary-use clarification adequately defused the forced-surveillance framing, and B's insulation reframe created a live route to victory for vulnerable tenants that A never closed. Both sides avoided fake citations and argued with plausible grounding throughout; the edge goes to B for superior comparison and weighing in the later rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's handling of A's insulation pressure question (Rebuttal 2, B4.3–B4.4): instead of conceding that raw data cannot distinguish tenant fault from building failure, B argued that logs confirming normal sounds at violation-level transmission actually shift blame from the resident to the landlord who built the substandard structure. This converted A's sharpest pressure question into an affirmative argument for the proposition and was never squarely neutralized by A's closing claim that landlords will simply evict rather than remediate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won because it better fit the motion’s modest claim: allowing certified noise evidence more often does not make it dispositive. A’s “evidence, not verdict” framing answered much of B’s strict-liability and context-blindness case, especially by emphasizing duration, frequency, timing, and continued human judgment. B’s strongest points on preemptive surveillance, landlord weaponization, and lack of retroactive defense made the debate competitive, but B too often treated admission of evidence as automatic eviction or rigid numerical liability, which overstated the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 1 was decisive: it directly answered the toddler/subwoofer objection by distinguishing raw spikes from sustained patterns and framed certified logs as contextual evidence rather than a replacement for reasonableness.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side A won by more convincingly exposing unaddressed practical burdens like mandatory preemptive monitoring for defense, context erasure in rushed adjudications, and landlords using logs for eviction over repair; Side B's claims of voluntary shields and shifted accountability were left partially unanswered after the pressure round.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A1/A2 answers in rebuttal 2 directly neutralized B's Q1/Q2 on retroactive recording and insulation by showing timing and incentive problems, creating the decisive practical clash.
