- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON built a coherent middle-ground mechanism (AI-triggered auto-suspend plus fast, asynchronous human confirmation/hardship bypass) that directly answered PRO's harm and bias concerns while attacking the absolutism of "never." PRO never squarely rebutted this collapse in their final speeches, instead re-asserting the catastrophic-harm framing without engaging the timing distinction (before vs. after suspension) that could have salvaged their position. CON also more precisely contested PRO's severity claims (e.g., a payment freeze does not cause eviction within days), which PRO's closing failed to directly refute. PRO's case remained principled and clearly stated but grew repetitive and left the most damaging tension in their own concessions unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2/Closing turn, pointing out that PRO's own Pressure Question answers (stopping fraud at the "intake gate" and allowing human confirmation "in seconds") effectively conceded CON's auto-suspend-then-fast-review model, undermining PRO's absolute "never" framing — a point PRO's Closing never directly answered.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won the decisive comparison created by the word “never.” Its fraud-surge and immediate-risk scenarios established a plausible case where an automatic temporary hold prevents irreversible losses that synchronous human review cannot handle at scale. Side B powerfully explained false-positive harms, but never fully solved the surge-capacity problem and relied on technical intake controls or rapid human checks without showing that they could protect every transaction before suspension.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange was its response to Side B’s proposed intake controls and seconds-long review: it argued that stolen-identity claims can implicate real accounts and that stopping the transaction first is operationally distinct from reviewing it afterward. Side B did not adequately explain how meaningful individualized review could precede every payment during a bot flood.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by effectively dismantling Side A's absolute "never" stance. Side B forced Side A into concessions regarding "intake gate" technical controls and "seconds-long" human verification, which Side B correctly identified as functionally identical to the AI-triggered suspensions with prompt post-review that Side B defended. Side B also provided a more realistic assessment of the harms, contrasting the permanent depletion of safety net funds with the temporary, curable nature of a payment pause, neutralizing Side A's harm asymmetry argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it trapped Side A in its own concessions, demonstrating that Side A's proposed "intake gate" blocks and "seconds-long" verifications were exactly the AI-first, human-later mechanisms Side A claimed to ban.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): B (PRO) built a cleaner, more durable case by drawing and defending a sharp distinction between blocking a fraudulent transaction/routing channel (which B conceded agencies may do quickly with minimal human confirmation) and suspending a person's entitlement based on an individual risk score (which the motion forbids). This let B absorb A's strongest scenario — mass botnet/account-takeover attacks — without conceding the motion, since a supervisor confirming a shared attack pattern in minutes still satisfies "meaningful human review." A never cleanly rebutted this distinction, instead asserting that halting a payout "is" suspending the claim, which is conclusory rather than argued. A also weakened its credibility with an unsupported, oddly precise "1,600 labor hours" claim, a clear instance of brittle fake precision the rules flag for discount. B's harm-asymmetry framing — reissued benefits/back-pay versus eviction, job loss, or unlawful presence from an auto-suspended visa — was introduced early, defended through rebuttals, and never squarely answered by A, which kept repeating the "vault gets emptied" framing without engaging the irreversibility point. Both sides correctly followed the A1/A2 pressure-question format, but B's answers more precisely tracked and neutralized A's questions, while A's answers to B's questions (especially on why the victim's own benefits must be suspended rather than just the fraudulent channel) largely restated the need for speed without addressing the substitutability of a channel-level block. B's closing crystallized the clash effectively; A's closing mostly recapped without confronting the channel-vs-entitlement distinction that had come to dominate the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2 plus B4.1–B4.3), where it reframes A's offshore-routing-hijack example as proof that a targeted transactional block satisfies both security needs and the motion, directly undercutting A's central "circuit breaker" justification for suspending the person's entitlement.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by distinguishing an emergency block on a compromised payment channel from suspending a person's underlying benefit, visa, or claim, while still allowing rapid pattern-level human authorization. Side A presented a credible fraud-speed concern, but largely relied on treating those two actions as equivalent and never adequately weighed eviction, job loss, or immigration harms caused before post-hoc review.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both questions directly, allowed a supervisor to confirm and block a mass-routing attack, and showed that this did not require an AI score alone to deprive each claimant. Side A called that a concession to automated holds, but did not answer the human-confirmation distinction.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins decisively by exploiting Side B's failure to address the full scope of the motion. The proposition explicitly covers benefits, visas, and fraud claims. Side B built its entire case around a financial circuit breaker analogy for freezing payouts to prevent syndicate theft, completely ignoring visas and injury claims. Side A relentlessly hammered this gap, pointing out that you cannot put a temporary administrative hold on a visa without triggering irreversible harms like unlawful presence and deportation. Furthermore, Side A drew a sharp, highly effective distinction between an AI flagging a shared infrastructure pattern, which PRO allows a human to quickly block, versus flagging an individual person as suspicious based on correlations, which PRO forbids. Side B's financial arguments were strong, but its inability to adapt to the non-financial elements of the motion and its failure to answer Side A's pressure questions regarding the person versus transaction distinction made Side A's path to victory much cleaner and more comprehensive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it dismantled Side B's circuit breaker analogy by distinguishing between freezing a fraudulent routing channel and suspending a person's underlying entitlement, while highlighting that Side B's financial framework completely collapses when applied to visas and the irreversible harm of deportation.
