### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by showing that consumer protection can supplement rather than replace criminal prosecution. Its combination of scalable emotional grooming, victim resistance, and pre-loss intervention justified distinct treatment. Side B raised strong definitional and evasion objections, but never established that partial evasion makes platform and payment-rail prevention valueless, and its claim that consumer protection requires a legitimate seller was too narrow.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest rebuttal exposed Side B's crime-versus-consumer-protection framing as a false dichotomy: deception can remain prosecutable while specialized rules target platforms, banks, and other intermediaries before losses occur. Side B's evasion examples limited those tools but did not neutralize that dual-track case.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): CON won a close debate by keeping the decisive clash on ontology and enforceability: romance scams are theft-by-deception with no regulable market/seller, so consumer-protection tools bind compliant platforms and rails while scammers move victims to encrypted apps, crypto, gift cards, and mules. PRO’s dual-track analogies and before-vs-after timing frame were strong, but CON showed specialization already lives inside ordinary-fraud institutions and that PRO’s prevention layer largely disappears once the scam leaves the storefront—leaving resource misdirection as the live cost of the distinct label.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 / closing on supervised-market intermediaries vs. direct thief contact (telemarketing/securities bind repeat record-keepers; AI romance fraud has none), paired with the WhatsApp/crypto/gift-card evasion pressure that PRO never fully neutralized.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): CON wins by showing that PRO's consumer-protection framework misfires against criminals who operate outside regulated markets. CON successfully argued that AI romance scams lack a legitimate seller or repeatable market transaction, so platform mandates and cooling-off periods bind only compliant intermediaries while thieves simply move to encrypted apps, crypto, gift cards, and mules. CON also neutralized PRO's claims of structural novelty by demonstrating that human-run pig-butchering rings and affinity-fraud crews already achieved the same depth and scale of emotional manipulation, making AI a cost amplifier rather than a category changer. Across the exchange, CON's rebuttals were sharper, their pressure questions exposed real burdens, and their closing crystallized the core defect in PRO's model.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's sustained attack on the "no market, no regulable seller" problem (developed in A2, A3, A4, and A5), which showed that consumer-protection tools police storefronts while the actual burglary happens off-platform via WhatsApp, crypto, and gift cards that consumer regimes cannot reach.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s A4.1-A4.4 response was decisive: it operationalized the AI distinction through automation and synthetic verification while explaining that broad safeguards can protect human-catfish victims too, substantially weakening Side B’s line-drawing and second-class-treatment objections.
