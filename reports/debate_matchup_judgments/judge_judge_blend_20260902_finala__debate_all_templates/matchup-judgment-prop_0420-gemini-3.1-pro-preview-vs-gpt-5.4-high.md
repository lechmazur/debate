### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): CON built the cleaner comparative path to victory. Their core argument—that a targeted feasibility rule (schools may refuse surveillance-heavy or disruptive demands) already solves PRO's stated problem without the risks of a broad reduction in parental authority—was never neutralized. PRO's strongest live claim was that even "saying no" under the current system is costly and requires a structural presumption shift. CON answered this effectively in Rebuttal 2: PRO's proposed solution doesn't end conflict; it just moves the battle to a vaguer, more ideological question of which family norms qualify as "mainly personal lifestyle preferences." That definitional problem, sharpened by CON's Q1, was PRO's critical vulnerability throughout. PRO's A1 answer (the "enforcement burden" test) was a reasonable attempt, but CON correctly pressed that this still hands institutions a broad license to override family values by default. PRO also never adequately specified what "structurally reducing parental power" would look like in practice or demonstrated why it produces less administrative conflict than a clearer feasibility standard. CON's repeated, accurate observation—that PRO's own concession ("once a request requires active policing, schools may refuse it") makes the broader motion unnecessary—was a live point PRO never cleanly extinguished. PRO had real strengths: the administrative-burden-of-saying-no argument was genuinely sharp, and the passive/active enforcement distinction was analytically useful. But CON's comparative framing was tighter: solve the narrow problem narrowly rather than weaken the broader home-school partnership. The clipping of CON's Rebuttal 2 conclusion was a minor execution cost but did not meaningfully change the balance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turning PRO's own A1 answer against them: PRO conceded "the moment a parent demands a public educator actively police it, the request can be refused," which CON correctly identified as making the broader motion overbroad and unnecessary. That move—using PRO's clarification to collapse the motion—was the most decisive exchange in the debate.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): B wins because it consistently narrows to the motion’s language of “highly restrictive” active enforcement, shows that A’s feasibility rule leaves teachers in endless defensive battles, and answers pressure questions directly while crystallizing the pluralism vs. conscription clash. A’s case is undercut by the clipped Rebuttal 2 and by repeatedly widening the debate to benign accommodations that the motion explicitly excludes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2’s direct A1/A2 answers contrasting passive accommodation with continuous surveillance, exposing that A’s conceded refusal power still leaves structural entitlement intact.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side A won because it more faithfully interpreted and defended the motion's specific targeting of highly restrictive rules that require active teacher enforcement. Side B spent crucial rounds defending passive accommodations (menu opt-outs, head coverings) that fall outside this scope, and while B raised a valid concern about who decides what counts as a preference, A answered with a workable enforcement-burden standard. A also successfully pressed that B's case contained a tension: B conceded schools should refuse surveillance-heavy demands, yet resisted the structural reduction in parental power that would prevent the exhausting administrative battles those demands create. B's clipped Rebuttal 2 and failure to fully neutralize A's teacher-burden mechanism left A's path to victory clearer after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's sustained exposure that Side B defended passive accommodations rather than the active teacher surveillance the motion targets, combined with the pressure question showing that restrictive social rules inherently require the constant monitoring B admitted was unreasonable.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by building and maintaining a cleaner limiting principle throughout the exchange. The core move—distinguishing passive accommodation (child declines a cupcake, wears modest clothes within code) from active enforcement (teacher confiscates food, polices friendships on parental instruction)—held up under pressure. When Side B pushed back that "for young children, enforcement is the accommodation," Side A correctly identified this proves too much: it collapses the distinction entirely and grants every restrictive parental code institutional muscle. Side A's Rebuttal 2 answer to Q1 was particularly strong: the test is not how sincere or religious a belief is, but what schools are being *asked to do*—monitor, punish, or isolate. That reframe sidesteps the majoritarian-bias critique by shifting from judging theology to judging conduct, which is a workable standard. Side B's most durable argument—that administrators will subjectively dismiss minority religious practices as mere "preferences"—was never fully neutralized, but Side A's practical conduct-based test is a more specific response than CON acknowledged. Side B's offered limiting principle, "reasonable accommodation," is largely circular: it restates the question of what counts as reasonable rather than answering it. The incompatible-demands pressure question also went PRO's way; CON's answer ("parents only direct their own child") addressed the logistics but not the deeper authority question. Side A suffered execution costs from two clipped speeches, but the critical arguments came through before each clip. Side B deployed the "young child under peer pressure" image compellingly but leaned on it too heavily as a substitute for a genuine limiting principle. The closing speeches reflected the overall pattern: PRO crystallized a clear standard; CON repeated the majoritarian-bias concern without advancing it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's answer to Side B's pressure question Q1 (Rebuttal 2, A4.1): rather than defending an administrator's ability to assess belief profundity, PRO reframed the test entirely as what schools are being *asked to do*—is staff being asked to monitor, punish, or isolate versus quietly accommodate? This shifted the debate from a subjective theological ranking to a practical conduct-based standard, directly defusing CON's strongest "majoritarian bias" argument and giving the proposition a workable limiting principle that CON never adequately answered.

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by drawing a cleaner, more defensible line between accommodation and enforcement, and showing that Side B's standard collapses into unlimited private control of public classrooms.
