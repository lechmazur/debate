### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): B (PRO) built the more resilient case by consistently distinguishing blasphemy/insult laws from incitement laws, a structural point A never squarely answered. B's Pakistan example was more damaging than A's, since B turned it around to show the statute legitimizes mobs rather than restraining them, and A's rebuttal ("violence happens because enforcement fails") never explained why incitement law alone couldn't do the same preventive work. B's pressure questions (Q1 on why blasphemy-specific statutes succeed where incitement law fails; Q2 on the mob-veto slippery slope) exposed the load-bearing weakness in CON's case, and B's answers to A's own questions (rejecting the gasoline analogy, citing France/Norway/Netherlands as abolition without collapse) directly neutralized A's strongest rhetorical question. A's closing repeated the "grandmother/brick" imagery without engaging these unresolved structural points, while B's closing tightened the "ransom note" and "no principled stopping point" framing that had been building since rebuttal 1. B did more work narrowing the debate and answering pressure; A relied more on vivid repetition than on new resolution of the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to A's Q2 pressure question (A2 in Rebuttal 2), citing France/Norway/Netherlands as abolition without sectarian collapse, directly dismantled A's strongest rhetorical challenge and reframed the "no successful precedent" claim as evidence of majority abuse rather than a reason to preserve the laws.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B won by distinguishing religious insult from direct incitement and showing that blasphemy laws can validate mob grievances, empower majority censorship, and be abused against minorities. Side A offered a forceful prevention case but repeatedly assumed that insult makes violence inevitable, never established why ordinary incitement laws are insufficient, and narrowed the disputed laws to calculated provocations rather than defending blasphemy laws as generally written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s decisive response was that punishing speech because third parties threaten violence grants the mob a censorial veto, while violence and direct incitement can already be prosecuted. Side A’s proposed limiting line—gratuitous insult calculated to cause killing—did not justify broader religious-insult statutes and largely described conduct existing incitement rules could address.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A wins by successfully turning Side B's core impact. While Side B argued that blasphemy laws prevent violence by acting as a procedural buffer, Side A convincingly argued that these laws actually validate mob theology and fuel violence by codifying the grievance. Side A also effectively exposed Side B's "predictable harm" argument as a heckler's veto with no principled stopping point, framing it memorably as a "ransom note." Side A's responses to pressure questions were sharper, logically rigorous, and grounded in plausible historical examples, whereas Side B's attempts to draw a line at "gratuitous insult" failed to resolve the slippery slope.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, specifically the dismantling of the gasoline can analogy by distinguishing physical accelerants from speech, and the use of historical examples (France, Norway, Netherlands) to refute the claim that abolition inevitably leads to sectarian collapse.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): B built a tighter, three-pronged case (weaponization/mob incentive, no neutral line, coerced respect fails) and sustained it with sharper metaphors ("gasoline vs. extinguisher," "sanctify vs. channel") that made the causal story memorable. B's pressure questions were genuinely pointed (lynching correlation with enforcement, lack of minority protection) and exposed real weaknesses in A's "courthouse channels grievances" theory. A's Rebuttal 1 was cut off mid-sentence, an execution failure that left a key response to B's vigilante argument incomplete, and A's answer to B's Q2 in Rebuttal 2 dodged the direct question (no country named) by reframing rather than answering, which the rubric treats as a real cost. A did land a fair counter-punch by calling B's "lagging damage" explanation unfalsifiable, and the majority-consent/self-determination framing was a legitimate strategic angle, but B's rights-based rebuttal ("no society gets to democratically vote for tyranny") answered that challenge more directly than A answered B's parallel pressure. Overall B progressed the clash more cleanly across rounds and left fewer live, unaddressed threats.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's reversal of the "imperialism" charge (B2.4–B2.5, reinforced in B4.3) — arguing that blasphemy laws are themselves majority imposition and that barring criminalization of belief is a guardrail, not an override of self-determination — most damaged A's central democratic-consent argument, and A's attempt to answer it in A4.5 did not fully neutralize the point.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side B won by combining the stronger rights-based principle with a clearer causal account: criminalizing insult legitimizes outrage, empowers majorities, and cannot neutrally define sacred offense. Side A offered a plausible courthouse-versus-mob mechanism, but its first rebuttal was clipped, it largely dodged the minority-protection question, and its France and Denmark chronology was weakly grounded. B overclaimed with universal language, but still left more decisive routes to abolition standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer that democratic consent cannot justify majority tyranny decisively undercut A’s liberal-imperialism framing. A never adequately explained why majority preference should outweigh unequal criminalization of minority or dissenting speech.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A built a highly persuasive causal story that blasphemy laws do not merely channel pre-existing outrage, but actively validate and sanctify it, thereby manufacturing the moral justification for mob violence. Side A also effectively neutralized Side B's "democratic consent" argument by framing minority protections as fundamental democratic guardrails rather than imperial overreach. Side B presented a strong counter-narrative about the courthouse replacing the street, but suffered a significant execution cost when its first rebuttal was abruptly clipped at 70 words. Side A's superior rhetorical sharpness, flawless format adherence, and cleaner handling of the core causal clash secure a solid win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it sharply dismantled the "liberal imperialism" charge by distinguishing between overriding self-determination and enforcing basic minority guardrails, while forcefully maintaining that state validation of outrage inherently fuels vigilantism.
