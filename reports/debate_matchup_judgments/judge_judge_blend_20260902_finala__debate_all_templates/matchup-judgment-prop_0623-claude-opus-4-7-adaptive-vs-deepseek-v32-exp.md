- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing the debate around the core trade-off: whether protecting marginal businesses that rely on underpayment is worth systemically suppressing wages. - **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by grounding its case in working contemporary examples (Australia, the Nordics, Austria, the Netherlands) that directly undermine CON's central rigidity thesis, while CON never moved past special pleading about "social trust" or union density to explain why those models cannot travel. PRO also landed the decisive reframe that sectoral floors help small diners by stopping chains from undercutting them on labor costs, and crystallized the debate around whether preserving a margin built on underpayment is worth systemic wage suppression. CON's alternative of portable benefits and firm-level bargaining was shown to be either a complement to, not a replacement for, sectoral frameworks or a collapsed model in the very industries at issue. CON's closing mischaracterized PRO's argument as moral rather than empirical, avoiding the comparative weighing on jobs and productivity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's first-rebuttal turnaround of the "family diner" argument ([B2.2]–[B2.3]), which showed that a sectoral floor prevents national chains from winning via wage suppression and that tiered/regional differentials already exist in sectoral systems. This reframe exposed that CON was defending incumbent scale advantages, not small-business dynamism, and CON never recovered from it.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON)): Side A (PRO) won by controlling the central clashes and offering cleaner comparative weighing. PRO effectively flipped CON's small-business argument by explaining how a sectoral floor actually protects independents from chains' wage-squeezing advantages, and noted that tiering mechanisms handle industry heterogeneity. PRO also neutralized CON's "Nordic model doesn't travel" claim by pointing to legal extension mechanisms in Australia and France, arguing that firm-level bargaining is actually more vulnerable to capture in low-union environments. Crucially, PRO addressed both pay and standards (safety, scheduling), while CON focused almost exclusively on wages and rigidity. PRO's pressure questions successfully exposed CON's reliance on preserving low labor costs as a competitive margin, and PRO's closing crystallized this trade-off decisively. CON was competent but repetitive on cultural-trust arguments and failed to adequately answer PRO's empirical counters on automation and standards.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 response to the "family diner vs. chain" argument and the "1970s Europe" rigidity claim. A flipped the small business point by showing how chains currently win via wage suppression, and dismantled the historical rigidity argument by contrasting current high-productivity sectoral economies with misattributed southern European statutory codes, setting up a dominant comparative frame for the rest of the debate.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO)): Side A consistently dominated the strategic framing and mechanical clash of the debate. A established a clear distinction between democratically accountable regulation (wage boards) and unaccountable private negotiation (sectoral bargaining). Side B was forced to concede in the pressure round that gig workers do not get a literal seat at the table, which A expertly leveraged to prove B's model is effectively just regulation handled by unaccountable cartels.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 capitalized brilliantly on B's A1 response. By pointing out that "legal inclusion" instead of a literal seat equates to regulation, A successfully argued that such regulation should remain in the hands of accountable voters rather than private incumbents.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO)): CON won by offering a cleaner comparative path: it showed that sectoral bargaining amounts to regulation imposed by private incumbents, producing unavoidable ceilings, rigidity, and outsider exclusion, while wage boards and statutory standards deliver the same floors with democratic accountability. PRO never answered why binding private agreements are preferable to adjustable public ones, and conceded that gig workers get only legal inclusion rather than voice, which CON effectively turned into a regulation-versus-accountability frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing turned PRO's admission that gig workers receive only legal inclusion—not an actual seat—into the decisive argument: if enforceable standards are simply being imposed by incumbent unions and large employers, the mechanism is regulation, and democratic wage boards can deliver identical protections without cartelization or democratic unaccountability.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully framing democratic accountability and institutional design as the decisive clash. When Side A conceded that marginalized workers receive legal inclusion rather than direct representation at the bargaining table, Side B effectively turned this into a win for its alternative: if the outcome is functionally regulation, it should be delivered through democratically accountable wage boards and statutes rather than delegated to private incumbent parties. Side B also cleanly decoupled Side A's bundled benefits (apprenticeships, training) from the sectoral bargaining mechanism, showing governments can adopt those supports directly without the rigidity and insider capture risks. Side A's proactive versus reactive framing was rhetorically strong but ultimately failed to answer the structural accountability deficit or prove that sectoral tables inherently generate the complementary institutions needed to avoid adverse outcomes. Side B's comparative weighing was sharper, more disciplined, and resolved the core tradeoffs more convincingly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 response to the representation and Germany questions. By highlighting that Side A's admission of legal inclusion concedes the mechanism is regulation, not bargaining, Side B cleanly pivoted to its accountable wage board alternative and dismantled the necessity of Side A's framework. This directly neutralized Side A's inclusion argument, exposed the bundling fallacy, and crystallized the accountability clash that decided the debate.
