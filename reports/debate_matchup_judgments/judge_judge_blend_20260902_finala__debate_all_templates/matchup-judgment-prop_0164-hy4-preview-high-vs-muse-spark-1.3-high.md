### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built genuinely strong, tightly matched cases, but A's insurance/public-goods framing survived the exchange better. A conceded the core cost (fewer novel grants) from the outset and consistently defended the tradeoff as worthwhile, while offering a direct answer to why discretionary replication funding systematically loses (replication is "derivative," lacks career incentive, and dedicated calls are cut first)—a structural claim B's Pressure Q2 answer effectively conceded ("nothing should stop a check being skipped... that is the virtue of discretion"), weakening B's central alternative. B landed real damage with the "rare and ignored" reversal of false positives, the early-career-lab example flip, and especially the "discretion with a new label" charge against A's Rebuttal 2 concession (a few percent, exemptions, rollover). That charge exposed real tension in A's position, but A's closing offered a coherent (if compressed) recovery: the point of a floor is guaranteed availability, not uniform size or unconditional application. B's vivid "ask which three disappear" closing restates a cost A had already conceded rather than adding new leverage, whereas A's closing more directly ties the visible/invisible cost comparison back to the unresolved incentive-bias argument. The debate is close, but A's throughline held up marginally better under cross-examination.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 reversal of A's own example—arguing early-career labs and urgent-threat work are where delay, not false confidence, is costliest—was the sharpest single hit, forcing A to concede displacement explicitly; but A's Rebuttal 2 answer that discretionary replication is scored as "derivative" and loses to novelty (reinforced when B's own Pressure Q2 answer bit the bullet that skipped checks are simply "the virtue of discretion") was the more decisive exchange for the final verdict, since it left B's alternative mechanism looking structurally unreliable exactly where it mattered most.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively conceding the value of verification but dismantling the mechanism of a rigid quota. Side A's framing of the 'timing reversal'—that discovery is urgent and must surge during crises, while verification can wait until a finding shows real-world traction—was decisive. Side B struggled to defend the rigidity of the fixed share, eventually suggesting unspent funds could roll over, which Side A cleanly weaponized to show the quota would idle funds while the frontier starves.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 effectively trapped Side B's 'rollover' concession, pointing out that if the quota doesn't bind it fails its purpose, but if it does, it idles critical funds during crises while early labs and urgent discoveries are cut.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): B won a close, substantive clash by keeping the decisive comparison on means: fixed reserve vs targeted discretion. B made the certain-vs-uncertain cost tradeoff clearer (killed frontier grants, young labs, crisis windows vs rare built-on errors), forced A to soften the fixed share into exemptions/rollover/“few percent,” and showed that post-traction triggers plus open data can buy verification without a rigid tax. A’s incentive/public-goods case and “discretion gets cut first” pressure remained live, but B’s answers and timing-reversal weighing gave the cleaner reason to reject the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3: treating A’s flexible few-percent/exempt/roll-forward reserve as either binding (and killing payline science) or non-binding (and no longer the motion’s insurance).

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases with vivid framing (leaky bucket/insurance premium vs. treadmill/doorbell-inspection), and the pressure-question round genuinely sharpened the debate. B's strongest weapon was the size dilemma — forcing A to admit the earmark is "single-digit," which risks making it too small to be the incentive-changing commitment device A's opening promised. A partially defused this by reframing "meaningful" as protected-and-predictable rather than large, and backed it with a durable point B never fully answered: that follow-up studies and self-correction bury failures as "boundary conditions" rather than exposing them, so discretionary will has produced little despite decades of opportunity. B's alternative (universal data/code mandates plus ad hoc "crisis" strikes) sounds appealing but was left vague on who funds and staffs those strikes without a standing budget — a gap A explicitly exploited ("will is cheap talk") and B never closed. B's spend-it-or-lose-it mechanism was a strong, underexplored risk, but A's analogy to existing triage in costly trials and cohorts blunted it enough to keep the exchange live rather than decisive. On balance, A's cost-benefit framing (false leads compound downstream costs far exceeding a small verification premium) went essentially unanswered in kind, giving A the cleaner final weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response to the size dilemma (single-digit share still creates standing career lines and journal expectations, unlike today's near-zero baseline) directly answered B's sharpest pressure question, even though B kept the tension alive through the closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully defending against both of Side A's main attacks. B resolved the "size dilemma" by explaining how even a small, single-digit percentage creates necessary infrastructure since the baseline is near zero. B also brilliantly dismantled A's "cheap checks/treadmill" argument by pointing out that grant panels already manage to fund slow, expensive clinical trials under similar budgetary mechanics without defaulting to cheap surveys.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (A2), which expertly neutralized Side A's argument about the quota forcing cheap replications by pointing out that panels already evaluate and fund complex, multi-year projects using rollover and importance thresholds.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): A built the clearer path by establishing the reliability/incentives problem and treating the fixed share as a commitment device that turns near-zero verification into standing capacity. B landed real pressure via the size dilemma and spend-it-or-lose-it gaming, but A answered both directly (single-digit from a zero baseline still rewires careers/journals while leaving >90% for novelty; panels already fund costly slow work and can fence the earmark the same way). B's alternatives (will + mandates + occasional strikes) never escaped A's core showing that discretion has existed for decades yet left load-bearing claims untested. The tradeoff therefore favors A on the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1–A4.2 dissolving B's size/spend-pressure dilemma while keeping the commitment-device claim intact
