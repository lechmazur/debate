- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): B won the decisive mechanism clash: whether a court-ordered plain-language explanation is a pleading catalyst or a defendant-authored shield. A’s dignity/access case was strong and consistent, but B repeatedly showed that PRO’s own illustrations (healthcare cost proxy; “insufficient credit history”) actually do audit or file-correction work—B’s preferred remedies—while the narrated sentence supplies the lawful alternative that can defeat plausibility and cannot be shown “false” the way human pretext can. B’s pressure questions forced the retreat, and the second rebuttal and closing crystallized who writes the record and why that ends the case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 (B4.1–B4.6): turns A’s healthcare-spending sentence into an “obvious alternative explanation” for dismissal and shows every alleged benefit of explanation is really audit/file-correction work.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by demonstrating that a compelled explanation functions as a legal shield rather than a catalyst for justice. PRO (Side B) never resolved the core "shield vs. catalyst" tension: under pretext doctrine, a stated reason must be showably false to be actionable, but ML surrogate explanations are typically accurate-yet-incomplete, providing defendants with facially neutral "obvious alternative explanations" that actually help secure dismissal. CON successfully showed that every benefit PRO claimed—catching data errors, uncovering the healthcare cost-proxy bias, detecting discrimination—was actually delivered by CON's preferred tools (file-access/correction rights and statistical outcome audits), not by narrating the model's logic. When pressured for the specific sentence that would help a plaintiff plead discrimination, PRO offered a facially neutral criterion that CON rightly argued would read as a cost-conscious business justification to a court.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2 rebuttal dismantling PRO's pretext theory (A2.5-A2.6): arguing that model explanations are not "false" in the way human pretext is, because both debt-to-income and racial proxy can be simultaneously "true drivers" in a trained model, making an accurate-but-incomplete explanation a court-certified shield rather than an evidentiary target.

Side A argued that an explanation acts as an evidentiary catalyst for civil rights claims by allowing plaintiffs to cross the plausibility pleading threshold. Side B countered with a highly persuasive legal and technical argument: machine learning explanations are often post-hoc approximations that provide technically true but incomplete reasons (e.g., "historical expenditures"). Under civil procedure, this functions as an "obvious alternative explanation" that gets cases dismissed before discovery, rather than evidence of pretext (which requires the stated reason to be demonstrably false). Side B effectively used Side A's own healthcare proxy example to prove that such biases are found via aggregate audits, not individual explanations, leaving Side A's "right to explanation" as a tool that inadvertently helps defendants write their own court-certified alibis.
