### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's two core analogies: the technical analogy to spam detection and the legal analogy to employer/landlord liability. Side B effectively demonstrated that inferring coordination from ambiguous social behavior generates unacceptable error rates compared to fixed spam hashes, and that the "decades" needed to mature a reasonableness standard would force immediate, defensive mass-censorship by platforms. Side A powerfully argued the need for upstream prevention, but Side B successfully proved the proposed mechanism would fail in practice.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal (B4.1 and B4.4) masterfully dismantled Side A's defenses, pointing out that "timing and language" are correlational noise unlike CSAM hashes, and that waiting for a "reasonableness standard" to mature case-by-case guarantees the very over-removal Side A denied.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): CON built the stronger path to victory by exposing a persistent mechanism gap in PRO's case. PRO relied on the claim that platforms can "already see" coordinated harassment and act on it, but CON successfully broke the analogy to spam and CSAM detection by showing those systems use fixed technical signatures while harassment requires inferring intent from ambiguous social behavior—retweets, timing, and mentions that look identical for defenders and attackers. This rebuttal was never squarely answered; PRO simply repeated that platforms use "the same signals" and that error is acceptable. CON reinforced this with the litigation-maturation asymmetry: reasonableness standards for employers and landlords stabilized over decades, but platforms face immediate suit under vague standards, making over-removal the rational choice. PRO's powerful upstream-versus-downstream framing lost comparative force because CON showed the duty would likely burden open, traceable platforms while missing encrypted coordination where the worst harm already occurs. Across the exchange, CON sharpened the core clash—can platforms reliably distinguish harmful coordination from legitimate counter-speech in real time?—and PRO never provided a plausible mechanism that survived scrutiny.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's distinction between hash-based spam/CSAM detection and inference-based harassment coordination (Turn 4 and Turn 8), which broke PRO's central analogy and showed that "coordination" cannot be detected by existing technical triage because intent must be inferred from behavior that is identical for defenders and attackers.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): CON won the central clash on workability. PRO's case rested on platforms already detecting coordination like spam/CSAM. CON directly answered in [B4.1]/[B2.3] that those are fixed hashes vs intent inference, why error rates are high even on simple calls, and why rational compliance is over-removal of defenders. PRO never rebutted that distinction and never answered [B4.3] that doxxing/threats already violate ToS, so the new duty would bite only the ambiguous middle. Upstream vs downstream framing was rhetorically strong but did not resolve the narrow-enough vs broad-enough contradiction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R2 A1: dismantling PRO's spam/CSAM analogy as hash-matching vs correlational intent inference, combined with the point that true emergencies are already covered by existing takedowns, which made PRO's journalist-at-door example non-unique to the new duty.
