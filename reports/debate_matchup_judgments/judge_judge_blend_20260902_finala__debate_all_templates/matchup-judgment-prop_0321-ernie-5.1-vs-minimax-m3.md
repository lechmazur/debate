### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON won on the two decisive live clashes. First, the marginalized-creator asymmetry: CON's cookie-banner analogy was concrete and PRO effectively conceded the asymmetry (B4.2) while offering only a "one-click toggle" as the fix—but never addressed the discovery problem (creators must know to opt out before they can). CON correctly branded this a "wish, not a solution," and that pressure was never neutralized. Second, the principled-line problem: CON repeatedly asked why generative AI training is uniquely different from search engines, recommendation systems, and commercial research that also ingest public content at scale for profit. PRO's answer—"scale transforms learning into extraction"—was challenged with examples that share the same features. PRO never supplied a clean distinguishing principle, and CON's closing hammered this silence. PRO's strongest arguments (public posting ≠ commercial consent; power imbalance; economic replacement) were genuinely compelling and kept the debate competitive, but PRO's own cookie-banner concession undermined the opt-out remedy, and the search-engine analogy gap was left unresolved through the closing. CON's rebuttals were sharper and more adaptive across rounds, and the closing crystallized the clash cleanly with memorable framing ("sells protection and delivers extraction"; "velvet rope, not a door"), while PRO's closing largely recycled earlier points without resolving the live pressures.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answering PRO's A1 (A4.1–A4.3): CON took PRO's own cookie-banner concession and turned it against them—PRO acknowledged the asymmetry but offered only platform-built infrastructure as a fix, without addressing why marginalized creators would discover the opt-out mechanism. The "velvet rope" framing was sharp, and it permanently damaged PRO's claim to be protecting the most vulnerable creators.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON won by consistently winning the decisive clash on opt-out asymmetry (cookie-banner evidence) and equal representation in the status quo, while showing AI training fits existing public-learning norms; PRO's extraction framing and consent claims never neutralized that comparative burden across rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.5–A2.6 and A4.1–A4.3 on velvet-rope opt-out harming the vulnerable, directly answering B's pressure questions and PRO's own evidence.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by successfully pressing two decisive burdens: the practical failure of an opt-out mechanism for marginalized creators, and the lack of a principled distinction between AI training and existing data-economy practices like search engines. Side B (PRO) made a strong moral case for consent and against exploitation, but never adequately resolved the cookie-banner asymmetry problem or explained why scale alone transforms learning into taking when other systems operate at similar scale. Side A's closing crystallized this trade-off cleanly, showing how the proposition would protect the legally sophisticated while leaving the vulnerable exposed, ultimately failing its own stated goal.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built and maintained a more coherent case across all five rounds. Its three-pillar structure — control, competition, consent — held throughout without internal contradiction. The most decisive single move was PRO's identification in Rebuttal 2 that CON simultaneously claimed existing opt-out tools constitute workable consent AND called for better mechanisms and collective licensing. That contradiction was real and never fully resolved: CON's A2 response said "collective licensing already functions under opt-out defaults," but the ASCAP/BMI analogy actually describes opt-in collective rights management, undercutting the very comparison. PRO's robots.txt point (designed in 1994 for server throttling, not commercial AI training authorization) was sharp, grounded, and unanswered in any substantive way. CON's strongest line — that opt-in would concentrate power in large platforms who can afford legal compliance, squeezing out smaller innovators — was genuinely good pressure, but PRO had pre-empted a version of it (the status quo already lets a few firms absorb everything and pay nothing), and CON never resolved the tension between that argument and the earlier claim that existing tools are functional. The art student analogy was a liability for CON: PRO's pressure question restoring scale and direct substitution was sharp, and CON's defense ("the category is what matters, not the volume") was circular and didn't engage the marketplace substitution point. Both closings crystallized existing clash without late evidence dumps, so no execution cost there, but PRO's closing was cleaner at identifying the live voting issues. Margin is clear but competitive because CON's power-concentration argument was substantive and partially answered the PRO case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 crystallization that CON cannot simultaneously claim existing tools constitute adequate consent and campaign for "better consent mechanisms and standardized licensing" — this exposed a genuine internal contradiction CON never cleanly escaped, and it directly answered CON's own pressure question about whether PRO's two positions were consistent.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B won by exposing CON's inconsistent consent claims (opt-out already works vs. needs fixing) via pressure questions and Rebuttal 2, while preserving a coherent default-right argument grounded in scale and substitution; CON's art-student analogy and power-concentration rebuttals were undercut by the contradiction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 directly answering Q1/Q2 and crystallizing the inconsistency as the decisive clash.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) secured a clear win by maintaining a tighter case and successfully exploiting a strategic vulnerability in Side A's argument. Side A tried to defend the status quo while simultaneously advocating for "better consent mechanisms" and collective licensing. Side B sharply turned this into a concession that the current default is inadequate, framing opt-in as the necessary prerequisite for the licensing markets Side A wanted. Side B's pressure questions were more incisive, and their rebuttals effectively dismantled Side A's "art student" analogy by restoring the dimensions of industrial scale and commercial substitution. Side A raised valid concerns about startup access and innovation but relied on shaky analogies and overconfident legal generalizations that failed to neutralize PRO's core clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, which directly answered Side A's pressure questions while turning Side A's call for improved consent tools into a decisive concession that the status quo fails creators, collapsing CON's defense of the current default.
