### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built sophisticated, well-grounded cases, but A ultimately controlled the decisive clash: whether non-oil assets earn independently of oil price. A supplied concrete, plausible evidence (Dubai's non-oil GDP structure, DP World, Emirates, Jubail petrochemicals holding margin through the 2015-2020 low-oil period, Saudi non-oil GDP share, VAT/tax base, job and participation gains) and directly turned B's strongest counter—fiscal strain and VAT hikes—into evidence of adaptive diversification ("that's not PR... you learn to tax"). B's structural critique (subsidy-with-walls, toll-booth logic, "ministry paying itself") was conceptually sharp and forced real engagement, especially in the pressure-question exchange, but B's own admission that "there is none" for a labor-absorbing non-oil sector without state support handed A a clean reductio: by that standard, no historical diversifier (including Korea) would count either, undermining B's chosen test as the right metric for the motion. B's closing reasserted the fiscal-survival test rather than rebutting A's reframing of it, leaving the goalpost dispute unresolved in A's favor. The debate progressed well across rounds with genuine mutual engagement, but A ended with the cleaner, more decisive framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 turning B's own point about VAT hikes and fiscal strain ("what collapsed was the old fiscal model... that's not PR, concrete you keep and you learn to tax") into evidence for diversification, combined with B's concession in the pressure-question exchange that no state has achieved labor-absorbing diversification without state support—an admission A leveraged to expose B's test as an unattainable standard.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully exposing Side B's impossible "purity test" for economic diversification. By demonstrating that all industrial transitions require initial state support and leverage structural advantages, Side A proved that the Gulf's efforts are substantive and following a proven historical model. Side B argued beautifully that oil still backstops the economy, but failed to overcome A's core point that expanding tax bases and maintaining non-oil throughput during low-crude cycles prove these aren't just PR exercises.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal effectively neutralized the "subsidy with walls" argument by comparing Jubail's cheap gas advantage to Texas chemicals, demonstrating that leveraging structural advantages is standard economics rather than fake diversification.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully reframing the debate from "total macroeconomic independence" to "substantive asset creation that survives low-oil cycles." B made strong points about fiscal deficits but ultimately conceded the assets were substantive, just "too narrow," which failed to meet the CON burden of proving they were mostly PR exercises.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled B's "purity test" for subsidies by pointing to Texas and Korea, and proved the non-oil assets survived the 2015-2020 low-oil stress test while the state adapted its broader fiscal model.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B's "activity vs. autonomy" framing proved more durable under pressure. B's disanalogies (Norway's pre-existing productive base vs. Gulf rent-recycling; Singapore/Korea surviving brutal export markets vs. Gulf firms needing constant sovereign equity injections) directly undercut A's central comparisons and were never adequately answered. Critically, when A's flagship example—Dubai—was tested, B's specific point that Dubai's 2015 slump occurred while global growth continued (unlike 2009/2020 global crises) exposed a real gap in A's "it's just global cycles" defense, and A's closing never returned to rebut this. B also forced a fair, falsifiable test in its pressure answers (survive $40 oil for five years without a sovereign checkbook) rather than an unfalsifiable one, defusing A's "rigged test" charge. A's strongest unanswered point—that costly reforms (VAT, subsidy cuts, female workforce entry) wouldn't happen for pure PR—was met with a plausible "fiscal triage to preserve rentierism" counter-story from B that, while not conclusive, kept the exchange live rather than lost. Overall B controlled the comparative framing more precisely and landed the more damaging, unrebutted blow.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 point that Dubai's 2015 downturn happened despite continued global growth (contradicting A's "global crisis" explanation) and its follow-up on Dubai's 2009 Abu Dhabi bailout and reliance on federal oil for defense/infrastructure—left unanswered in A's closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully framing the debate around "autonomy versus activity," proving that non-oil infrastructure heavily relies on regional oil liquidity rather than true economic independence. Side B's specific examples of Dubai's 2015 economic slump (despite global growth) and its 2009 oil bailout decisively neutralized Side A's primary case study.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized Side A's defense of Dubai by pointing out the 2015 slump—driven by low oil despite global growth—which Side A subsequently failed to address.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a more realistic and economically sound definition of diversification (building globally competitive non-oil export sectors) and decisively refuting the "mostly PR" claim by highlighting politically costly domestic reforms like VAT, subsidy cuts, and shifting women into the private workforce. Side B's reliance on an impossibly strict standard of total fiscal autonomy at $40/bbl oil weakens their case, and their "recycling" argument fails to account for the genuine value-add and global competitiveness of the Gulf's logistics and aviation hubs. While Side B landed strong points on fiscal breakeven prices and the 2015 oil-price correlation, Side A successfully framed these as expected features of a state heavily investing in its transition rather than proof of an illusion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's argument that public-relations regimes do not impose politically painful reforms like VAT, cut fuel subsidies, or push women into the private workforce, effectively neutralizing Side B's claim that the diversification drive is mostly spin.
