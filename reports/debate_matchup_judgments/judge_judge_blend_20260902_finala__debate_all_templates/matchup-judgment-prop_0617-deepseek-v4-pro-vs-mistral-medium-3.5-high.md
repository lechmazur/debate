### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built a stronger cumulative case across all rounds. Its most decisive move was establishing that a burden shift is not a ban—a conceptual clarification CON never effectively answered, instead continuing to treat the presumption as a near-veto throughout. PRO's pressure Q1 was genuinely sharp: if the "substantial consumer benefit" standard is too vague to meet, CON cannot simultaneously and confidently assert Google could not have met it for Android—a real logical contradiction that CON sidestepped in Rebuttal 2 rather than resolving. PRO also landed the comparison between its proposed standard and the existing "substantial lessening of competition" test, which materially neutralized the vagueness objection. CON's Android example was repeatedly invoked but kept backfiring: PRO showed that Google's open-OS-breaking-carrier-lock-in story was exactly the kind of articulable consumer benefit the presumption would accept, while Instagram offered no comparable story. CON's Rebuttal 1 was notably short (well under the cap), leaving PRO's Instagram-as-killer-acquisition framing unanswered. CON's closing was largely repetitive of its opening Android point without sharpening the comparative weighing. PRO, by contrast, consistently adapted—crystallizing in the closing that the rule would have allowed Android while stopping Instagram, a clean and concrete decision rule that CON never matched. CON's VC funding argument had some residual force but was adequately handled by PRO's redirect-not-eliminate response. The lateness and repetitiveness of CON's speeches, combined with PRO's more coherent and adaptive framing, give B a clear edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure Q1 was the most decisive exchange: it exposed a direct contradiction in CON's case—claiming the standard is too vague to satisfy while simultaneously asserting with confidence that Google could not have satisfied it for Android. CON never resolved this tension, and PRO's follow-through in Rebuttal 2 (comparing the standard to existing "substantial lessening of competition" tests) converted the pressure into a durable win on the vagueness clash.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by making the presumption look like a targeted burden shift rather than a ban, and by tying that shift to network effects, irreversible tipping, and the limits of after-the-fact enforcement. Side B’s Android and VC-exit objections were strong, but A gave a plausible standard for substantial consumer benefit and better answered why dominant-platform acquisitions of nascent rivals create unique risks. B’s Instagram answer never fully explained how common ownership intensifies competition rather than neutralizing an independent threat.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was A’s response to the “sledgehammer” framing: A argued the rule would have allowed clearly beneficial integrations like Android while blocking threat-neutralizing deals like Instagram, shifting the clash from whether acquisitions are ever good to whether dominant platforms should bear the proof burden.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a clear structural warrant for why post-hoc enforcement fails in digital markets and effectively defending the proposed standard. Side A successfully mitigated the VC funding DA by noting exits to non-dominant firms remain open, and delivered a decisive comparative point that the new standard is no vaguer than existing antitrust tests. Side B hammered the Android example well but offered a weak defense of the Instagram acquisition and left Side A's core warrant about tipped markets largely uncontested.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's argument that the 'substantial consumer benefit' standard is no vaguer than the existing 'substantial lessening of competition' standard, effectively neutralizing Side B's claim that the rule is an arbitrary sledgehammer.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Both sides made real arguments, and both incurred execution costs (A was clipped multiple times; B's Rebuttal 2 was only 142 of 340 words). The decisive clash was A's sustained charge that "substantial consumer benefit" is an impossible standard for pre-revenue startups: platforms cannot produce technical benchmarks, third-party validation, or integration roadmaps that amount to *substantial* verifiable gains when they are buying a tiny team with an unproven algorithm. A raised this in its opening, pressed it in Rebuttal 1, crystallized it as a pressure question, and then in Rebuttal 2 correctly noted that B's answer (platforms can model benchmarks) simply does not address the pre-revenue scenario. B never closed this hole. A also partially neutralized B's strongest asymmetry claim—that false negatives are permanent but false positives are correctable—by arguing that startups never formed are equally permanent losses, with no corrective mechanism. That reframe was not answered in B's closing. B's core ex-post-timing argument (harm is proven too late) was real and never fully answered by A, but B's proposed corrective depended on a workable standard that A demonstrated was not workable. B's venture-capital "pivot to IPOs" response was speculative and less grounded than A's mechanism showing why acquisition exits drive early-stage funding. On balance, A's implementational objection accumulated more unanswered weight across the rounds, giving it the narrow but clear edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's multi-round assault on the "substantial consumer benefit" standard: introduced in the opening as a "mirage," sharpened in Rebuttal 1 as impossible for early-stage acquisitions, formalized as Pressure Question Q1, and then confirmed in Rebuttal 2 when B's answer ("technical benchmarks, third-party validation") demonstrably failed to address the pre-revenue startup scenario A had specifically named. This was the most sustained, progressively sharpened, and ultimately unanswered line of attack in the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A made a solid pro-presumption case on network effects, under-enforcement, information asymmetry, and irreversible false negatives. But Side B won the central comparison by pressing that the proposed safety valve is vague and close to unusable for early-stage acquisitions, and that false positives could chill the startup funding pipeline. Side A mostly answered this with assertions that venture capital would pivot and that platforms could show benchmarks or roadmaps, but did not make those answers convincing enough. Side B also framed its closing more comparatively, turning A's irreversibility argument against the presumption itself.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's attack on A's answers to the pressure questions was decisive: it argued that technical benchmarks and roadmaps do not prove substantial consumer benefit for pre-revenue startups, so the rebuttable presumption would function as a near-ban while also weakening startup formation.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B won by successfully proving that the proposed 'safety valve' is practically impossible to meet for early-stage startups, effectively acting as a ban. Side B also decisively won the weighing on irreversible harm by turning Side A's argument, demonstrating that chilling venture capital creates an 'invisible graveyard' of unfunded startups that is just as permanent as an entrenched monopoly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2 systematically dismantled Side A's claim that venture capital would simply pivot to independent IPOs, pointing out that cutting off the primary liquidity event causes capital to flee, not pivot.
