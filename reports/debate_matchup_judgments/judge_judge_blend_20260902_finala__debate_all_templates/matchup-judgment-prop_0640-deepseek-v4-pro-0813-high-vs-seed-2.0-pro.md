### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won by preserving a clear causal case: scalable institutional accumulation raises entry barriers, separates control from farming, and reduces distributed decision-making. Side B offered a sharp liquidity-and-cartel countermodel, but repeatedly treated a cap as a total ban and asserted without support that institutions are the only outside bidder and that A conceded “90%” of the harm. Those overclaims made B’s false-choice framing less persuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to the incompleteness objection was decisive: leaving other absentee ownership unregulated does not show that limiting the most scalable accumulator is harmful. A also exposed that institutional competition need not break local cartels because funds may simply become major landlords or lease to dominant operators.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by maintaining a consistent, unrefuted economic mechanism: institutional buyers uniquely divorce land prices from farm income because they buy for inflation-hedging and portfolio scaling. Side A's strongest push is that the cap is "performative" because it leaves local cartels untouched, but A concedes under pressure (A4.2) that institutions might just act as absentee landlords anyway, weakening their claim that institutions break up monopolies. B successfully frames the policy as a necessary, if incomplete, step to tether land prices back to agricultural reality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 expertly handles A's pressure questions, pointing out that an incomplete solution is not a reason to leave the most scalable absentee accumulator unregulated, and exposing the flaw in A's assumption about tenant stewardship.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A demonstrated that the cap is underinclusive to the point of being counterproductive: it removes the only transparent outside bidder while leaving larger unregulated absentee owners untouched, strengthening local cartels and reducing liquidity without placing additional acreage into working farmers' hands. Side B's theoretical framework distinguishing capital from control was coherent, but never overcame Side A's live causal story that the policy would backfire on its own stated goals of protecting rural communities and resilience.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 [A4.1] reframed Side B's incompleteness defense into an active harm, arguing that removing institutional buyers forces retiring farmers to sell only to local cartels at lower prices, consolidating rather than dispersing control.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing that the proposed cap poorly targets the alleged harms and carries meaningful costs for farm capital and retiring owners. Side A had a plausible affordability mechanism, but repeatedly relied on unsupported universal claims, brittle figures, and an ungrounded 2022 example. It also failed to resolve who would actually buy the land or absorb the loss in retiring farmers' wealth. Side B sometimes overstated a cap as a ban, but its conduct-regulation alternative and comparative analysis remained stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered both pressure questions, challenged the assumed causal link between absentee ownership and drought idling, and used Side A's concessions on evasion and credit to narrow the cap's case to lower prices alone.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A wins by cleanly neutralizing Side B's practical examples and maintaining a focused comparative advantage. Side A successfully argued that conduct harms should be addressed through conduct regulation (supported by plausible mechanisms like satellite monitoring) rather than a blunt ownership cap. Side A also capitalized on Side B's failure to address how young farmers would outbid large industrial farms, turning Side B's core mechanism into a harm against retiring farmers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 directly dismantled Side B's 2022 drought example by offering plausible alternative causes (water cuts, insurance) and correctly noting that a cap wouldn't prevent idling anyway.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by showing the identified harms are conduct-based rather than inherent to institutional ownership, so the cap misses the target while imposing real costs. CON successfully established that existing agricultural compliance systems already regulate leases, soil, and water without perfection; that a cap does not supply credit to young farmers or prevent developer purchases; and that B’s 2022 idling example conflated ownership with water access. B’s closing “fails safe” framing was undermined by CON’s demonstration that the cap would strand retiring farmers and weaken patient capital without touching the actual conduct harms. CON’s rebuttals were sharper, directly answered the pressure questions, and left B’s causal link between deed status and stewardship largely unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s dismantling of PRO’s rural-community and young-farmer benefit claims, where CON showed that a cap removes buyers without creating credit, depressing prices and leaving developers and large industrial farms—not young operators—as the likely purchasers while eroding retiring farmers’ wealth.
