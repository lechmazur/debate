### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won a competitive debate by forcing PRO to concede no historical episode of cheaper cognitive tools shrinking total white-collar employment, then reducing PRO’s key “savings exceed complements” mechanism to ordinary productivity growth that repeatedly failed as an elimination forecast. A’s latent-demand and quality-elasticity reply better answered the net-jobs comparison; A also damaged distinctive early-prep policy by showing portable benefits are ordinary safety-net tools and occupation-loss triggers fire on ordinary churn. PRO’s breadth/scalability and sparse-complements story remained coherent but never cleared the base-rate and burden problems A kept live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.4–A4.5: turning B’s “wherever adoption is economical, savings exceed complementary labor costs” into the definition of productivity growth that wrongly predicted mass white-collar elimination after ATMs, spreadsheets, and prior tools.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): PRO won by isolating the decisive comparison: cheaper cognitive output expands markets, but it does not automatically expand human labor demand faster than AI productivity. CON proved output and new categories would grow, yet never showed why those new tasks would remain human-performed rather than AI-assisted, especially after PRO distinguished AI's breadth from narrower precedents like ATMs and spreadsheets. On policy, PRO's contingent trigger framework—activating only on observed displacement and sunsetting after recovery—answered CON's ordinary-churn objection and captured the motion's risk asymmetry. CON's base-rate framing was powerful, but PRO's epistemic critique—that aggregate historical growth confounds technology-specific job creation—left the induction too blunt to override the concrete micro-mechanism of scalable substitution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's first rebuttal distinguishing AI's encroachment on interpretation, drafting, and communication from the narrow task automation of ATMs and spreadsheets, which defanged CON's core historical analogy and shifted the burden to showing labor demand would expand faster than productivity.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side A wins decisively by trapping Side B in a historical and logical bind. Side B's core argument—that AI's labor savings will outpace the cost of new oversight and thus reduce headcount—is brilliantly dismantled by Side A, who points out that this exact dynamic (savings exceeding complementary costs) is the standard definition of productivity growth that has historically *expanded* white-collar employment due to quality-elasticity and competitive demand. Side A effectively shifts the burden, forcing Side B to rely solely on AI's "breadth," which Side A counters by arguing breadth just means larger reallocation, not net loss. On policy, Side A neatly dissects Side B's "triggers," showing they are either generic safety-net tools (which CON already supports) or misdirected interventions based on a flawed premise. Side A's rhetoric is exceptionally sharp, crystallizing the clash into a clear choice between a recurring historical error and a misdiagnosed policy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it weaponizes Side B's concession that "wherever adoption is economical, the savings necessarily exceed those complementary labor costs" by pointing out this is true of the printing press, ATM, and spreadsheet—all of which increased net white-collar employment—thus exposing Side B's mechanism as a recurring historical error rather than a novel AI insight.
