### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): The debate turned on CON's Pressure Question ("what happens to a law-abiding immigrant who never masters the language or accepts the norms?"). PRO's answer conceded exactly the structural claim it had spent the debate denying: such an immigrant remains a legal resident but is denied citizenship, certain public-sector jobs, and welfare benefits until compliance. CON seized on this ("that is exactly the probation class you claim to oppose") and used it devastatingly in both Rebuttal 2 and Closing, tying it back to PRO's own abused-mother example to argue PRO's model deepens rather than escapes the trap. PRO's attempt to rehabilitate its causal story with a Canada counter-example was reasonable but CON's rebuttal (selection effects, settlement funding) went largely unanswered in PRO's closing, which mostly reasserted earlier framing rather than repairing the exposed contradiction. CON also maintained a consistent, coherent alternative framework (rights/tools now, fluency later) throughout, while PRO's case fractured under its own pressure-round admission. PRO's two clipped speeches are also a real execution cost. CON built the clearer, more resolved path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Question forcing PRO to admit its model denies citizenship, public jobs, and welfare to law-abiding immigrants who don't meet the language/norms baseline—followed by CON's closing turn of that admission against PRO's own "trapped mother" example.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing that A's supposedly equal baseline actually creates unequal legal membership: law-abiding immigrants could be denied citizenship, public jobs, and benefits for lacking fluency or approved attitudes, while native-born citizens face no equivalent condition. B also offered a coherent alternative of immediate rights, translation, schooling, and practical support. A had credible arguments about language access and vulnerability, but overclaimed from France, the UK, and Canada, was clipped twice, and ultimately made access to some resources conditional on the integration it said those resources would enable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response to A4.1 was decisive. A directly specified penalties for a law-abiding immigrant who failed its baseline, and B showed that this conceded the two-tier system at the heart of its critique while also undermining A's vulnerable-mother example.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): CON wins by exposing that PRO's model creates the two-tier system it claims to prevent. PRO conceded that law-abiding immigrants who do not master the language would be denied citizenship, public jobs, and some welfare—a permanent probation class that CON crystallized as a "cultural cover charge." CON's rights-first framework offered a cleaner liberal alternative and directly answered PRO's vulnerable-woman scenarios by showing that conditional access withholds the very resources needed to escape harm. PRO's recurring clipped speeches and inability to defend policing unwritten norms without licensing state overreach into private belief further weakened their case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's overturning of the two-tier critique in B2.1 and B4.1-B4.2, demonstrating that PRO's conditional membership model—denying citizenship and benefits to law-abiding immigrants who lack fluency—is itself the actual two-tier system, with the closing reinforcing this concession as the decisive collapse of PRO's case.
