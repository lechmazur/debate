### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A built a more internally consistent comparative case across the full debate. Its core anchor—that deterrence research establishes certainty of apprehension matters more than severity of punishment—was used with discipline throughout and was actually conceded by Side B in Rebuttal 2 ("deterrence research shows certainty of being caught matters more than severity"), which then undermined B's own claim that a long sentence is more personally certain than a visible officer standing on the exact corner. That self-contradiction was the most damaging single moment in the debate. Side A's pressure questions were also sharper: Q2 exposed a genuine double standard—displacement supposedly nullifies hot-spot policing while network replacement is said not to nullify incarceration—and Side B's answer in Rebuttal 2 was the weakest passage of the debate, essentially asserting without strong reasoning that a successor's sentencing risk slows the cycle more than a geographic shift does. Side A's geographic framing ("you cannot incarcerate a street corner") crystallized a real and unanswered comparative point: incapacitation removes a person but leaves the territory, crew, and feud intact, meaning the intervention point is mis-targeted. Side B's strongest argument—displacement—was answered adequately (violence is territorial, displacement is itself evidence the tactic works locally, and the move is not as frictionless as asserted). Side B's accountability-kills-proactivity argument was repeatedly asserted but never survived Side A's rebuttal distinguishing focused deterrence from indiscriminate stops. The debate was genuine and competitive; Side B did land real pressure on displacement and incapacitation durability, keeping this from being a blowout.

This concession-then-pivot was the most decisive exchange, handing Side A's deterrence-certainty argument more force than Side B intended.
