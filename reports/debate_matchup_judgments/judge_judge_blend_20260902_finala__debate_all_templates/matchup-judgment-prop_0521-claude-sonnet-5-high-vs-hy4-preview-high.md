- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by sustaining the distinction between portable credentials and portable employer funding. A’s employer-cutback mechanism remained the stronger live route, despite some overstatement of its own.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s decisive rebuttal was that building-trades portability works because all employers contribute to a jointly governed pool, not because one employer’s unused stipend simply follows a worker to its rival. Side A proved that without a mandatory sector-wide pool, making individual accounts portable destroys the employer's incentive to fund them.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 distinction between collectively financed trades trusts and the motion's individual portable accounts neutralized Side B's primary real-world example.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won the central incentive clash. Side B repeatedly treated diffuse sector-wide gains and possible recruiting benefits as repayment to the specific firm funding training, but Side A showed why neither reliably compensates that firm's immediate cost. Side B also asserted rather than established that training funds are deferred compensation already earned. Its credential-based test reduced the classification objection, but did not answer the stronger concern that mandatory portability would reduce employer-funded training, especially for workers unable to self-fund.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's answers in Rebuttal 2 were decisive: the paying firm cannot book a diffuse benefit to its sector, and a certain present training expense is not symmetric with the uncertain possibility of later recruiting someone trained elsewhere. This directly defeated Side B's main answer to the free-rider problem.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's economic mechanisms and highlighting a critical contradiction in Side B's framework. Side A successfully argued that individual firms fund training for retention, and Side A's distinction between concrete firm outlays and diffuse 'sector gains' neutralized Side B's main impact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Turn 8 response to Side B's 'sector gain' argument decisively showed that because the sector doesn't write the check, aggregate benefits cannot justify individual firm budgets, directly linking to the consequence of lost training for low-wage workers.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won the central clash on what the employer buys. PRO framed the account as deferred pay for past labor, but A showed it funds future output, so retention IS the return. A exploited B's concession [B2.2] that clawback is fine when leaving the sector - exposing that PRO keeps the investment-protection principle but exempts the most damaging exit to a rival. The sector-gains vs firm-loses abstraction and the certainty-asymmetry (known cost vs maybe-hire) left PRO's free-rider answer [B2.2] unanswered, making CON's claim that portability shrinks training for low-wage workers the live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.4-A2.5 / B4.3-A4.2 exchange: CON turns PRO's own clawback exception into proof that return-protection is legitimate, then shows PRO arbitrarily carves out competitor moves where firm-level return is fully lost, collapsing PRO's sector-level compensation story.
