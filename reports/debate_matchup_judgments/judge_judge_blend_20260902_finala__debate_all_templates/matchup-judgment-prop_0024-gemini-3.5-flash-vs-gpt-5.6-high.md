- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): A ran a clean, conventional supply-side case (filtering, deadweight loss of price caps) but largely repeated the same points across rounds without closing the gaps B opened. B's key strategic move—disaggregating "rent control" from broader "tenant-protection expansions" (just-cause eviction, anti-retaliation, fair hearings)—exploited the resolution's own bundling and forced A to prove that non-price protections suppress construction, which A never substantiated beyond a bare "risk premium" assertion in Rebuttal 2. B also pressed the "permission is not homes" and timing/targeting critique of filtering throughout, and A's answer to the direct pressure question about guaranteed timely supply was largely a restatement rather than a mechanism. B's closing crystallized this asymmetry cleanly (shortage-address vs. affordability-delivered), while A's closing mostly re-asserted its opening thesis without engaging the live tenant-protection/rent-control distinction. B's Rebuttal 2 was clipped for length, a minor execution cost, but the core analytical work was already delivered before the cutoff.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 point that A answered "permission, not homes" with assertion rather than mechanism, combined with B's insistence that just-cause eviction and anti-retaliation rules are not rent caps and were never shown to suppress construction — a challenge A's later "risk premium" answer did not adequately meet.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by successfully narrowing the debate to a comparative effectiveness question and showing that PRO could not bridge the gap between permitting reform and actually delivered, timely affordability. CON separated non-price tenant protections—such as just-cause eviction and anti-retaliation rules—from crude rent freezes, demonstrating that these measures prevent displacement directly without suppressing new construction, a burden PRO never squarely met. CON also undermined PRO’s filtering and “sponge” arguments by highlighting induced demand, land-value effects, and the fatal timing problem: a tenant evicted next month cannot wait for a years-long vacancy chain. PRO’s scarcity framework remained intuitively plausible, but its concession that zoning reform offers no timely guarantee and its speculative “risk premium” response to CON’s pressure left its comparative superiority claim unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s first rebuttal, where it dismantled PRO’s filtering mechanism as indirect and vulnerable to induced demand and land-value effects, separated modern rent stabilization with new-construction exemptions from a crude freeze, and showed that just-cause and anti-retaliation rules are not rent caps—thereby exposing PRO’s burden to prove supply expansion beats protections that do not constrain construction.

A effectively attacked B's "exempt new construction" defense by highlighting the two-tier market and the harm to newcomers, a point B struggled to fully resolve. - **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A built a tighter, more internally consistent case around a single clear mechanism—vacancy chains and "shrinking scarcity vs. rationing it"—and used that frame to consistently pressure B's claims. Both sides followed Q&A formatting correctly, but A's answers to pressure were sharper and more responsive, giving it the cleaner, more decisive path to the judgment.

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built the stronger path to victory by framing affordability as a shortage problem and showing that rent control redistributes a fixed scarcity while streamlining supply actually creates homes. B showed that a new unit houses a household who would otherwise bid on older stock, neutralizing the “luxury” objection, and then made this the clear weighing device for the debate.
