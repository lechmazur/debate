### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully proving that the policy targets the wrong access point. Side A's 'selection effect' argument cleanly dissects Side B's 'friction' mechanism, showing that the policy mostly deters low-risk buyers while high-risk buyers simply shift to illegal handgun markets—exactly the tradeoff the proposition highlights.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's distinction in Rebuttal 2 between total method restriction (like jumping barriers) and a mere retail purchase limit, which leaves household and informal access wide open during acute crises.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side A (CON) built the stronger path by establishing the core mismatch between the policy's narrow legal channels and the actual routes (illegal handguns, household access, informal transfers) driving homicide and urgent suicide, then using that to show weak net reduction after substitutions. Side B answered pressures directly but over-relied on broad lethality and friction without fully neutralizing selection effects or enforcement limits. Clash narrowed clearly around whether blocked purchases save lives at scale.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON Rebuttal 1 and answers to pressure Qs: highlighted private-sales enforcement weakness and selection (violent actors pivot easily while low-risk wait), directly undercutting B's assumption of broad homicide/suicide reduction.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): CON wins by successfully decoupling the policy's mechanism from the actual acquisition pathways driving youth homicide and suicide. While PRO effectively leverages the lethality gap and the theoretical value of friction during impulsive crises, CON's selection critique proves decisive: the marginal buyers actually stopped by a purchase-age limit are disproportionately low-risk, while high-risk actors already rely on household access, informal networks, borrowing, or theft. CON sharply demonstrates that an age limit on retail/private purchase does not function as a true access barrier at the moment of crisis, meaning the projected reduction in deaths is narrow while the shift toward illegal acquisition concentrates among the very population driving the violence. PRO's friction argument remains plausible but fails to overcome CON's empirical mismatch between the legal bottleneck and real-world high-risk behavior.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 dismantles PRO's core mechanism by distinguishing broad environmental method restriction (e.g., gas detox, bridge barriers) from a narrow purchase-age limit. CON shows that the latter leaves household guns, social borrowing, and informal transfers entirely untouched, directly neutralizing PRO's "waiting is survival" claim and clarifying why the policy fails to intercept weapons at the actual moment of crisis for the highest-risk groups.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by maintaining a clear, consistent causal mechanism: raising the age acts as a filter that introduces friction, which disproportionately stops impulsive, transient crises from becoming immediately lethal. Side A argued well that determined actors will substitute or use illegal networks, but ultimately conceded that the law filters "marginal actors." Side B successfully capitalized on this by showing that filtering those exact marginal, impulsive actors is the core purpose of the policy and the primary driver of net death reductions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal effectively dismantled Side A's "gang network" argument by pointing out that 18-to-20-year-old violence also heavily includes domestic disputes, suicidal crises, and first offenses, where the "magic vending machine" of the black market is not readily accessible.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B built the stronger overall case by centering the proposition's focus on firearm rates and demonstrating a plausible causal mechanism: the policy filters immediate legal access for a high-risk impulsive age group where guns convert transient crises into deaths more reliably than substitutes, while illegal channels add real friction for marginal actors. Side A effectively highlighted substitution and peer networks but reframed the motion toward total deaths (against the proposition's wording on firearm rates) and did not sufficiently neutralize B's asymmetry arguments on why offsets remain incomplete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's direct A1/A2 answers in Rebuttal 2, stressing incomplete substitution for impulsive scenarios and peer-network limits on hardened offenders, most damaged A's offsets while staying within live clashes.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): CON wins by successfully turning PRO's core mechanism. PRO argued that blocking legal purchases adds friction that stops impulsive, marginal offenders. CON dismantled this by showing that peer networks make illegal acquisition trivial for the exact demographic most likely to commit violence, meaning the age limit primarily filters isolated, low-risk individuals. CON also effectively deployed the hanging substitution argument, noting that cords and fixed points are universally available at home, which neutralizes PRO's delay benefit for impulsive suicides. PRO's rhetoric was sharp, but three clipped turns and a failure to fully answer the network-access turn or meet the total-death burden cost them the round. CON cleanly crystallized why PRO's own premises undermined the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 response on peer networks and hanging availability, which inverted PRO's friction and impulsivity arguments by demonstrating that high-risk offenders face trivial acquisition delays through older friends, while impulsive suicidal actors still have immediate access to a nearly equally lethal substitute at home.
