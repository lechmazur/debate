### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): PRO (Side B) edges out CON on three decisive grounds. First, PRO's pressure question Q1 exposed a genuine logical tension: CON simultaneously argued domestic courts are too weak and politically compromised to run criminal cases against powerful actors, yet defended civil fines as more reliably enforceable through those same systems. CON's answer—that administrative enforcement is lower-profile and less politically costly than criminal prosecution—is partially valid, but it does not explain how civil fines would actually be collected when the actors in question are powerful enough to resist criminal proceedings entirely. Second, PRO's core "priced in as a business expense" argument was never structurally dismantled. CON repeated that civil fines are more immediate and enforceable without ever showing that large fines actually deter the dominant actors driving catastrophic harm; the mechanism linking civil penalties to changed corporate or state behavior was asserted but not demonstrated. Third, PRO's complementarity argument—that the ICC functions as a backstop forcing domestic codification and prosecution—was the more sophisticated structural claim and CON never adequately answered why domestic systems that lack independence to run criminal ecocide trials would nonetheless run robust civil enforcement. CON's strongest unrebutted point was sovereign immunity for sitting ministers and state actors, which PRO never fully addressed and which exposed a real coverage gap in the personal liability argument. CON also had a live claim about weaponization by wealthy states against Global South development. These were genuine live wounds on PRO's case, making the margin clear but competitive rather than dominant. PRO won the framing war and the pressure questions exchange more decisively than it won the substantive clash, but that combination is sufficient for a clear win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 in the Pressure Questions round was the most decisive exchange: if domestic courts are too corrupt or politically compromised to enforce criminal penalties (CON's own premise), how can those same courts reliably enforce the civil fines CON prefers? CON's answer in Rebuttal 2—that administrative fines require lower political capital to collect than criminal prosecutions—was plausible but left the hardest cases (state-owned enterprises, powerful foreign executives, sitting ministers) largely unaddressed, undercutting CON's positive alternative rather than just attacking PRO's proposal.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins narrowly because its central comparative claim survived: civil fines are often priced in by institutions, while international criminal recognition creates personal risk for top decision-makers. Side A did overclaim some legal mechanisms, but made the clearer case that imperfect criminal liability is still a necessary escalation beyond the current regulatory model.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was the attack on complementarity, immunity, and extradition limits; however Side A’s pressure-question and closing turn that civil fines are easier precisely because they do not seriously threaten powerful actors was the most decisive comparative rebuttal.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO built the stronger comparative case by showing that personal criminal liability fundamentally alters incentives where civil fines are simply priced in as costs of business. The exchange forced CON into a narrower defensive distinction in Rebuttal 2 and revealed a persistent crack in CON's enforcement story.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON built and sustained the debate's sharpest analytical pressure: the "redundant or intrusive" dilemma [A2.1–A2.2]. If an act is already illegal domestically, international prosecution is redundant; if it is legally authorized by a sovereign state, the ICC must override that sovereign legal determination to act—which is precisely the politicized overreach CON warned against. PRO's complementarity response [B4.1] was the right move but never closed this gap: complementarity applies when states are unwilling or unable to prosecute under their own law, not when they have affirmatively authorized the conduct as lawful. CON correctly identified this distinction in rebuttal [A4.1], and PRO never directly answered it. CON also maintained a genuine and damaging distinction between hidden atrocities (where universal jurisdiction already works) and state-sponsored, openly subsidized economic industries, making the torture analogy PRO kept deploying imperfect and costly for PRO's enforcement story. PRO's concrete examples (Niger Delta, Amazon) were vivid and its status-quo-failure argument had genuine force, and PRO correctly noted that CON's preferred alternatives have also failed for decades—CON's closing did not fully resolve this. But PRO's two main positive arguments (definition with guardrails; enforcement via third-state arrest) were both materially damaged by CON without clean repair: the guardrails argument did not resolve the legal authorization problem, and the arrest-during-travel mechanism, while real, does not address the diplomatic retaliation consequences when states openly treat extractive executives as architects of national economic strategy. On balance, CON's logical attacks on PRO's central claims were more damaging than PRO's rebuttals were to CON's, giving CON a clear but not dominant edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's [A2.1–A2.2] "redundant or intrusive" dilemma—if the act is already unlawful domestically, the international charge is superfluous; if it is legally authorized, the ICC must criminalize sovereign development policy—was the debate's most analytically precise strike. PRO's complementarity response in [B4.1] pointed to the right mechanism but failed to address CON's follow-up in [A4.1]: legal authorization by the state means the state is neither unwilling nor unable to prosecute, so complementarity cannot trigger ICC jurisdiction without first unilaterally overriding a lawful domestic determination. This exchange remained live and unresolved through the closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A built a plausible justice-gap case for deterrence and universal jurisdiction, but relied too much on asserting that threshold language would prevent overreach and that diplomatic backlash would be minor. Side B made the decisive comparison clearer: ecocide prosecutions would often target open, state-authorized economic policy rather than hidden rogue atrocities, making enforcement politically unlike torture or war-crimes cases. Side B also pressed the dilemma that if conduct is already illegal domestically, international criminalization adds less, while if it is legal domestically, courts must criminalize sovereign development choices. Side A answered, but mostly by repeating guardrails rather than resolving the hardest cases.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange was the rebuttal that universal jurisdiction for torture is a false analogy because ecocide would target state-sponsored extractive policy, creating retaliation and chilling development rather than quiet prosecutions of rogue actors.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): CON built the cleaner structural critique by exposing a persistent redundancy-overreach dilemma in PRO's definition and a false equivalence in PRO's enforcement analogy. PRO vividly demonstrated the impunity gap but never fully resolved how the ICC would avoid either duplicating weak domestic law or overriding sovereign legal development choices, nor how universal jurisdiction would function against state-sponsored actors without the diplomatic catastrophes CON predicted. CON's rebuttals sharpened the decisive clash more effectively across rounds, leaving PRO's enforcement model theoretically appealing but practically incoherent.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1-B2.2 redundancy-overreach trap: if ecocide targets acts already illegal domestically it is redundant, but if it targets legal state-sanctioned development it becomes sovereign overreach, a contradiction PRO never fully escaped despite appeals to complementarity and threshold standards.
