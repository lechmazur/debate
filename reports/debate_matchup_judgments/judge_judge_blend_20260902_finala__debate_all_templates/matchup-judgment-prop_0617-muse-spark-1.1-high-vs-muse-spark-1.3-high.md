### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by showing that Side B's presumption turns unavoidable uncertainty about future benefits into a barrier against beneficial scaling. A directly answered the information-asymmetry case with existing investigative powers and convincingly challenged B's claim that false blocks cause only delay or paperwork. B's treatment of venture incentives was also too dismissive, and it never adequately answered the modern TikTok counterexample to permanent tipping. B framed the stakes memorably, but its claim that Android and YouTube would receive near-automatic clearance relied heavily on hindsight and an underdefined benefits standard.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's attack on B's “morning clearance” answer was decisive: if Android's benefits were genuinely verifiable in 2005, B needed more than a speculative investment plan. This exposed tension between demanding proof of substantial benefit and claiming uncertain, unprecedented projects would easily pass.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that in tipping markets, false approvals lock in monopoly irreversibly while false blocks can be fixed with conditions or refiling, and by defining a workable forward-looking standard—verifiable investment and openness commitments—that lets pro-competitive deals clear while catching nascent rival removals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal distinguishing expansions backed by concrete investment and interoperability commitments (Android/YouTube) from silent removals of rivals (Instagram/Waze), directly answering Side B's pressure questions with a standard of verifiable commitments rather than future certainty.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully defending the "substantial consumer benefit" standard against Side A's strongest attack: that it would kill speculative but highly beneficial deals like Android. Side B's distinction between verifiable, funded expansion plans (which would pass the test) and silent acqui-hires (which would fail) was decisive and effectively neutralized Side A's primary examples. Furthermore, Side B won the crucial error-cost weighing by persuasively arguing that a blocked beneficial deal can be restructured and refiled, whereas an approved killer acquisition permanently tips the market. While Side A's argument about chilling venture capital funding was strong, Side B's "build-to-compete versus build-to-sell" reframe handled it cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they answered the Android pressure question by explaining that a heavily funded, open-source expansion plan is exactly the kind of verifiable benefit their test rewards, effectively defanging Side A's claim that the presumption would automatically kill speculative innovation.
