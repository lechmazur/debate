### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B built a tighter, more disciplined case around a clear asymmetry (physical bottleneck vs. diffuse commercial cost) and exploited a genuine logical tension in Side A's position: if intermediate chips and software optimization were "close enough" for military-relevant capability, competitors' desperate, costly pursuit of frontier chips would make no sense. Side A never squarely resolved this tension, instead reasserting that desperation merely accelerates indigenous capacity without explaining why states would burn massive capital if substitutes were already adequate. B also correctly reframed the burden in rebuttal 2 (the standard isn't "never any drag," but "drag smaller than military gain"), which A's pressure question had inadvertently invited by demanding an unreasonable "never" metric. A's core claims—that high-margin volume loss meaningfully slows U.S. process nodes and that the resulting lag is 'self-cancelling'—were asserted with real force but not substantiated against B's repeated point that this required stacking multiple speculative assumptions, while B's physical-constraint story stayed comparatively concrete throughout. Both sides handled the Q&A format correctly, but B's closing better crystallized the decisive clash and used A's own concession (that the lag window is real, possibly a decade) against it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1's turn on A's "temporary friction" claim—arguing that competitors' desperate pursuit of frontier chips itself proves those chips are not fungible with intermediate substitutes—was the most damaging exchange, and A never fully neutralized it in the rounds that followed.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. It established an immediate physical bottleneck, contrasted it with a less certain chain from lost sales to decisive U.S. decline, and turned A's concession that fabrication parity could take years or a decade into substantial military value. A persuasively showed that mature chips retain operational utility and that controls encourage self-sufficiency, but never demonstrated that those effects outweigh the conceded frontier-compute delay. B somewhat overstated total exclusion and underanswered circumvention, yet still supplied the cleaner comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 most decisively argued that A needed both severe commercial drag and successful indigenous parity, while converting A's admitted multi-year fabrication lag into a compounding advantage in doctrine, force structure, and deterrence.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) keeps the asymmetric comparison alive: physical denial via EUV, fabs, and allied supply chains is immediate and total, while commercial harm is marginal because domestic+allied markets are the bulk of demand. Side A (CON) asserts optimized inference and indigenous fabs deliver close-enough military value and that controls self-cancel, but never resolves B's pressure: if intermediate nodes are enough, why desperate pursuit of frontier chips, and why assume decade-scale fab parity is guaranteed. B correctly reframes the burden to comparative magnitude, not zero harm, and shows years of denied training compute compound into doctrine/deterrence before any indigenous base matures. That cleaner weighing sustains the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R1 flip that massive state capital flowing to domestic fabs proves, not disproves, that compute is the bottleneck, sharpened by Q1 in Pressure - if intermediate nodes were sufficient, frontier-chip smuggling would be irrational - and A's A1 answer concedes frontier still accelerates progress while relying on speculative parity.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but CON (A) built the more dynamic and adaptive case. A's contradiction catch—that PRO cannot simultaneously claim lost China sales don't matter to U.S. revenue and that those sales are large enough to meaningfully arm adversaries—was never fully resolved by PRO, who could only gesture at "higher-margin remaining demand" without reconciling the tension. A's Huawei analogy (forced self-sufficiency permanently removes leverage versus temporary dependency) was grounded and went largely unanswered on its strongest point: that Huawei "surviving" as an independent supplier is strategically worse than continued dependency, regardless of parity. A also introduced a sharp late reframe distinguishing frontier training chips (where PRO's scarcity argument is strongest) from deployed/edge military AI chips (easier to domesticate), and PRO's closing never engaged this point, leaving a live gap. PRO's physical-scarcity mechanism (EUV tooling, packaging, interconnect) was the strongest single argument in the debate and A never fully neutralized it beyond conceding no fixed timeline, but PRO's failure to close the revenue-contradiction loop and its silence on the edge-processor rebuttal in closing cost it the tighter final exchanges.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 point that PRO cannot claim lost Chinese chip sales are both revenue-irrelevant to U.S. innovation and militarily significant enough to justify restriction was the sharpest, most damaging catch of the debate, and PRO's later answer (geopolitical risk "stripped out") never fully resolved it.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing a clearer counterfactual: forced substitution may accelerate, but access to restricted frontier chips would accelerate military AI much faster, while hard tooling, yield, packaging, and interconnect bottlenecks preserve a strategically valuable delay. Side A’s dynamic framing was strong, but it largely asserted that catch-up and lost revenue would compound without showing that these harms outweigh immediate denial, especially amid scarce supply and substantial alternative demand. Its edge-processor argument also arrived late.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s answer that permanent divergence is unnecessary was decisive. It neutralized Side A’s demand for a permanent silicon gap and refocused the motion on whether a multi-year delay outweighs industry harm, which Side A never convincingly disproved.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by turning PRO's static snapshot into a dynamic trade: showing the multi-year lag narrows while revenue loss, flywheel erosion, and third-party hedging compound. A exposed PRO's capacity-as-binding vs revenue-drives-capacity contradiction, corrected the strawman on Huawei/leverage in A4.2, and narrowed the military value with edge-inference. PRO kept asserting physical scarcity but never answered why the window doesn't narrow or why losing leverage is preferable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R1 [A2.3-A2.7] - turning Huawei from 'not parity' into permanent loss of leverage, exposing the revenue/capacity contradiction, and extending harm to neutral customers hedging against politically conditional supply.
