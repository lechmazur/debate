### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO)): B won by making the timing asymmetry decisive: hard-to-retrofit envelope choices are cheapest before walls are closed, while later retrofits are voluntary, disruptive, and likely underused. A had a real affordability and housing-supply objection, but it depended on treating readiness as expensive near-Passivhaus performance and asserting large supply suppression without enough grounding. B successfully framed that as inflating the mandate beyond the motion and turned the clean-grid argument by noting inefficient homes force unnecessary generation overbuild.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal was most decisive: it directly answered the cost questions, separated net-zero-ready from net-zero-now, and used A's concession that later retrofits are not legally required to show CON's model likely locks in decades of waste.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won by maintaining a clearer, more coherent path to victory through the full exchange. A's central asymmetry— that envelope work is cheap when timed with construction but expensive and destructive as retrofit—was never squarely answered by B. Instead, B oscillated between claiming readiness required expensive structural specs and asserting that standard modern homes were already efficient enough, never reconciling the tension. A's most damaging move came in Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2, where A turned B's own concession that second buyers are "not legally forced to retrofit" into a trap: under B's model, the cheap construction step is skipped and the later retrofit is voluntary, meaning inefficient envelopes persist for decades and the clean grid must overbuild to feed waste. B never escaped this trap, and A crystallized it effectively in the closing. A also successfully separated the low bar of "readiness" (conduit, basic insulation in open walls, orientation) from B's inflated strawman of certified Passivhaus luxury, blunting the affordability objection. While B's housing-affordability argument was competitive and rhetorically potent, it depended on the premium being large and retrofits being reliable, and A showed neither condition held. The debate was competitive but A built the stronger comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A in Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7) trapped Side B between two conceded positions: B's affordability case required the construction premium to be large, while B's emissions case required retrofits to actually happen—yet B had already conceded that second buyers are not legally forced to retrofit, meaning the later fix is largely optional. A showed that under B's world, neither the cheap upfront step nor the later retrofit occurs, leaving leaky envelopes to waste clean energy for 80 years. B never recovered from this turn.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by decisively controlling the definition of "net-zero-ready" and exploiting CON's concession that future retrofits would be voluntary. PRO's core asymmetry—that envelope work is cheap during construction but ruinous later—remained intact because CON had to inflate the spec to "double-stud framing" to make the cost argument work, which PRO correctly identified as conflating "ready" with "certified perfect." PRO also effectively turned CON's grid argument by noting that leaky homes waste clean energy, forcing expensive grid overbuild. CON's affordability concerns were legitimate but ultimately relied on an overstated cost premium and failed to answer why voluntary, disruptive retrofits would reliably happen later. PRO's weighing in the final speeches crystallized this clash cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 directly answering the pressure questions by separating cheap timing-based steps from expensive optional specs, then pivoting to weigh the debate around CON's fatal concession that second buyers aren't forced to retrofit, leaving CON's model reliant on a fix that "mostly never happens."

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A won by making the decisive comparison about scale and priority clearer: new builds are a small share of the stock, while existing homes dominate emissions, so the “rather than retrofits” ordering misallocates scarce money and political capital. Side B had a plausible prevention case, but leaned too heavily on repeated assertions that upfront costs are negligible and future retrofits are ruinous, without sufficiently answering A’s marginal housing-supply objection or the per-ton comparison. A’s interpretation of “rather” was somewhat aggressive, and the clipped Rebuttal 2 costs execution, but B effectively accepted the sequencing clash and never fully displaced A’s cheapest-tons-first framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange was against B’s faucet/backlog argument: A conceded new-build standards can help but argued the faucet is only one-to-two percent while the existing-stock “drain” is the overwhelming present emissions problem, so prioritizing new-build perfection over retrofits loses on scale and urgency.

PRO also failed to engage CON's live affordability argument about the marginal unit that fails to get built, repeatedly answering with energy bills for homes that do exist rather than housing supply at the margin. Sharp reframing, consistent weighing, and clearer route to victory give CON the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's repeated demonstration that PRO's affordability answers addressed energy bills while ignoring the marginal home that never gets built, combined with the unanswered "cheapest tons first" comparison, made the prioritization error of the resolution concrete and decisive.

CON also cleanly dismantled PRO’s affordability defense by distinguishing long-term utility savings from upfront development viability, showing how marginal cost increases shelve needed projects in a shortage. PRO’s responses were competent but often reverted to total-cost-of-ownership talking points that sidestepped the live supply and prioritization clashes. CON’s sharper framing, direct pressure answers, and cleaner comparative weighing provide the decisive path to victory.
