### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): This was a genuinely close, technically engaged debate. B (PRO) built a strong initial case: the definitional argument that E2E's whole purpose is eliminating a readable intermediary, the WannaCry example, and the "lose-lose" framing (evasion by sophisticated criminals plus degraded security for billions) were well-reasoned and repeatedly reinforced. A's pressure question forced a real concession from B (A1: live-crime content is sometimes unrecoverable under blocking), and A's closing capitalized on this cleanly, framing "slow but possible" against "categorically impossible" as the decisive public-safety tradeoff. A also proposed a specific mitigation mechanism (per-device hardware enclaves, threshold-split keys, judicial quorum) that gave CON's case more engineering texture than a bare assertion, and used B's own admission about forkability to construct a real tension in B's "mass compromise vs. trivial evasion" claims. B's rebuttal to that tension (different populations bear different costs) was reasonable but was never fully re-defended in the final round because B's closing was cut off mid-argument. B was also clipped in four of five speeches, including the decisive closing, which is a real execution cost per the judging frame: the reader never sees B's final synthesis completed, while A's closing landed intact and unrebutted on the concession point. Substantively the two sides were close, but A's cleaner execution and the last-word advantage on the conceded live-crime gap give it the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure question forcing B to concede that live-crime content recovery is impossible under blocking (A3.1 → B4.1), followed by A's closing exploitation of that concession ("slow is survivable, impossible is not"), was the most decisive exchange, especially since B's attempted rebuttal to A's "contradiction" charge was cut off before it could be reinforced in the closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): A narrowly won the overall comparison. B effectively showed that encryption can obstruct urgent investigations, but its proposed per-device threshold system remained speculative and its claim that bulk compromise would be mathematically useless was insufficiently established. A better weighed the systemic attack surface against benefits that sophisticated targets can partly evade. A's repeated clipping and weak answer that lawful access might be slow kept the debate close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3-A4.4 directly defeated B's claimed contradiction: sophisticated criminals can migrate to unaffected tools while billions of mainstream users still bear added risk. B repeated the contradiction rather than resolving that distinction.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B wins decisively on both substance and execution. Substantively, B brilliantly turned A's "lose-lose" argument (that backdoors compromise everyone while criminals just use other apps) into a logical contradiction: if evasion is trivial, the backdoor cannot be a single global master key that dooms everyone, and network effects keep the most relevant criminals on default apps. Execution-wise, Side A suffered a massive penalty by being clipped for exceeding the word limit in four separate turns, leaving arguments incomplete and failing to fully resolve B's logical traps in the final speeches.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): B (PRO) built a more resilient core mechanism argument — that any lawful-access design still requires the endpoint to produce plaintext on demand, making it inherently extractable by malware or insiders regardless of hardware-binding — and sustained that point across rounds with sharp, memorable framing ("the attacker robs the endpoint, not the HSM," "malware doesn't need your bunker, it calls your mandated API"). A (CON) offered reasonable counter-analogies (secure enclaves, certificate authorities, nuclear command) and a genuine cost argument about unsolved crimes, but never squarely defeated B's structural point that plaintext must exist somewhere reachable, and its final rebuttal and closing were both clipped for exceeding word limits — a real execution cost that weakened delivery of its strongest late-round material. B also answered pressure questions more directly and used the net-safety framing (victims from vulnerability abuse count too) to blunt A's "name a victim quota" question, while A's own quota question was rhetorically pointed but didn't advance a clear comparative metric. Overall B controlled the central clash more convincingly and left a cleaner, less contested line to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's closing rebuttal to A4.1 — arguing that any hardware-bound, per-device mechanism still must let the endpoint reconstruct plaintext, so "the attacker robs the endpoint, not the HSM" — decisively undercut CON's central design defense and went essentially unanswered due to A's clipped final speech.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): A won the overall safety comparison. B plausibly showed investigative benefits and challenged the master-key framing, but relied on an idealized per-device mechanism without adequately weighing insider abuse, authorization infrastructure, authoritarian replication, criminal migration, or the aggregate exposure created by mandated access. A consistently compared those systemic risks against targeted investigative alternatives and limited coverage of adaptable criminals. B also incurred costs from repeated clipping, incorrect reference IDs, and mischaracterizing A as claiming zero cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer to B's hardware-bound proposal was decisive: even scoped access requires endpoints to produce plaintext and an authority capable of issuing valid access tokens. B reduced the likelihood of a universal skeleton key but did not neutralize abuse of that access infrastructure or show why its investigative gains outweighed the broader added attack surface.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A wins solidly due to superior technical rebuttals and a much cleaner execution. Side A successfully dismantled Side B's "per-device, hardware-bound" proposal by explaining that the endpoint must still reconstruct plaintext, making the device itself the vulnerability to malware or insider abuse. Side A also effectively defended the use of consumer E2EE apps in critical infrastructure coordination. Crucially, Side B suffered a severe execution cost by being clipped in three separate speeches (Opening, Rebuttal 2, and Closing), which disrupted its narrative flow and left key thoughts incomplete. Side A's framing of mass everyday prevention versus marginal investigative convenience was highly persuasive and directly answered the prompt's focus on public safety "overall."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's technical takedown of Side B's "hardware-bound per-device" mechanism. Side A clearly explained that because the endpoint must ultimately reconstruct plaintext, the vulnerability shifts from a central bunker to the billion endpoints in the wild, where malware or compromised tokens can bypass the intended legal constraints.
