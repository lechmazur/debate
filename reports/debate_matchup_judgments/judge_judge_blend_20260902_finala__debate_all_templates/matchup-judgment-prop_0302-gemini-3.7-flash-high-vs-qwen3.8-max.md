### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON more successfully controlled the decisive definitional clash over what counts as "structural." CON's opening distinguished concentrated subgroup failure from a sex-wide structural crisis and noted that affluent boys still convert schooling into labor-market success, which directly undercuts PRO's "structural" framing. PRO's strongest move—getting CON to "concede" that assessment norms and discipline practices disadvantage boys—was clever, but PRO never supplied a limiting principle for why that reformable mismatch counts as structural rather than merely a fixable policy failure, and CON's pressure question on this ("what stops every group difference from becoming a structural emergency?") exposed that gap. PRO's answer (scale, institutional origin, persistence) was asserted rather than demonstrated, and its answer to CON's second question (mechanism for affluent boys who still succeed) largely reasserted the claim rather than resolving the tension. CON's closing crystallized this cleanly with "targeted seriousness beats overbroad panic," while PRO's closing mostly recapitulated earlier claims without closing the definitional hole CON had opened.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q1 pressure question ("what limiting principle stops every group difference from becoming a structural emergency?") and PRO's thin, non-mechanistic answer in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive exchange, since CON then used that gap to reframe the whole debate as reformable practices versus a genuine structural class.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by connecting broad, persistent educational gaps to specific institutional practices and explaining why affluent boys having buffers does not negate a structural pattern. Side A offered useful targeting concerns, but its requirement of exclusion or blocked opportunity was too narrow: reformable institutional defaults can still constitute a major structural problem. B also showed that A's proposed literacy, discipline, and vocational reforms substantially conceded the institutional mechanism, though B occasionally overstated causation and used faulty reference labels.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest response was that structural disadvantage need not involve formal exclusion: persistent pipeline-wide gaps generated by default assessment, discipline, and classroom norms can qualify. Its explanation that affluence buffers absolute harm without eliminating relative underperformance directly answered A's best counterexample.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing that persistent gaps, while real, are concentrated among disadvantaged subgroups and driven by reformable practices rather than a universal structural barrier to boys as a class. CON successfully argued that treating male underperformance itself as the major structural problem would scatter resources, obscure intersectional risk, and lacks a limiting principle, whereas targeted reforms in literacy, discipline, counseling, and poverty address the actual levers. PROs institutional-mismatch argument remained plausible but overreached by conflating aggregate gaps with structural anti-boy design and could not fully explain why affluent boys still convert schooling into stable outcomes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CONs rebuttal that PROs definition of structural would make every persistent demographic gap an emergency, and that reformable classroom practices do not require labeling all boys as structurally harmed to fix them crystallized in chase the mechanisms, not the banner.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO built a coherent, policy-relevant definition of "structural" (widespread, persistent, cumulative, and alterable by institutional design) and used it consistently to answer CON's strongest attacks: the "universal remedies" objection was met with the plausible tools-vs-targeting distinction (the health-system analogy), and the "affluent boys thrive" objection was met by noting that a group can be advantaged on one axis (elite STEM representation) while structurally disadvantaged on another (discipline, retention, completion). CON's case was solid and well-organized, repeatedly stressing that class/race/poverty are the real drivers and that gender-specific framing risks stereotyping and resource misallocation, but its rebuttals largely reasserted the same claims (affluent-boy success, universal tools) rather than dismantling PRO's counter-analogies. The pivotal exchange was the threshold pressure question: CON's answer ("an institutional barrier that caps a group regardless of resources") effectively required boys to fail uniformly across income levels before the label "structural" could apply, and PRO's closing exposed this as a standard that conveniently screens out the very disparities in question. CON's closing did not directly rebut this characterization, leaving PRO's final framing unanswered and giving it the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing turn on CON's threshold answer (B4.2) — pointing out that requiring an institutional barrier that persists "regardless of resources" effectively demands that affluent boys fail too, which quietly filters out real disparities — landed without a direct CON response in the final speech.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B won by establishing a persistent pipeline of male disadvantage and explaining why universal remedies can still be targeted toward a predictably high-risk group. Side A repeatedly equated structural disadvantage with an anti-male ceiling affecting even affluent boys, an unnecessarily restrictive definition that did not answer institutional patterns interacting with sex, class, and race. Side A also asserted that gender differences were merely modest developmental variance without adequately grounding that dismissal, and its closing used nonexistent reference labels.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response that universality describes the intervention rather than the targeting strategy decisively answered Side A’s central contradiction claim. Disaggregated tracking and accountability can prioritize boys’ recurring failures without requiring boy-only instruction or denying support to struggling girls.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON successfully showed that the effective interventions PRO proposed—early literacy screening, systematic phonics, restorative discipline—are universal best practices, undermining the claim that male underperformance constitutes a distinct structural crisis requiring gender-based framing. CON’s argument that affluent boys thrive and that within-SES gaps reflect developmental variance rather than institutional ceilings on maleness provided the cleaner causal story. PRO never overcame the objection that naming a "male structural crisis" adds no distinct policy value beyond delivering universal reforms with diagnostic precision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON in Turn 4 (A2.1) pointed out that every remedy PRO listed was a universal, skill-based reform, then leveraged this to argue that the problem is a failure of basic instructional delivery and resource equity—not an anti-male structural defect—sharpening the debate’s central clash and forcing PRO into a defensive posture for the remaining rounds.
