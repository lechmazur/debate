### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won by attacking both sides of the proposed trade: higher rates may reduce headline prices while worsening mortgage payments, rental supply, employment, and financial stress. Side B’s temporary-recession-versus-permanent-feudalism framing was forceful, but it depended on asserting that tightening reliably improves affordability and that recession damage is merely cyclical. A showed that targeted housing and macroprudential policies can distinguish investors from owner-occupiers, whereas broad rate hikes cannot, and made the admitted inability to control an overshoot decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both pressure questions directly, showed that targeted rules can discriminate by borrower type while rates cannot, and exposed Side B’s weak answers on rents and recession calibration. Side B’s concession that overshoot was simply a risk to accept strengthened A’s blank-check framing.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON wins on mechanism and weighing. PRO frames housing inflation as permanent and recession as temporary, but never answers CON's core that higher rates raise the monthly payment, suppress construction, and therefore raise rents, while missing cash/speculator buyers. CON's differentiation argument for targeted LTV/DDI limits beats PRO's Q2, while PRO's A2 concedes no dial for recession depth – a blank-check cost that CON crystallizes. Benefit fails, cost unbounded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 – A1/A2 plus D-B-4 exploitation: shows payment > price, rents follow scarcity not overpaid mortgages, and B4.2 admission that overshoot is just accepted risk, leaving PRO with no benefit mechanism and uncontrolled downside.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B wins by decisively dismantling Side A's core mechanism. Side A's case relied heavily on the premise that raising interest rates would reduce the cost of housing and cure the "permanent disease." Side B effectively demonstrated that rate hikes actually increase monthly mortgage payments (the true cost for a buyer) and suppress new construction, thereby keeping rents high due to scarcity. Side B also brilliantly neutralized Side A's pressure questions by explaining how macroprudential tools can be targeted by borrower type and occupancy, unlike blunt interest rate hikes. Side A's admission that a recession's depth cannot be calibrated and that an overshoot is simply an "accepted risk" gave Side B the ammunition to frame the proposition as an uncontrollable wrecking ball that fails to deliver its promised benefit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to Side A's pressure questions, specifically explaining that macroprudential tools (like LTV limits) can be differentiated by borrower and occupancy status to target speculators without crushing first-time buyers, whereas interest rates cannot make such distinctions. This completely deflated Side A's attempt to trap them on the flaws of targeted regulation.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won by framing continued housing inflation as a compounding, distributional harm and repeatedly forcing Side A to identify what would restrain rents while supply reform proceeds. Side A persuasively established recession scarring and the damage high rates can do to construction, keeping the debate competitive. However, its answer that stable growth lets wages catch up accommodates housing inflation rather than reducing it, and it repeatedly treated an increased recession risk as an intentional or guaranteed crash. Side B therefore offered the cleaner path under the motion’s actual tradeoff.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s closing response to A4.1 was decisive: rising real wages may soften affordability pressure, but it is not a mechanism for slowing housing inflation. That exposed the central gap in Side A’s alternative, while Side B’s risk-versus-ratchet comparison supplied a direct decision rule.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): PRO won the central trade-off: CON counted costs of tightening but offered no mechanism to reduce housing inflation during its admitted decade for supply reform, falling back to "real wages rise" which is accommodation, not reduction. PRO explained why cheap money capitalizes into land not supply, why forward rents answer lag, and why a ratchet that hits every renter every month outweighs recession risk. CON's strong supply-freeze and cash-buyer points were answered enough to leave PRO's path live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure on mechanism and B5 crystallization that CON's final answer was to tolerate the ratchet, not stop it, exposed the gap in CON's alternative.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by successfully framing the core tradeoff as a choice between a temporary recession and a permanent, compounding housing ratchet. While Side B offered strong rhetorical pushback regarding the blunt nature of interest rates and the pain of unemployment, Side A decisively won the clash on mechanisms. Crucially, when pressed on their alternative to reduce housing inflation, Side B conceded that their plan relied on real wages rising to absorb costs—which Side A correctly identified in the closing as mere accommodation of inflation, directly failing the motion's mandate to reduce it. Side A's closing crystallization was exceptionally sharp, securing a solid victory despite a minor execution cost from a clipped first rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing dismantling of Side B's "real wages" answer, exposing it as an accommodation of inflation rather than a reduction, which fatally undermined Side B's stance on the motion.
