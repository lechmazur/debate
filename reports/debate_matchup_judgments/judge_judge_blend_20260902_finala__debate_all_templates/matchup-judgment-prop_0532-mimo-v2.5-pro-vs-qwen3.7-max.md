### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): PRO wins on two decisive clashes. First, PRO's distinction between trip-demand inelasticity and curb-time elasticity is genuinely smart: CON never squarely answered it, only rebutting that you cannot deliver a hot lunch at 4 AM (a point about trip demand, not about how long a vehicle occupies the curb). Second, PRO lands and sustains the enforcement contradiction throughout: CON admits cities lack capacity to enforce even static time limits, yet proposes "rigorous enforcement of strict time limits" as the core alternative. CON's counter—that dynamic pricing requires even more surveillance and overhead—is the strongest moment of the rebuttal exchange, and it is a real point, but it is undercut by PRO's reply that dynamic pricing generates compliance through incentive rather than policing, and that the airport model already demonstrates brief-stop pricing is administratively tractable. CON's regressive-tax argument is contested effectively by PRO's counter that the status quo imposes invisible regressive costs on bus riders, cyclists, and pollution-exposed residents. CON's revenue-capture concern and the "double-park in the unpriced lane" scenario are live pressure but not knock-out blows: PRO addresses both adequately. CON's own closing amplifies the enforcement contradiction rather than resolving it. Three clips to PRO's speeches are a real execution cost but do not change the outcome because the core arguments and answers were visible in the clipped portions. Both sides used plausible general knowledge without fake precision, so epistemic discipline is roughly even.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange is PRO's repeated exposure of CON's enforcement contradiction. CON explicitly concedes in Rebuttal 1 that cities lack administrative capacity to enforce basic static rules, then proposes "rigorous enforcement of strict time limits" as the entire alternative to pricing. PRO crystallizes this in Q1 of the Pressure Questions round and hammers it through the Closing. CON's sharpest counter—that dynamic pricing demands even more complex surveillance infrastructure—is genuine, but PRO's answer (incentive-based compliance versus officer-dependent compliance, plus revenue funding the deficit) holds up better across the full exchange.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): B won by making the core scarcity comparison clearer: free curb space already rations through double-parking, circling, blocked buses, and unsafe streets, while pricing targets curb-time, idling, timing, and turnover rather than eliminating the underlying trip. A raised strong concerns about evasion, surveillance, and regressivity, but relied on expanded free loading zones and strict time limits without solving the same enforcement and scarcity problems B pressed throughout. B’s turns were slightly hurt by clipping, but the main comparative path remained stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s distinction between inelastic trip demand and more elastic curb-time demand, paired with the attack that A’s free time-limit alternative depends on enforcement cities already fail to provide, was the decisive exchange.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side A wins by effectively turning Side B's critique of enforcement capacity against them, demonstrating that dynamic pricing requires even more complex surveillance and still relies on the very physical enforcement Side B deemed impossible in order to prevent double-parking.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing brilliantly exposed the contradiction in Side B's case: if surge pricing clears the legal curb but makes it expensive, drivers will just double-park in the unpriced lane, requiring the exact physical enforcement Side B mocked as impossible.
