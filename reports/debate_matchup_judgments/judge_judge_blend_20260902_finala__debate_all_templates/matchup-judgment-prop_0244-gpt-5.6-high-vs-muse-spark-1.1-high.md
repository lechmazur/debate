  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1–B2.5 collapsing CON's modest-but-larger-than-predicted frame and redirecting to the expectation gap rather than camera-vs-nothing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by stabilizing the expectation baseline. It showed that the serious policy advocates promoting body cameras framed them explicitly as "one tool, not a cure"—a modest evidentiary upgrade rather than a transformative fix for institutional accountability. Against that measured standard, CON demonstrated concrete, repeatable mechanisms (daily supervisory review, non-activation penalties, discovery access, and the shift from "the officer's memo is the official story" to "the report must survive video") that met or exceeded what was promised. Side B (PRO) offered a crisp chain-of-accountability framework, but it fell into a damaging definitional trap by demanding perfect activation, retention, independent review, and consequence—conditions so strict that no tool could satisfy them. PRO also failed to show that the actual reform advocates expected cameras to independently reform institutional gatekeepers, and its alternative explanations for the complaint-and-force pattern never cleanly explained why both metrics would move together across varied departments. CON's sharper handling of the expectations question and its more grounded empirical pattern gave it the cleaner route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it answered Side B's pressure questions by showing that the promised outcomes—deterrence, resolution, discipline—were exactly what an evidentiary upgrade produces, then exposed that PRO's definition of accountability required a perfect institutional chain that no technology could satisfy, and crystallized that routine supervisory checks against video were precisely the "checkable truth" advocates had requested.
