### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): PRO built and defended the debate's decisive clash: cash cannot solve coordination failures in markets where supply is structurally absent. The "cash signals demand; public systems build supply" distinction was introduced cleanly in Rebuttal 1 and survived the full exchange. PRO also turned CON's supply-bottleneck argument against itself—more cash in thin markets drives bidding wars and price inflation, not new pediatricians or daycare slots. CON's best moments were the "price queue vs. time queue" reframe and the night-shift-worker motif, but PRO answered the night-shift case with the design-vs.-inherent-flaw distinction (Pressure Q2) and CON's response ("utopian cop-out") was circular rather than substantive. CON's trusted-neighbor answer to PRO's Q1 essentially conceded that cash produces informal, unregulated workarounds rather than actual supply—precisely PRO's point. CON's "magical version of the state" framing was rhetorically effective but did not materially answer why cash performs better when the underlying infrastructure doesn't exist. PRO's Medicare administrative-cost claim is broadly accurate as common knowledge and wasn't credibly refuted. The clipped closing cost PRO a small execution point but left enough crystallized comparison to hold the win. Overall, PRO's core argument had more live routes to victory after the full exchange: coordination failures require coordination solutions, and CON never provided a structural counter to that logic.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 turning the supply-bottleneck argument: CON argued universal services can't conjure workers; PRO responded that injecting cash into inelastic markets drives up prices rather than expanding supply, while public systems directly fund training pipelines and salary scales that actually build capacity. This reframe—"cash signals demand; public systems build supply"—was never squarely neutralized and became the load-bearing claim of the debate.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): Side A (PRO) built a more durable and consistently defended path to victory. Its central thesis—that cash in inelastic-supply markets creates bidding wars that price out the poor rather than expand access—was introduced clearly in the opening, survived pressure questioning, and was sharpened in subsequent rebuttals. The "waitlist allocates by need, bidding war allocates by wealth" formulation in A4.3 is the single most decisive comparative claim in the debate: CON never squarely neutralized it, offering only the partial response that waitlists may also fail to deliver. More decisively, PRO's Q1 pressure question directly exposed the fundamental evasion in CON's "we can do both" argument [B2.4]: the motion specifies the same money, so CON's simultaneous infrastructure build-out has no funding source. CON's capital-vs-operating-budget response in B4.2 was real but incomplete, and PRO had already partially pre-empted this by noting that operational funding for physical assets persists longer than cash stipends. CON had genuine live points—political vulnerability of service guarantees, operational funding as fragile as cash transfers, and the real-world failure of universal systems—but these were largely absorbed by PRO's cross-class constituency argument and the structural point that inelastic supply makes cash a pure redistributive instrument toward the wealthy. CON's grocery store analogy was its weakest moment, conceding the very distinction PRO needed (competitive vs. monopoly markets). The clipping of CON's closing is a minor execution cost but does not change the outcome. PRO maintained the cleaner, narrower decision rule throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure question exposing CON's "both/and" argument as evasive under the same-money constraint was the most decisive exchange. CON had repeatedly claimed cash doesn't preclude infrastructure investment, but the motion explicitly compares spending the same money on services versus cash. PRO forced CON to answer where the capital comes from, and CON's capital-vs-operating-budget distinction in B4.2, while plausible in isolation, was too narrow to rescue the broader argument. This moment also paired with PRO's A4.3 rebuttal—waitlist allocates by need, cash allocates by wealth—to create a double-bind CON never escaped: if supply is fixed and cash goes in, the poor lose the bidding war; if supply can be built, that requires exactly the kind of public investment PRO advocates.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side B won by establishing a clear economic framework around inelastic markets, successfully proving that cash without supply merely triggers bidding wars that exclude the poor. Side A's attempt to evade the motion's zero-sum budget tradeoff by suggesting capital infrastructure could be funded separately weakened their comparative stance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's breakdown of why basic needs aren't like groceries, proving that handing out cash in supply-constrained markets causes inflation and prices out the poor rather than expanding access.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): A wins: built clearer case on inelastic supply, infrastructure creation, and same-money constraint; answered pressure Qs directly while B's flexibility and bureaucracy points were repeatedly neutralized on the core comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 on waitlists vs. bidding wars plus A1/A2 answers to pressure questions.
