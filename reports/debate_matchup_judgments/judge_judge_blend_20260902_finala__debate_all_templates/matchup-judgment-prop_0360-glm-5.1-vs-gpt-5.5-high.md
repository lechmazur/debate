### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): CON won the decisive clashes despite three format-violation clips that imposed a real execution cost. The pivot point was the resource-allocation argument: PRO claimed finite enforcement resources justified concentrating on high-capacity vehicles; CON flipped this cleanly in the closing—"scarcity makes bad proxies more costly, not less"—by showing that category sorting consumes officer hours and camera placements that could instead be aimed at actual hotspot locations where violations, from any vehicle type, actually occur. That flip was not answered. CON also won the truck analogy clash: continuous operational risk from mass at every moment is genuinely different from discretionary capacity that imposes no externality when not exercised, and PRO never gave a principled rejoinder beyond asserting that "normal operation" of a performance car includes its full capability. CON's pressure question on administrability (how to exclude quiet luxury EVs and include loud modified ordinary cars) exposed a real definitional gap, and PRO's engineering-threshold answer conceded that loud non-category vehicles still need conduct enforcement while quiet high-capacity ones get preselected—exactly the mismatch CON diagnosed. The "suspicion of capacity" framing accurately captured PRO's residual problem: after separating compliant cars from harmful conduct, PRO's case rests on probabilistic surveillance of what a vehicle could do. PRO's execution was cleaner (no clipping, coherent progression, hospital-triage and airport analogies landed), which keeps the margin competitive, but CON's argumentative precision on the three live clashes—resource flip, truck distinction, and administrability—outweighed PRO's rhetorical tidiness.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's closing line "scarcity makes bad proxies more costly, not less" directly inverted PRO's strongest argument. PRO had built the entire second half of its case on resource constraints justifying category targeting; CON showed that resource constraints are precisely why misclassification errors (quiet high-performance cars surveilled, loud modified cars missed) are unaffordable, and that location-based hotspot enforcement achieves the same concentration without the proxy problem. PRO had no prepared answer for this inversion.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B successfully demonstrated that targeting luxury and performance vehicles is a crude proxy for harm, effectively arguing that location-based conduct enforcement captures the actual externalities without missing loud non-performance cars. Side B systematically dismantled Side A's analogies and turned the 'scarce resources' argument by showing that hotspot enforcement is more efficient than categorizing vehicles by specifications.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they exposed the contradiction in Side A's framework—relying on conduct enforcement for modified cars while demanding category enforcement for performance cars—and clearly explained how scaled penalties address under-deterrence.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins: stronger overall case by consistently showing category enforcement is an inaccurate proxy that misallocates scarce resources, while direct conduct rules better track actual harms. PRO's resource-concentration argument was effectively countered by location-based alternatives and the discretionary vs continuous distinction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2: cleanly separated truck-style continuous risks from performance capacity, answered both pressure questions on deterrence and definitions, and exposed PRO's shift between harm and potential without new widening.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): CON built the cleaner and more durable path to victory by holding PRO to the proposition's actual language throughout. The decisive structural move came in Rebuttal 1 and was never neutralized: PRO's own concession that noise and acceleration are separate harms, assessed separately, means the operative enforcement categories are already "loud vehicles" and "fast-accelerating vehicles," not the class the proposition names ("luxury and performance cars"). CON correctly identified that PRO kept sliding between the proposition's class categories and conduct-based descriptors (sport-exhaust coupes, high-torque EVs), and that this slippage was not precision—it was a concession that the proposition as written doesn't work. PRO's strongest live argument was the burst-harm/ex ante prevention point: once a launch or exhaust crack harms residents, after-the-fact penalties are too late, so predictive targeting by vehicle class adds something. But CON answered this directly and accurately in Rebuttal 2: noise cameras and acceleration sensors already function as real-time ex ante prevention tools that trigger on conduct, making the class surcharge either redundant (it adds nothing beyond what conduct-based tools already flag) or perverse (it penalizes a silently driven grand tourer while a straight-piped economy car at baseline). PRO never explained what additional harm the class layer prevents that conduct-based enforcement misses. The modified-Civic and EV examples entered early and were never cleanly resolved, maintaining their force. Multiple PRO speeches were clipped, which, combined with the unresolved category problem, leaves CON's core argument standing without a decisive answer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 observation that PRO's own "precision" concession (noise and acceleration are separate harms assessed separately) requires abandoning the proposition's named categories entirely. CON showed that once you accept noise enforcement targets "loud vehicles" and acceleration enforcement targets "fast-accelerating vehicles," the "luxury and performance" class designation does no independent work—and PRO never recovered from this in any subsequent speech.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): CON won by systematically dismantling PRO's analogies (sobriety checkpoints, heavy trucks) and successfully demonstrating that PRO was conflating class-based categories with conduct-based categories. PRO's execution suffered significantly due to repeated clipping from exceeding word limits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's first rebuttal cleanly reversed PRO's sobriety checkpoint analogy, noting that checkpoints stop everyone at a location rather than profiling by vehicle class.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by exposing the mismatch between luxury/performance classes and actual concentrated harms through concrete counterexamples (quiet fast EVs, loud beater cars) and by showing PRO's responses collapse into conduct-based targeting that does not support the proposition as written. PRO's prevention focus is neutralized by A's demonstration that conduct tools (cameras, sensors) achieve ex ante effects without class proxies. Clipped turns add minor execution costs but do not alter the core clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 (chef's knife/ceiling vs harm) plus Rebuttal 2 pressure answers, which force PRO to concede separate harm channels and abandon class categories in favor of conduct descriptors.
