### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B won by making the central comparison clearer: catastrophic long-term care need exists whether society pools it or leaves it to families, and atomized families are least able to absorb it. Side A’s strongest point was scarcity—governments cannot print caregivers or money—but B persuasively answered that family care is also rationing, just hidden and imposed on unpaid workers, and that pooling is still superior to forced middle-class spend-down. A’s regressive-transfer argument was blunted by B’s progressive-funding response and by the point that the wealthy already self-insure while middle-class households are destroyed. A remained competitive with fiscal and labor-supply concerns, but leaned on repetition and broad claims about rationing without fully answering adverse selection, caregiver exit from the labor force, or how reformed means-testing avoids catastrophic ruin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s most decisive exchange was the answer to “governments cannot print caregivers”: B reframed family reliance as the worst form of rationing, because it removes prime-age workers from paid employment while still failing to create professional care capacity.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): PRO built the stronger path to victory by grounding the debate in three mutually reinforcing mechanisms: adverse selection destroying private insurance, catastrophic individual unpredictability making family savings impossible, and the macroeconomic damage of informal family caregiving. CON's counterarguments about fiscal sustainability and workforce rationing were rhetorically sharp but less substantiated; CON never squarely answered how private markets overcome adverse selection or how reformed means-testing avoids middle-class bankruptcy. PRO's pressure questions exposed the vagueness of CON's "targeted support," and PRO's closing crystallized the core clash effectively: if society cannot afford pooled care, atomized families certainly cannot.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal, particularly the pivot after answering CON's pressure questions: "If a society supposedly cannot afford to manage elder care collectively through a massive, pooled risk fund, how can atomized, middle-class families possibly afford it individually?" This exposed the central tension in CON's fiscal sustainability argument.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully turning Side A's core objections. B effectively neutralized the caregiver shortage argument by demonstrating that care demand is biological rather than discretionary, and that a universal system can professionalize and fund the workforce instead of covertly rationing care through family burnout and labor-force exit. B also consistently highlighted how strict means-testing mandates middle-class bankruptcy, a burden A never fully resolved despite late adjustments to asset thresholds. B's framing of long-term care as an unpredictable catastrophic risk requiring universal pooling was clearer, more economically coherent, and better adapted to the demographic realities both sides acknowledged.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to the caregiver shortage and demand inflation [B4.2, B5.2], which turned CON's scarcity argument by proving that demand exists regardless of the funding model and that universal pooling manages physical labor constraints more efficiently than forcing untrained relatives to abandon their careers.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side B wins because its core claim that long-term care costs already exist and are being inefficiently dumped onto families, unpaid caregivers, and forced spend-downs remained live after the exchange. Side A’s fiscal and intergenerational-equity pressure was strong, especially the inheritance-protection framing and the care-worker shortage argument, but it often assumed universal care meant unlimited free coverage for affluent seniors and never fully answered B’s hidden-cost argument about family caregivers leaving work and shrinking the tax base. B more directly tied the motion to risk pooling, private-market failure, and avoiding family impoverishment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive rebuttal was that CON’s “sustainable” model does not eliminate costs but hides them on family balance sheets through unpaid labor and forced asset liquidation, undermining A’s claim to protect young workers.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by showing that its opponent's means-testing and family-care alternative imposes severe, uncounted macroeconomic costs—shrinking the labor force, destroying caregivers' retirement security, and mandating middle-class impoverishment—while Side B relied largely on static fiscal assertions and never fully rebutted the hidden economic drag of unpaid caregiving.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal exposing the macroeconomic drag of unpaid family care—shrinking the tax base and decimating caregivers' retirement security—which Side B never answered beyond asserting taxes would be worse.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side B (PRO) secured the win by successfully reframing the fiscal and generational equity clash. Side A (CON) built a sharp case around universal care acting as a regressive transfer that taxes young renters to protect older homeowners' inheritances. However, Side B effectively neutralized this by distinguishing middle-class survival from wealthy estate planning and, more decisively, by flipping the "burden on the young" argument. Side B convincingly demonstrated that the hidden costs of CON's model—forced workforce exit, lost tax revenue, and derailed careers for younger adults providing unpaid care—constitute a heavier, more economically destructive drag than a progressively funded universal system. Side B's Rebuttal 2 directly answered pressure questions while tightening this macroeconomic comparison, whereas Side A leaned heavily on repeating the tax-versus-inheritance framing without fully resolving Side B's core point that elder care costs are inevitable and currently externalized inefficiently. Side B's closing crystallized the "we already pay, just poorly" mechanism cleanly, offering a clearer comparative path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2), which directly answered Side A's pressure on caregiver wages and intergenerational equity by pivoting to the "silent tax" of unpaid care and the macroeconomic dividend of returning family caregivers to the formal workforce, effectively turning Side A's core "protect the young" burden.
