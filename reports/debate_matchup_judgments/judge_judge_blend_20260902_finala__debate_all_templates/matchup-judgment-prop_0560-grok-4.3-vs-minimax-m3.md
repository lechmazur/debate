### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON built the stronger path to victory by identifying and relentlessly exploiting one decisive weakness in PRO's case: the instability of the word "marginal" as a stable clinical category. In the opening, CON correctly called "marginal" a rhetorical device rather than a clinical term. PRO's Rebuttal 1 attempted to anchor the definition to "weeks or months of survival at extreme unit cost," but CON's Rebuttal 1 correctly replied that futility protocols already handle that narrow slice, so the motion's real work must operate in a broader zone—targeted therapies, curative resections in elderly patients, immunotherapies—where the clinical line is genuinely contested. This was a live, unanswered argument heading into the pressure round. CON's pressure questions were well-targeted: Q1 exposed that the clinical line is fuzzy in practice; Q2 pressed PRO on the duty owed to the specific ICU patient. PRO's Rebuttal 2 answers were brief and strategically damaging: A1 conceded that a prognosis shift from six months to two years moves the patient out of the marginal category—a concession CON's closing exploited precisely, arguing that administrators, not clinicians, operationalize this shifting boundary under cost pressure, producing predictable scope creep. CON's answer to PRO's Q1 (listing administrative overhead, pharma negotiation, fraud recovery as alternative levers) was more concrete than PRO's response, and CON's answer to PRO's Q2 turned the question: growth in end-of-life costs proves the motion's definition is broader than futility rules, not narrower. PRO's closing was too brief and repetitive to close these gaps. The decisive rebuttal was CON's closing exploitation of PRO's A1 concession: by admitting the prognosis line is real and movable, PRO inadvertently confirmed that the same uncertainty that makes prognosis hard makes "marginal" an administratively-driftable category—exactly CON's central charge. PRO's core case (prevention has better population-level ROI) is sound and important, but PRO never resolved how "marginal" would be defined stably enough to avoid the harms CON described, leaving that route to victory open at the end of the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's closing exploitation of PRO's A1 answer in Rebuttal 2. PRO conceded that a prognosis shift from six months to two years removes a patient from the marginal category under existing rules—a seemingly reasonable answer that CON immediately weaponized: because prognosis is uncertain and boundary-drawing is done by administrators under budget pressure rather than oncologists at the bedside, the "marginal" category will expand outward over time to encompass exactly the patients CON named (targeted therapies, curative resections in elderly patients, meaningful immunotherapy extensions). PRO had no remaining answer to this administrative-drift argument, and the concession made the problem concrete rather than hypothetical.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side B won because it kept the motion anchored to marginal, high-cost end-of-life expansions and showed why finite marginal dollars buy more health and reduce future severe disease when spent on prevention. Side A's line-drawing and patient-duty objections were strong, but they often attacked broader or meaningful end-of-life care that the motion and B explicitly excluded, and the slippery-slope claim became overbroad and partly late-supported. B better answered the core comparison: prevention can be prioritized while preserving palliative and genuinely restorative care.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Most decisive was B's response that CON's elderly surgery and targeted-therapy examples fall outside the marginal category, while low-yield terminal additions can be deprioritized without abandoning comfort care.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON collapsed the proposition by showing that the decisive term "marginal" cannot be clinically stabilized. PRO needed "marginal" to mean only futile, brief extensions already screened out by existing protocols, but CON demonstrated that in practice such categories are drawn by administrators and drift outward to capture patients with meaningful survival. CON also undermined PRO's necessary trade-off premise by identifying larger non-clinical budget levers, making the forced choice illusory. PRO conceded too much on the boundary-drawing problem and never recovered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question on how to draw the clinical line when prognosis shifts, and its subsequent exploitation of PRO's answer to show that prior-authorization regimes inevitably expand from obviously futile cases to meaningful treatments, rendering the motion's central category politically unstable.
