### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON (Side A) landed the more damaging structural hit: it forced PRO to concede, mid-debate, that the rule only works if you read in exemptions (transformative use, estate consent, good-faith-search safe harbors) that are nowhere in the motion's text. A's pressure questions exposed this gap, and PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer confirmed it by inventing carve-outs on the fly rather than defending the proposition as written—an inconsistency A explicitly weaponized in Rebuttal 2 and Closing ("no exemption written in... PRO now smuggles those limits in"). PRO never squarely returned to defend the literal text after that, instead reasserting that "existing law already distinguishes" without answering why the motion itself contains no such distinction. A also sustained an underexplored but strong point—that scammers, the real harm-doers, will simply ignore any consent regime, so the rule's practical burden falls almost entirely on compliant platforms and lawful creators (parody, docudrama, accessibility dubbing), which PRO's "compliance departments" reply doesn't really rebut. PRO's scale/product-vs-performance distinction and speed-of-harm argument were genuinely strong contributions, but they didn't resolve the overbreadth/textual problem, and PRO's final rebuttal speech was flagged as clipped for length, costing it a clean close on its strongest late material.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Questions (Q1 on parody/certification, Q2 on deceased/unfindable persons) forced PRO to invent unstated exemptions in Rebuttal 2, which A then used in Rebuttal 2/Closing to show PRO's workable case is not the case the motion actually states—this exchange was the debate's turning point.

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): B won by holding A to the motion as written and making overbreadth/chill the decisive live route: commercial distribution covers most influential speech, exemptions A relies on are not in the text and invite delete-first compliance, while scammers evade either way. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON's decisive line was the scope mismatch: the motion as written requires consent for ALL commercial distribution of identifiable voice clones, sweeping in labeled satire and political commentary, not just deceptive scams. PRO's harms (child-voice scams, fraudulent candidate audio, private-person impersonation) are all deception-based, but PRO never narrowed the remedy to match — and PRO's closing concession that critics can "use your own voice, animate, hire an impressionist" instead effectively confirms that the motion does grant subjects a veto over the specific expressive tool of a realistic clone, which is exactly CON's overbreadth complaint. CON's pressure question about the impressionist and its follow-through in Rebuttal 2 exposed that PRO's deception-based reasoning doesn't justify a technology-triggered, consent-for-everything rule. PRO's strongest counter — that after-the-fact remedies fail ordinary people because right-of-publicity is patchwork and celebrities-only — was raised but CON's answer (false light, ToS, existing impersonation law) was asserted rather than fully demonstrated, leaving a live but secondary tension. On balance, CON built the clearer, more sustained comparative case that the remedy overshoots the harm, and PRO's rhetorical framing ("toll for trading identity," "ask first before you cash in") was vivid but didn't resolve the structural veto problem CON kept pressing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2/Closing point that PRO's own fallback ("use your own voice, hire an impressionist") concedes the motion blocks the specific tool of a labeled AI clone even for legitimate satire, confirming rather than refuting the veto-power critique — paired with PRO's evasive A2 answer to the impressionist pressure question, which sidestepped whether the trigger is deception or merely the AI tool.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by relentlessly exposing the gap between Side B's deception-based harms and the blanket prior-restraint remedy of the motion. Side A's argument that requiring consent at the 'seed' merely bans legitimate satire to prevent downstream misuse went largely undefeated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 expertly capitalized on the pressure questions, showing that stopping the 'seed' to prevent label-stripping just results in blocking legitimate, labeled parody altogether.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): B won by keeping the decisive clash fixed on the mismatch between PRO’s deception/scam harms and the motion’s blanket consent rule covering labeled satire and commentary. A’s identity-vs-ideas and seed-prevention answers were strong but never closed that overbreadth gap; B repeatedly showed existing law already targets the bad cases while consent adds a prior veto the powerful can use. Cleaner comparative path and sharper crystallization across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s labels/context-collapse turn: if stripped reposts are the harm, consent at the original blocks legitimate labeled speech without stopping the stripper—exposing remedy overbreadth.
