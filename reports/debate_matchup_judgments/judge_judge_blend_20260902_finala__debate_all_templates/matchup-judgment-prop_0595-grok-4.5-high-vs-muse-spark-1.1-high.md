- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): CON built a durable structural attack early with the "multi-use tool" problem (a single AI feature is simultaneously assistant, monitor, and training input, so there is no clean "class of tools" to negotiate once), the fissuring-to-contractors point, and the wage/productivity mechanism. PRO's framework defense never resolved the core ambiguity of what its mandate actually requires. The pressure-question exchange was decisive: CON's Q1 forced PRO to choose between a consent veto and toothless-but-slower consultation. PRO's answer ("management may proceed only after exhausting that process") effectively conceded the "delayed consultation" horn, and CON's second rebuttal (A4.5) exploited that concession directly by turning PRO's own dismissal of "toothless consultation" back on it. PRO's closing reasserted the stakes but never cleanly closed that trap or explained how vendor/contractor dodges are enforced against a large employer's council-bargained terms. CON's case progressed cleanly across rounds, sharpened the dilemma each time, and ended with the clash crystallized in its favor. PRO's case remained coherent and normatively appealing but left the central mechanism question live and unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q1 in Pressure Questions (forcing PRO to pick between consent-veto and toothless consultation) combined with CON's A4.5 in Rebuttal 2, which caught PRO's answer ("management may proceed after exhausting process") as conceding the very toothless-consultation problem PRO had dismissed in its own opening.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by cleanly exposing a dilemma in Side B's mechanism during the pressure questions. A forced B to clarify if the mandate meant a veto or just bargaining to impasse, and then successfully argued that B's answer either resulted in the 'toothless consultation' B initially attacked or the 'permitting bottleneck' A warned against.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's R2 perfectly capitalized on the pressure questions by showing that B's admission of 'management may proceed after exhausting process' contradicted B's opening premise that consultation without a veto is useless.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing a structural contradiction that PRO could not resolve: the mandate is either a toothless consultation (management proceeds after process) or a binding veto (councils block deployment), and either outcome undermines PRO's case. CON's multi-use AI critique showed that no stable "framework" exists for tools that simultaneously assist, monitor, and train, making PRO's "material threshold" trigger unworkable and creating a de facto permitting process. CON also landed that direct statutory protections would be more predictable than a thousand bargained private constitutions, and that a size threshold would accelerate fissuring by pushing AI work to contractors. PRO's democratic framing remained rhetorically forceful but could not repair the ambiguity at the core of its mechanism or convincingly show how councils would translate into higher wages rather than slower productivity growth.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question and subsequent rebuttal forcing PRO to clarify whether the mandate is a duty to bargain to impasse or a duty to obtain consent. PRO's answer in B4.1—that management may proceed after exhausting process but councils can reject unconscionable terms—left the mechanism stranded between toothlessness and veto. CON crystallized this in A4.5 and A5.1 as the decisive contradiction, showing that PRO's model is either the weak consultation it dismisses or the hostage-taking veto it denies.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but B built the more concrete and durable case. B's "material-change trigger" mechanism directly answered CON's central "continuous retraining" objection by analogizing to existing new-technology clauses in collective bargaining, giving PRO a workable answer to the dynamism critique that A never fully dismantled (A kept reasserting that boundaries "shift" without engaging the specific trigger categories B offered). B's pressure questions exposed a real tension in CON's case — that existing anti-discrimination/privacy law "already polices abuse" while also requiring collective access to prove harm — and CON's answer (statutes can mandate disclosure without councils) never resolved the timing problem B kept pressing: ex-ante prevention versus ex-post litigation after wages are already lost. Tellingly, A's own closing conceded this point outright ("After-the-fact remedies leave paychecks cut, true enough") and pivoted to a cost-benefit framing rather than refuting it. That concession, combined with B's clearer mechanism (enforceable grievance/arbitration, selective friction distinguishing safety tools from punitive scoring), gave B the cleaner path to victory. A's competitive-disadvantage argument remained live but stayed somewhat abstract and speculative ("rivals capture markets"), and never grappled with the fact that its own proposed alternative (agency notice-and-comment, litigation) is not obviously faster than negotiated rules.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2) turning CON's "existing law already polices this" into a contradiction with the proof-before-harm problem, sharpened by B's closing line "protection before the pay cut versus litigation after you've lost the check" — a framing CON's closing implicitly conceded.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing AI as algorithmic management and exposing the timing flaw in Side A's reliance on existing law. By demonstrating that Side A's solutions (litigation and discovery) only offer post-harm remedies, Side B established a clear need for ex-ante negotiation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Q1 and subsequent capitalization in closing forced Side A to concede that their statutory and litigation remedies only apply after the worker has already suffered the harm (e.g., losing a paycheck).
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by framing AI deployment as a management-labor issue requiring proactive worker voice, not after-the-fact litigation. Their "rules not retrains" distinction and material-change trigger blunted Side A's institutional-lag attack, while the "protection before the pay cut versus litigation after" frame made the comparative stakes decisive. Side A's statutory alternative remained plausible but could not overcome the impression that isolated workers lack timely leverage against opaque algorithmic harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 [B4.1-B4.5], where they answered both pressure questions by showing how good-faith bargaining creates enforceable contract terms without a veto, and how material-change clauses cleanly separate routine updates from new deployments requiring renegotiation—simultaneously neutralizing CON's "permanent lag" critique and exposing the inadequacy of after-the-fact individual remedies.
