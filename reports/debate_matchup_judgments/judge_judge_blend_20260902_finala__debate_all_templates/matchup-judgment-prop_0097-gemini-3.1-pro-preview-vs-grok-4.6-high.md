### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won by breaking A’s false choice between immediate architecture and waiting for definitive mass unemployment. B offered earlier occupation-level signals, existing unemployment systems, and gradual firm adoption as a credible intervention window. A’s conditional triggers and policy lead-time argument kept the debate competitive, but its case still depended heavily on an insufficiently grounded efficiency cliff, simultaneous displacement, and catastrophic macroeconomic cascade. B more convincingly showed that premature programs could be mistargeted and politically difficult to unwind.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was that “code can update overnight; payroll does not.” It directly answered A’s speed claim by distinguishing software availability from firm adoption and layoffs, then paired that distinction with vacancies, hours, hiring, and wages as signals that can trigger targeted policy before economy-wide collapse.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a durable asymmetry-of-risk frame: the cost of proactive preparation is modest administrative spending, while the cost of waiting is potentially catastrophic and unrecoverable. A effectively dismantled B's historical analogies by distinguishing AI as cognitive substitution rather than tool augmentation, and maintained that standard unemployment insurance is structurally mismatched to permanent white-collar devaluation. B's strongest objections—fiscal waste and lack of visible breaks—were answered by A's trigger mechanism and administrative-lag argument. B failed to fully reconcile their reliance on early labor-market signals with the structural inadequacy of existing UI for wage cuts, leaving A's "bridge to nowhere" critique live at the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal of B's historical analogy and the "bridge to nowhere" critique of standard unemployment insurance (A2.1, A4.4-A4.6, A5.2), which exposed that B's reactive framework had no credible answer for workers permanently forced into lower-wage roles.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by systematically dismantling Side A's core premises. B effectively clarifies that "waiting" does not mean ignoring early labor market signals like vacancies, hours, and wage pressure, neutralizing A's repeated "lagging indicator" strawman. Furthermore, B capitalizes on A's concession of an "adoption lag" to prove that lead time exists for targeted policy. B's argument that building wage insurance before knowing which skills are devalued is epistemically flawed, and that creating the bureaucratic architecture generates a permanent constituency, decisively undercuts A's "dormant fire extinguisher" framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they turn Side A's admission of an "adoption lag" into proof that early occupational signals will appear before any sudden collapse, and dismantle the "dormant triggers" defense by explaining that funding streams and portals create entrenched constituencies regardless of whether the macroeconomic trigger fires.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by framing preparation as scalable institutional capacity rather than immediate mass spending, then showing why Side B's proof threshold would trigger only after substantial, lagging harm. A directly answered both cost and retraining-mismatch objections with dormant rules, limited administration, employer-linked training, and wage insurance tied to actual vacancies. B raised legitimate concerns about bureaucracy and misallocation, but repeatedly assumed billions in immediate costs and guaranteed higher-value replacement jobs without adequately establishing either claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange exposed B's false choice between billion-dollar deployment and empty theater. The unemployment-insurance analogy showed how rules and administrative capacity can exist before claims surge, while B's eventual trigger of sustained structural unemployment conceded that its preferred response begins only after widespread damage is measurable.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by showing that waiting for definitive macro proof institutionalizes delay until after structural damage is irreversible, while their "pipes" framing convincingly separated standby institutional design from massive immediate spending. Side B never squarely answered the lag problem or why standard UI suffices for a compressed, task-level transition, and their "downward mobility" critique underestimated the value of keeping mid-career workers attached to the labor market.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 answer to the "retrain for what" and cost pressure questions: the UI-analogy for standby design, and the shift to employer-tied short modules (claims work plus judgment, code review plus product sense) that need not predict specific job titles, deflated CON's strongest attack and exposed their wait-for-proof standard as a functional construction ban.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by decisively exposing the fatal flaw in Side B's "wait for proof" standard: macroeconomic data lags, meaning that by the time Side B's trigger is met, the structural damage is already done and it is too late to build the necessary institutions. Side A's "pipes" and "fire station" metaphors effectively framed the necessity of standby design, and their defense of wage insurance as a tool to prevent labor market exit for older workers neutralized Side B's "downward mobility" attack. Side B argued competently but ultimately fell into the trap of defending a reactive approach to a structural shock, relying on standard UI which Side A proved was insufficient for the specific nature of AI displacement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's "downward mobility" attack on wage insurance. Side A effectively reframed wage insurance not as subsidizing defeatism, but as a necessary bridge to keep mid-career workers (like a 45-year-old paralegal) attached to the labor force, whereas Side B's alternative would simply result in mass exit and long-term economic scarring.
