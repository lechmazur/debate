### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): PRO built a durable framework early (benchmarks as a necessary-but-not-sufficient floor, shadow mode/pilots as evidence-gathering distinct from reimbursed deployment, and a clean risk-allocation frame: vendors absorb uncertainty rather than patients). CON's opening was strong (validation paradox, Goodhart's Law, complacency), but PRO's Rebuttal 1 largely dissolved the validation paradox by distinguishing shadow-mode evaluation from routine, reimbursed deployment, and CON never fully answered that distinction—instead repeating that shadow mode is "real deployment," which begs the question. PRO's "smoke alarm/fire code" move (insufficiency does not negate necessity) directly rebutted CON's central inference and went largely unanswered. CON's claim that "continuous surveillance IS the admission rule" is weak, since surveillance by definition operates post-admission, and PRO's closing exploited this cleanly. CON's case also suffered real execution costs: both Rebuttal 2 and Closing were clipped for exceeding word limits, cutting off argument development at critical junctures, while PRO delivered complete, on-point speeches throughout, including a clean A1/A2 structure after pressure questions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 point that "because a gate is not sufficient, it supposedly is not necessary" (the fire-code/smoke-alarm analogy) directly targeted CON's core inferential move and was never squarely answered, compounding with CON's shaky claim that ongoing surveillance alone constitutes a coherent "admission rule."
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A wins by cleanly resolving the core clash over the order of operations for AI deployment. Side A proves that Side B lacks a coherent admission rule, pointing out that post-deployment surveillance inherently requires exposing patients to unvetted risk. Side B also suffers execution costs, clipping in both their second rebuttal and closing, and struggling to reconcile their support for 'shadow testing' with their opposition to a pre-market gate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal and closing decisively exploited Side B's claim that surveillance is an 'admission rule,' noting that surveillance only happens after admission, meaning patients bear the risk of initial discovery.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side B defended the pre-deployment gate by showing that shadow-mode evaluation and supervised pilots can supply real-world data without granting routine reimbursement or clinical authority, dissolving CON’s validation paradox. Side A’s claim that benchmarks displace vigilance was blunted by PRO’s concession that monitoring continues post-deployment and by the unanswered need for a principled admission rule before scale. CON’s final rebuttal and closing were clipped, leaving its response to PRO’s key pressure incomplete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s second rebuttal (B4), which answered CON’s pressure questions directly, distinguished evidence collection from reimbursement entitlement, used the smoke-alarm analogy, and exposed that surveillance-only offers no principled standard to exclude dangerous tools before patients are exposed.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Both sides argued well, but A's core structural claim—that CON's "risk-based governance" ultimately relies on institutional self-policing (vendor-chosen categorization, private data-sharing among purchasers/regulators, discretionary suspension authority) and thus reproduces the opacity that motivated the proposition—was never fully defused. B's answers to the pressure questions (an "independent regulator" classifies tools, public summaries plus confidential technical files to insiders) actually conceded much of A's structural critique: B admits vendors "submit evidence" and classification is done by an accountable body, which sounds close to A's public-benchmark model just with narrower publication scope. B's strongest point—the dilemma between bespoke benchmarks (no floor) and standardized ones (blocking beneficial locally-tailored tools)—was clever and repeated effectively, but A's response that different risk tiers can each have their own publicly reported (not necessarily single universal) benchmarks somewhat blunts this, since the proposition's "publicly reported" requirement doesn't preclude task-specific standards. B never fully rebuts A's point that gaming/private selection is worse under CON's confidential-file model. A's "research pilot" distinction was contested well by B (arguing the text doesn't create that exception), and this remains a genuine unresolved tension, but A's broader "public accountability vs. governance by procurement contract" framing landed as the more memorable and less resolved point, and A's closing pressed this home despite being clipped. Overall a close, well-matched debate, but A's closing point about structural vs. discretionary accountability was the more decisive unanswered thread, plus B lost format economy (two clipped speeches for B's clipped opening too, but A had two clipped speeches vs B's one, which is a wash but slightly favors B execution-wise). Given the format penalty is roughly even, the substantive edge goes to A for pressing the accountability/enforcement asymmetry that B's answers implicitly conceded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A3.2/B3.1 exchange on who sets benchmarks and B4.1's answer conceding an "independent regulator" classifies tools—which А then leveraged in Rebuttal 2/Closing to argue CON's oversight is still discretionary and insider-controlled—was the most decisive exchange, as B never fully closed the gap between "regulator access" and true public accountability.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B successfully leveraged the absolute wording of the proposition to demonstrate that mandatory public benchmarks would create a catch-22 for evidence generation and force a choice between inflexible standardized metrics and gameable bespoke ones. Side A relied on a 'research exemption' not found in the text and suffered execution errors by exceeding the word count and being clipped in three different speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 effectively dismantled Side A's 'research exemption' by pointing out that the proposition strictly governs 'permitting' tools, meaning supervised pilots on real patients would be blocked, directly harming safety-net hospitals.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): CON exposed a structural dilemma that PRO never resolved: publicly reported accuracy and bias benchmarks are either standardized enough to block net-beneficial tools or tailored enough to be gamed, making the gate hollow or harmful. CON also showed that the text contains no research exemption, that safety-net pilot access matters, and that its own risk-based alternative delivers real accountability without a rigid scorecard. PRO’s intuitive transparency frame could not overcome the fairness-definition problem or the local-validation objection, and its rebuttal and closing were clipped.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 collapsed PRO’s “whatever benchmarks exist” framing into a standardized-versus-tailored dilemma, proving the proposition supplies either no enforceable floor or a rigid barrier that falsely reassures while blocking beneficial, clinician-reviewed tools.
