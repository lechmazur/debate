### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing that interactive systems require playable preservation and by offering a plausible conditional licensing mechanism that preserves publisher ownership. Side A raised credible costs involving third-party rights, moderation, and future relaunches, but repeatedly treated limited authorization as a waiver of copyright and practical control without answering B’s explanation of revocable, non-commercial licensing. A also narrowed the motion into compelled operation of a full service, underplaying its archival-mode and mod-support alternatives. B’s irreversible-erasure versus manageable-risk weighing was ultimately clearer, though A retained a competitive concern about cannibalizing future revivals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.4 most decisively answered A’s pressure: B specified how a limited license could authorize preservation without surrendering ownership, then distinguished preserving records of play from preserving the playable system itself. A’s next speech largely repeated the waiver claim rather than defeating that mechanism.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): CON won by establishing a clean framework distinction between preservation and compelled operation. PRO argued persuasively that games are interactive systems requiring play to be preserved, but CON's cathedral and theme-park analogies demonstrated that cultural stewardship does not require keeping a commercial service running under someone else's control. CON also effectively neutralized PRO's "no competing interest" claim by identifying concrete harms—waived IP control, brand liability, and cannibalization of future revivals—while showing that documented archives and permitted mods offer a genuine preservation alternative. PRO's moral frame remained resonant but could not overcome the analytical gap between preserving a work and mandating a private server.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's demolition of PRO's chess analogy in the closing by noting that chess is an unowned rule set while a shuttered game is copyrighted code and trademarked service, combined with the cathedral analogy in Rebuttal 2 showing that preservation does not require functional operation, decisively reframed the clash and exposed the category error at the heart of PRO's case.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won decisively by controlling the definition of "preservation" for interactive media. While Side A relied heavily on the current legal reality that players only license a service, Side B effectively argued the normative case that erasing an interactive world is cultural vandalism. The turning point was the clash over alternatives: Side A argued that wikis, footage, and static archives constitute preservation, but Side B dismantled this by pointing out that games are systems of interaction, using the highly effective analogy that "chess is not preserved if you ban playing it and keep only notation." Side A's attempts to counter this with analogies to demolished cathedrals fell flat, as physical structures differ fundamentally from executable code and rule sets. Side B also provided a concrete, plausible legal mechanism (a revocable, non-commercial EULA addendum) to address Side A's IP and security concerns, leaving Side A's harms looking speculative and curable compared to the irreversible erasure Side B highlighted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled Side A's claim that static archives and footage equal preservation, arguing that interactive media requires interaction to be preserved and deploying the memorable "chess notation is not chess" analogy.
