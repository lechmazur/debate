### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B's "carbon offshoring/hidden reporting" mechanism proved to be the most resilient unrebutted line: A repeatedly answered the sovereignty and decoupling fears with the EU CBAM precedent, but never squarely addressed B's specific claim that nations would stay in the trade system while simply ceasing emissions reporting and routing coal through unmonitored channels—a distinct mechanism from the "decoupling" A kept knocking down. B also sharpened the analogy gap (border tariffs on traded goods vs. dictating domestic power generation) that A's WTO/IMF comparison never fully closed. A's pressure question forced B into conceding it offers no enforceable global mechanism, which is a real weakness, but B reframed this credibly as "don't burn the fragile bridge," and the debate's structure (opposition to a specific policy) does not strictly require a counter-mechanism to win. A's closing being clipped before reaching its full weighing argument is a real execution cost that let B's clean, complete closing land the final, more memorable framing of the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 distinction between the EU's border-crossing carbon tariff and this proposal's attempt to dictate domestic power-plant operation, later reinforced by the "hidden reporting/unregistered plants" mechanism in Rebuttal 2, was the exchange A never fully neutralized.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): PRO won by combining climate urgency, the demonstrated weakness of voluntary pledges, economic leverage, and transition finance into a plausible mechanism. CON correctly challenged the leap from narrow border measures to a sweeping domestic-energy condition, but its alternative causal story relied on unsupported absolutes: universal hidden coal use, zero reductions, and the collapse of all climate cooperation. Massive grid-connected coal assets are not obviously easy to conceal, and CON never sufficiently established why penalties would produce no compliance. PRO’s clipped closing was an execution cost, but its path to victory was already clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s second rebuttal most effectively answered the sovereignty objection: states routinely accept domestic-policy constraints in exchange for markets and finance. CON’s partial-cheating response resolved the alleged contradiction between exit and concealment, but did not prove that evasion would overwhelm the proposal’s incentives and monitoring.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): CON won the decisive feasibility clash. PRO leaned on EU CBAM as proof penalties work; CON distinguished it as a border goods tariff, not a mandate over domestic power plants - a distinction PRO's WTO/IMF analogy never fully repaired. PRO also did not answer the firm baseload readiness pressure (B Q2) with a firm-replacement example, only trajectory claims, while CON credibly explained partial cheating as the default outcome and resolved A's contradiction charge. A's clipped closing adds execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1-B2.4 bait-and-switch: CBAM only taxes imports vs. this proposal penalizing what a country builds/burns domestically — directly undercutting PRO's central precedent.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Both sides built coherent, competitive cases, but A executed more cleanly across the whole debate. A's opening pre-empted the development-justice objection with the 2040 timeline and the "unabated only" carveout, and its rebuttals consistently forced B into a bind: either the policy is truly voluntary (in which case China/India simply opt out and nothing changes) or it is enforced via border measures (in which case B's own sovereignty complaint about coercion applies). A's pressure questions were sharp and well-targeted (why are existing "middle ground" tools failing right now at record coal levels; why hasn't CBAM caused the retaliation B predicts), and A answered B's own pressure questions with proper A1/A2 structure and substantive content. B's arguments were often strong in isolation—the China/India non-compliance problem, the equal-rules-versus-equity distinction, the CBAM category-error point—but B's case suffered a real execution cost: three of five speeches (opening, rebuttal 2, and crucially the closing) were clipped for exceeding word limits, cutting off B's alternative-policy explanation each time, including at the most important moment, the final closing. This left B's strongest constructive alternative underdeveloped and denied it the chance to land a complete final impression, while A delivered a full, focused closing that crystallized its "status quo vs. imperfect progress" framing. That combination of comparably strong substantive material for B but consistently incomplete delivery, against A's cleaner and more complete execution, gives A the clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Q&A exchange on the voluntary/enforcement contradiction (A's Q1/Q2 forcing B to choose between "policy is toothless if voluntary" or "policy is coercive if enforced") was the most decisive clash; A's answer citing border penalties as closing the free-rider loophole was reasonably effective, while B's rejoinder identifying that this still mainly hits smaller exporters rather than China/India's domestic coal use was strong but got cut off before it could be fully pressed home in the closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by exposing the unresolved tradeoff between voluntary participation and effective global enforcement, while showing that equal penalties impose sharply unequal development burdens. Side B offered a strong collective-action case, but relied on a false choice between penalties and helplessness, introduced its coalition-border design late, and never adequately answered financing, retaliation, or transition-capacity concerns. Side A's repeated clipping was an execution cost, but its decisive case remained visible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's opt-out answer was decisive: once non-signatories are targeted through coalition border penalties, the policy is no longer merely a voluntary common commitment, and Side B still had to show that coercion would secure major coal users without disproportionately harming poorer exporters or fracturing cooperation.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): CON kept a live route that PRO never closed: the voluntarism-enforcement contradiction. If joining is voluntary, China/India opt out and penalties hit smaller exporters like Bangladesh/Vietnam; if it is coercive via border penalties on domestic energy choices, it triggers sovereignty backlash and retaliation. That equal-rules vs unequal-burdens and CCS-is-hollow critique survived both rebuttals, while PRO's strongest point about 30 years of voluntary failure was blunted by CON's false-binary response. Clipping costs limit margin to narrow.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B R1 false-binary + voluntary/coercive incoherence: showing PRO cannot have both universal enforcement and voluntary opt-in, and that equal 2040 deadlines impose radically unequal burdens while CCS defense is practically empty — points PRO largely sidestepped in R2.
