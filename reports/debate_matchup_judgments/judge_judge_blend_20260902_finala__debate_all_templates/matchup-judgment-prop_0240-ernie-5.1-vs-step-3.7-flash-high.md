### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Both sides argued with real force and internal consistency, making this a genuinely close call. PRO (Side B) maintained a coherent enabling-condition framework throughout—legitimacy is what allows technocratic advice to be adopted, funded, and sustained—and defended the NZ example capably under pressure, correctly noting that democratic trust transformed epidemiological models into mass compliance. PRO's "engine with no steering wheel" metaphor cleanly captured the existential danger of competence without legitimacy. CON (Side A), however, landed the debate's most decisive single argument: for catastrophic and irreversible harms—Brexit's permanent GDP loss and poverty increase, Iraq's hundreds of thousands of deaths—no accountability mechanism can restore what was lost. PRO's answer to this pressure ("legitimacy mobilizes collective action for existential threats") was a partial deflection rather than a direct resolution; it assumes democratic mobilization channels competent judgment, which is precisely the condition CON contests. CON also successfully recontested PRO's flagship examples: the NZ success was shaped by unelected epidemiologists (a point PRO addressed but could not fully neutralize), and EU austerity backlash was driven substantively by the policy's economic incompetence, not merely by the perception of technocratic imposition. PRO's framework is philosophically sound when legitimacy channels good expertise, but it becomes circular when democratic majorities endorse incompetent decisions—and CON exposed that circularity repeatedly. PRO's one execution advantage (CON had two clipped speeches versus PRO's one) softens but does not erase CON's comparative edge on the irreversibility clash, which remained live and unresolved through the closing. CON's specific precision on Brexit figures is marginally brittle, but the broader examples are plausible and well-grounded in background knowledge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's irreversibility pressure (crystallized in CON's Q2 and sustained through closing) versus PRO's response that legitimacy mobilizes collective action against existential threats. PRO's answer did not engage the specific failure mode—democratically legitimized but technically incompetent decisions causing permanent harm—and instead assumed the outcome (collective action will be competently directed) that CON was contesting. This left the irreversibility argument as the clearest unresolved clash in CON's favor and is why CON's path to victory is marginally cleaner.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): A won by making the more decisive comparison: legitimacy can help implementation and accountability, but it cannot undo irreversible harms from incompetent decisions like Iraq, Brexit, or failed pandemic policy. B had a strong usability and compliance frame, but too often equated technocratic competence with undemocratic imposition and never fully answered why post hoc democratic correction outweighs preventing catastrophic error in the first place.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange was against B's accountability claim: replacing leaders after Iraq or Brexit does not reverse deaths, economic loss, or degraded public capacity, so democratic legitimacy is not an adequate substitute for competence in high-stakes decisions.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A won because its core route—irreversible harms from competent failures cannot be fixed by legitimacy—remained live after every exchange; Side B's legitimacy-first framing was repeatedly neutralized by the Brexit/Iraq examples and the NZ/EU reversals, with clipped rebuttals adding execution cost to B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's direct A1/A2 answers to B's pressure questions on EU austerity and Iraq irreversibility cleanly dismantled B's "precondition" claim without widening the debate.

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO built the stronger live route to victory by framing democratic legitimacy not as a competing value but as the precondition that allows any expertise to deliver real public benefit. This framing was resilient: even CON's best examples kept feeding it. CON's Hartz argument was damaged by PRO's pressure question, which correctly noted massive protests, a destroyed SPD coalition, and a decade of harm—and CON's A1 response essentially conceded that short-term harm to real populations is an acceptable price for long-term technocratic gain, a position that is hard to defend under the motion as written. On Eurozone austerity, PRO offered a coherent causal mechanism—the technical multiplier error was enabled by excluding affected populations from deliberation—that CON never squarely neutralized, instead just reasserting it was a math problem. The Washington carbon tax, CON's strongest standalone point, was substantially neutralized by PRO's reply: voters rejected it not from irrationality but because the technocratic design skipped the deliberation that would have addressed regressivity, turning what CON framed as legitimacy-overriding-competence into evidence that absent democratic input, even technically sound policy is dead on arrival. CON's closing line "not who voted—what works" is rhetorically crisp but argumentatively circular, because the debate showed repeatedly that policies designed without legitimacy frequently don't work. Both sides incurred clipping costs in later rebuttals, roughly symmetric in Rebuttal 2, though PRO was clipped in Rebuttal 1 as well—a real execution cost that prevented a wider margin. Overall PRO made the comparative case cleaner: legitimacy corrects technical blind spots, builds compliance, and creates accountability, meaning its absence is not just a fairness problem but a technical competence problem too.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's answer to CON's Washington carbon tax pressure question was the most decisive exchange. CON framed the voters' rejection of a technically sound emissions policy as proof that legitimacy can override good outcomes. PRO responded that the rejection happened precisely because technocratic design bypassed the deliberation that would have caught regressivity concerns before the ballot—turning CON's clearest win into evidence for PRO's core thesis that skipping democratic grounding creates the policy failures it then blames on democratic legitimacy.

A’s Hartz and central-bank style examples kept the debate close, but B’s broader legitimacy-as-foundation framing survived.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s best rebuttal was turning the Washington carbon tax: rather than conceding voter rejection as a democratic failure, B argued the rejection exposed a technocratic design that lacked public deliberation over regressive impacts, showing why legitimacy is necessary for competence to become implementable policy.
