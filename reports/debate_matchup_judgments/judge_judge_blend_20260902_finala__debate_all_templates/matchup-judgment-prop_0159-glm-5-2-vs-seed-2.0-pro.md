### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO (B) built and maintained a cleaner structural framework: AI training is checkpointable batch processing, making it the paradigm case for demand-response scheduling rather than a protected continuous-process load; the proposition removes a special exemption rather than imposing a special burden; and scheduling costs, while real, differ in kind from irrecoverable physical destruction. That distinction held through the exchange and was never squarely neutralized. PRO also correctly flagged CON's EU relocation claim as brittle fake precision and effectively reframed the relocation argument as a logic that would veto all grid regulation. CON's strongest contributions were genuine: the fiber-mobility asymmetry (compute shifts grids over a fiber line; steel mills cannot) was a real structural point PRO only partially answered with the "vetoes all regulation" move, and the observation that PRO's own advocacy—calling AI "the most flexible large load"—functionally argues for curtailing AI first, creating tension with PRO's "equal footing" claim. PRO's Q1 pressure question directly exposed CON's plain-language overreach, and CON's A1 response, while not evasive, essentially conceded that in practice operators would curtail AI first, which PRO used well. CON's closing was rhetorically tight but repetitive rather than sharpening the live clashes. PRO's closing was clipped (format execution cost) but had crystallized the key distinction across earlier rounds. On balance, PRO's framework was more coherent and better defended, giving it a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure question [B3.1]—exposing that "no priority over other discretionary loads" means equal footing, not a first-to-curtail mandate—forced CON to defend a reading of the proposition that depended on PRO's advocacy rather than the plain text. CON's A1 response [A4.1-A4.2] argued operators would inevitably read it that way given PRO's framing, which was a real counterpoint, but PRO's follow-up in B4.4-B4.5 cleanly separated "strips special status" from "imposes special burden" and noted that CON had been arguing for priority treatment all along without justifying it. This exchange was the most decisive in the debate because it clarified what the proposition actually requires and left CON defending an inference rather than the text.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): A won by keeping the debate tied to the proposition: AI training should not receive priority over other discretionary loads during peaks. A gave the cleaner mechanism that training is a flexible, checkpointable load whose curtailment costs are mainly delay and money, while peak power imposes socialized reliability and emissions costs. B had real pressure on lost compute and relocation, but repeatedly overread the motion as mandatory first-in-line curtailment and leaned on brittle, unsupported claims like the EU relocation example and exact operational timelines. A answered the cost point directly and made B's priority claim look like special pleading.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer that B's own lost-progress argument proves the loss is a scheduling cost rather than physical destruction was the key exchange; it neutralized B's strongest technical comparison and reinforced why AI belongs in the curtailment pool.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO successfully established that AI training is a flexible load whose interruption costs are recoverable scheduling delays rather than irrecoverable physical losses, making it the appropriate load to curtail during peaks. While CON landed effective points about jurisdictional arbitrage and real costs, CON's attempt to equate compute delays with ruined steel never recovered from PRO's difference-in-kind rebuttal, and CON's claim that relocation leaves grid peaks unchanged contained a logical gap that weakened its causal story. PRO maintained a cleaner comparative frame: removing a special exemption is not the same as imposing a special burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's distinction between recoverable scheduling delays and irrecoverable physical destruction (ruined steel, scrapped chemicals), which decisively undermined CON's core analogy between AI training and continuous-process industrial loads.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Both sides debated competently throughout, but CON (Side A) edges ahead on two decisive contributions. First, the "emissions laundering" counter in Rebuttal 2 was CON's most original and damaging move: PRO's own A2 answer — "we do not care which grid runs the training" — became a concession that CON exploited by pointing out that relocated training runs continuously for weeks on dirtier grids, not just during narrow regional peak windows. PRO never cleanly resolved this: the regional/time-bound response addressed narrow peak displacement but not the continuous higher-emissions baseline that would result from long-term facility relocation. Second, CON's "last-place rule vs. equal treatment" framing was sharper than PRO acknowledged. The proposition does say "required to curtail" — implying a standing obligation — while CON correctly noted every other discretionary load retains voluntary demand-response participation with compensation. CON's alternative (integrate AI into existing demand-response markets rather than mandate curtailment) was coherent and was never fully rebutted by PRO; PRO's response that AI is currently exempted from demand-response only confirmed the policy gap CON identified, without explaining why mandatory curtailment beats simply extending the existing market framework. PRO scores real points: the life-safety framing remained emotionally live, the "uniquely flexible load" argument was substantively strong and understated by CON, and PRO's Q1 pressure question (name another discretionary load with formal grid priority) was only partially answered — CON cited firm contracts rather than formal priority designation. But PRO's failure to squarely address continuous emissions from relocated training, and the unanswered tension between "equal treatment" and "standing curtailment mandate," left CON with the cleaner comparative route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's exploitation in Rebuttal 2 of PRO's A2 concession ("we do not care which grid runs the training") was the most decisive exchange. PRO intended this as a reframing — peaks are regional and time-bound, so shifted training lands in another grid's off-peak hours — but CON correctly turned it back: training runs continuously for weeks, not hours. Relocating the facility to a jurisdiction without curtailment rules means continuous higher emissions on that dirtier grid around the clock, not just avoidance of a local peak. PRO never recovered a clean answer to this in closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by making the mandate-versus-market clash decisive. A established an intuitive public-safety and flexibility case, but leaned on some unsupported precision and never fully answered B's point that ending priority does not require a unique standing curtailment mandate. B persuasively argued that demand-response and emergency authority capture flexibility without targeted burden, and kept the relocation and emissions-leakage objection live through closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was pressing A on why the proposal was equality rather than a last-place rule, then answering A's priority question with firm industrial contracts and arguing that the better fix is extending demand-response participation rather than imposing a blunt mandate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON undermined PRO's central equality framing by demonstrating that the proposition would impose a unique mandatory curtailment order on AI training while other discretionary industrial loads continue to operate under voluntary demand-response markets and firm power contracts. CON's market-integration alternative was never substantively rebutted by PRO, and the siting/emissions-leakage argument—exposing that a mandate could push continuous base-load training to dirtier grids—directly weakened PRO's life-safety framing. PRO's claims about unique AI grid priority remained contested status-quo assertions, and without proving that a blunt mandate outperforms extending existing market mechanisms, PRO's path to victory collapsed under the comparative burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal that permanent facility relocation means training runs around the clock on a potentially dirtier grid, turning PRO's "we do not care which grid runs the training" into an argument about emissions laundering rather than public safety; this materially damaged PRO's core framing and was never recovered.
