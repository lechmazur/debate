### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a clear but competitive debate on three main grounds. First, PRO's institutional-capture argument—that CON's "mission alignment" rationale is a blank check for any faction controlling the institution, because the same public-funding logic that justifies "equitable access" pledges today justifies "patriotic virtue" oaths tomorrow—was the debate's strongest unrefuted claim. CON tried to answer it by distinguishing professional-duty affirmations from personal ideological declarations, but never demonstrated that this distinction is enforceable against a hostile legislature, which is precisely where the risk lives. Second, PRO's pressure question Q2 forced CON to effectively concede that written pledges are not better evidence of future performance than professional records—a concession that substantially undercut CON's affirmative rationale for allowing such statements at all. Third, CON suffered a real execution cost from three clipped speeches, each of which trailed off before completing arguments (notably the limiting-principle development in the Rebuttal 2 closing). PRO consistently answered CON's pressure questions directly with clear A1/A2 labels and without evasion, while CON's answers, though substantively reasonable, were weaker on the critical institutional-trust question. CON did make a genuinely useful distinction between professional-duty commitments and ideological commitment declarations, and PRO could not entirely eliminate it—the two positions converged more than either side acknowledged. But PRO's structural argument that even a coherent line provides no real-world protection against partisan capture remained live at the end of the debate, and that is the decisive reason PRO wins.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answering CON's pressure questions: A1 articulated the clean professional-duty-vs.-ideological-declaration distinction that PRO had been developing, and A2 demonstrated that traditional hiring mechanisms handle the "imperfect past record" problem CON raised—without the chilling cost of compelled declarations. Combined with the crystallizing observation that CON's framework requires trusting every future legislature and governing board to stay within the line, this exchange was the moment PRO most clearly dominated: it answered the live pressure, sharpened the comparison, and surfaced the institutional-capture risk that CON never squarely neutralized.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): B won by making the proposition easier to decide: public universities can require performance of job duties, but should not require ideological loyalty statements. A’s public-mission examples mostly collapsed into ordinary duty requirements, which B repeatedly allowed through job descriptions, interviews, records, and reviews. B also pressed the stronger limiting-principle argument: once “mission alignment” justifies compelled ideological affirmations, hostile future boards can impose their own orthodoxies. A had a plausible concern about overbreadth, but repeated it without proving that ideological pledges are necessary or distinct from enforceable professional duties.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it directly answered A’s pressure questions by separating duty commitments from ideological belief statements, then turned A’s evidence point by arguing written pledges are worse evidence than records and performance review.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side B won decisively by drawing a sharp distinction between agreeing to perform professional duties (which remains allowed) and compelled ideological declarations (which the motion bans). ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but CON (Side A) identified and sustained the debate's most decisive unanswered pressure: the motion provides no neutral adjudicating mechanism for the distinction PRO insists is clear. PRO's repeated answer—the line is drawn by "core job functions"—is circular; it names the criterion without identifying who resolves contested cases. CON pressed this gap effectively through every round, using DEI statements as a genuinely hard case PRO never fully escaped. PRO's strongest move—that formal loyalty tests are systematically more exclusionary than informal bias, and that the prohibition is narrow—was substantive but incomplete: PRO never squarely answered CON's Q1 (who adjudicates?) or CON's reinforcement that courts and legislatures, as the real enforcers, carry their own political agendas. CON also landed a live unrebutted point in PRO's B4.1: when PRO said the line is "not drawn by partisan legislatures or courts," CON immediately showed that answer is aspirational rather than operational, and PRO's closing did not resolve it. CON's own weakness—why existing legal tools have failed—was exposed by PRO's Q2 and never fully addressed, preventing a larger margin. Overall, CON's governance route to victory survived the exchange more intact than PRO's workability claim, giving CON a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's Rebuttal 2 response to PRO's A1 answer. PRO claimed the line between professional commitments and ideological tests is drawn by "core job functions, not partisan legislatures or courts." CON immediately identified this as circular and evasive: when a chemistry professor's statement on supporting diverse lab students sits on the exact border PRO insists doesn't exist, someone must adjudicate—and the actual adjudicators are courts and legislatures carrying political agendas. This turned PRO's own framing against it and left the motion's central workability problem live through the closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the motion focused on political or ideological loyalty tests rather than ordinary professional standards. Side A had the strongest objection on line-drawing and government micromanagement, especially around DEI statements, but leaned too heavily on treating the ban as inevitably reaching legitimate job-related commitments. Side B gave the cleaner comparative reason: formal ideological requirements institutionalize factional exclusion and directly threaten academic freedom, while conduct and professional standards can remain intact. Side A also failed to directly answer Side B's pressure about why existing legal tools have not been enough.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's most decisive rebuttal was that Side A conflated professional competency standards with ideological loyalty tests, and that formal ideological statements make exclusion systematic rather than merely hidden or informal.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by consistently arguing that formal loyalty tests institutionalize ideological bias, while effectively exposing a flaw in Side A's case. Side A structurally evaded B's second pressure question about why existing laws have failed to stop current litmus tests, leaving A's defense of the status quo unsupported. Additionally, A contradicted itself by arguing that formal statements are good because they can be litigated, while simultaneously arguing a ban is bad because it invites litigation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal, which effectively dismantled A's 'underground bias' argument by noting that formal tests provide bureaucratic cover for systematic exclusion, citing mid-century loyalty oaths.
