### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by successfully turning PRO's central framework against it. PRO's case rested on the irreversibility of transferring physical manufacturing bottlenecks, but CON demonstrated that the export ban itself manufactures the irreversible outcome: a domestic tool industry with a guaranteed order book and captive demand. CON layered this with the servicing-lever argument—foreign installed tools create a reversible, decade-long dependency on spare parts, calibration, and software, whereas the ban destroys that leverage in exchange for an unnamed amount of delay. PRO never resolved CON's dilemma that either the frontier node matters militarily (in which case building the adversary's indigenous path is catastrophic) or it does not (in which case the ban achieves little). When pressed to name the delay, PRO declined, while CON crystallized the clash in a memorable closing that framed the policy as choosing the irreversible risk over the reversible one.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's "order book" turn: the export ban removes proven foreign tools from sale, forcing the adversary's fabs to become the first paying customers for domestic equipment, thereby funding the very indigenous substitution PRO fears.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): CON wins the irreversibility weighing. PRO establishes equipment as a chokepoint, but CON shows military AI runs largely below that node, older tools can be stretched to near-leading-edge, and the ban itself creates the paying order book that lets a domestic tool stack learn. That double-bind – little near-term delay yet accelerated long-term self-sufficiency – survives PRO's 'tutor not kill switch' reply, while PRO never quantifies delay and under-answers the revenue/design-out cost. The lever of a serviced foreign installed base is more reversible than a guaranteed domestic champion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4 ladder argument – prototype needs customers, ban supplies them – plus the A3 pressure answer that turns PRO's mature-node claim against the chokepoint thesis.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side A won by decisively dismantling Side B's core "lever" argument. A's framing of foreign servicing as a "tutor, not a kill switch" exposed the critical flaw in B's assumption that an adversarial military would tolerate permanent dependence on foreign engineers for its strategic fabs. A also demonstrated excellent epistemic discipline by refusing to invent a specific year-count for the delay when pressured, instead focusing on the comparative weighing of irreversible physical transfer versus finite, shareable commercial costs. While Side B's "order book" and revenue feedback loop arguments were strong, A successfully countered that the physical machines themselves are the prerequisite for indigenous substitution, making the physical chokepoint the decisive factor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it answered pressure questions by dismantling the "servicing lever" (explaining that field engineers transfer tacit knowledge rather than providing a permanent kill switch) and cleanly refused the trap of inventing a fake year-count for the delay, pivoting back to the core weighing of irreversibility.
