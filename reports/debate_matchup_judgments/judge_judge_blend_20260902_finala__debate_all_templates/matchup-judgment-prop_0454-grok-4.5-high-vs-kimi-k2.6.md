### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently and followed format well, including proper A1:/A2: compliance after pressure questions. PRO built a tighter, more consistent core case: automatic transfers act as insurance, the debt is time-bound by the same trigger that creates it, and refusing support produces permanent scarring that raises the future fiscal burden more than the temporary debt does. This "manageable temporary debt vs. permanent structural damage" framing stayed coherent through the closing. CON raised legitimate targeting and supply-shock/inflation concerns, and its distinction between conditional job-linked subsidies and unconditional cash (in answering Q1) was a genuine sharpening move. But CON's own proposed alternative — "pre-authorized" wage subsidies and firm credit that disburse "with equal speed" — quietly reintroduces the same automaticity CON's core objection condemns in PRO's plan, an internal tension CON never resolved and PRO could have pressed harder but at least implicitly benefited from. PRO's answers to pressure questions were serviceable though somewhat thin (asserting legislatures "retain power to suspend" without squaring that with PRO's own speed argument), but PRO's overall throughline was cleaner and less self-undermining than CON's, giving PRO the marginally stronger path to the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The pressure-question exchange on the ratchet problem: B's Q1 (asking whether wage subsidies escape the same constituency-ratchet CON criticizes) forced A into distinguishing conditional vs. unconditional payments, but A's later reliance on "pre-authorized" fast triggers for its alternative undercut its own automaticity critique, which PRO's framing was positioned to exploit.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by establishing that rapid demand support prevents compounding layoffs, business failures, and fiscal scarring, then weighing those harms against temporary debt. Side A raised meaningful supply-shock, targeting, and political-ratchet concerns, but often assumed features not required by the proposition, such as universal identical checks, non-scaling benefits, and an inability to suspend the rule. Its preferred wage subsidies and firm credit also never fully answered B's aggregate-demand mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response that automaticity creates a rebuttable default rather than abolishing legislative override substantially weakened the supply-shock objection. Combined with the argument that firm-side tools cannot replace lost household demand, this preserved B's central case while Side A's answer relied on an unsupported claim that automatic transfers cannot be calibrated to downturn severity.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling the mechanical nature of the proposed automatic stabilizers. They effectively demonstrated that a blunt unemployment trigger cannot distinguish between demand and supply shocks, nor calibrate to the depth of a crisis, leading to uncalibrated waste and inflation risks. Side A also provided a superior comparative alternative (pre-authorized wage subsidies tethered to job matches) and defended it brilliantly against Side B's pressure questions, explaining why it avoids the political ratchet better than unconditional cash. Side B relied heavily on the speed argument but struggled to defend the mechanism's bluntness, conceded too much on targeting leakage, and undermined their own automaticity argument by relying on legislative overrides for supply shocks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they answered the pressure questions by explaining why wage subsidies avoid the political ratchet (because they are tethered to productive activity and naturally expire when job matches dissolve) and exposed the uncalibrated waste of a mechanical trigger that pays the same benefit whether unemployment is at 6% or 12%.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, competent cases, but PRO's core mechanisms held up better under pressure. PRO's crowding-out-in-recession argument ("crowding out is a danger when resources are fully employed, not when millions are idle") was never squarely rebutted—CON kept reasserting that a larger debt stock reprices risk without engaging the idle-resources distinction. PRO's analogy that existing automatic stabilizers (progressive taxes, UI) already expand and contract without political battles was a strong answer to CON's "political entrenchment" worry; CON's response ("thresholds drift") was largely assertion without a mechanism explaining why this program would behave differently from existing ones. CON's strongest and most consistent line—that automatic cash dulls search intensity and mobility exactly when vacancies reappear—was maintained well but met by a plausible countervailing mechanism (destitution itself destroys mobility via lost housing/transport), leaving that exchange roughly a wash rather than a CON win. The Pressure Questions round was sharp on both sides and both answered with proper A1/A2 formatting, but PRO's answers more directly closed the loop on self-reversal and reallocation, while CON's answer to "how does UI reform help the gig worker next month" was somewhat evasive (deferring to pre-crisis reform). PRO's Rebuttal 1 being clipped is a real execution cost and slightly tempers the win, but the surviving content and the later rounds still carried the decisive mechanisms, giving PRO the cleaner path to victory overall.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's point in Rebuttal 1/2 that "crowding out is a danger when resources are fully employed, not when millions are idle" directly undercut CON's debt-spiral mechanism and was never squarely answered, becoming the most damaging unresolved exchange of the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B won by making speed, demand preservation, and irreversible household scarring outweigh temporary downturn debt. B also answered the incentive objection comparatively: destitution can destroy the housing, transport, and networks needed for reemployment, while transfers contract as unemployment falls. A never adequately established that an unemployment-linked formula would remain elevated after its trigger reversed, and its claims about permanent entrenchment and sovereign repricing were largely asserted. B's clipped rebuttal and malformed reference IDs reduce the margin, but do not overturn its clearer causal and comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was on automatic wind-down. It argued that a predetermined unemployment trigger contracts without a politically difficult repeal vote, directly undercutting A's claim that automaticity inherently locks in higher spending. A answered with possible constituency pressure, but did not explain why that would defeat the formula absent new legislative action.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B won by successfully dismantling Side A's core mechanisms. Side B's argument that destitution destroys the physical assets (cars, housing) and networks necessary for labor reallocation was a brilliant, original, and decisive rebuttal to Side A's claim that cash transfers dull search incentives. Furthermore, Side B effectively neutralized Side A's macroeconomic crowding-out argument by correctly noting that sovereign borrowing costs typically fall during demand-short recessions, a point Side A failed to adequately recover from. Side A's reliance on an idealized, timely discretionary legislative process was consistently undermined by Side B's highly effective "speed of reality vs. speed of political theater" framing. Side B's minor clipping in Rebuttal 1 was a negligible execution cost that did not materially harm its otherwise dominant case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's flip of Side A's labor reallocation argument, explaining that a cash floor preserves the physical assets and stability required for geographic mobility, whereas destitution and eviction trap workers in place and cause permanent scarring.
