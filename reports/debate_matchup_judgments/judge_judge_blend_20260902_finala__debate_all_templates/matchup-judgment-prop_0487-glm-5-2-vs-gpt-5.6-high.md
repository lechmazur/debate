### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and the debate stayed tightly focused on the real fault line: whether pre-tested, funded standing hardware/launch readiness is meaningfully different from—and superior to—an enhanced, permanent research/preparedness posture. PRO's lead-time mechanism (deflection requires years of rehearsed capability, and assembly/validation during a crisis eats the very window that makes deflection possible) was never fully rebutted; CON's closing answer ("long warning permits adaptation; short warning can defeat even a stockpile") restated the claim rather than engaging the mechanism. PRO also successfully turned CON's asteroid-heterogeneity/certification objection into a tu quoque (bespoke response faces the identical unknown-parameters problem, only later and under worse time pressure), and CON's rebuttal to that point was thin. CON's strongest and most damaging move was the equivocation charge—that PRO's "permanent infrastructure" quietly slid from ready flight hardware to platforms, templates, and authority, collapsing into what CON already conceded as permanent preparedness. This exposed a real definitional inconsistency in PRO's case and was pressed effectively through both rebuttals and the closing. But PRO's A1 answer partially defused it by pointing to a funded, non-discretionary maintenance obligation and a concrete readiness benchmark (launch within the orbital-mechanics window), giving PRO a live distinction that survived, even if imperfectly articulated. On balance, PRO's core causal case (lead time governs feasibility) remained more intact than CON's core case (bespoke flexibility suffices), even though CON's equivocation pressure was the sharpest single line of attack in the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's tu quoque on the certification problem ("nor can CON's bespoke approach be [pre-certified], since it also faces unknown composition and spin... Fragmentation and corridor-shift risks exist identically under improvised crisis response—and worse") most damaged CON's central heterogeneity objection, and CON's closing did not directly re-engage it, leaving that exchange live in PRO's favor.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON kept the decisive clash on what the proposition actually requires—dedicated permanent deflection hardware versus adaptable research-centered preparedness—and forced PRO into progressive redefinition that collapsed the distinction. PRO’s lead-time and funding-permanence points were real, but never showed why target-blind stockpiles outperform contingency contracts, demonstrators, and flexible capacity on variable asteroids; CON’s marginal-value and variation replies held after exchange. Clipping cost B some polish but not the comparative core.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1–B2.2 and B4.4: PRO’s pivot from ready impactors to “platform family/templates” is just re-labeling CON’s preparedness while still claiming the proposition’s infrastructure win.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side A won because it maintained a clear, intuitive distinction between permanently funded research and preparedness (prototypes, teams, plans, launch contracts) and the costly, perishable deployment of completed interceptors and dedicated launch assets. It exposed Side B's shift from ready hardware to abstract "platform families" and showed that unknown asteroid variation makes pre-certified generic readiness illusory. Side B's lead-time argument was neutralized: long warnings permit bespoke adaptation, while short warnings defeat even stockpiled hardware. Side A's closing cleanly crystallized that it supports permanent preparedness without mandating a possibly mismatched fleet, while Side B's closing slightly mischaracterized Side A as opposing permanent funding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 charge that Side B redefined "infrastructure" from ready vehicles and impactor stocks to "templates and standing authority," and Side A's Rebuttal 2 clarification that deployment begins at continuous maintenance of completed interceptors—drawing a line Side B could not convincingly erode.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): A built a cleaner, more resilient case: define "permanent" as readiness (certified modular systems, rehearsed authorization, reserved launch access) rather than frozen hardware, then leverage the core asymmetry that missed deflection windows are irreversible while standing costs are reversible/upgradeable. This asymmetry argument was never squarely defeated. B's strongest weapon was the "collapse" move—arguing that once PRO's infrastructure is modular and upgradeable it is indistinguishable from a well-funded research program—but this is largely semantic: B's own answers (contingency contracts, rapid-response authority, design flexibility) concede most of what PRO demands while quibbling over the "flight-certified shelf-ready" detail, and B never explains why that residual distinction should decide the motion. B's opportunity-cost and obsolescence points were also blunted: A's "portfolio" reply and "rolling tests keep designs current" went largely unanswered in substance, with B mostly reasserting institutional-inertia claims without engaging the reversibility asymmetry. B's opening being clipped for length is also a real execution cost that limited its foundational case. Pressure questions were sharp on both sides, but A's answers (A4.1–A4.2) were more concrete and responsive, while B's answers leaned on repeating the collapse framing rather than resolving "who guarantees timely creation." Overall A narrowed the dispute more effectively and left B's central rebuttal looking more terminological than substantive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.5), especially the reply to the "collapse" argument distinguishing flight-certified, production-ready, authority-backed readiness from open-ended research capacity, combined with the asymmetry point that standing systems can be upgraded but missed windows cannot be recovered—this was the exchange B never fully neutralized.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): PRO won a competitive debate by making the irreversibility of missed launch windows and the middle-warning continuum clearer and stickier than CON’s opportunity-cost and obsolescence case. A successfully refused frozen-hardware caricatures, forced the distinction between pure research outputs and flight-certified operational chains, and showed that CON’s “agile” model either underprovides deployable capacity or silently becomes permanent readiness. CON’s binary (empty motion vs shelf obsolescence) was rhetorically sharp and kept pressure on the definition, but it never fully neutralized the continuum/asymmetry weighing or answered who guarantees conversion of know-how into timed launch without pre-built readiness. B’s clipped opening was a minor execution cost; A’s progression across rounds narrowed and resolved the clash more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4’s A1/A2 answers naming a certified operational chain (bus, software, tooling, assemblable package) plus the concrete ~4-year middle case, exposing CON’s “months or decades” binary and forcing the research-vs-readiness distinction into the open.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by establishing that permanent operational readiness—modular but flight-certified designs, production tooling, and rehearsed command authority—differs materially from a research niche and is necessary for intermediate-warning scenarios. Side A (CON) landed strong blows on opportunity cost and institutional inertia, but its clipped opening left its initial case incomplete, and its later attempt to collapse all readiness into research failed to explain how a research-first agency could reliably secure emergency appropriations, reconstitute suppliers, and field a target-specific mission within the narrow windows where deflection is viable. CON's forced binary—either obsolete shelf hardware or empty relabeling—could not fully account for PRO's continuum framing, which showed that readiness moves the survival boundary across warning times and that the asymmetric risk of delay favors front-loaded preparation. PRO's redefinition of permanent infrastructure as upgradeable operational capacity rather than frozen rockets was the pivotal exchange that neutralized CON's most intuitive attacks and left the core risk comparison leaning PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal (Turn 3 / B2), where PRO redefined "permanent infrastructure" as modular readiness and reserved operational chains rather than rusting rockets, forcing CON to abandon its strongest cost and obsolescence arguments and recast the debate around whether research alone could replicate an operational delivery guarantee.
