### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON built a tighter throughline: discretionary review is not just delay but the trigger point where replacement units, relocation assistance, and demolition conditions attach, so by-right removes the forum in which those protections are imposed even though it doesn't repeal them by name. CON's "reversible delay vs. irreversible removal" and "contestable rent hike vs. uncontestable demolition permit" framings gave the judge a clean decision rule that PRO never fully dismantled. PRO's core reply—that the no-density counterfactual isn't actually stable either—was a legitimate and important point, but in Rebuttal 2 PRO's own answer to the arithmetic question (the new units "absorb demand pressure" for other tenants while the displaced family is "gone either way") created an internal contradiction that CON caught cleanly in A4.5: PRO's defense either assumes protections exist (not in the motion) or concedes the named tenants are lost regardless (spent knowingly). PRO's closing restated the baseline argument but did not resolve that contradiction or seriously re-engage the reversibility/contestability distinction. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions format correctly with proper A1/A2 labeling, but CON's answers advanced its case (the "trigger" answer) while also landing a decisive follow-up punch in the next speech, giving it the edge in the decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.5 catch that PRO's own arithmetic defense (B4.4) internally splits into two incompatible claims—either protections exist for the displaced family (not required by the motion) or she is displaced regardless while only other tenants benefit from absorption—was the most damaging and unanswered exchange of the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B won the baseline comparison: blocking by-right density does not preserve stability when existing tenants still face scarcity-driven rent increases, renovation, and conversion. Side A powerfully emphasized irreversible, concentrated harm, but repeatedly treated by-right approval as necessarily eliminating demolition, replacement, and tenant protections. Side B correctly explained that objective protections can coexist with by-right zoning, and the motion does not require their repeal. Side A never established why discretionary bargaining was uniquely necessary or preferable to mandatory standards. Its nonexistent P2.3 and P2.5 references were also a minor execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its answer to the ten-versus-six hypothetical: Side A’s arithmetic assumed all ten existing units remain affordable absent densification. By exposing that counterfactual and distinguishing by-right approval from repeal of objective protections, Side B undercut the foundation of Side A’s case.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully distinguishing between "fightable" displacement under the status quo (rent hikes, discretionary negotiations) and "unfightable" displacement under by-right (licensed demolitions without municipal leverage). Side A sharply exposed that Side B's reliance on mitigating protections was incompatible with the by-right mechanism, which removes the very proceedings where such conditions are attached. Side B's macroeconomic scarcity argument was strong, but Side A's micro-level focus on the specific tenants facing demolition proved more decisive and better answered the motion's "even if" concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled Side B's "absorption" defense by pointing out that the new units absorb demand for the corridor's other tenants, while the specific family in the demolished building is simply spent for aggregate supply, crystallizing the "fightable vs. unfightable" harm tradeoff.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): PRO built a coherent, economically grounded case (capitalization of transit value, induced-vs-absorbed demand, homeowner capture of discretionary hearings) and used it to press a genuine fork: CON's own remedy ("allow density with conditions") concedes that protections can attach to by-right approval, undercutting the claim that discretionary review is necessary leverage. CON's strongest counter—that by-right can manufacture rather than merely absorb demand—was raised but never fully answered PRO's rebuttal that transit-driven land value capitalizes regardless of construction, meaning blocking supply just channels that value into rent hikes on existing tenants. CON's closing reasserted that by-right harm is "ungovernable too" without the same mechanistic backing PRO offered. PRO's opening being clipped is a real execution cost, but its rebuttals and pressure-question answers (properly labeled A1/A2) more decisively neutralized CON's central claims than CON neutralized PRO's, giving PRO the clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.4/B4.1-B4.2, where PRO shows that transit-driven land value capitalizes regardless of construction and that CON's own "density with conditions" remedy concedes the motion's substance, forcing CON to defend discretionary review purely on the ground that homeowners, not tenants, actually wield it.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won the central comparison: blocking new homes does not remove demand but redirects it toward existing tenants, creating diffuse displacement without project-linked remedies. Side A compellingly emphasized severe local harms and the motion’s failure to mandate protections, but repeatedly conflated by-right approval with protection-free development even though clear anti-displacement conditions can be codified in advance. B’s clipped opening and some overbroad empirical claims reduced the margin, but its causal comparison remained stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response to the induced-demand argument was decisive: transit and jobs already generate demand, so preventing new units often forces newcomers into the existing stock. B also showed that replacement, affordability, and return rules can coexist with by-right approval, weakening A’s density-with-conditions alternative.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won by decisively dismantling Side A's core argument that discretionary review provides necessary tenant leverage. Side B effectively demonstrated that discretionary review is structurally captured by homeowners and produces regional scarcity, whereas by-right densification creates actual housing stock to which protections can be attached. Side B's weighing of visible, governable displacement against invisible, systemic scarcity displacement was highly persuasive and rhetorically sharp. Side A's repeated reliance on the motion's lack of mandated protections was adequately neutralized by Side B's point that the status quo offers no such protections either, but with worse regional outcomes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to Side A's "leverage" argument, crystallized in the closing with "Conditions need a project to condition," which perfectly exposed the logical flaw in relying on discretionary vetoes to secure tenant protections.
