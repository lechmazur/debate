### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO won by establishing that supply-chain dominance is a reversible policy outcome concentrated in refining and manufacturing, not an immutable geographic fate, and by showing that CON's cost-focused alternative collapsed into strategic deference to a potentially coercive supplier. While CON's cost and speed arguments remained live, PRO's closing crystallized the security dilemma more cleanly: CON's plan for an export-license crisis was to rely on a globally integrated network, which PRO exposed requires exactly the kind of diversified capacity that CON's policy prevents anyone from building. PRO also successfully rebutted CON's claim that hardware cost is the absolute bottleneck by pointing to interconnection, permitting, and labor constraints, and reframed CON's comparative-advantage rhetoric as dependence on a state-built monopoly. The clipped Rebuttal 2 was an execution cost for PRO, but the remaining material answered both pressure questions directly and maintained the upper hand in the decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure questions and closing exposed the central tension in CON's case: CON portrayed the dominant supplier as simultaneously a benign, overcapacity provider of cheap goods and a dangerous actor who would retaliate with export bans. PRO forced CON into the position of arguing that security depends on avoiding provocation, which PRO reframed as costly strategic deference rather than genuine market comparative advantage.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO's live route - security insurance for manufacturing chokepoints (refining/wafer/cell, not geology) and risk-weighing overbuild vs underbuild - survived CON's provocation rebuttal. CON's strongest hits on EV sticker-shock and fiscal ouroboros landed but didn't neutralize the morning-export-license question, which closing crystallized as "diversity is factories." Clip cost for B2 kept margin narrow, but B still framed the vote more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q2/A2 exchange on export-licence denial - A answered "rely on globally diverse partners" and B crystallized that diversity requires building factories, turning CON's fallback into PRO's policy.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by building a much stronger comparative framework around security and the true nature of supply chain chokepoints. While Side A effectively highlighted the consumer costs of localization (the "economic ouroboros"), Side A failed to provide a viable alternative for security, ultimately falling back on relying on "diverse trading partners" without explaining how those partners would acquire the technology if the dominant supplier controls the chain. Side B's closing crystallized this flaw perfectly ("diversity is not a mood — it is factories"). Side B also successfully navigated the prompt's explicit concession of "higher prices" by weighing transitional premiums against the existential risk of foreign coercion, whereas Side A spent too much time arguing against the very costs the motion asked the judge to accept.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing dismantling of Side A's answer to Pressure Question 2. Side A suggested relying on "diverse trading partners" during an export ban, and Side B sharply pointed out that alternative suppliers only exist if someone paid to build the factories—the exact industrial policy Side A opposed.

### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): CON won by exposing that PRO's insurance premium was a permanent tax on decarbonization. CON cleanly rebutted PRO's security analogy with the stock-versus-flow distinction, showed localization fails to secure upstream minerals, and forced PRO to concede both unlimited price tolerance and permanent protectionism. PRO's admission that blocking cheaper imports was a "feature not a bug" proved fatal, as CON demonstrated this coalition would slow the transition every year to hedge a temporary, bounded risk. CON's framing of cheap clean tech as the real resilience provided the clearer comparative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's stock-versus-flow rebuttal (B2.2-B2.4) dismantled PRO's energy-weaponization analogy, and CON's follow-up pressure questions (B3.1-B3.2) forced PRO to concede any permanent premium and protectionist politics, which CON turned into a decisive "permanent tax on decarbonization" frame.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON won the central trade-off. PRO conceded in B4.1/B4.2 any premium 20-50% forever and permanent protection as a feature. A4 framed that as a permanent tax on decarbonization vs a bounded, self-punishing delay, with stock-vs-flow, warehousing, and existing diversification (Korea/Japan/India) plus geography limits on refining still unanswered. That made localization look like insurance that costs more than the accident.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.5 turning B's honest A1/A2 concessions (any premium forever, never accept cheaper imports) into permanent slowdown vs temporary delay, plus stock continues generating and exporter forfeits market.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins decisively by dismantling PRO's core security analogy (distinguishing between "stock" clean tech and "flow" fossil fuels) and ruthlessly exploiting PRO's fatal strategic concessions in Rebuttal 2. By forcing PRO to admit they would accept up to a 50% permanent price premium and that domestic champions would permanently block cheaper imports, CON proved that the motion guarantees a slower, vastly more expensive transition to hedge against a bounded, self-punishing supply shock. CON's rhetoric was exceptionally sharp, and their final weighing of mass consumer adoption (where price dictates the transition) versus sovereign ribbon-cuttings was highly persuasive and left PRO's case in ruins.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they weaponized PRO's pressure question answers to demonstrate that PRO's proposed "insurance premium" is actually a permanent, unbounded tax on decarbonization defended by a protectionist coalition, effectively proving CON's core thesis that the policy causes the very failure it claims to prevent.
