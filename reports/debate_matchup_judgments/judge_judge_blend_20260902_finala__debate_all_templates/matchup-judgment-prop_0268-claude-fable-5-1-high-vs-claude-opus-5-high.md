### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's practical objections. A's response to the expiry argument (a 12-month flag is better than 0 days of notice) and the substitution argument (banning the cheapest deception raises the cost of deceiving) neutralized B's best offense. B made a clever attempt to weaponize A's point about category-wide shrinking, but A's core principle that shoppers deserve to know about hidden price hikes remained intact and compelling.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal (A4.1-A4.2) elegantly destroying the expiry argument by pointing out that a normal price increase also just becomes the new normal, meaning a 12-month flag is vastly superior to the status quo's zero days.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): PRO kept the core burden alive — a shrunken pack is a conceded price rise — and turned CON's strongest pushes into contradictions. The habit-shopper point answered the legibility objection: repeat purchase means cross-product unit-price comparison fails and category-wide shrinking is invisible. The expiry answer reframed the 12-month flag as longer, not shorter, than an honest sticker hike's visibility. CON's quality-substitution and uniform-warning objections remained plausible but speculative and compatible with a both/and remedy, while PRO's pressure Qs on why firms pay to retool moulds if the sticker isn't read exposed the mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 A1-A2 on expiry and successor-mapping: an honest price rise is visible for one day then normalized, so 12 months of notice beats zero days, and false positives are true statements about replacement — turning CON's escape-hatch charge into proof that the rule raises the cost of the cheapest deception.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by building a more cohesive case and executing a devastating rhetorical trap in the closing. Side B's strongest offensive argument was that shoppers lack the attention to read shelf-edge annotations, relying only on the familiar headline price anchor. However, Side B's proposed alternative was to make the unit price larger and more legible. Side A brilliantly exploited this contradiction in the closing: if the shopper ignores annotations and only reads the familiar headline number, then Side B's remedy of a larger unit price fails by Side B's own logic. Side A also effectively neutralized Side B's "expiry date" and "wall of identical notices" arguments by reframing them as features, not bugs—a year of disclosure is better than the zero days a standard price hike gets, and a wall of notices accurately reveals category-wide hidden inflation. Side B's points on quality shrinkflation (skimpflation) were strong, but Side A's economic rebuttal that reformulation is costlier than shrinking a bag held up well. Side A's rhetoric was sharper, more memorable, and ultimately more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing synthesis exposing Side B's contradiction: Side B argued shoppers skip prose annotations to focus on the familiar headline price, yet proposed making the unit price (an annotation) larger as the solution. Side A pointed out that if attention is that scarce, Side B's own alternative dies first, while Side A's flag could simply be integrated into the number the shopper already reads.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side A won by decisively proving that 'delta' pricing (was/now) is highly salient to consumers and is currently withheld by sellers when it harms them. Side B's substitution argument (ingredient swaps) was strong, but A successfully mitigated it by pointing out that one of B's own substitution routes (headline price rises) achieves the exact transparency A seeks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's observation that the industry heavily uses 'was/now' framing for discounts dismantled Side B's argument that shoppers only care about cross-shelf 'levels' and wouldn't read a change flag.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): PRO wins the central information clash. The level-vs-change point [A2.1-A2.2] that unit-price levels require memory of past values for habitual buys was never answered by CON's sideways-only claim [B2.1], and the was/now analogy [A4.4] turns CON's own inattention argument into evidence of manufactured salience. Pressure Q1 about lobbying vs Carrefour exposes that the flag matters, and B's stigma reply [B4.1] concedes harm without showing no benefit. CON's best route — substitution to quality cuts — is blunted because PRO shows one substitution route (headline rise) is precisely the visible outcome sought, and ingredient cuts are undetected under either regime, leaving PRO with a live low-cost, high-salience fix.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 + Rebuttal 2 move from level to delta plus was/now framing — a disclosure that exists in form fails in function — which directly collapses B's "already printed, shoppers walk past it" and revealed-preference claims and sets up the pressure on why manufacturers fought Carrefour.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B wins because its central analytical argument—the substitution effect (the "three doors")—was never fully neutralized by Side A. Side B convincingly argued that stigmatizing shrinkflation (which is auditable) would push manufacturers toward ingredient downgrades (which are invisible), ultimately harming the consumer. Side A's rhetorical flourish about not keeping the lights off in one room effectively conceded that the mandate would lead to worse, un-auditable product quality. Side B also successfully reframed Side A's Carrefour evidence as a supply-chain shaming tactic rather than proof of consumer demand, and effectively defended the status quo's "sideways" shelf comparison against Side A's "historical delta" argument. Side A was highly persuasive on the hypocrisy of "was/now" pricing, but Side B's impact calculus on the actual outcome for the shopper was stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's "Three Doors" substitution argument, which forced Side A to concede that the mandate would push cost-cutting into invisible ingredient downgrades, effectively winning the impact calculus for the penny-counting shopper.
