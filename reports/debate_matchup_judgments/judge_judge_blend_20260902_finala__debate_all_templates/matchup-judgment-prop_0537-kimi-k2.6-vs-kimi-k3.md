### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): The decisive clash was the "imported chips" concession. B conceded early ([B2.2]) that sovereign hardware still depends on foreign-fabricated silicon, and A relentlessly exploited this throughout: sovereign compute cannot escape the same supply chain it claims to insure against, yet has only one pipeline and one spares drawer versus a hyperscaler's multi-jurisdictional inventory and failover capacity. B's answer (warehousing spares, domestic allocation control) delays but does not resolve this asymmetry, and A named this precisely in Rebuttal 2 and Closing: PRO's catastrophe scenario requires simultaneous betrayal by every global provider, while sovereign failure requires only one snapped supply line. B never found a clean rebuttal to this framing; instead it kept restating "control over allocation" without addressing that allocation is moot once the single pipeline is severed. A's multi-cloud/diversification answer was more responsive to B's own tail-risk scenario than B's "build more sovereign nodes" answer, which A correctly noted concedes cost blowout while worsening correlation risk (one ministry, one budget, one insider-threat surface). Pressure questions were sharp on both sides, but A's answers (A1/A2) directly closed the loop on the chip-supply asymmetry, while B's answers reasserted the domestication-of-allocation claim without fully rebutting the "one pipeline vs many" point. A's closing crystallized the debate's central tension cleanly; B's closing repeated earlier framing without adding a new rebuttal to A's asymmetry argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's [A2.1]-[A2.2] turning B's own chip-import concession into the decisive weakness of sovereign compute during the exact crisis scenario B relies on, reinforced in Rebuttal 2 and Closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side A won by effectively exposing the contradiction in Side B's worst-case scenario: if global supply chains collapse, the sovereign compute facility will also fail because it relies on imported silicon. Side A consistently used Side B's concessions (modest workloads, imported chips) to dismantle the need for billion-dollar state-owned facilities, offering jurisdictional diversity as a cheaper, more robust alternative to a finite stockpile of spare parts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2 effectively framed Side B's solution as insuring against a global catastrophe with an asset (imported chips) that the catastrophe would destroy, reducing B's grand vision of sovereignty to a depleting drawer of spare parts.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B wins on the import-dependency symmetry. A concedes chips are imported [A2.2] yet never solves replacement; stockpiling [A4.3] only delays failure. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): B built a tighter, more disciplined case: the opening framed dependency as an operational off-switch problem, and every subsequent speech pressed that frame rather than widening the debate. B's pressure questions were the turning point — asking how A's own fallback ("stockpile GPUs") differs from sovereign capacity except by being "unpowered and unusable," and noting that routing states toward "European sovereign clouds" is just depending on different foreigners. A's answers (national labs running readiness drills, capital-discipline flexibility) effectively conceded the operational core of B's case while trying to rebrand it, and A never satisfyingly closed the gap between "hours to activate a warehouse stockpile" and B's framing that a triage system dies in minutes. A's cost/depreciation and diversification arguments were clever but were shown by B to cut symmetrically against A's own remedies (burst contracts and multicloud also depreciate and also run on foreign chips), which neutralized rather than won those exchanges. A's Rebuttal 1 was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost that left some responses unfinished. B's closing crystallized the clash memorably ("sovereign compute capacity with the lights off," "an umbrella that folds in the rain"), while A's closing mostly repeated prior lines without resolving the stockpile-equivalence problem B exposed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question 1 ("who operates that stockpile... except that yours sits in a warehouse, unpowered and unusable?") followed by B's Rebuttal 2 pressing that A's own fallback is "sovereign compute capacity with the lights off" — this exchange forced A into a position that conceded the operational premise of B's case rather than refuting it.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won by decisively trapping Side A in a contradiction. When Side A proposed stockpiling GPUs instead of building a sovereign facility, Side B expertly demonstrated that this stockpile would suffer the exact same depreciation and foreign-chip vulnerabilities Side A warned about, while lacking operational readiness in a crisis.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing dismantled Side A's 'surge layer' of stockpiled GPUs by pointing out that relying on 'pre-negotiated burst contracts' with foreign providers during a crisis is 'an umbrella that folds in the rain.'
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): PRO wins the core security clash. CON conceded the off-switch risk and then proposed a state GPU stockpile [B2.5] and European sovereign clouds [B2.2] - which PRO exposed as sovereign capacity by another name, unpowered [A3.1] and dependent on other foreigners [A3.2]. PRO's operational vs genealogical distinction - minutes for a cloud suspension vs months for chips/firmware - survived, while CON's depreciation and theater objections boomeranged against its own stockpile. Clipped Rebuttal 1 was an additional execution cost for CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's turn in A4-A5 using CON's own B2.5 stockpile concession to show obsolescence and foreign-chip critiques dissolve CON's alternative and amount to sovereign capacity with the lights off.
