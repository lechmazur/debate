### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): B won the timing and domestic-capability comparison. A compellingly defended contestable procurement and exposed subsidy lock-in, but conceded that foreign incumbents may capture demand and never fully showed how output-linked support finances first-of-a-kind plants before competitive production. B offered the clearer sequence: disciplined producer support creates an initial supply base, after which demand can select winners.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5 was decisive: it used A’s reliance on above-market offtake and willingness to buy foreign packs to show that A either recreates producer de-risking indirectly or allows manufacturing learning to accrue abroad.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): CON won by exposing a structural timing problem: for first-of-a-kind battery and clean-tech plants, demand-side payments arrive too late to finance upfront CAPEX and low-yield burn, so favoring demand over producer subsidies leaves domestic supply undeveloped and cedes learning-by-doing to foreign incumbents. PRO’s response—bankable offtake and procurement—was partially turned when CON showed that above-market offtake for unproven domestic output is functionally producer de-risking by another name, blurring the boundary PRO relied on. PRO’s political-economy warnings about capture and gluts remained live but were narrower than CON’s central argument that the motion inverts the necessary sequencing for capital-intensive manufacturing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 turning offtake into disguised producer support: B4.2 and B4.5 showed that once PRO conceded above-market offtake is needed to finance first-of-a-kind CAPEX, the demand/producer distinction collapses, and the motion’s preference becomes incoherent.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): CON won the sequencing clash. PRO’s claim that private capital follows bankable offtake [A2.2][A4.1] was directly undercut by B’s valley-of-death timing: per-sale credits pay only after a competitive pack ships [B2.1], so demand cannot fund first-of-a-kind CAPEX and low-yield burn. B’s pressure that above-market offtake is producer support by another name [B3.1] was not answered, collapsing PRO’s demand vs producer distinction, and PRO conceded foreign winners are “doing its job” [A4.3], leaving B’s import-the-product/export-the-industrial-base warning [B1.3][B2.8] live. PRO’s picking-winners and hostage-plant critique was strong but B’s milestone/clawback + retained learning answer narrowed it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's A1/A2 and B2-B4 sequence that demand credits can't fund commissioning, and that PRO's own bankable offtake financing is functionally producer de-risking, plus the failure-mode weighing – you can write down a bad factory, you cannot claw back an industrial base you never built.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): A won a competitive debate by better defending the motion’s actual preference rather than an absolute ban on supply support. Its model tied procurement, phased domestic content, and contingent financing to real deployment, while B never fully established why speculative domestic capacity should outweigh faster abatement and lower white-elephant risk. B’s import-dependence critique was powerful, but often relied on defining any effective demand-backed support as producer policy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal directly answered B’s bootstrap challenge: guaranteed price, volume, and domestic-content commitments can make capacity financeable while preserving performance discipline. Its charge that B circularly relabeled such instruments as producer subsidies blunted B’s central objection, though B’s response remained strong.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by offering the cleaner framework and risk story. It successfully reframed the motion around cheap, deployed abatement rather than ceremonial domestic factories, and showed that demand-side policy disciplines capital by tying support to bankable offtake and staged performance. Side B’s “import invoice” objection was sharp, but Side A defused it by showing early imports can be leverage for localized content in later tranches, whereas B never fully answered the stranded-asset and corporate-welfare risks of paying for factories before customers exist. Side A’s closing crystallized the choice—who bears the risk?—in a way that made the comparative vote clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over whether guaranteed offtake is bankable for domestic plants or merely finances foreign incumbents, where Side A showed that staged procurement turns early imports into leverage while Side B argued that uncompetitive domestic lines cannot be made bankable without hidden producer subsidies.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): CON won the core sequencing clash. PRO's demand-pull avoids white elephants but never solved the lender test: offtake is bankable only for the plant that can hit the winning cell price. CON showed durable demand without upfront capital finances the already-subsidized incumbent, staged content without capitalized plants is waived, and PRO's "contingent, clawback-backed" support is producer policy relabeled. PRO conceding foreign cells winning as "success" undermined the industrial-policy aim of domestic supply.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.3-B2.5 and B4.1-B4.3 — offtake finances foreign expansion unless price is raised; if raised, it's a hidden producer subsidy, and LNG vs. cell line shows contracted cash flow doesn't close a subsidized cost gap.
