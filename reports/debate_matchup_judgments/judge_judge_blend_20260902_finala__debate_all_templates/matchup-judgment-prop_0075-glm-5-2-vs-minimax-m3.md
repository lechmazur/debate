### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins on three overlapping grounds. First, PRO held the limiting-principle advantage throughout: the proposition specifies what is protected (worship, clergy, sacraments, doctrinal teaching) and what is not (commercial marketplace activity). CON's counter-principle—"religious institutions order their own affairs"—was never bounded, and PRO's pressure question on the "we'd rather close than comply" leverage went unanswered in any principled way. Second, the bookstore concession [A2.1] was CON's own admission that functional distinctions are possible in principle; CON spent subsequent rounds trying to limit it to the "easy case," but the concession structurally helped PRO more than it hurt. CON's best counter—"a nurse in a Catholic ICU does identical work to a secular nurse, so the functional test has no grip"—is genuinely strong, but PRO's answer (the question is whether the employment relationship operates within the public marketplace, not whether the tasks are identical) is adequate and was partially covered by the tax/zoning parallel. Third, CON's Opening, Rebuttal 1, and Closing were all clipped for exceeding word limits—a repeated execution cost that left key arguments incomplete as presented. CON's concrete closure examples (Catholic Charities) were real and PRO's "targeted public investment" response was thin, keeping that sub-argument competitive, but it wasn't enough to offset the structural and execution deficits. The theological-judgment concern was CON's most powerful thread, but PRO's functional-distinction parallel (tax law, zoning) provided a workable, if imperfect, response, and CON never closed it out cleanly given the clipping. On balance PRO offered a cleaner path to a decision: a defined limiting principle, leverage from the opponent's own concession, and full speeches throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's leveraging of CON's bookstore concession [A2.1] was the most decisive exchange. CON conceded that a denomination's bookstore is not ministry, proving functional judgment is possible. PRO then spent the rest of the debate pressing that concession: if the line exists, the dispute is only where it sits, making CON's "impossible line-drawing" objection a policy disagreement rather than a constitutional impossibility. CON never fully escaped this bind—attempts to reframe it as "only the easy case" still left the structural point alive.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully defending its functional standard and exposing Side A's lack of a limiting principle. While Side A argued that regulating a Catholic hospital nurse requires forbidden theological judgment, Side B effectively countered that because the Catholic nurse's duties are functionally identical to a secular nurse's, courts can simply regulate the secular function without ever judging the underlying theology. Side A also failed to answer Side B's pressure question about what limits a religious organization from claiming any exemption by merely threatening to close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, which used existing tax and zoning laws to prove courts already draw functional lines without making theological judgments, effectively neutralizing Side A's First Amendment attack.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B built the stronger case by establishing a workable functional distinction (supported by tax/zoning analogies), directly leveraging A's bookstore concession to neutralize the theological-judgment attack, answering pressure questions without evasion, and weighing third-party harms against service continuity with concrete comparisons. Side A raised valid indivisibility concerns and closure risks but never supplied a limiting principle, repeatedly circled the same constitutional objection, and left key burdens (e.g., "where does the line sit?") unresolved after repeated pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4) directly answering both pressure questions with A's own concession and reframing the rural-hospital scenario as solvable policy rather than constitutional crisis.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) builds a cleaner comparative case across the debate. Its core distinction — the test sorts acts (clergy selection, doctrinal formation) not institutional identity — is workable and consistently defended. PRO correctly identifies that CON's "connected to mission" alternative has no stopping point: every religious employer can connect any decision to theology, collapsing the framework into case-by-case sincerity analysis. PRO also lands concrete humanizing costs — the gay couple denied a hotel room, the pregnant employee turned away — that CON never adequately counterweights. CON's strongest moment is its late argument that PRO "performs the ranking earlier, hiding it inside the definition of the act": calling a hospital bed 'accommodation' rather than 'ministry' is itself a theological classification. This is genuinely sharp and partially lands, but PRO's response — that asking what kind of act occurred is less entangling than evaluating sincerity of mission — holds up better under the full exchange. CON's alternative (compelling-interest framework) is already operative law and doesn't resolve the stopping-point problem. Two clipped turns for Side B are a real execution cost that keeps the margin narrow; neither side escaped the format penalty entirely cleanly. Overall, PRO drew the clearer, more actionable line and gave the stronger reason to prefer its test over CON's in the specific context the proposition demands.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The sharpest exchange is CON's late rebuttal [A4.5–A4.6] against PRO's "what the act is" framing: "Determining what an act IS already requires theological judgment. When PRO calls firing a billing clerk for her marriage 'employment, not formation,' it assumes the marriage policy isn't theological. For a Catholic institution, marriage theology IS core doctrine applied to staffing." This is the debate's most incisive turn. PRO's answer — that its test asks what the act is rather than what the institution feels — holds up better across the full exchange, but CON's challenge meaningfully constrains PRO's confidence that its line is clean.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A won by effectively demonstrating that Side B's "functional test" is intrinsically entangled with theology, while providing a clear alternative (the compelling-interest framework) that resolved Side B's worst-case scenarios. Side B's arguments were strong initially but suffered from two clipping penalties and failed to adapt to Side A's explanation of how the compelling-interest test handles commercial discrimination.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which pointed out that categorizing the firing of a billing clerk as "employment, not formation" is itself a theological judgment, cleanly neutralizing Side B's claim of objective neutrality.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B maintained the sharper functional distinction throughout, directly answered pressure questions on entanglement and bad-faith scrutiny, and crystallized a clean stopping-point contrast in later rounds. Side A’s compelling-interest alternative never neutralized the no-boundary critique.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 cleanly sorted acts vs. institutional self-description and exposed CON’s lack of threshold.
