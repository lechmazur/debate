### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON built its case around a single, well-sustained dichotomy: ecocide either stays narrow enough to prosecute (in which case it largely duplicates existing dumping/toxic-waste and civil liability regimes) or it tries to capture the systemic harms PRO's opening actually invoked (poisoned rivers, collapsed food systems), in which case it fails on causation and individual culpability. PRO opened with the systemic framing, then under pressure retreated to "bounded decisions," and CON explicitly and repeatedly exploited that shift ([A2.1], [A3.1], [A4.4], [A5.2]) without PRO ever fully closing the gap. PRO's reconciliation attempt ("bounded act, systemic consequence") was reasonable but largely asserted rather than argued through, and PRO's "redundancy is not disqualifying" line ([B4.4]) was thin against CON's overlap argument. PRO's strongest surviving point — that a captured or complicit state makes domestic remedies structurally unavailable — was never fully answered by CON (which mostly repeated "fix domestic institutions"), but this alone wasn't enough to offset the traction CON gained from the systemic/bounded contradiction. PRO's case was also weakened procedurally by two word-limit clippings (opening and closing), costing clarity and completeness precisely where PRO needed to land its strongest framing. CON's speeches stayed disciplined, consistently reinforced one clear throughline, and closed with the sharper, cleaner reason to vote against the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's exploitation of PRO's shift from "systemic harms" (opening, [B1.1]) to "bounded decisions" (rebuttal, [B2.3]), crystallized in [A2.1]-[A2.2] and pressed again in the Pressure Questions and both closings, was the most decisive exchange — PRO's attempted reconciliation in [B4.1] softened but never fully resolved the tension.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO built the clearer path to victory by centering the live gap—state-authorized catastrophic destruction where permits, corporations, and captured institutions shield decision-makers—and showing that existing tools (domestic regulation, civil liability, treaties) structurally fail precisely then. CON's definitional, cumulative-causation, selectivity, and institutional critiques created real pressure and forced PRO to narrow, but PRO adapted by treating "bounded" as the adjudicable act and "systemic" as the scale of proved consequence, while keeping high thresholds and complementarity as safeguards. CON never supplied a non-circular fix for the minister-with-a-permit case and overplayed the retreat narrative; PRO's answers under pressure and the final imperfect-accountability-versus-guaranteed-impunity comparison therefore decide the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO A4.1 / A4.5 distinguishing bounded culpable authorizations from systemic consequences while crystallizing the permit-as-shield comparison against CON.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO won the central bounded-act vs systemic-consequence clash. B defined ecocide as a provable authorization that can produce systemic injury, defusing A's retreat charge, and showed international recognition adds a backstop where permits become shields. A never named an existing international personal-liability offense for the state-authorized water poisoning hypo and its domestic-enforcement fix begs the question when the state is the authorizer. B also answered weaponization/selectivity pressure more directly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1 answering A3.1 by distinguishing bounded culpable conduct from systemic scale of harm – collapsing A's unworkable vs redundant dilemma and supporting the permit-as-impunity frame.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): The debate turned on whether ecocide's proposed definition ("severe, widespread, long-term, or systematic" harm plus knowledge/recklessness) provides the fair-notice legality that criminal law requires. CON's most damaging move was distinguishing ecocide from existing Rome Statute crimes: crimes against humanity and genocide are bounded by independently recognizable underlying acts (murder, torture, intent to destroy a group), while ecocide lets open-ended ecological adjectives do the entire work of defining the wrong. PRO never squarely answered this distinction—its analogy to "gravity thresholds already used by the ICC" repeatedly asserted parity without engaging the specific structural difference CON identified. PRO's strongest moves (flipping the selectivity objection into a reason for recognition, and noting existing tools have coexisted with ongoing ecological collapse) were rhetorically vivid and landed some points, but did not resolve the core ex ante clarity problem CON pressed in both pressure questions and rebuttals. CON's narrow-vs-broad dilemma also went essentially unrebutted in substance, with PRO's closing calling it "proving too much" without showing why the definitional distinction fails. CON does carry an execution cost, having two speeches clipped for exceeding word limits versus PRO's one, but the clipped material appears to be largely concluding flourish rather than load-bearing argument, so it modestly narrows but doesn't erase CON's substantive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3–A4.5), distinguishing ecocide's ecological adjectives from the independently recognizable underlying acts (murder, torture, group-destruction intent) that anchor existing international crimes, which PRO's closing conceded ground on by only asserting parity rather than refuting the distinction.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON built the cleaner path by keeping the live clash on fair notice, causation/intent, and structural selectivity, then forcing PRO into the narrow-or-broad dilemma that PRO never fully escaped. PRO’s gap/moral-signal case and ICC analogies remained strong and progressive, but repeatedly treated “gravity thresholds like CAH” as sufficient while CON showed those crimes sit atop independently recognizable wrongs and civilian-attack context that open-ended ecological adjectives lack. Existing-tools failure (Amazon/carbon) proved urgency, not that personal international criminalization is the superior fix. Both sides paid some clipping costs; CON still resolved the decisive design objections more cleanly for a third-party reader.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1/B2.7 and later B4.3–B4.6 dismantling the CAH/Rome-Statute analogy: existing crimes are bounded by structure PRO’s severity terms alone do not supply, turning clean-villain examples into ordinary fraud/pollution and contested development into post-hoc prosecutor judgment.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): CON won the decisive legality and equity clashes. While PRO pressed moral asymmetry and ICC precedent, CON answered both pressure questions with clear distinctions: genocide/CAH have independent wrongs and contextual elements, ecocide makes open-ended adjectives do all the work; and selectivity is uniquely fatal when lawful development trade-offs are at stake, concentrating risk on developing states. PRO's ex ante answer that impact assessments reveal an objective bar collapsed under rebuttal, leaving the narrow-or-broken dilemma live. Existing tools critique did not establish that a vague, retrospective personal crime would prevent harm better.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3-A4.6: A shows CAH calibration works because murder/torture are independently recognizable and bounded by civilian attack, while ecocide lets "severe/widespread" define the wrong itself, and that impact assessments are disputed projections that do not give fair notice, exposing PRO's Q1 answer as inadequate.
