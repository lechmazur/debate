### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by distinguishing private stolen property from loss-making public institutions and consistently tracing restitution costs to present students and taxpayers. Side B established a strong moral presumption against uncompensated benefit, but its claims about surplus endowments and existing arbitration machinery were insufficiently grounded, while its closing wrongly reduced substantive concerns about overlapping claims, legal stability, and public-service tradeoffs to mere paperwork. Side A also offered the sharper final comparison between targeted present-day assistance and perpetual land-linked obligations.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered both questions, challenged the feasibility of funding restitution from restricted endowments or executive pay, and exposed the tension between calling the remedy stolen-property restitution while accepting a small discretionary share rather than return of the asset.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) built the cleaner path by repeatedly distinguishing public title and equal-use institutions from private stolen-property cases, exposing the redefinition of “profit” as avoided rent plus the 1% inconsistency with genuine restitution, and crystallizing the equal-citizenship cost of perpetual ancestry-based encumbrance against a direct-funding alternative. Side B’s moral consistency claim was strong and limited in theory, but never neutralized the public-goods, overlapping-claims, and stability burdens that A kept live and decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 turn on redefined profit + 1% as political tax (not property law), which locked the inconsistency B could not square.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by systematically dismantling Side A's core analogies and exposing the practical contradictions in the proposition. B effectively demonstrates that public institutions do not generate "profit" in the private sense, exposing A's pivot to "avoided rent" as a tax on public services and current students. B also highlights the fatal flaw in A's framing: if the land is truly "stolen property," the legal remedy is full return, making A's 1% revenue share an arbitrary political tax rather than strict restitution. Furthermore, B's distinction between sovereign treaties and private theft, along with the sharp point that existing claims panels are designed for one-time settlements to extinguish claims rather than create perpetual rent, neutralizes A's procedural defenses. Side A's closing relied too heavily on repeating the initial stolen property analogy without adequately addressing B's structural critiques of public title.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it capitalizes on Side A's pressure answers by pointing out that redefining "profit" as "avoided rent" concedes there is no actual surplus to share, and that federal claims bodies were designed for one-time payments to close claims, not to establish perpetual ancestry-based rent.
