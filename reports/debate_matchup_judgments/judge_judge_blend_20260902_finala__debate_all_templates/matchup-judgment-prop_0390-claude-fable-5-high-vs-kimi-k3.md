### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by establishing the sharper marginal tradeoff: portability greatly expands the consequences of false flags while sophisticated offenders can evade the shared identifiers once. A showed a real benefit against ordinary persistent harassers, but weakened its due-process weighing by defending a no-safeguards world while still calling wrongful exile reversible. B repeatedly exposed that unsupported assumption and made the downside comparison easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5-B4.7 was decisive: B held A to its explicit acceptance of portability without safeguards, asked who could reverse an error when no tribunal existed, and showed that hashing identifiers still entails universal screening. This materially undercut A's recurring “reversible error versus compounding harm” frame.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): CON won the core weighing. They dissolved PRO's central contradiction by distinguishing pros who evade easily from innocents who cannot, then forced the marginal analysis: one spoofed kit defeats a shared list everywhere, so portability adds near-zero deterrence over single-platform removal while multiplying error cost to total exile and requiring universal hash-checking. PRO's Yes to defending the no-safeguards world undercut its own "reversible error" promise, leaving no reverser. The consent vs compulsion answer neutralized the surveillance tu quoque.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON Rebuttal 1-2: lock that stops only honest people + one evasion kit defeats every gate simultaneously, so professionals pay once while falsely flagged pay everywhere – turning PRO's toll-at-every-gate into maximal marginal error for minimal marginal gain.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively exploiting the "even if" clause in the motion, forcing Side A to defend cross-platform bans in a world where due-process safeguards never materialize. While Side A argued in the closing that market incentives (the profit motive) would naturally correct false bans, Side B delivered a fatal rebuttal, pointing out the principal-agent problem of a federated system: a company enforcing another company's ban has no profit motive to investigate an appeal and will simply rubber-stamp the flag. Side B also successfully resolved Side A's attempted "contradiction" regarding evasion and maintained a more rigorous structural critique of the surveillance and error costs throughout the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's final rebuttal to Side A's closing argument, dismantling the "profit motive" appeal mechanism by highlighting that companies enforcing another company's flag have every incentive to rubber-stamp and none to investigate.
