### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO's core logic—later hours are a commercial privilege generating concentrated, predictable costs, so user-pays conditions are the only honest bargain—proved resilient across the exchange. PRO also handled the small-venue objection adequately with the pooled/templated plan move, and the deterrence argument (A4.3—a contract doesn't lose force because breach is rare) is standard but valid contract theory that CON only partially rebutted. CON landed the debate's most memorable blow by exploiting PRO's "occasional revocation" concession (B4.1: "a contract rarely enforced is not a contract; it is a press release") and the compliance-measurement challenge (B3.2, B2.5) was genuinely sharp. CON's public-budget-versus-license-paperwork comparison (B4.3) was the strongest original move of the debate. But CON never resolved the free-rider problem in its own model, and the claim that cities have successfully fixed late-night problems through public management alone was asserted rather than demonstrated—mirroring the evidentiary gap CON kept pointing to in PRO. The enforcement-capacity critique weakened PRO's mechanism but didn't defeat the normative core. PRO's case was cleaner and more self-consistent after the full exchange, giving it a clear but competitive win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.1 exploitation of PRO's "occasional revocation" admission—turning PRO's own language into "a contract rarely enforced is not a contract; it is a press release"—was the sharpest single rebuttal move. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A built a more coherent and durable path to victory across all five rounds. Its central structural claim — that the conditional enables more late hours by giving officials cover to say yes, and that without it unmanaged late hours historically produce backlash and blanket bans — survived the exchange largely intact. The historical pattern argument (Berlin, London, Meatpacking, LA) was invoked in both rebuttals and the closing; B never countered it with contrary examples or a causal story explaining why the electoral accountability alternative would break the pattern. A also gave the strongest answer to B's sharpest pressure question: if plans are enforceable permit terms, failure to deliver becomes a regulatory violation, not merely a political disappointment, which gives residents formal standing absent under unconditional hours. Side B landed several real blows — the "cover works both ways" reframe in Rebuttal 1 was the debate's best single counter-move, and the small-venue compliance cost argument (acoustic engineers, 3am sanitation contracts) made a specific causal claim that standardized templates only partially answer. The administrative sabotage point about vague "stronger" language was also genuine; A conceded the risk and offered "clearer rules" as the fix, correctly noting it wasn't a ground for abolishing the condition, though B was right that clearer rules aren't in the motion. Where B ultimately fell short was in its alternative: electoral accountability requires trusting the very councils that A argued routinely fail late-night districts, and B's own Q1 to A ("what prevents a city from approving a paperwork plan while providing no new service?") applied with nearly equal force to B's own mechanism. The "Shield for City Hall" framing in closing was memorable but didn't resolve the asymmetry. A's closing turned B's alternative against itself cleanly: "The 'trust voters' alternative concedes exactly the outcome it says it fears." That crystallization of the live clash, more than any single argument, tips the verdict.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 counter that "cover works both ways" — arguing the same conditional that A claims lets officials say yes more comfortably also lets councils say no without accountability — was the debate's most damaging single move. A never fully closed the asymmetry; A's response established that the mechanism exists without showing it's structurally biased toward approval rather than rejection. However, A's answer to B's Q1 in Rebuttal 2 (approved plans become enforceable permit terms, creating formal standing for residents) was the sharpest answer to the debate's sharpest question and kept A's route to victory live where B's would have collapsed it.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B won by making the motion look like a workable precondition for sustainable late hours rather than a ban. Side A had strong points about vague standards, small-venue burdens, and privatization, but those depended heavily on reading the motion as forcing operators to buy public services. Side B more plausibly argued that cities can require coordinated, enforceable plans without necessarily privatizing transit or sanitation, and that Side A's alternative of electoral accountability is too reactive and risks backlash into blanket bans.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pressure and second rebuttal were decisive: asking what actually compels transit and cleanup without conditions exposed the weakness of Side A's alternative, and Side A's answer of electoral accountability was less persuasive than Side B's precondition-versus-backlash framing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B won a fiercely contested debate by systematically dismantling Side A's alternative of "electoral accountability." Side B persuasively demonstrated that waiting for public pressure over unmanaged nightlife inevitably results in voters demanding blanket bans, not better municipal services, thereby turning Side A's mechanism into a fatal harm for the independent venues Side A wanted to protect.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing argument that Side A's "electoral accountability" chain runs through sleep-deprived residents who will demand closing times, not late-night buses, brilliantly neutralizing Side A's core alternative.
