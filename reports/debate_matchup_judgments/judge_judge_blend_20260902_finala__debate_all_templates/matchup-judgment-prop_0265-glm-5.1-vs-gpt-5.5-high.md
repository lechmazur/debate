### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins on three decisive axes. First, PRO's core framing—"a mandatory charge is a price; hiding it is deception by sequencing"—was never genuinely rebutted; CON's counter-proposal of "early prominent disclosure" sidesteps rather than refutes it. Second, PRO's best single move was weaponizing CON's own opening concession ("consumers are harmed by seeing charges too late, not by seeing components") to argue that mandatory inclusion eliminates that harm completely, while CON's alternative merely relocates the burden of assembly onto the diner. Third, PRO's structural-incentive argument—that disclosure regimes predictably degrade into fine print because the displayed-price incentive is permanent—was not countered with anything more than "inclusive pricing needs enforcement too," which is true but doesn't address the incentive asymmetry. CON's strongest live point (conditional charges force PRO to allow notes anyway, so absolute inclusion is impossible) was answered adequately in PRO's A1: the standard-transaction price includes the charge; conditional variations get the note. PRO partly concedes notes for edge cases, but this limits rather than defeats the core rule. CON's tipping-underinclusiveness argument is valid but peripheral—it attacks the proposition's scope, not its logic, and tips are genuinely voluntary, as PRO correctly insists. Three clipped CON turns represent a real execution cost, disrupting rhetorical flow and occasionally leaving the impression of argument avoided rather than delivered. PRO's closing framing—"CON requires enforcement vigilance; PRO makes honesty the default"—landed cleanly and was not neutralized. CON's closing correctly used PRO's concession about notes but couldn't convert it into a reason to vote against the proposition's core standard.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 opening: turning CON's own concession ("harmed by seeing them too late, not by seeing components") into direct support for inclusion was the sharpest single move in the debate—it reframed CON's distinction as a reason to vote PRO rather than CON, and CON never fully escaped that trap.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B wins by making the core clash decisive: separate disclosure regimes predictably devolve into overlooked fine print (airlines/banks precedent), while inclusion makes the real total the displayed default, shifting burden from customer to business. B directly resolves pressure on conditional charges via standard-inclusive plus notes, and neutralizes tipping fairness by distinguishing voluntary from mandatory. Side A lands solid points on context-specific distortion but leaves its early-notice alternative exposed to the same enforcement and incentive problems B highlights, with less progression in rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2: directly answers Q1/Q2 with standard-price inclusion + notes distinction, then weighs against CON's disclosure enforcement claim to crystallize behavioral change.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): CON successfully showed that the proposition overreaches by conflating "separate" with "hidden" and "checkout ambush." By distinguishing check-level contextual charges from item prices, CON demonstrated that mandatory bundling can distort menus and obscure what diners pay for. PRO's strongest argument—that inclusion is the only way to ensure honesty—was undermined when PRO conceded that conditional surcharges (dine-in vs. takeout, large parties) could still appear as separate notes "where relevant." Once notes are necessary, the clash collapses into whether disclosure is clear before ordering, which is CON's standard. CON also landed a durable internal critique: because tips are not legally mandatory, PRO's rule leaves tipping restaurants displaying lower base prices while burdening transparent no-tipping models, failing its own comparison goal. PRO's historical analogy about disclosure regimes degrading remained plausible but was adequately countered by CON's point that inclusive pricing also requires enforcement. The better-argued path to victory belongs to CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal and closing crystallized that PRO's conditional-charge concession (allowing notes "where relevant") destroyed the absolutist "displayed price equals real price" framework, reducing the debate to CON's preferred standard of pre-order clarity rather than mandatory bundling.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO's core claim — that a mandatory charge is part of the price and the headline number should be what the customer unavoidably pays — was well-grounded and never squarely neutralized. CON's "structural clarity" defense argued that "$20 plus 20%" gives more information than "$24 includes 20%," but PRO answered this directly in B4.1 and B5.3: the menu can simultaneously display the all-in price and the surcharge percentage, so no information is actually lost. CON's response in A4.1 effectively ignored that answer and restated the objection as if PRO had proposed eliminating the percentage. CON's variable-charges argument had some traction, but PRO's rebuttal — that restaurants already manage context-specific pricing and can adjust all-in prices by context — was adequate. The sales tax analogy gave CON a useful frame, but PRO's reply (government-imposed, jurisdiction-standardized, not a restaurant pricing strategy) was sharp and not effectively answered. PRO's pressure questions (especially Q2, forcing CON to say whether universal charges should still be separated) exposed a real evasion: CON declined to concede even the universal case, leaning entirely on variable-charge complexity that doesn't govern the ordinary situation. PRO's answers to CON's pressure questions (A1: the percentage survives under PRO's format; A2: real price belongs in the decision-relevant context) were direct and structurally coherent. CON's closing, though partially clipped, maintained the "structural clarity" argument but didn't resolve why an unusable base price deserves headline status when the breakdown can coexist with an all-in total. The debate was close and CON's structural argument was genuinely interesting, but PRO built the cleaner path: the mandatory total is what the diner pays, and both the total and the rate can be displayed simultaneously.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B4.1 answer to CON's pressure question directly defused CON's central claim. CON insisted that PRO's format eliminates surcharge-rate visibility; PRO answered that a menu reading "$24, includes 20% mandatory service charge" preserves both the unavoidable total and the pricing structure. CON's subsequent A4.1 in Rebuttal 2 largely restated the objection without engaging this direct answer, which left CON's main information-loss argument standing on shaky ground for the rest of the debate.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by building a stronger case for separate disclosure: it preserves total cost via calculation while keeping structural comparisons (base price vs surcharge rate differences across restaurants) visible and prominent, even for universal charges. Side B (PRO) effectively pressed consent and headline totals but failed to neutralize the info-loss point, as all-in formats still obscure rate variations despite notes; B's handling of variables was adequate but A better adapted and weighed throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2: directly answered both pressure questions on % visibility and variables, then weighed that PRO's concession on breakdowns makes their total-dominance rule the weaker format for comparative transparency.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): CON successfully showed that integrating mandatory charges into headline prices obscures independently decision-relevant structural information—base price and surcharge rates—that diners use to compare pricing models across restaurants, and that the harms PRO identified (surprise and comparison distortion) are better solved by mandatory prominent disclosure without bundling. CON's variable-pricing scalability objection and reverse-arithmetic point survived PRO's responses largely intact, while PRO's concession that breakdowns can coexist with all-in pricing conceded too much of CON's information-preservation framework.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal that "$20, 20% service charge applies" gives diners the base, rate, and total after one simple multiplication, while "$24, includes 20%" forces reverse division to recover the base and erases structural differences between restaurants—demonstrating that PRO's format obscures the very transparency it claims to advance.
