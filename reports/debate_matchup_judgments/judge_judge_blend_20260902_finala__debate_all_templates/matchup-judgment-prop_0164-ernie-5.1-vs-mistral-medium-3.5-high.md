### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built a more coherent and consistently executed case across all rounds. Its core detection/delay argument—that flexible alternatives require funders to notice a crisis before acting, and history shows they don't—was never adequately answered by Side B. CON's best response in Rebuttal 2 (targeted RFAs can launch within months of detecting instability) actually concedes the problem: detection must still come first. Side A's reframing of field variation (replication applies not to re-running telescopes but to verifying statistical models and software pipelines) was a genuinely useful answer to CON's strongest line, and the "immunity loophole" point effectively neutralized CON's field-differentiation claim. Side A also reversed the speculative/concrete framing in the closing, arguing convincingly that downstream waste from irreproducible findings is measured in failed trials and retracted papers, while the "lost" novel funding is mostly low-expected-return tail projects. CON had real arguments—field variation, perverse incentives, serendipity—but suffered from a clipped opening (execution cost), a notably shorter and less developed Rebuttal 2, and a closing that largely repeated earlier points without sharpening the comparison. The perverse-incentives argument about gaming was pressed well but PRO's counter (career incentives already push toward meaningful verification; no floor means replication stays optional and gets underfunded) remained largely intact. Side A's sustained pressure on the enforcement mechanism gap proved the decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 answer to B's field-variation pressure question: rather than defending a blanket "redo the telescope," PRO reframed the replication share as funding verification of statistical models, code, and analytical pipelines—a credible burden even in astronomy and math—and added that a universal floor closes the loophole where any discipline can declare itself immune. This directly neutralized CON's strongest recurring argument without overclaiming, and CON never produced a clean counter to the "immunity loophole" point in the remaining rounds.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): B won by making the clearer comparative case that replication is structurally underfunded unless funders create a mandatory floor. A’s strongest point was that a universal quota misallocates funds in fields like mathematics and astronomy, but B partly answered with the broader point about hidden analytical and software fragility and, more importantly, kept the debate focused on enforcement and prevention. A’s targeted-fund alternative never fully solved B’s charge that funders detect crises only after downstream waste has occurred. The opportunity-cost argument was plausible but remained less weighed than B’s insurance-frame defense of reliability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive rebuttal was that targeted replication calls are reactive and lack enforcement, while a fixed share changes incentives before unreliable findings generate costly follow-on work.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by more effectively crystallizing the core trade-off and adapting to live pressure. While Side B raised valid concerns about field variation and incentive gaming, Side A successfully mitigated them by reframing replication to include code, pipeline, and statistical verification in traditionally "stable" fields, and by arguing that flexible alternatives historically fail because they are reactive. Side A's weighing—concrete downstream waste versus the speculative opportunity cost of trimming the low-probability tail of novel grants—was sharper, consistently defended, and directly answered Side B's serendipity framing. Side B's later speeches grew repetitive, relied on romanticized historical accidents without addressing enforcement mechanisms for their proposed alternatives, and did not fully engage with Side A's adaptive reframing. Side A's closing cleanly flipped the cost-benefit comparison and provided the clearer, more actionable voting issue.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 response to the astronomy/mathematics objection. By arguing that irreproducibility is not binary and that a fixed share would fund verification of statistical models and software pipelines rather than redundant observations, Side A directly neutralized Side B's strongest field-variation argument while preserving the universal mandate's logic and demonstrating strong adaptive weighing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): CON wins because it successfully executed the more analytically precise distinction throughout the debate: clinical trials embed replication because their methodology specifically demands it—it is targeted and context-appropriate—while PRO's proposal is a blanket fixed percentage imposed on every grant regardless of field or merit. PRO repeatedly invoked clinical trials as proof that structural embedding works, but CON turned this into a liability rather than an asset by consistently pointing out that targeted solutions and blanket mandates are not the same thing. PRO never adequately answered this distinction. PRO's closing declared the clinical trials example "settles the debate," but it does not—it actually cuts against a one-size-fits-all fixed share unless PRO can explain why the mechanism is equivalent, which they never did. CON's Q2 pressure question—asking PRO to account for concrete innovation losses alongside speculative savings—also landed meaningfully, and CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO's Q2 (honestly conceding that targeted incentives have not been implemented at scale, then pivoting to argue this doesn't validate a rigid quota) was the most candid and structurally honest moment in the debate. PRO's Q2 ("why hasn't psychology's crisis been solved if incentives work?") was strong and put real pressure on CON, but CON absorbed it by distinguishing between the current zero-resource status quo and a genuine targeted alternative—framing the choice as between PRO's blunt instrument and a better unimplemented mechanism, rather than PRO's mechanism versus nothing. CON's main weakness—never fully specifying what targeted incentives look like—costs it some points but does not override PRO's failure to resolve the clinical trials distinction. Both sides were competent and well-structured; the margin reflects a consistent analytical edge for CON on the mechanism question rather than a dominant performance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Turn 4 rebuttal distinguishing clinical trials as targeted, methodology-driven replication from PRO's proposed blanket fixed share. PRO repeatedly cited clinical trials as proof their model works; CON turned this into a contradiction by demonstrating that clinical trials succeed precisely because replication is context-specific, not because a fixed percentage was mandated. PRO never supplied a satisfying answer to this distinction across three subsequent speeches, leaving it as CON's most durable live argument.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): B won because it gave the clearer structural reason for a fixed reserved share: replication is systematically underprovided under novelty-driven incentives, and predictable funding changes that equilibrium. A landed a good distinction between targeted replication and blanket quotas, especially on clinical trials, but overstated the innovation tradeoff by assuming a modest fixed share would have starved breakthroughs like mRNA without proving that causal link. A also offered targeted incentives as an alternative, but B pressed that such flexible approaches have not solved the underfunding problem and that peer review can still screen replication quality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was answering A's opportunity-cost and targeted-incentives case by arguing that unfunded replication remains career-risky and ad hoc, while a reserved share makes verification predictable and meritorious rather than optional.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side B wins by successfully navigating the mechanism clash. Side A attempted to trap Side B using the clinical trials analogy, but Side B cleanly distinguished methodology-embedded verification from a rigid, cross-field budget levy. Side B consistently weighed concrete opportunity costs (starving speculative, high-impact novelty) against Side A's broader waste-savings claims, and articulated why targeted incentives outperform a blunt fixed quota. Side A's later rounds grew repetitive, leaning heavily on the clinical trials comparison without adequately justifying why a mandatory fixed share is superior to flexible, field-specific alternatives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, which directly answered the clinical trials pressure by separating field-specific design from blanket budget mandates, and sharply exposed Side A's conflation of "replication is valuable" with "a fixed quota is the optimal mechanism."
