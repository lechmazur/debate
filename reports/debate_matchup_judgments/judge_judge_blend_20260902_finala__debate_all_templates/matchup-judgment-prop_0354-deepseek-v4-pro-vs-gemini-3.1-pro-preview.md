### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A built a coherent, well-grounded core: defaults govern behavior through inertia, reversing the default harnesses that inertia for protection, and the system works by throttle-capping rather than braking so sensor errors produce mild inconvenience rather than acute hazard. That argument was never squarely neutralized. Side B's strongest move was identifying an apparent contradiction between A's "instinctive floor-it" framing in the pressure questions and the "soft, progressive detent" framing in Rebuttal 2. It landed real pressure and Side A never explicitly reconciled the two pictures. However, the underlying positions are actually compatible (different pressure = different override intensity), and Side B's exploitation of it didn't topple the behavioral-inertia foundation. Side B's own paths to victory had real weaknesses: the "only need worst speeders to opt out" reply to A's inertia argument is plausible, but it concedes that the vast majority of ordinary casual speeders remain covered by the default—exactly what A needed. The ABS distinction (assists vs. blocks intended action) was clever but Side A had already established that throttle-capping is qualitatively different from braking, so the analogy worked mainly against a system A never defended. Side B never produced a clear comparative argument for why optional ISA produces better net safety outcomes than default-on ISA; the case mostly argued against default-on without defending the opt-in alternative on its own terms. Side A's execution costs (clipped opening and closing) are real but the core case came through intact. On balance, A's argument that defaults are the decisive variable, paired with the proportionality point (thousands of deaths vs. occasional brief throttle cap), remained the live route to victory after the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 contradiction identification [B4.3-B4.4] was the sharpest single move: pointing out that A characterized the override as both an "instinctive floor-it" motion (pressure question) and a "soft progressive detent requiring just a bit more pressure" (Rebuttal 2 answer), then arguing that neither version saves the system—easy bypass means worst offenders ignore it, heavy kick-down means dangerous hesitations. Side A's closing failed to explicitly reconcile these pictures, leaving the contradiction partially live and making it the most decisive rebuttal exchange of the debate.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B won because their Rebuttal 2 exposed an inconsistency in Side A's override description (instinctive floor vs. progressive detent) that directly undermined claims of seamlessness and no delay, while keeping pressure on real maneuver hazards and high-risk driver opt-out; Side A had a solid behavioral-default foundation but failed to neutralize these live lines despite pressure questions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 highlighting the shift in Side A's accelerator override account (from [A3.2] instinctive flooring to [A4.1] soft progressive pressure) and why that dooms the safety case.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): B wins because it successfully undermined A's technical defense of the override mechanism by highlighting A's contradictory descriptions, and because B's argument that high-risk speeders would selectively disable the system neutralized A's default-inertia advantage. A's repeated word-limit clips and failure to cleanly resolve the override mechanics left B's hazard-and-frustration framing as the live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal catching A's contradiction between describing the ISA override as an instinctive floor-pedal motion and as a soft progressive detent, which undermined A's claim that the system is both unobtrusive and effective.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A built a cleaner, more progressive case across the exchange. Its core argument—default ISA flips the behavioral baseline so that compliance is the path of least resistance for the majority while forcing reckless drivers to make a conscious, effortful choice to speed—remained largely intact after the full exchange. The ABS-pulsing analogy to explain why kickdown resistance becomes predictable rather than distracting was the strongest single rebuttal move, directly neutralizing CON's "cognitive surprise" objection. The seatbelt analogy anchored the weighing cleanly: policy is not designed around the bypass fringe. Side A also partially neutralized CON's strongest attack (aftermarket hacks disabling the full safety suite) by noting that ISA and AEB are not architecturally compelled to be linked, then falling back on a "design for 99%, not 1%" frame that CON never squarely answered. Side B's cascade-failure argument—that default ISA erodes trust, prompts aftermarket flashing, and strips AEB from vehicles—was genuinely compelling and the best threat to PRO's case, but it relied on a chain of inferences (trust erosion → permanent disabling → full-suite loss → net harm exceeds benefit) that Side A interrupted at multiple links. Critically, Side B was clipped four times for exceeding word limits, cutting off conclusions at decisive moments (most damagingly in the closing). These are real execution costs: arguments that are formally cut before the judge see the landing are arguments that did not land. Side A's case was shorter but complete; Side B's was sometimes stronger per sentence but repeatedly truncated. On balance, Side A controlled the comparison better and left fewer live burdens unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 response to the aftermarket-hacking cascade (A2): arguing that automotive architecture does not inherently link ISA disabling to AEB, invoking the 99%/1% policy design principle, and deploying the seatbelt-bypass analogy. This move interrupted CON's strongest chain of inference at two links simultaneously and reframed the minority-behavior objection as a reason to preserve, not abandon, a strong default—a reframing CON's clipped closing never fully answered.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by establishing a clearer causal path from default ISA to reduced habitual speeding via behavioral defaults and crash physics, then neutralizing B's edge-case rebuttals on overrides and hacking as policy-irrelevant extremes rather than systemic threats. B raised plausible trust erosion but could not convert it into a decisive net-safety reversal without over-relying on speculative cascade failures that A directly weighed against status-quo speeding deaths.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A1/A2 answers in Rebuttal 2 cleanly reframed B's pressure questions on minority hacking and pedal surprise as non-dispositive for the majority baseline.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins because it built a more complete and disciplined case leveraging the behavioral power of defaults, the physics of kinetic energy, and the seatbelt analogy to show that optional systems fail the drivers who need them most. Side B’s trust-erosion narrative was plausible but speculative and repeatedly undermined by clipped speeches that left key burdens unresolved. A’s consistent format adherence and sharper answers to pressure questions gave it the clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over whether default ISA triggers aftermarket hacks that disable entire safety suites (A4.1–A4.2 vs. B4.1–B4.4), where A exposed B’s causal chain as an unquantified minority behavior that should not override systemic safety gains, while B struggled to prove the backfire effect outweighed the default’s benefits.
