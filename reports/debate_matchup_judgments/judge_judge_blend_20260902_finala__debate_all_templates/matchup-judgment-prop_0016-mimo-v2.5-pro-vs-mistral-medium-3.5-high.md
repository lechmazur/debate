### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): CON (Side B) wins on the back of two arguments that PRO never adequately neutralized. First, the national/state versus city-tools distinction: PRO's flagship evidence (Finland's nationwide Housing First, Utah's state-level program) required federal or state action that city governments cannot replicate alone. PRO's counter-examples (Project Roomkey, tiny home villages) were plausibly deflated in CON's Rebuttal 2 as emergency-scale or insufficient against citywide need—and PRO never rebuilt the evidential foundation. Second, CON's acute-psychotic-crisis question was a genuine pressure point. PRO's answer simply restated that housing enables engagement, which does not address how a city intervenes for someone actively in crisis who refuses voluntary contact. On the other side, PRO's strongest move—enforcement destroys IDs, medications, and trust—was blunted by CON's "bad enforcement, not all enforcement" reframe pointing to LEAD-style diversion courts and co-responder teams. The cost-efficiency argument from PRO went largely unchallenged but was not by itself sufficient to win the governance-level question. PRO's closing was repetitive and added little new weighing; CON's closing, though clipped (a real execution cost), correctly zeroed in on the word "primarily" and the city-tools argument that remained live throughout. On balance CON had more undefeated routes to victory after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 [B2.2–B2.4]: CON turned PRO's own concession that San Francisco, Portland, and Seattle failed despite massive service spending into proof that cities cannot scale housing fast enough to matter on the timeline of public-order crises, then compounded it by showing PRO's positive Finland/Utah examples required national or state action beyond city control. This double move—weaponizing PRO's admission while deflating PRO's evidence—left PRO's primary evidentiary base materially damaged for the remainder of the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A successfully narrowed the debate by focusing on the specific constraints of city governments and the immediate timelines of public order crises. By contextualizing Side B's housing examples as reliant on state/federal resources or emergency pandemic funding, Side A proved that cities cannot primarily rely on tools they do not fully control. Side B leaned heavily on the truism that housing ends homelessness, which Side A conceded as a long-term destination while winning the debate on the immediate governance framework required to get there.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 effectively dismantled Side B's interim housing examples by pointing out Project Roomkey's reliance on expired federal pandemic funding and noting that even rapid deployment leaves a time gap requiring immediate enforcement.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side B wins by successfully grounding the debate in the specific actor and framing of the motion: cities and "primarily." B's jurisdictional argument—that cities lack direct control over the funding, vouchers, and zoning required to scale housing, but do control police, sanitation, and courts—created a structural burden A never fully cleared. A's reliance on Finland and Utah played directly into B's hands, as those are national/state programs. When A pivoted to city-level interim housing (Project Roomkey, tiny homes), B effectively mitigated them as emergency exceptions or insufficient scale, preserving the timeline clash. B also cleanly deflected A's attacks on sweeps by distinguishing bad enforcement from the structured frameworks B actually advocated (diversion courts, LEAD, co-responders), and successfully pressed A on the voluntary-only gap for individuals in acute psychotic crisis. A's case was morally coherent and well-delivered, but functionally answered a "what solves homelessness" question rather than the "how should cities primarily govern it" question on the ballot. B's late speeches were clipped, incurring an execution cost, but the substantive advantage on jurisdiction, timeline, and the mechanism-for-engagement clash was clear enough to decide the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 sharply turned A's San Francisco/Portland/Seattle examples: if the wealthiest, most progressive cities couldn't scale housing fast enough despite massive spending, that proves cities cannot realistically treat housing as the primary frame on a relevant timeline. This pivot forced A onto the defensive for the rest of the debate and anchored B's jurisdictional win condition.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won on the decisive comparative clash. Its core causal story — homelessness is a housing scarcity problem, so policing only relocates rather than resolves it — held throughout the debate and was never squarely neutralized. The Houston example survived Side B's "exceptional resources" objection after Side A correctly reframed it as a question of political prioritization rather than unique institutional capacity. More damaging, Side A's Q1 pressure question ("what does success look like under your framework?") exposed the fundamental incoherence of policing-primary: Side B answered with "restored public order" and crime reduction, but those metrics describe displacement of suffering rather than reduction in homelessness, which is what the proposition asks about. Side B's strongest argument — that the hardest cases (severe mental illness, addiction) refuse voluntary housing — was competently answered by Side A, which explained that assertive outreach, conservatorship, and crisis stabilization operate under a housing-and-services umbrella and that police lack the mandate or capacity to connect anyone to psychiatric treatment. Side B's Seattle counter-example was plausibly rebutted (attribution error: Seattle's failures stemmed from fragmented spending on shelters and sweeps, not Housing First methodology). Side B's "permanent dependent class" argument was exposed as weaker than the dependency created by jail-and-ER cycling. The "policing as foundation for housing to work" argument arrived late and was underdeveloped. Side B's case was coherent and identified a genuine limitation of voluntary models, but it never completed the causal chain from policing to actually reducing homelessness. Side A's closing crystallized this cleanly: policing aims at visibility, not at the problem. Both sides met the A1/A2 format requirement. Side A had two clipped speeches, a real execution cost, but what remained was stronger argument-for-argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal of the Seattle example was the most decisive single exchange. Side B introduced Seattle's billion-dollar failure as disqualifying Housing First, but Side A pointed out that Seattle's spend went largely to shelter beds, sweeps, and fragmented programs rather than permanent placement with services — attributing the failure to the policing-and-shelter mix, not Housing First. The analogy ("blaming antibiotics because someone took vitamins instead") made the comparative point memorable and shifted the burden back onto Side B to defend its own preferred model's track record.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side B won by successfully proving that housing and services are more effective at solving the root cause of homelessness, while dismantling Side A's claim that only police can handle severe mental health cases by showing how medical outreach teams under a services umbrella can better initiate conservatorships.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 explanation that involuntary holds and mental health interventions are better handled by street medicine teams and crisis units than by police officers processing arrests.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by decisively dismantling Side B's core mechanism: the claim that policing effectively manages the "hardest cases" and sustainably restores order. A sharply demonstrated that arrests and sweeps function as an expensive displacement treadmill that cycles individuals back to the street without compelling lasting treatment, directly undermining B's primary justification. A also effectively neutralized B's attacks on coordination and refusal by framing coordination as a replicable political choice and incorporating crisis stabilization and conservatorship into the service-access framework, showing that a housing-primary approach can still address severe resistance. While B articulated a clear case for immediate public order, defined concrete success metrics, and answered pressure questions directly, B could not overcome the empirical reality of the enforcement cycle or A's comparative weighing that policing fails even on its own stated terms. A's rhetoric was sharper, the clash was narrowed effectively, and the path to victory was cleaner despite minor clipping costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing sequence, where A directly answered B's pressure questions while turning the coordination argument, then decisively exposed that B's policing framework lacks a real mechanism for lasting psychiatric or addiction treatment. A's crystallization that policing merely "clears a block for a news cycle" and fails even by B's own standards materially damaged CON's live route to victory.
