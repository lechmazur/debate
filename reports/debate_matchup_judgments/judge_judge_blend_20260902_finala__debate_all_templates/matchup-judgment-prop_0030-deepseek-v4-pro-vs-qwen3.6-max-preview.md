### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): PRO built a cleaner, more grounded case across the full exchange. The three-channel framework (trade, investment, labour) gave concrete, present-tense damage that CON never squarely neutralised. PRO's decisive move in Rebuttal 2 A1 — "Germany and France still enjoy full single-market access; Britain replaced frictionless commerce with its largest trading partner with customs checks, rules of origin, and regulatory divergence" — directly deflated CON's central global-headwinds defence. That argument identified a UK-specific drag that sits on top of shared shocks, and CON had no answer. CON's counter-case rested almost entirely on future-tense promises: fintech and biotech fast-tracking, Indo-Pacific services expansion, productivity upgrades from labour rebalancing. After eight years, none of these benefits were receipted in any concrete way. PRO's pressure question — "why hasn't the predicted non-EU export surge materialised after eight years?" — remained live through the closing, and CON's answer ("it takes time") is an unconvincing concession when the time already elapsed is substantial. CON's best move — the "category error" reframe (don't measure strength by customs ledger) — was rhetorically neat but substantively evasive: it asked the judge to ignore all measurable present costs while crediting speculative future gains. PRO's closing line, "CON asks judges to bet on an agility dividend that never lands; PRO asks them to read the bill," crystallised this asymmetry cleanly. The real execution cost for PRO was three clipped speeches, which leaves gaps in the visible argument and signals poor word-count discipline. This prevented a larger margin. CON was consistent in format and never clipped, but its arguments recycled the same EU-stagnation and global-headwinds points across multiple rounds without meaningful development or direct engagement with PRO's receipts demand.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive exchange. CON's repeated "Germany and France face identical slumps" argument was CON's primary shield throughout the debate. PRO punctured it cleanly: the mechanism is not that EU membership erases global headwinds, but that Brexit adds a distinct, self-inflicted layer of UK-specific damage — new customs barriers, supply-chain relocation, labour shortages — on top of shared shocks that EU members still avoid. Germany weathers the same storm with single-market access intact; Britain weathers it with one hand tied behind its back. CON never adequately answered this distinction, which left the "common shock" defence hollow for the rest of the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side A won by relentlessly tying the debate to the specific temporal constraint of the prompt: 'stronger today'.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's pressure question about global headwinds was devastating, correctly pointing out that Brexit layered self-inflicted damage on top of inevitable global shocks, cleanly neutralizing Side B's main defensive parity argument.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A held the decisive ground throughout: the core comparison between frictionless access to roughly 40% of British trade and marginal Pacific deals was never adequately neutralized. CON's most interesting move—reframing Brexit as liberation from a stagnating EU—fails to address the counterfactual cleanly, because remaining in a slow-growth bloc while retaining access to it is still a different and plausibly better baseline than leaving it and building distant substitutes from scratch. CON's fintech/digital-services answer to A's sharp Q1 was the best individual moment for CON, but the claim that it offset lost goods and services volume is asserted rather than demonstrated, and A's response—that proximity and scale drive commerce—is more grounded. CON's answer to Q2 ("transitory uncertainty shock") does not adequately explain why the investment stagnation preceded the pandemic by years or address A's compounding-floor argument in closing. CON also suffered repeated execution costs: the opening, first rebuttal, and closing were all clipped, which meant full arguments went unseen. PRO's claim that self-imposed friction compounds on top of shared global shocks—while EU peers absorbed identical headwinds without erecting new trade barriers—was the decisive clash and was never squarely answered. A's closings crystallized the comparison cleanly, while CON's late-stage EU-stagnation framing, though rhetorically interesting, contained increasingly brittle specificity (investment "tracked peers closely," "record foreign investment") without strong grounding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Q1 (Germany/France recession): A drew a sharp causal distinction between external shocks hitting EU members and the additional self-imposed layer of trade friction and labor market tightening unique to the UK. This directly defused CON's strongest reframe—that all advanced economies face similar strain—by showing that shared headwinds and voluntary structural drag compound differently, and CON never fully answered the compounding-floor argument thereafter.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by effectively conceding that the EU faces global macroeconomic shocks, but framing Brexit as a self-imposed friction layered on top of those shared headwinds. Side A's argument that lost investment compounds over time definitively neutralized Side B's defense that capital spending eventually recovered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's R2/Closing response on compounding capital and baseline integration cleanly defeated Side B's reliance on Germany's recession and later UK investment tracking.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B built a stronger case by maintaining focus on direct frictions from lost EU access, investment timing tied to the referendum, and baseline integration effects, with clean answers to pressure questions that exposed shared shocks plus added self-costs. Side A offered useful reframes around regulatory agility and EU weaknesses but did not credibly demonstrate today-level volume replacement or full investment recovery against the added barriers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answers on investment divergence and single-market baseline neutralized A's global-shocks pivot while sharpening the scale comparison.
