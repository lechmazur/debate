### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-rebutted cases, but A's central mechanism—that identification in the DNA/search-engine era collapses the "paper vs. reunion" distinction B relied on since the opening—was never fully neutralized. A's irreversibility framing (a name once state-certified cannot be un-published, whereas an adoptee's ignorance can be mitigated through registries, counseling, and updated medical history) gave the debate a clean asymmetry that B mostly answered by reasserting the equality/consent principle rather than directly refuting the harm comparison. B's pressure questions exposed a real tension in A's "sealed for the child" rationale, but A recovered adequately by shifting to the birth-parent reliance interest, and B's own compromise-critique ("sealed records with a waiting room") was clever but did not overcome A's core claim that guaranteed, state-certified exposure is categorically worse than a status quo where identity is merely unknown. A's closing crystallized this asymmetry ("a wound that can be eased" vs. "a breach that can never be repaired") more sharply than B's closing reasserted its veto-framing, giving A the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's repeated "paper is the person" turn (A2.5, A4.3), which undercut B's foundational distinction between accessing identifying information and enabling contact/harassment, was the most damaging exchange; B's answer (stalking is already illegal, DNA already leaks) mitigated but did not fully resolve the asymmetry A raised about state-guaranteed, irreversible exposure.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by framing parentage as part of the adoptee’s own civil identity, separating access from any right to contact, and showing that consent-based alternatives preserve a permanent unilateral veto. Side A made the strongest case through reliance and irreversible exposure, but repeatedly treated private disclosure as though it automatically caused public outing and relied on speculative placement harms. B’s autonomy and equality case therefore remained more intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s answer that intermediaries merely preserve the original veto was decisive. It directly undermined Side A’s proposed compromise and clarified why ordinary protections against unwanted contact do not require permanent government-enforced ignorance.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully translating the abstract right to a "name" into the concrete digital-age reality of exposure, and by providing a clear, decisive weighing mechanism in the closing (mitigable ignorance vs. irreversible breach). Side A's philosophical case was strong, but it faltered on practical impacts, particularly when Side A framed the birth mother's privacy as "deception," allowing Side B to deliver a sharp rebuttal about the right to privacy without forced confessions of past trauma. Side B's impact calculus and grasp of the practical stakes ultimately provided the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to Side A's "deception" pressure question, distinguishing privacy from a forced confession of past trauma, followed by B's decisive closing weigh between mitigable ignorance and irreversible exposure.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-signposted cases, but A controlled the decisive clash. B's central weapon was an asymmetry argument (disclosure harms are fenceable; ignorance harms are not), but A directly reversed it late in the debate ("The harm was never the knock. The harm was being found... Unmasking cannot be undone"), showing exposure is equally irreversible. Crucially, in the Pressure Questions exchange, A forced B into a costly admission (A1: "Yes — the name is still released even over objection"), which validated A's opening framing that the right is "a warrant against someone else's" identity rather than mere self-knowledge. A exploited this concession repeatedly and effectively in the following rebuttal and closing. A also neutralized B's strongest practical pillar—medical necessity—by proposing an anonymous agency-forwarding channel plus the adoptee's own DNA testing, leaving B's "identity hunger" argument standing largely on emotional appeal rather than a hard practical need. B's closing restated the asymmetry theme without directly countering A's "being found" reversal, since A had the final speech and used it to drive home the concession-based critique. B's case remained emotionally resonant and rhetorically vivid throughout, but structurally it never recovered from admitting that the right operates over explicit parental objection, which is precisely what A's whole case turned on.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's exploitation of B's own pressure-question answer ("A1: Yes — the name is still released even over objection") combined with A's closing line "The harm was never the knock. The harm was being found" — this pairing directly undercut B's asymmetry-of-remedy argument and reframed the entire debate in A's favor at the point where B had no further speech to respond.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by isolating the irreversible harm the proposal itself creates: state-compelled identification over an objecting birth parent's wishes. Side B's contact preferences and harassment remedies addressed later contact, not the loss of anonymity, while Side A offered narrower routes for medical information. Side B made a strong autonomy case but never fully justified why informational interest must override another adult's reliance and privacy interests.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest response was that contact safeguards come too late: once identifying information reaches the adoptee, no preference form, blocked message, or changed address can restore anonymity. This directly weakened Side B's central claim that disclosure harms are meaningfully reversible while secrecy harms are not.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively neutralizing Side A's core weighing mechanism. Side A argued that the harms of disclosure could be fenced by no-contact orders, whereas the harms of sealed records (ignorance) could not. Side B effectively countered that the primary harm to birth parents is not unwanted contact, but the irreversible loss of anonymity (being found by spouses or later children), which no-contact orders cannot fix. Side B also capitalized well on Side A's concession during pressure questions that identifying information would be released over objection, solidifying the breach of the confidentiality promise.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response in Rebuttal 2 and Closing to Side A's asymmetry of harm argument. By distinguishing between unwanted contact (the knock) and the loss of anonymity (being found), Side B proved that a released name cannot be unreleased and that no-contact orders do not restore privacy, dismantling Side A's claim that disclosure harms are easily mitigated.
