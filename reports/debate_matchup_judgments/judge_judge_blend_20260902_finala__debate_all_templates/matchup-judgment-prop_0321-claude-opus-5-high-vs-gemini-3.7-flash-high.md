### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): A narrowly won by establishing a consequential asymmetry: dominant hosts can cheaply compel bundled consent, while independent developers lacking an upload relationship face costly rights clearance. B offered a strong autonomy case and aggregation answer, but never fully resolved that distinction, and its closing repeated a contradiction that A had already answered. B also did not sufficiently prove that a waivable default would produce meaningful creator leverage rather than mandatory checkboxes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.2 was decisive: it directly answered B's claimed contradiction by distinguishing hosts that control upload choke points from decentralized developers that must clear dispersed public works. That preserved A's monopoly-moat argument against B's strongest attack.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side A(CON) wins by exposing a structural asymmetry that undermines the entire PRO case. CON showed that a stronger opt-in default would be cheap for dominant platforms to satisfy through upload chokepoints—making the right illusory for creators—while remaining prohibitively expensive for decentralized open-source developers and independent researchers who lack contractual relationships with millions of creators. This turned PRO into a policy that entrenches monopoly rather than empowering individuals. CON also successfully defused PRO’s central dilemma (consent cannot be both cheap and expensive) by proving the cost depends on who controls the distribution channel, not the legal rule itself, and undermined PRO’s licensing-deal evidence by distinguishing payments for curated pipelines and legal indemnification from abstract permission to learn.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Turn 8 answer to PRO’s pressure-question dilemma (A4.2–A4.3), where CON demonstrated there is no contradiction between bundled consent being effortless for platforms and impossible for open-source developers because the asymmetry is driven by control of the upload chokepoint, not the default rule itself.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO kept the decision on who bears the cost of asking and made it stick. The invoice point [A2.1] undercut CON's "mere observation" claim, and the cheap-vs-expensive contradiction pressed in PQ [A3] was never fully resolved—CON's host-vs-crawler distinction [B4.2] explains the asymmetry but concedes PRO's core that platforms already coerce consent for free [B4.4], which is the status quo PRO indicts. ### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by showing that a waivable default does not create meaningful bargaining power when creators cannot leave dominant platforms. Its click-through and incumbent-moat analysis directly exploited Side B’s own account of creator dependence. Side B persuasively established that output remedies miss diffuse market displacement, but it largely asserted rather than demonstrated that a default right would produce collective licensing, compensation, or hosting without forced consent. Side A sometimes overstated the rule’s reach and incurred a clipping penalty, yet still offered the cleaner comparative account of who would actually benefit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange was its answer that bargaining power requires an outside option: if the illustrator must accept Instagram’s terms to retain an audience, affirmative consent becomes a checkbox rather than leverage. Side B’s appeal to third-party protection and ASCAP-like licensing did not establish that the creator could refuse the host platform or obtain meaningful payment.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON showed the default right would become consent theater absorbed by platform terms, fail to reach foreign or licensed models, and functionally benefit incumbent catalogue holders over freelancers. PRO's extraction narrative had moral force but could not overcome CON's structural analysis that output remedies reach the actual harm while the input right misses it. CON's crystallizing frame—"move the remedy, not the checkbox"—cleanly resolved the comparative dispute.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's dismantling of PRO's collective-licensing defense by showing ASCAP-style pools pay by market share (enriching catalogue holders), paired with exposing PRO's fair-use concession on style which collapsed the promised protection for illustrators.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): B wins on structure and inconsistency. CON showed a default opt-in is contracted around by platforms with click-through boxes, becoming consent theater that burdens open-source/academic alternatives while entrenching Big Tech and catalogue owners like Disney/Getty — the opposite of PRO's goal. PRO's reply that the right enables ASCAP-style licensing was undercut by B's market-share payout point, and PRO's promise to protect style collapsed when A conceded remixers keep fair use. CON's output-focused alternative, while imperfect on style, at least binds offshore and licensed models and directly targets the impersonation harm PRO identified.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1 / B4.3 - the outside-option critique plus who-can-obtain-permission-at-scale: exposing that if creators can't leave platforms, a checkbox gives no leverage, and the permission requirement becomes a moat for those who already own the ToS box and the catalogues.
