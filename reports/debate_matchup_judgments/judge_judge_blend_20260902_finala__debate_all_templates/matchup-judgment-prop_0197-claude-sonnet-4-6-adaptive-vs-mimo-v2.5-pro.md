### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by making the treaty look unable to deliver A's central promise. A established the debris harm well and had a strong prevention-versus-reaction frame, but B's later rebuttals exposed a damaging tension: if low-altitude kinetic tests are allowed because they avoid long-lived debris, the treaty does not degrade ASAT credibility; if they are banned, A's verification and dual-use answers become much harder. B also answered A's compliance challenge with the existing Liability Convention and then effectively attacked A's Security Council enforcement mechanism through the P5 veto problem. A remained persuasive on the severity and irreversibility of debris, but too much of the treaty mechanism was left vulnerable, and A's repeated clipping plus the closing's claim that B had not answered compliance symmetry hurt execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's decisive exchange was the low-altitude testing point: A framed non-persistent-debris tests as acceptable because they avoid the harm, and B showed that this undercuts A's claim that the treaty degrades operational ASAT capability.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): CON wins by exposing two fatal internal contradictions in PRO (verification loophole allowing tested weapons; UNSC enforcement vetoed by the states PRO needs to constrain) and by anchoring alternatives in the already-ratified Liability Convention that avoids fresh consent. PRO's norm-building and upstream-prevention claims are left damaged after the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON Rebuttal 2 (A2) directly neutralized PRO's verification fix and UNSC enforcement claim in one move, leaving PRO's core credibility-degradation argument internally inconsistent.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON)): CON won by exposing that the proposed treaty fails on the axes that matter most for the proposition. CON demonstrated that liability frameworks already bind the major problem states through the existing Liability Convention, directly reversing PRO's compliance pressure and showing the treaty requires fresh consent from actors unlikely to give it. CON also collapsed PRO's enforcement mechanism by noting that P5 veto power makes UN Security Council referral against those same states architecturally impossible. Finally, CON exposed a live tension in PRO's case: the verification logic used to distinguish missile defense from ASAT tests creates a material loophole for low-altitude intercepts, undermining the claim that the treaty degrades operational credibility. PRO's closing mistakenly claimed CON never answered the liability compliance challenge, when CON had already done so decisively, and PRO's word-limit clipping across multiple speeches left key burdens unresolved. PRO's prevention framing retained intuitive force, but CON showed the specific instrument cannot deliver the prevention it promises.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO's first pressure question (Turn 8, B4.1): demonstrating that the 1972 Liability Convention is already ratified by China, Russia, and the United States, so liability frameworks impose costs through existing law without requiring new consent from reluctant actors—a direct reversal of PRO's compliance objection.
