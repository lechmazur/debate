- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B built the clearer path to the proposition: platforms materially generate, host, and profit from the output, are better positioned to prevent infringement, and can receive a diligence-based safe harbor rather than face strict liability. Side A's pencil-and-desk response understated the platform's active generation, hosting, and billing role, while B's burden-shifting safe harbor answered the strict-liability dilemma.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): A won a competitive debate by keeping the motion live as fault-sensitive shared risk with a real burden-shift reform, not empty current-law restatement or strict liability. A’s joint-production and least-cost-avoider path survived better than B’s pure-tool frame: once B’s illustrator analogy was flipped, B never cleanly explained why the party that synthesizes, hosts, and bills should walk away while a human renderer does not. A answered pressure with clear A1/A2 structure and turned residual-risk allocation into a crisp vote (incentive vs shrug). B’s best weapon was the overblocking asymmetry and “public pays in never-made works,” which stayed live and forced A to concede diligent platforms don’t pay; but B paid execution costs from two clipped turns, weaker direct answers on what non-immunity liability looks like, and less decisive crystallization. Clear but competitive edge to A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s illustrator-pressure exchange (A3.1 → A4.5): renderer liability undercuts B’s neutral-tool / user-only targeting story and forces residual risk onto the platform that actually fixes the copy.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by decisively capturing the core analogy clash. ### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by isolating the proposition's marginal effect. Existing fault-based doctrines already cover inducement, knowing contribution, and supervised profit, while Side B ultimately admitted that even a licensed, filtering, notice-honoring platform should remain liable. Side A persuasively framed that addition as strict liability with discounted damages, then connected it to litigation exposure, overblocking, and incumbent advantage. Side B made a strong control, profit, and remedy case, but never supplied a convincing limiting principle for compliant platforms, and its two clipped turns slightly weakened execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's B4.1 admission was decisive: a fully compliant platform would still pay. Side A immediately used that answer to distinguish liability for bad actors, already available under existing doctrine, from the motion's added liability for careful platforms, defeating Side B's claim that the dispute concerned only the trigger.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): B won by forcing and then exploiting A's admission that even a licensing/filtering/notice-honoring platform still pays disgorgement, showing the motion's only real addition is default liability on careful actors that drives over-refusal, while existing fault doctrines already reach bad actors. A landed strong remedy and participant/copy-shop points and exposed notice-whack-a-mole problems, but never cleanly resolved the redundant-vs-catastrophic contradiction and suffered two clipped turns that blunted progression.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's A1/A2 answers and closing that convert A's "compliant platform still pays" into proof the motion adds only strict-ish liability on the careful
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully distinguishing AI platforms from passive toolmakers through the "who supplies the expression" principle, noting that AI models have the copyrighted corpus baked into their weights, unlike Photoshop or Xerox. Side A also effectively dismantled Side B's notice-and-takedown remedy as an unworkable "whack-a-mole" for ephemeral, on-demand generation. While Side B ran a highly disciplined, legally grounded case and effectively forced Side A to defend liability for compliant platforms, Side A's willingness to bite that bullet (via disgorgement for innocent infringement) and its superior rhetorical framing in the closing ("the platform is the car, the driver's hands, and the cashier") secured a narrow victory. Side A's minor word-limit clipping was a real execution cost but did not fatally undermine its core analytical moves.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's distinction between desktop Photoshop (where the user supplies all pixels) and generative AI (where the platform supplies the protected expression via its training corpus), which effectively neutralized Side B's Sony Betamax and cloud software analogies.
