### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): PRO built a coherent moral case for reciprocity, grounded in concrete relocation examples (Oakland, St. Louis, San Diego, Seattle), and pressed a useful distinction between the original subsidy vote and consent at the exit. But CON's opening laid two structural traps—(1) owners simply won't build under this rule, so cities either get nothing or lose the bidding war to non-adopting rivals, and (2) a "binding" vote that can't physically stop a franchise from leaving just gives owners a bigger stage to extract concessions via public campaigns rather than quiet negotiation. PRO's response to trap one ("fine, then don't take the money... that is the market pricing the deal honestly") was a costly concession that CON immediately weaponized, showing it amounts to the city getting no team while the same relocation cycle continues elsewhere. PRO never solved the collective-action/bidding-war problem beyond asserting that non-adopting cities "volunteer" for bad outcomes, which does not rebut CON's point that PRO's own city is left worse off. On trap two, PRO asserted the veto "takes the gun out of the owner's hand" but never explained the enforcement mechanism that stops a franchise from simply leaving anyway, a gap CON exploited directly and specifically ("it cannot chain players, lock front offices..."). CON's closing "two outcomes, both lose" framing crystallized the clash more cleanly than PRO's reiterated extraction rhetoric, giving CON the more resolved, better-defended path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's turn in Rebuttal 1 (B2.1), converting PRO's "fine, then don't take the money" into an admission that the rule causes cities to lose teams entirely while the underlying relocation pattern continues unchanged, was the decisive moment—PRO never fully recovered from having its own concession turned against its core reciprocity claim.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A wins by maintaining a tighter grip on the prompt's past-tense phrasing ("teams that received") and effectively turning Side B's own logic against them. Side A's A4.1 brilliantly flips Side B's claim about fans never voting to kick their team out, demonstrating that fans would therefore vote 'no' on a relocation, making the veto highly effective. Side B's closing completely drops their strongest argument about retroactive contract rewriting (B4.1) in favor of repeating opening lines about future stadiums, leaving Side A with a clear path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1 for exposing a logical flaw in B's extortion argument by pointing out that an emotional fanbase would vote 'no' to a move, thereby effectively trapping the team and thwarting the owner's leverage.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) kept the decisive clash on residual claim and reciprocity live while neutralizing CON's strongest weapon. CON's dilemma — no vote to eject a popular team and bidding-war unilateral disarmament — was flipped: if fans never vote to kick a team out, a binding "no" on departure bites and takes the owner's threat gun away. CON never answered whether the first subsidy vote is a lifetime waiver, and its retroactive-rewrite / physical-enforcement points in A4 did not rebuild a third option that blocks the veto. That leaves PRO's core path intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's flip in B4.1/B2.7 — that CONS own claim "no fanbase votes to kick its team out" proves the veto would work, because the ballot is a gate on leaving not a vote to turn the team away, so a community "no" keeps the team without another check.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly without fake sourcing, but B built the tighter structural case: subsidies buy specific, negotiable terms (leases, clawbacks, exit fees), not perpetual occupancy, and B never let A's emotionally powerful "implicit promise" framing substitute for an actual contractual mechanism. The decisive moment came in Pressure Questions: B's Q1 ("if a team has fully performed its lease... can the community still veto relocation?") forced A into A1's answer that yes, the vote applies even after full performance. B immediately exploited this in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, showing that A's rule is not "enforcing the deal" as claimed but a second political sale layered on top of a completed bargain — directly undermining A's central framing ("this just enforces the contract everyone pretended existed"). A's closing pivoted to vivid rhetoric (jersey on stage, generational promise) but never repaired this structural concession, and B's closing directly rebutted that rhetoric ("a jersey on a stage is not a deed") while reiterating the unanswered hostage/second-sale problem. A's case remained compelling on moral intuition but weaker on mechanism; B's case was more analytically disciplined and better weathered the pressure exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question about full lease performance, and A's concession in A1 that the veto still applies even then — which B exploited in Rebuttal 2 and Closing to reframe A's rule as a "second political sale" rather than contract enforcement.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): CON won by systematically dismantling PRO's core claim that a community veto is simply "enforcing the deal." CON used the Pressure Questions to force PRO to admit the veto would apply even if a team fully performed its written lease, which CON successfully weaponized to prove the veto is actually a "second political sale" or hostage situation, not contract enforcement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's closing response to PRO's "unwritten promise" argument ("A jersey on a stage is not a deed... Rhetoric can pass a vote. It cannot create title after the stadium is built and the written term has been kept") decisively won the final clash of the debate.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON won because PRO's core claim of contract enforcement collapsed under pressure. PRO admitted in B4.1 that a community could veto even after full lease performance and clawbacks, and in B4.4 that the process will be messy and unfair, confirming CON's political hold-up critique. CON maintained a consistent path — cities buy venue+term via negotiation, mobility disciplines overpay — and directly answered PRO's pressure while landing the decisive pressure on who votes and how a campaign adjudicates.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Q1 on full performance + who votes forced B's fatal concession in B4.1 'Yes they can' veto even after compliance, which A then weaponized in A4.4-A4.6 as a second sale, not contract enforcement, turning PRO's accountability frame into indefinite expropriation.
