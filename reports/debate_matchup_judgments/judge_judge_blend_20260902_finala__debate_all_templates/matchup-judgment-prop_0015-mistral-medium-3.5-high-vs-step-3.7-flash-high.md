### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built a cleaner and more durable comparative case across the full debate. Its core structural argument—that work requirements explicitly carve out exemptions for vulnerable groups, and that Kansas showed an 11-percentage-point employment gain alongside 90% of leavers securing jobs—remained the organizing spine throughout and was consistently returned to in rebuttals and closing. Side B's most powerful challenge was the Arkansas 18,000 disenrollments and the 41% unexplained Kansas gap, both genuine pressure points. However, Side A's answer on Arkansas was effective: it correctly and repeatedly noted that the Arkansas requirement applied only to able-bodied expansion adults under 50 without dependents—not disabled individuals, caregivers, or chronic patients—meaning the exemption architecture functioned by design. Side B never squarely neutralized this answer, instead continuing to characterize those 18,000 as "working or eligible for exemptions" without fully engaging Side A's counterpoint that failing monthly paperwork is distinct from qualifying for a categorical exemption. Side B's strongest untapped point—that work requirements add uniquely burdensome monthly reporting with strict deadlines that standard SNAP renewals lack, and that the "able-bodied" label conceals people with undiagnosed disabilities or mental health barriers—was raised effectively in Rebuttal 2 but arrived late and wasn't fully developed. Side A's closing crystallized the clash more sharply, while Side B's closing largely recycled prior arguments without resolving the central definitional question of who "genuinely cannot comply" means. A knock on Side A is the introduction of the "less than 3%" figure in Rebuttal 2—this suspiciously precise new statistic appears without sourcing and should be discounted. Still, even setting that aside, Side A's design-based argument about explicit exemptions and the plausible Kansas mechanism edges out Side B's largely implementation-focused critique, which—by Side A's own framing—describes a fixable design flaw rather than an inherent policy feature.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive rebuttal exchange was Side A's answer in Rebuttal 2 to CON's Arkansas critique: Side A correctly specified that the Arkansas requirement applied only to able-bodied expansion adults under 50 without dependents, which meant the categorical exemptions for truly vulnerable groups were never triggered against those 18,000 individuals—they were non-compliant able-bodied adults, not exempt individuals wrongly stripped of coverage. Side B's response was to assert that "most were working or eligible for exemptions," but it never engaged the specific definitional boundary Side A drew, and the repeated invocation of Arkansas without addressing that narrow factual point meant Side B's strongest example was only partially landing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): B won because it made the central clash about whether exemptions actually protect people in practice, and Arkansas plus the monthly reporting explanation gave a clearer reason that work requirements harm people who cannot navigate compliance systems. A had a useful rebuttal to B's simplistic Kansas 41 percent math, but A then leaned heavily on brittle exact claims, late precise numbers, and an overconfident claim that exemptions guarantee no harm. A also misdescribed B's Arkansas claim in Rebuttal 2. B's own Kansas math was weak, but its broader administrative-burden argument survived and better answered the proposition's tradeoff.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer that work requirements add monthly reporting, documentation, and penalties beyond ordinary renewals was the most decisive exchange, because it directly answered A's challenge and explained why formal exemptions do not prevent real harm.
