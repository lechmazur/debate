### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO built a causal case (zoning outlawed cheap, low-barrier gathering space) and sustained it under pressure, directly answering CON's questions about subsidy cost (deregulation is free; seeding is small-scale) and mechanism (prevention vs. treatment framing). CON's opening "third places evolved, not died" framing was strong, but its own answer to PRO's pressure question—that a broke teen can just go to the library or park—conceded that public infrastructure is the safety net, which PRO's closing effectively exploited as proof of its own proposition. CON's later rebuttals largely repeated earlier claims (land values spike, cures depression) without directly engaging PRO's specific counters (scarcity as price inflator, small-scale incremental reuse, prevention/treatment distinction), giving PRO the last clean word on the decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's answer to CON's own pressure question—pointing out that CON's fallback ("the broke teen goes to the library") is literally the funded/zoned public infrastructure PRO is defending—turned CON's strongest rhetorical move into a concession that PRO's closing then crystallized decisively.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won decisively by adapting to the clash and providing strong comparative weighing, particularly the explanation of how physical spaces serve as 'prevention' rather than 'cure' for isolation. Side A severely undermined their case by heavily copy-pasting paragraphs from their first rebuttal into their second, completely ignoring Side B's nuanced answers regarding supply-side zoning impacts and mental health. Side B capitalized on Side A's concession regarding libraries to cleanly win the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 effectively dismantled Side A's claim that rec centers don't 'cure' depression by correctly framing third places as structural prevention rather than medical treatment.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins because it maintained a coherent causal story—government zoning created scarcity of free, accessible third places, and correcting that zoning plus funding public infrastructure is a legitimate government role—while successfully neutralizing Side A's key objections. A's core strategy relied on painting B's proposal as a subsidy for private cafes and a market-distorting zoning overhaul, but B clarified that the core ask is public libraries, parks, and rec centers plus low-cost deregulation, and A never recovered from the concession that libraries and parks are already valuable public infrastructure. A failed to answer B's live question of where a broke teenager can linger if single-use zoning bans the very public spaces A claims to support, and A's repetition of "land value spikes" and "clinical depression" never engaged with B's prevention-versus-cure logic or the distinction between small-scale legalization and luxury upzoning. B's closing crystallized this clash effectively, showing A had no answer for the zoning barrier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where B distinguished deregulation (which saves money) from modest public funding, clarified that "seeding" meant small-scale support like kiosks in public buildings rather than venture capitalism for private cafes, and explained the prevention mechanism by which public rooms interrupt isolation pathways before they become crises. This exchange neutralized A's strongest economic and public-health objections and kept B's route to victory intact.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): CON built a tighter, more consistently applied thesis ("chosen vs. mandated") and used it to land two decisive blows PRO never fully cleared: (1) the equity argument was turned on its head — CON showed that low-income neighborhoods already sustain low-cost, permissionless gathering (stoops, church basements, barbershops), and that funding new anchors would tax those thin margins to subsidize empty competitors; (2) the maintenance-backlog point undercut PRO's funding mechanism, since land-value taxes and endowments still draw from the same fiscal pool that already can't keep existing libraries and parks staffed. PRO's strongest rebuttal to the "informal spaces" point (barbershops/stoops still require money or property) came only in the closing and was undercut by CON's earlier parallel point that libraries also gatekeep via fines, IDs, and policing. CON's pressure question on unfalsifiability was sharp, and PRO's answer ("measured by quantifiable loss of non-transactional space") didn't fully resolve the circularity of defining decline as anything commercial. PRO's case remained coherent and well-organized but repeated its core public-goods framing without adequately neutralizing CON's mechanism-level attacks on equity and fiscal feasibility.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point that low-income neighborhoods already sustain gathering through low-cost, permissionless spaces (stoop, laundromat, church basement), and that active funding would tax those spaces to build a vacant, subsidized competitor — this directly dismantled PRO's central equity argument and was never adequately answered.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A consistently stayed ahead of the definitional clash, effectively proving that historic and functional third places are often commercial, informal, and chosen by regulars rather than mandated by city planners. Side B leaned heavily on defining third places exclusively as non-transactional public goods, but struggled to defend the practical reality of maintaining a vast new network of government-funded centers against Side A's maintenance backlog arguments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Turn 8 response outlining how low-income neighborhoods already gather informally (barbershops, church basements, stoops, laundromats) without municipal capital, which directly answered Side B's pressure question while reinforcing the deregulation thesis.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by proving that the claimed decline of third places is partly definitional (excluding thriving commercial gathering spots) and that active government funding is fiscally reckless when existing public spaces already face maintenance backlogs. CON successfully turned PRO's equity argument by showing that new funded centers often fail in underserved neighborhoods while deregulation empowers the low-cost, informal gathering (stoops, laundromats, church basements) that already works. PRO's commodification argument remained resonant but could not overcome CON's comparative demonstration that active programming produces sterile, vacant "windswept" spaces rather than the scruffy, chosen places that define real community life.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's fiscal and equity turn in Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.3), where CON demonstrated that active funding draws from the same strained municipal budgets that already cannot maintain libraries and parks, and that PRO's equity promise collapses because new centers in low-income neighborhoods enter the same deferred-repair cycle while deregulation would let existing informal spaces multiply.
