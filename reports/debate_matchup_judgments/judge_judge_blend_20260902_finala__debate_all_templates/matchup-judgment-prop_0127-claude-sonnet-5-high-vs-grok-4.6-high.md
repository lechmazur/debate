### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that voluntary incentives leave disposal available whenever it remains easiest, while a markdown or donation duty changes the default. B plausibly separated recovery of existing surplus from preventing future overstock and explained workable compliance through ordinary sales and pickup systems. A raised a real ex ante under-ordering risk and a strong distinction between voluntary and mandatory verification, but overstated inevitable scarcity and repeatedly relied on burdens facing corner or five-employee grocers despite the proposition targeting large supermarkets.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s scarcity rebuttal was decisive: food already destined for disposal can be recovered, while reduced future over-ordering prevents waste rather than necessarily depriving customers. A showed that ordering decisions occur under uncertainty, but never established that the resulting adjustment would cause meaningful shortages rather than trim wasteful excess.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON won the decisive mechanism clash. PRO's moral case that waste is externalized and recoverable was strong, but CON turned ordering-less from virtue to vice: risk attaches at 8am before surplus is knowable, so managers shave all orders, hurting 8pm availability. PRO asserted two distinct streams [B4.1-B4.3] but never explained how to sort future pantry vs customer items ex ante. The verification asymmetry—voluntary credit claimed when beneficial vs duty-checked on every item with penalties—survived B's attempt to equate them. That left an incentive path to PRO's own recovery goal without statutory scarcity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A2 in R2: manager cannot know at ordering which yogurts become surplus, so compliance risk suppresses both waste and sales, plus voluntary vs duty verification asymmetry — directly damaged B's 'stop manufacturing waste' and 'same paperwork' claims.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by decisively exposing a fatal flaw in Side B's mechanism: a punitive disposal mandate creates compliance risk at the time of ordering, not just at closing. Because managers cannot predict exactly which items will be surplus hours later, the rational response to a mandate is to cut orders across the board, creating scarcity for actual paying customers. Side B's argument that "ordering less is the point" fails to address the collateral damage of mid-evening stockouts, and Side B's attempt to equate voluntary tax credit verification with universal mandate verification is effectively dismantled by Side A's asymmetry argument. Side A's structural critique of supermarket inventory incentives is highly persuasive and remains unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 [A4.2], which explains that compliance risk attaches at the 8am ordering moment before surplus is known, forcing managers to shave all orders and creating scarcity, decisively breaking Side B's attempt to separate "existing surplus" from "future orders."
