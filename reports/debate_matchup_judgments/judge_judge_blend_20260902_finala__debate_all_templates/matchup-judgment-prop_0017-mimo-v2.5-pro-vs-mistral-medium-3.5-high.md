### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): CON won by consistently turning PRO's own evidence against the proposition. The decisive move was exposing that Houston and Salt Lake City achieved their results through simultaneous enforcement and service expansion—not by waiting until a service threshold was fully met as "only when" requires. PRO never squarely answered this. When PRO claimed in Rebuttal 2 that those cities "built real capacity then enforced" (sequential), CON rightly called this a characterization that contradicts how those programs actually operated. CON also successfully distinguished between what courts have actually demanded (a constitutional floor against criminalizing status with zero shelter capacity) and the proposition's broader three-part "genuinely available" test, which CON correctly identified as having no defined ceiling. The "genuinely" veto argument stayed live throughout because PRO's answer—courts set the standard—was repeatedly shown to conflate the legal floor with the proposition's more demanding language. CON's pressure questions were sharper: Q2 forced a dilemma PRO couldn't escape without conceding either that the proposition is too absolute or that its own evidence undermines it. PRO's moral foundation was emotionally strong and the court framework had initial force, but PRO's specific statistics were sometimes brittle, and the shorter closing failed to resolve the sequential/simultaneous problem that CON had put at the center of the debate. CON's closing crystallized the core clash cleanly and without introducing late new evidence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 pivot on the Houston and SLC examples: PRO introduced these cities as proof the proposition's standard enables effective governance, but CON showed both cities expanded housing capacity and enforced encampment rules simultaneously—not sequentially. This turned PRO's anchor evidence into a liability. The "only when" clause structurally forbids the parallel approach that actually produced results, meaning PRO's own best evidence refuted the proposition. PRO never recovered from this, and CON hammered it through Rebuttal 2 and the closing.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side B wins because it identified and exploited the proposition's absolute "only" and "genuinely" language as an unworkable veto, then used PRO's own Houston/Salt Lake City examples to show simultaneous enforcement succeeds while the motion requires sequential completion. B's pressure questions and direct A1/A2 answers exposed this inconsistency; A's responses were repetitive and failed to resolve the logical gap or address the SCOTUS challenge. Later rounds show B crystallizing the parallel vs. permission-structure distinction without widening the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 and closing, which used the pressure questions to distinguish the constitutional floor from the proposition's unbounded three-part test and forced PRO to defend examples that contradict the "only when" structure.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by demonstrating that the proposition's "only when" language structurally mandates a sequential approach that prohibits the parallel enforcement-and-services model that Side B's own examples employed. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built and maintained the stronger path to victory across all rounds. Their core argument—that punishing an involuntary biological necessity without offering any lawful alternative criminalizes status, not conduct—held up throughout the exchange. Three specific execution advantages sealed the win. First, A's pressure question about the incentive gap (what compels cities to build shelter if it isn't required?) was the most decisive single move in the debate; B's answer ("voter demand and cost-effectiveness") was empirically weak given decades of persistent crises despite those incentives existing. Second, A's clarification in A4.1 that the standard operates person-by-person—not as a blanket encampment-wide veto—directly neutralized B's most repeated attack. Crucially, B never engaged with this clarification in their subsequent speech or closing, continuing to characterize A's position as an "individual veto" even after A explicitly rebutted it; this is a significant execution failure. Third, A's clean distinction between punitive anti-camping ordinances and public health emergency interventions (A4.2) deflated B's strongest recurring example (hepatitis outbreaks), and B never found an adequate counter. Side B did score points on the Martin v. Boise conduct/status distinction and on the real harms caused by encampments, but these were partially neutralized by A's rebuttal that dispersing camps without alternatives worsens conditions and makes outreach harder. B's case also suffered from notable brevity in the opening (219 of 340 words), leaving the core case underdeveloped early when it mattered most. B's closing repeated the same scenarios without addressing the evolved state of the debate, while A crystallized the incentive argument cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A4.1 answer clarifying that the proposition's standard is individualized—not a blanket veto for an entire camp—was the debate's most decisive rebuttal. It directly neutralized B's most repeated and feared scenario. B's failure to engage with this clarification in the second rebuttal or closing meant their strongest attacking argument lost its force precisely when it needed to land.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won because the condition creates accountability for shelter expansion (via the incentive question A raised and B weakly answered with failed voter pressure), distinguishes sleep as involuntary necessity, and separates emergency health tools from criminalization; B's paralysis/veto framing was refuted by A's individualized enforcement and was undercut by real-world examples of capacity-first success. B's safety examples were turned against them without adequate weighing. Clipped A speeches count as minor execution cost but visible content still created clearer path to vote for proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's direct A1/A2 answers to B's pressure questions (no blanket veto; emergency powers distinct from anti-camping ordinances) that crystallized the non-paralysis case and exposed B's evasion on incentives.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side B won by maintaining a clearer, more coherent path to victory across the exchange. B successfully defused A's two strongest attacks: first, by clarifying in Rebuttal 2 that the standard applies person-by-person rather than as a blanket veto on encampment-wide enforcement, and second, by distinguishing public health emergency interventions (like hepatitis outbreak response) from punitive anti-encampment criminalization. A never recovered from these answers, instead repeating the same "impossible standard" and "outbreak" claims in the closing as if they were still live. B also kept effective pressure on the incentive question: A conceded cities "can and do" pair enforcement with services but offered no mechanism to ensure they must, whereas B's condition creates a direct legal driver for shelter provision. A's failure to adapt to B's distinctions and B's sharper comparative weighing in the closing made the decision clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 distinction between public health emergency powers and criminal anti-encampment ordinances, which directly answered CON's persistent hepatitis outbreak scenario while exposing CON's conflation of emergency relocation/treatment with punitive sleeping bans.
