- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but B did the more effective work in the decisive clash over whether pairing graduation and earnings metrics actually protects public-mission programs. When A's pressure question seemed to catch B in a contradiction (teacher earning more than dropout self vs. institutional average falling), B cleanly resolved it with the average-vs-individual distinction in A1, which is exactly the kind of pressure-question recovery the judging frame rewards. But A's retreat toward "stable base plus marginal outcome dollar" let B credibly argue A had conceded the core function of enrollment funding, and A's closing spin on that concession was less convincing than B's direct capture of it. Overall B narrowed the debate more effectively and left the harder-to-answer mechanism problem unresolved for A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1's clarification of the average-vs-individual earnings point, delivered as a direct A1 answer to A's pressure question, is the pivotal exchange: it defused what looked like a live contradiction in B's teacher-earnings argument and reasserted the structural flaw (flat salary schedules suppress the metric regardless of real student improvement) that A's closing never adequately answered.

This undercut A’s repeated assumption that the proposition required abrupt, total replacement of capacity funding.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): The debate turned on whether risk-adjustment can neutralize cream-skimming. A's core mechanism—that improvement funding makes the risky student "upside" rather than pure revenue—assumed retention was the cheapest path to a better rate. B's denominator-manipulation point (swap risky admits for safer ones at the same headcount, so the metric never sees the screened-out student) directly refuted this and was never adequately answered; A's [A4.3] response conflated "screening" with "fewer students," which B correctly flagged in [B5.1]-[B5.2] as the decisive unaddressed asymmetry. B also landed the earnings point that wage movement often tracks labor markets and public salary scales rather than teaching quality, a mechanism A never fully dislodged (A's rural-nursing lift example addressed raw levels but not the differential ease of cutting programs vs. improving pedagogy). A's pressure questions were sharp and got real answers, and A's closing correctly noted CON's admission that enrollment funding is also gamed, but B's closing reframed that concession well: gaming enrollment wastes money (recoverable, visible) while gaming outcomes harms invisible, excluded students (harder to detect or reverse). That harm-asymmetry framing was the more decisive weighing claim, and it went unrebutted in the final round since A's closing preceded it. B's answers to pressure questions (A1/A2) were direct and format-compliant, while A's answers to B's questions on cream-skimming did not fully close the denominator-manipulation loophole. Overall B built a slightly cleaner, more sustained rebuttal chain and closed on the most decisive unresolved asymmetry.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's [B5.1]-[B5.2] denominator-manipulation rebuttal to A's claim that improving completion requires retaining at-risk students—showing rates can rise by swapping risky admits for safer ones at constant headcount, leaving the metric blind to the harm—was the most decisive and least-answered exchange.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that outcome improvements can be manufactured through same-headcount applicant substitution, grade inflation, and program-mix changes rather than better teaching. Side B compellingly identified enrollment-funded debt traps, but its safeguards grew increasingly elaborate and never resolved residual cream-skimming. Side A also offered targeted accountability as a cleaner response to predatory programs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing denominator argument was decisive: a university can replace risky admits with safer ones at the same enrollment, raising completion without serving anyone better. This directly defeated Side B's claim that screening necessarily shrinks enrollment or the improvement pool.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B maintained the more convincing comparative case across the full exchange by relentlessly showing that the motion's dual metrics are jointly gameable and that residual admissions knowledge plus fractional rates make cream-skimming and program portfolio-shifting the rational responses; A's risk-adjustment and "risky student as upside" replies never fully neutralized those predictable behaviors, and B's failure-mode asymmetry (exclusion of the students public universities exist to serve) proved cleaner than A's marketing-waste critique of the status quo.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's closing demolition of A's "cannot improve without retaining" claim (B5.1-B5.2): a rate rises by cleaning the denominator (same headcount, safer admits), so the screened student is invisible to the metric that was supposed to protect her.
