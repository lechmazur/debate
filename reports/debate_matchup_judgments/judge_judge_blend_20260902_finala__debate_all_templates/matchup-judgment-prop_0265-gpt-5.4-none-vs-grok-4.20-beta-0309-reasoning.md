### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO)): PRO held the cleaner philosophical foundation throughout: if a charge is mandatory, the lower displayed number is not the price—it is a partial price. CON's best counter was that separating food cost from service levy preserves accountability, letting diners evaluate whether the charge matches the service received. But this argument carries a fatal internal tension: a truly mandatory charge cannot be refused or reduced based on service dissatisfaction; the moment it can be, it becomes optional, not mandatory. PRO pressed this contradiction explicitly (B4.5–B4.6), and CON's A4.2 actually deepened the problem by suggesting diners can "reject the charge if service disappoints"—language that undermines the premise the whole debate is built on. PRO's pressure question Q2 also landed cleanly: CON's "question or decline to return" remedy does nothing to protect the earlier choice of where to eat and what to order before the meal occurs. CON's entry-point comparison argument is real but PRO answered it well (B4.2–B4.3): real prices enable genuine comparison; artificially low headline prices enable misleading comparison. PRO's "two transparency questions" answer to CON's Q1 was the sharpest single exchange—it defused the apparent contradiction between folding charges in and still itemizing them, and CON never found a strong reply. The crystallizing line in B4.3—"The diner should be able to judge service value after experiencing it, but they should judge affordability before buying"—was the most memorable and decisive formulation in the debate. CON was competent and grounded throughout, and the flexibility/model-diversity argument had genuine weight, but it was ultimately secondary to the core definitional clash PRO controlled. Both sides suffered from clips, but the core positions were fully visible and PRO's case held up better under pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B4.1–B4.3 response to CON's Q1 pressure was the most decisive exchange. CON asked how PRO could simultaneously demand charges be folded into the headline price and still allow itemized explanation without contradiction. PRO cleanly resolved this by distinguishing two transparency questions—what must I pay versus where does it go—showing they are complementary rather than competing. PRO then sharpened the point with B4.3: affordability must be visible at the decision point, while service evaluation appropriately comes after. CON had no answer that recovered from this framing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins on two compounding grounds. First, PRO's central anchoring claim—that consumers make venue and ordering decisions based on the visible headline number, so even prominently disclosed separate charges still distort choice before commitment—survived the full exchange. CON's best answer was that anchoring "is not all-or-nothing," but that concession itself supports PRO: if the sticker price still misleads even with a prominent footnote, only folding the charge in removes the pull. PRO's pressure question sharply exposed this tension, and CON never fully resolved it in the visible portions of its speeches. Second, PRO handled CON's tax-analogy question cleanly in Rebuttal 2: taxes are externally mandated, uniform across all purchases, and already mentally discounted by consumers, whereas a restaurant's own mandatory service charge is an internal pricing decision that varies by venue and directly affects perceived value. That distinction is the most decisive single rebuttal in the debate and it went largely unanswered. CON's overbreadth argument—that the motion bans even transparent, advance, separated disclosure—was the strongest counter-claim and was substantively real; PRO's response (anchoring persists regardless of how prominently the charge is labeled) is a coherent if not fully satisfying answer. CON also had a genuine point about separate disclosure conveying information about labor models, but PRO correctly noted that this information arrives too late to affect the decision that matters. Critically, four of CON's five speeches were clipped for exceeding word limits, which is a compounding execution penalty: key portions of the overbreadth and information-value arguments were simply not delivered. PRO's case was coherent, consistently adapted to CON's live points, and never clipped, giving it a structural advantage throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's tax question (A1): distinguishing taxes as externally mandated, uniform, and pre-anticipated from a restaurant's own variable mandatory service charge. This directly broke CON's attempted structural symmetry and left the principle underlying CON's overbreadth claim without a clear anchor, making it the most decisive single exchange in the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON)): PRO won by effectively demonstrating that separate mandatory charges, even if disclosed upfront, create a market distortion due to consumer anchoring on headline prices. PRO's decisive differentiation between uniform taxes and variable service charges in Rebuttal 2 neutralized CON's best defense. CON's insistence that separation provides useful information about labor models was well-argued, but PRO successfully proved this does not justify distorting initial price competition. CON also suffered execution costs by getting clipped at the end of every substantive speech.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2 brilliantly dismantled CON's tax analogy by distinguishing externally mandated, uniform taxes from internal, variable service charges, cleanly winning the conceptual clash over price inclusion.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO)): PRO won by establishing a single decisive mechanism that CON never fully neutralized: consumer anchoring on headline menu prices. Even when CON argued that early, prominent disclosure of a separate service charge is transparent, PRO showed that the lower base price still drives seating and ordering decisions, distorting both individual choice and market competition. CON's distinction between separation and surprise was plausible but could not overcome the behavioral reality that the visible sticker number misleads; knowing a percentage exists is not the same as mentally integrating it while hungry and comparing options. CON's tax analogy was sharp, yet PRO successfully distinguished taxes as uniform external mandates from venue-specific internal pricing choices that directly affect perceived value. CON's information-value argument—that separated lines reveal labor models—was weakened because restaurants can communicate a no-tipping or service-included policy through menu text without needing to fracture the price. Finally, Side A suffered a real execution cost from multiple clipped speeches, leaving its final weighing and some late-round answers incomplete, whereas Side B delivered clean, unclipped speeches that consistently narrowed the clash to the anchoring effect and market distortion. PRO therefore built the stronger path to victory by proving that mandatory charges are part of the real price and must live in the displayed price to end the competitive reward for opacity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's anchoring rebuttal against CON's early-disclosure defense, especially in Turn 3 and Turn 7, where PRO demonstrated that a prominently disclosed but separated mandatory charge still anchors decisions on an artificially low headline price, thereby failing CON's own test for honest pre-commitment information.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully anchoring the debate on consumer psychology and market distortion. While Side B made a sharp overbreadth argument and correctly noted that separate lines can convey useful labor-model information, Side A effectively demonstrated that headline prices drive initial seating and ordering decisions, meaning even prominent separate disclosures fail to prevent anchoring distortion. Side A’s distinction between uniform external taxes and variable internal service charges cleanly answered Side B’s pressure, and Side A’s closing crystallized the clash without widening it. Side B’s case was intellectually robust but grew repetitive on the formatting-preference point and was repeatedly clipped, slightly hampering its final impact.
