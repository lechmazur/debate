### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): PRO built the stronger path across the whole exchange. Its opening framing ("monetized ignorance," the $18-vs-$27 example, "if they can surface a deal they can surface a cheaper pickup") was clean and specific without fake precision. Its first rebuttal converted CON's two anchors—"different product" and "materially higher vagueness"—into either an evasion or a mere implementation question, and the "right to obscure" tag line captured the ethical core memorably. PRO's pressure questions were a genuine trap: Q1 set up the logical dilemma that CON never escaped—if the notice can be ignored it causes no harm; if users act on it, the platform was winning through non-disclosure, not value. PRO's R2 answered CON's symmetric questions with a plausible, internally consistent mechanism (apps already ingest merchant data; trigger only when verifiable; if they can't verify, they need not claim) and then returned to the same trap. CON's rebuttals were shorter than the cap on multiple occasions, left the Q1 dilemma structurally unanswered by pivoting to "cart abandonment" (an empirically thin claim), and repeatedly restated the same two anchors with only superficial rewording. CON's most interesting move—framing the mandate as "compelled speech favoring rival channels" rather than transparency—arrived too late and was never developed into a full comparative argument. The commission-retaliation argument was turned against CON by PRO as direct evidence of gatekeeper leverage, and CON had no clean answer. PRO's closing crystallized the decisive comparison cleanly: no harm if ignored, revealed overcharging if heeded. CON's closing restated earlier points without sharpening the choice. On balance, PRO made the debate easier to decide and answered the live pressure more directly throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure Q1 ("if users truly want doorstep delivery, why would a brief notice cause harm instead of just being ignored?") created the central logical trap of the debate: the two wings of CON's case are mutually defeating. CON never closed this gap—R2 answered with "cart abandonment," which is plausible only in extreme edge cases and doesn't address the structural point. PRO then used the same dilemma to anchor its closing, making it the clearest reason to vote PRO.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing Side A's economic arguments (e.g., apps raising commissions if they lose revenue) as proof of abusive gatekeeper power rather than a reason to avoid transparency. Side B also cleanly defused Side A's feasibility and compliance objections by pointing to existing data ingestion and standard regulatory line-drawing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 completely dismantled Side A's compliance burden arguments by noting that apps only need to disclose when they can reasonably verify the price difference, eliminating the need for "perfect, real-time omniscience."
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins by consistently framing the mandate as a narrow, ignorable transparency rule that tests whether platforms earn their premium through value or opacity. PRO neutralized CON's core harms: the "friction" claim collapses against a brief, dismissible notice, and CON's warning that apps would raise commissions if forced to disclose cheaper options was cleanly flipped as an admission of gatekeeper leverage rather than a defense of the status quo. PRO handled implementation questions (data verification, threshold vagueness) with plausible mechanisms and standard regulatory reasoning, while CON treated ordinary line-drawing as a fatal flaw and grew increasingly repetitive. CON's "different product" and "compelled subsidy" frames never answered PRO's central weighing: if users truly want delivery, they ignore the notice; if they switch, the app was winning on non-disclosure. PRO's sharper rhetoric, cleaner progression, and decisive rebuttals make the path to victory clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response to CON's claim that apps would raise commissions or demote independents if transparency cuts overcharges. PRO flipped this from a practical warning into a structural indictment, arguing that preserving opacity to prevent intermediary retaliation is a "right-to-obscure" argument that proves the rule's necessity. CON's "basic business math" reply failed to recover the comparative ground, leaving PRO's transparency frame dominant.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built a cleaner, more durable path to victory. The core distinction between genuine transparency—requiring a platform to disclose its own pricing clearly—and compelled off-platform steering was established in the opening and never meaningfully neutralized. PRO's answer that it is "the same restaurant" is partially responsive but doesn't erase the product difference: delivery with dispatch, support, and tracked payment is not the same transaction as pickup or a direct-order site. CON held this ground effectively throughout. CON's pressure questions exposed two live burdens: the definition of "materially higher" and the reliability of external channel data. PRO's answers created new problems rather than resolving them. The invented 15%/$5 threshold was arbitrary and not in the proposition, and PRO's own concession—suggesting a disclaimer telling users to verify before switching—directly contradicted their earlier claim that "there is no guessing risk." CON exploited both admissions sharply in Rebuttal 2, showing that PRO's solution required contested regulatory judgments and admitted unreliability. PRO's Rebuttal 1 being clipped was a small execution cost that left CON's best technical objections (stale data, apples-to-oranges comparison) incompletely answered. PRO's closing tried to reframe the threshold as a "standard regulatory benchmark" without grounding, and the claim that the "only party harmed is apps profiting off user ignorance" was rhetorically punchy but ignored the restaurant-blame and consumer-misdirection harms CON raised. CON's closing crystallized the choice cleanly: show your own costs accurately versus compel a seller to advertise and effectively validate a transaction it does not control. That framing was sharper and more persuasive at the end of the exchange. PRO identified a genuine real-world problem with information asymmetry in food delivery, which kept the debate competitive, but the proposed solution as defended had too many implementation gaps that CON converted into decisive points.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's exploitation of PRO's disclaimer concession in Rebuttal 2: PRO had claimed "there is no guessing risk" because apps already hold partnership data, but then proposed adding a disclaimer telling users to verify the direct channel before switching. CON responded directly—"once the user must verify, there plainly is still guessing risk"—turning PRO's own solution text against their strongest technical claim and showing the "simple prompt" story was not so simple after all.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling the feasibility and fairness of the proposition. When pressed on how apps could reliably verify external direct-order channels, Side B conceded that apps might need to add a disclaimer telling users to verify the channel themselves. Side A ruthlessly capitalized on this in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, proving that the mandate would force apps to advertise unreliable external transactions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing weaponized Side B's "disclaimer" concession flawlessly, framing it as an admission that the mandated comparison is inherently unreliable and thus not a simple consumer protection rule.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully framing the motion as compelled off-platform steering rather than simple transparency and by decisively winning the technical feasibility clash. When Side B (PRO) answered pressure questions by inventing a specific 15% or $5 threshold and conceding that a disclaimer would be needed, Side A effectively used those admissions to prove the comparison is unreliable and the mandate is operationally complex. Side A's distinction between disclosing one's own fees and forcing a platform to validate an uncontrolled external channel remained unanswered in substance. Side B's claim that apps already possess real-time external direct-order pricing via partnerships was overconfident and weakly grounded, while Side A maintained realistic operational constraints throughout. Side A's closing crystallized the clash cleanly, making the path to victory clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which directly capitalized on PRO's invented threshold and disclaimer admission to dismantle the simple transparency framing and prove the mandate relies on unreliable comparisons and arbitrary rules.
