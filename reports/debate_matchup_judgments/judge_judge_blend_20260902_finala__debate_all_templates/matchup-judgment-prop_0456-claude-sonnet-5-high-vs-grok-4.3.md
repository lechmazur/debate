### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by consistently comparing a compulsory payment system with the demonstrated practical failures of individual enforcement and negotiation. Side A established genuine costs involving consent, substitution, and underpricing, but its alternative remained largely aspirational: better enforcement and voluntary pooling without a convincing account of how ordinary creators gain leverage. Side B made the choice easier to decide by emphasizing universal, imperfect compensation over rights that often yield no payment in practice.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s most decisive response was that Side A compared statutory rates with ideal negotiations rather than with the zero payment many scraped creators currently receive. Side A answered that enforcement could improve, but never supplied a comparably concrete route from that possibility to broad compensation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing the debate around how these regimes actually operate rather than how they theoretically should. Side B mounted a strong principled defense of the right to exclude and warned of industrial subsidy, but never overcame Side A's core empirical pressure: under the voluntary system Side B defends, the vast majority of creators currently receive zero payment and have no realistic leverage to negotiate, while only the largest players cut deals. Side A's "fictional veto for a real paycheck" frame crystallized this imbalance, and Side B's repeated recourse to "under-enforcement" lacked a concrete mechanism to fix it at internet scale. Side B's closing brevity, while elegant, left Side A's comparative challenge about midlist creators largely unaddressed, making Side A's practical case the cleaner route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's answer to Side B's Q2 in Rebuttal 2 (A4.3), where PRO demonstrated that CON compares imperfect statutory rates to an idealized negotiation that does not exist for creators without market power, while the status quo pays scraped breakout hits and midlist creators exactly nothing.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by grounding the debate in the practical realities of AI training, effectively arguing that the "veto" right Side B defended is unenforceable for most creators and results in zero compensation. Side A's contrast between an imperfect statutory paycheck and a theoretical negotiation that never happens was highly persuasive. Side B relied too heavily on the theoretical purity of copyright and failed to adequately explain how "better enforcement" would solve the power imbalance between tech giants and individual creators.

Side A effectively showed that market flooding is already happening via private deals and that statutory rate-setting bodies already handle tiered pricing, exposing Side B's reliance on an idealized negotiation market that doesn't exist for most creators.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side A won by exposing the unsupported premise beneath Side B's compensation-or-nothing framing: that unauthorized training is inevitable regardless of legal rules. A explained that infringement liability, injunctions, and refusal create the leverage driving voluntary deals, whereas compulsory licensing removes that leverage and leaves creators dependent on politically set rates. B repeatedly asserted practical inevitability but never answered this legal and causal distinction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A4.2-A4.5 was decisive: it answered B's pressure question directly, distinguished illegal scraping from a statutory entitlement to scrape, and showed why compulsory payment would replace rather than supplement creators' bargaining leverage.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by demolishing A's premise that uncompensated training is inevitable. B showed that copyright liability is the current engine driving voluntary deals, and that a compulsory scheme would eliminate this leverage by declaring the underlying act lawful, converting active legal pressure into a buyer-favored statutory entitlement. B successfully turned A's mechanical-license analogy into a cautionary tale of depressed rates and proved that payment merely prices unavoidable reputational harm rather than preventing it. A never recovered from this structural turn, repeating the "compensation or nothing" frame in the closing without engaging B's decisive argument about litigation leverage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 8), where B demonstrated that compulsory licensing does not layer payment atop existing legal pressure but instead deletes the infringement liability that currently forces AI firms to negotiate, leaving creators with a statutory rate set by deep-pocketed buyers and zero fallback leverage.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won decisively by systematically dismantling Side A's core premises. Side A relied heavily on the argument that AI training on public data is inevitable and that a compulsory license is the only way to ensure payment. Side B brilliantly countered this in Rebuttal 2 by pointing out that such training is not a fact of nature but an actively litigated legal contest; the very threat of infringement lawsuits is the leverage forcing companies to the negotiating table for the voluntary deals Side A admitted were emerging. Side B showed that a compulsory scheme would legalize the training, destroy the creators' litigation leverage, and leave them at the mercy of a buyer-favored regulatory rate-setting process—a flaw Side B effectively highlighted using Side A's own mechanical license analogy. Side A's closing was notably brief and merely repeated the "it happens anyway" claim without answering Side B's devastating point about how the proposition destroys the legal pressure that actually generates compensation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 8), which reframed the debate by explaining that current copyright liability and the threat of litigation are the actual engines of voluntary licensing, and that a compulsory scheme would permanently strip creators of this leverage by declaring the underlying training lawful.
