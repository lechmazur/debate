### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A built the cleaner and more durable path to victory. Their core fairness argument—secular charities, unions, and political nonprofits already face spending-based scrutiny; religious organizations should not get a permanent carve-out simply for invoking "God"—was introduced cleanly in the opening and never meaningfully neutralized. Side B's strongest structural counter (that religious mission is inherently world-engaging and therefore impossible to cleanly separate from politics) is real but incomplete: it explains why line-drawing is hard, not why the standard that already works daily for secular nonprofits suddenly becomes tyranny when applied to congregations. Side A exploited this directly in R1 by catching Side B drawing the very line it claimed did not exist. Side A's pressure questions were sharper: Q1 exposed the internal tension in Con's position (you admit the abuse is real—are you defending the loophole?), and Q2 exposed a factual inconsistency (measuring primary purpose by spending already works for everyone else). Side B's Q1 scored a partial hit—Side A effectively conceded no historical example of audit power hitting the powerful first—but Side A partially recovered by arguing the status quo already disadvantages small congregations, turning the concern back on the opposition. Side B's closing was rhetorically effective and the "storefront congregation" framing is vivid, but it rests on a risk about implementation rather than a principled objection to the standard itself. The distinction between conduct-based auditing (which A defends) and belief-based policing (which B fears) was never genuinely closed by Side B, leaving A's route to victory live. Side B scored on originality with its concrete faith-community examples but never converted those examples into a clean principle that defeated A's fairness framework.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's R1 move catching Side B in its own contradiction: Con's opening distinguished pastoral doctrine from candidate-specific voting instruction, which is precisely the line the proposition enforces. Side A named this directly ("Con just drew the bright line itself—then pretended it doesn't exist"), converting Side B's most rhetorically vivid examples into evidence for the proposition rather than against it. Side B never cleanly answered this reversal in later rounds.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won by keeping the motion narrow: loss of tax advantages for overt partisan political conduct, not punishment for religious teaching. Its fairness/subsidy framing and conduct-versus-belief distinction answered the core overreach objection more cleanly than Side B answered why religious groups should receive a broader tax privilege than comparable secular nonprofits. Side B’s weaponization argument was compelling and rhetorically strong, but it leaned heavily on repeated, under-supported predictions that enforcement would never reach powerful megachurches and would mainly crush marginalized congregations.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s most decisive rebuttal was that Side B’s own examples imply a workable distinction between moral teaching and candidate-directed partisan action, and that tax law can audit conduct and spending without judging theology.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully turning Side B's fairness frame and dominating the implementation clash. While Side B built a clean, principled case around equal treatment and the secular nonprofit analogy, Side A effectively argued that formal equality in auditing would produce deeply unequal outcomes, disproportionately harming marginalized congregations while well-resourced megachurches use legal teams to evade scrutiny. Side A's pressure question on the historical use of audit power forced a revealing concession from Side B, which Side A capitalized on to prove the cure is worse than the disease. Side A's closing metaphor crystallized the risk calculus more memorably, giving the clearer reason to reject the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's claim of neutral audits, turning the fairness argument by demonstrating how equal standards applied to unequal resources predictably shield the powerful and crush the vulnerable, while forcing Side B to concede the historical reality of audit targeting.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A built the cleaner, more grounded path to victory across the exchange. Its core moves held up under pressure: (1) The Johnson Amendment already applies this logic to churches and has stood for roughly 70 years without judicial invalidation—a concrete anchor B never squarely demolished, only countered with "never seriously enforced," which is a fair but incomplete reply given that courts have had ample opportunity to review the rule; (2) A's crucial clarification in Rebuttal 2—that enforcement targets auditable financial transactions (ad buys, bus rentals, campaign staff salaries), not sermon content—materially defused B's most vivid "IRS in the pulpit" concern; (3) A exploited B's own concession that the status quo is already selectively enforced, turning B's strongest empirical claim into support for A's position. B's core constitutional argument—that churches possess First Amendment protection secular nonprofits lack—is a real and important point, but B never explained the precise mechanism: the Free Speech or Free Exercise Clause does not guarantee a public subsidy, only the right to speak. B kept asserting unconstitutionality without showing why withholding a tax benefit (not a speech ban) constitutes viewpoint discrimination. B's pressure Q1 was partly a disguised mini-speech rather than a clean question. And B never answered A's 70%-budget hypothetical squarely, pivoting instead to "who decides the line"—an evasion that left the most damaging intuition pump unanswered. B's "soup kitchen" and selective-enforcement arguments were vivid but were adequately neutralized by A's "primarily" qualifier and the status-quo-already-broken turn. The debate was genuinely competitive because B's constitutional distinction is substantively real, but A was sharper in narrowing the clash and leaving fewer live routes for B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 response to B's "IRS policing sermons" concern: A specified that enforcement targets auditable line items—ad buys, rally transport, campaign staff salaries—identical to what is already audited for every other nonprofit, rather than sermon transcription. This concretely reframed the enforcement mechanism and deflated B's most emotionally resonant warning without abandoning any ground. B's subsequent attempt to revive the "who decides if a sermon counts as political" concern felt weaker after this distinction was on the table.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by keeping the motion narrowed to organizations that become primarily overt partisan actors, not ordinary religious moral speech or charitable work. A repeatedly answered the soup-kitchen and sermon concerns by pointing to primary function, organizational spending, and auditable campaign activity, while B often collapsed that distinction back into sermons on poverty or immigration. B’s constitutional line was rhetorically strong but under-supported and overclaimed, especially the repeated assertion that churches have a unique right to keep tax advantages while endorsing candidates. A also handled the pressure questions more directly, while B largely dodged the 70% attack-ad subsidy question.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s most decisive rebuttal was that the rule already applies to nonprofit tax advantages generally and would target organizations functioning like PACs, not one sermon or policy statement. That sharply reduced B’s charity-chilling argument and forced B back onto a vague “government in the pulpit” warning without answering the clearest partisan-actor cases.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): PRO wins by successfully framing the motion as enforcing an existing, workable nonprofit standard rather than creating a new speech-policing regime. PRO directly answers CON's pressure questions, clarifying that audits would target financial line items (ad buys, rally buses, staff salaries) rather than sermon content, which materially damages CON's "IRS in the pulpit" fear. CON's core constitutional claim—that secular nonprofits lack endorsement rights while churches uniquely possess them—is legally imprecise and repeatedly asserted without engaging PRO's subsidy-condition framing. PRO also turns CON's selective enforcement argument, showing the status quo already protects powerful partisan churches, making equal application the fairer path. PRO's closing cleanly crystallizes this choice, while CON relies on repetition and an unresolved constitutional premise.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 directly answers CON's pressure by distinguishing auditable financial expenditures from religious speech, dismantling the sermon-policing fear. PRO also flips CON's selective enforcement warning by showing the current system already shields powerful partisan churches, making the proposed rule a corrective to an existing double standard rather than a new risk.
