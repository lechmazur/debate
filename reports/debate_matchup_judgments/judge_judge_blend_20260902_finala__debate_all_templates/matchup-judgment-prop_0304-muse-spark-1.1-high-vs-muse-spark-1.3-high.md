### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly without fake sourcing, but B kept the debate anchored to the motion's actual wording — the aggregate class-composition effect ("reproduce hierarchy more than promote mobility") — while A's strongest and most repeated move was to shift the denominator to per-seat value-added. B directly named this as a denominator swap ("grading the escalator per rider... the motion asks what it does to the building") and used A's own concessions (no institution lifts like elite schools per seat; seats stayed flat while endowments tripled) as confirmation of the inheritance thesis rather than a rebuttal to it. A's causal point (universities inherit, not create, K-12 inequality) and the policy-risk argument (taxation could push hiring into unregulated private networks) were genuinely strong and never fully neutralized, but A's answer to B's more nuanced growth-conditioned tax proposal was thin, mostly restating "it threatens aid" without engaging the conditional structure B specified. Pressure questions were well-answered on both sides with proper A1/A2 formatting, but B's questions (legacy hypocrisy, flat seats despite tripled endowments) extracted more damaging admissions than A's did. B's closing crystallized the clash more sharply and turned A's concessions into rhetorical liabilities, giving it the cleaner path to a decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's "escalator for dozens, a fortress for thousands" reframe in Rebuttal 1/2, later leveraged in closing as "the confession" against A's concession that no alternative institution matches elite per-seat mobility and that seats stayed flat despite tripling endowments — this exchange best exposed the denominator dispute at the heart of the motion and tilted it toward B.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making aggregate class structure, rather than mobility per admitted student, the clearer metric for the motion. B showed how affluent enrollment, inherited admissions advantages, scarce credentials, and concentrated recruiting can actively reproduce hierarchy while conceding that a small number of low-income students benefit greatly. A offered a strong value-added and counterfactual defense, but never fully justified counting exceptional per-seat mobility as outweighing the institution-wide reproduction mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer that the motion concerns what the escalator does to the building, not merely each rider, most effectively neutralized A's per-seat framing. A's claim that no alternative matches elite lift conceded individual effectiveness while leaving B's scale and dominant-function comparison intact.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): PRO won by establishing that the decisive metric for the motion is aggregate institutional effect on class structure, not per-seat value-added. CON never overcame PRO's core argument that enrolling more students from the top 1% than the entire bottom half makes inheritance the system's dominant function, no matter how transformative the experience is for the few poor admits who squeeze through. PRO's reframing of artificial scarcity—flat freshman seats for decades while endowments tripled—as the engine of class closure was left largely unanswered, and CON's useful counterfactuals about hiring networks did not justify why policy should continue to subsidize fortress-like concentration.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal and closing crystallized the decisive clash by exposing CON's denominator error: grading the escalator per rider cannot outweigh the aggregate function of the building. PRO's line "You don't defund the free ride; you defund scarcity" sharpened the policy comparison, and the turn that broad-access publics move millions while elite lift is high precisely because of exclusion gave PRO a live alternative that CON's per-seat metric could not defeat.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but the decisive clash was over which metric of "mobility" governs the motion: B's composition/proportion framing ("photograph, not engine," "lifeboat math") versus A's counterfactual-impact framing (what happens to the marginal disadvantaged student because the institution exists). A developed this reframe consistently from Rebuttal 1 through Closing, forced B into real concessions under pressure — B's A1 conceded that stripping the credential would revert hiring to "open nepotism," merely calling that "more honest," and B's A2 conceded broad-access colleges only deliver "modest but sure" gains rather than the top-decile jump elite schools produce. A exploited both concessions cleanly, and closed by repurposing B's own "photograph" metaphor against them, giving a sharper final crystallization. B's mechanism arguments (legacy, early decision, feeder pipelines as institutional choices) remained live and were never fully dismantled, but A's counterfactual pressure did more damage to B's policy proposal than B's mechanism critique did to A's institutional-behavior defense.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question ("if the degree just launders privilege, why would erasing that stamp help—wouldn't hiring revert to family calls with no credential to force comparison?") and B's forced A1 concession that removal would yield "visible inheritance, easier to challenge" was the pivotal exchange, exposing a real tension in B's policy remedy that A exploited through the rest of the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won the literal comparative burden: elite universities may transform some disadvantaged students, but their larger institutional role is admitting affluent students through advantage-sensitive gates and carrying them into exclusive career pipelines. Side A’s mobility counterfactual was valuable, yet it relied on unsupported claims that elite institutions uniquely produce top-end mobility and repeatedly treated B’s conditional reforms as abolition or wholesale defunding. B better connected composition, institutional choices, scale, and policy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response that universities do not merely inherit an unequal pipeline but actively decide which purchased advantages count as merit directly defeated A’s attempt to externalize responsibility to K-12 inequality. B then clarified that policy would condition and de-center elite privilege rather than erase the credential, weakening A’s nepotism counterfactual.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A won by consistently winning the proportionality clash: even granting CON’s claim that a few hundred low-income students receive dramatic mobility, the institutional core remains thousands of affluent seats protected by legacy, early-advantage pipelines, and credential laundering. A’s framing of “inheritance with charity” and the lifeboat analogy made the “more than” comparison in the motion easier to resolve, while A cleanly rebutted CON’s financial-aid hostage argument by noting that billion-dollar endowments could fund need-blind access without public subsidy. B’s counterfactual about hiring without the stamp was vivid but never overcame A’s point that firms still overwhelmingly fish in the same small, affluent-heavy pond.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s rebuttal that the credential legitimizes inherited advantage as earned merit—making visible nepotism politically easier to challenge than a disguised pipeline—sharply undercuts CON’s claim that the stamp dilutes privilege.
