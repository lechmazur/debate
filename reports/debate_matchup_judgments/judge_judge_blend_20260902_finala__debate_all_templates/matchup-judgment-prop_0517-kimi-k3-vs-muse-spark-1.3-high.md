### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by showing that credential recognition can provide portable, useful standing without automatically granting a professional license or replacing employer scrutiny. Side B's memorable empty-or-dangerous fork depended on false binaries: easier recognition need not mean no verification, and recognition can supplement rather than eliminate testing. A also made the cleaner comparison to residency by emphasizing relocation burdens, difficult removal, auditable remote output, and domestic liability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest attack was that low-risk recognition is decorative while consequential recognition creates dangerous reliance. Side A answered decisively with the middle category of accounting and software credentials: meaningful but non-automatic standing, recorded and auditable work, domestic gatekeepers, and separate licensing for high-risk practice.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by establishing a masterful strategic dilemma: either state recognition of remote credentials is for high-risk work where it is dangerous (as the state cannot verify it), or it is for low-risk work where private markets already test competence, making state recognition decorative. Side B tried to escape into a middle ground of 'financial' work, but Side A cleanly proved this still outsourced scrutiny to victims and insurers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive, perfectly capturing Side B's concession that private firms test remote workers' skills and concluding that this renders the state's recognition either decorative or a source of moral hazard.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B successfully trapped Side A in an "empty or dangerous" dilemma. Side B convincingly argued that for low-risk remote work, state recognition is redundant because the market already tests competence, while for high-risk work, Side A conceded local licensing is still required. When Side A attempted to defend a "middle ground" of financial/software work, Side B effectively argued that describing the harm as "discoverable and insurable" concedes the state stamp fails as a preventive gate and merely outsources risk to insurers. Side A's points about the high administrative cost of deporting residents were valid, but Side B's focus on ex ante harm prevention, jurisdictional reach, and the verifiability of physical presence versus remote competence was ultimately more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's "middle ground" professions by demonstrating that calling the harm "financial, discoverable, and insurable" admits the credential acts as a cleanup crew rather than a preventive gate, thereby neutralizing Side A's core justification for state-backed recognition.
