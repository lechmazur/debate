### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides built genuine, well-grounded cases with plausible mechanisms rather than fake citations. CON's opening landed real punches: a mandatory override channel is a backdoor, abrupt shutdown can be more dangerous than continuing, and comms-denied domains make real-time human control impossible. But PRO's rebuttal reframed "hard" as "non-overridable by the swarm" rather than "always-on human joystick," and used that reframe consistently and coherently across every round: safe-park instead of power-cut, bounded envelopes/dead-man timers instead of continuous tether, and layered fast automatic containment plus a privileged human command for the parts where time allows. This directly answered CON's three headline objections (security, comms-denied ops, dangerous abrupt stop) with concrete distinctions rather than assertion. CON's strongest counter-move — "that's not a human interrupt, it's ordinary autonomous code, so you've conceded the debate" — was a sharp semantic pressure point repeated into the closing, but it never actually refuted PRO's substantive claim that humans still author and hold veto over the bounds; it mostly re-litigated definitions rather than showing the swarm was actually left unstoppable or unsafe. PRO also scored a clean structural reversal ("an uninterruptible swarm is not resilient, it is a permanent weapon once compromised") that CON's answer to Q1 (network isolation, cryptographic revocation) never squarely neutralized for a swarm with no privileged override at all. CON also largely dropped PRO's race-to-the-bottom/legal-mandate-vs-voluntary argument, leaving that pillar unrebutted. Both handled the Pressure Questions round with proper A1/A2 formatting, but PRO's answers did more work to close the loop on CON's own pressure than CON's answers did to close PRO's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's "an uninterruptible swarm is not resilient; it is a permanent weapon once compromised" (turning CON's security-vulnerability argument back on itself) combined with PRO's repeated reframe of "hard" as non-overridable-but-not-real-time, which CON pressured hard on redefinition but never fully dismantled.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by constructing an inescapable dilemma that Side B never resolved. Any genuine hard human-interrupt control requiring real-time human override fails on machine-speed cascades, jamming, and adversarial exploitation in contested environments; any retreat to pre-committed autonomous bounds or tripwires is simply decentralized safety engineering that Side A already supports and does not constitute a human interrupt. Side B&#39;s attempt to fold autonomous fail-safes into the definition of &quot;hard human interrupt&quot; stretched the term to meaninglessness, leaving their closing reliant on the intuitive &quot;imperfect brakes&quot; metaphor without answering why the legally mandated brake would not itself introduce fatal failure modes. Side A sharpened the clash progressively, whereas Side B largely repeated its layered-backstop framing without convincing a third-party reader that pre-programmed autonomous envelopes satisfy the plain meaning of a human-interrupt requirement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A in Rebuttal 2 ([A4.4]) turning Side B&#39;s own circuit-breaker analogy against it by noting that trading halts operate via automated algorithms at machine speed, not by human deliberation and transmitted command, thereby reinforcing the dilemma that Side B&#39;s examples either fail on speed or concede the debate.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A successfully trapped Side B in a decisive dilemma regarding the definition and physical feasibility of a "hard human-interrupt." By forcing Side B to choose between a real-time tether (which Side A proved is vulnerable to jamming and too slow for machine-speed cascades) and pre-committed autonomous bounds (which Side A correctly identified as standard software logic, not a human interrupt), Side A dismantled the core mechanism of the proposition. Side B's reliance on redefining the mandate as "pre-committed limits" allowed Side A to claim a structural concession, making Side A's case much cleaner and more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which crisply answered the pressure questions while exposing the fatal contradiction in Side B's framework: real-time overrides fail the physics of contested environments, while pre-committed tripwires concede the debate by relying on the very autonomous fault tolerance Side B initially dismissed.
