### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by tying the motion's educational carve-out to the central equity comparison. Side A persuasively showed that some placements would be deleted rather than converted, but B conceded that cost and weighed it against opportunities effectively rationed by private financial support. A's defense of supervised, short-term placements also repeatedly described cases that B could plausibly place within the educational exception, weakening A's claim that the motion necessarily destroys the first rung.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange was the dilemma that genuinely supervised learning remains carved out, while placements valued chiefly for productive output resemble unpaid jobs. Side A exposed a real grey area involving mixed-value placements, but never fully answered B's pressure about why low productivity justifies paying interns below the wage floor when it does not justify doing so for other workers.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically breaking down Side A's mechanics, proving that A's strict "non-substitution" test would effectively ban mixed-value placements and lead to deletion rather than conversion. B successfully showed this deletion disproportionately harms unconnected applicants who rely on formal postings for short or local internships.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal exposed a fatal contradiction in A's case: A's "non-substitution" test reimports the very binary that bans the "grey middle" of internships, ensuring that government stipends would be left with "nothing to attach to" once the slots vanish.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): PRO built the cleaner path to victory by framing unpaid non-educational internships as either exploitative labour (which wage law should catch) or as education (which the carve-out protects), leaving CON to defend a grey zone that PRO showed was largely a wealth filter. CON’s scarcity argument was blunted by PRO’s equity turn: a hundred desks that require a parental subsidy are zero real chances for the unconnected, and CON’s own concessions about informal privilege reinforced that unpaid internships industrialize the back channel rather than open it. CON’s line-drawing worry and claim that the carve-out would be written as a narrow university checklist were plausible but speculative; PRO offered a concrete, broader test in Rebuttal 2 that CON misread as restricting mixed placements when it actually allowed them, weakening CON’s key rebuttal. PRO’s closing crystallized the clash around CON’s admission that the slot “only makes sense if cash is not also required,” making the means-test frame stick, while CON’s closing, though punchy, could not recover the noticeboard metaphor after PRO had shown the noticeboard was already closed to those without spare rooms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s dilemma in A2.1—either the intern produces valued work (a sham job wage law should reach) or she does not (and falls inside the educational carve-out)—forced the central clash. CON’s “false binary” response in B2.1 was articulate but never fully escaped: PRO sharpened the test in A4.1 with supervisor/structure/non-substitution criteria that should have saved CON’s mixed placements, yet CON in B4.3–B4.5 misread that test as restricting them, leaving PRO’s framework intact.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a competitive debate by making PRO's benefit depend on an insufficiently established conversion from unpaid placements into paid roles. Side B powerfully showed that unpaid access filters by household wealth, but repeatedly treated vanished placements as no social loss and did not adequately answer A's conditional, means-tested stipend alternative or the persistence of inaccessible private favors. A therefore provided the cleaner comparative: restriction certainly removes some open opportunities, while the promised paid replacements remain contingent.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in A4.3 through A5.3 was decisive: it explained how conditional subsidies could fund excluded applicants without a ban, then pressed that Side B's named beneficiary gains only if a paid placement actually replaces the abolished unpaid one. Side B asserted that necessary work would be paid but never fully resolved the thin-budget cases.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by consistently exposing a logical gap in Side A's case: if the disadvantaged cannot access unpaid internships anyway, banning them provides no benefit unless the roles reliably convert to paid jobs. Side B effectively proved that this conversion was an unsupported assumption, and that a ban would merely destroy visible opportunities while leaving unregulatable private networking intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal effectively trapped Side A in a dilemma regarding the exclusion of the working class, pointing out that PRO cannot claim the ban both costs them nothing and solves their exclusion.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): PRO established that unpaid internships function as class-sorting labor extraction. CON landed sharp blows on conversion and credentialed exemptions, but PRO's structural critique—that the unpaid category itself drives entry-level work toward free labor, making stipends optional and trailing those already able to work unpaid—proved more resilient. Both had clipped speeches, but PRO's privilege pipeline and labor-substitution mechanisms survived CON's pressure more cleanly than CON's elimination narrative survived PRO's core framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q2 asking whether restriction bans unpaid campaign volunteering and what stops relabeling, met by PRO's A2 distinguishing civic volunteering from junior professional work via a function test, crystallized the implementation clash and showed PRO could absorb CON's sharpest skepticism.
