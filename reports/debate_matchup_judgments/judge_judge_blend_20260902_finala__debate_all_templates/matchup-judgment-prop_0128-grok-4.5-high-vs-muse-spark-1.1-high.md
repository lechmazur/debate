### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): The debate turned on how to read "genuinely necessary for performance." A defended a task-content test (does the job itself require the degree's unique preparation), while B reframed necessity as material lift in expected performance/ramp/error rate across a hiring cohort, paired with a concrete economic mechanism: the degree is a free, auditable, third-party-verified filter, and removing it shifts costly, legally fragile screening burdens onto firms least able to bear them (especially small businesses), flooding the applicant funnel that the degree filter currently manages. This funnel-management and litigation-risk argument was introduced in Rebuttal 1 and never squarely answered by A, who only asserted that alternatives "scale without flooding funnels" without engaging the mechanism. B's Pressure Question forced A to state its necessity test explicitly, and B's follow-up rebuttal effectively showed that test risks making the resolution nearly tautological (almost no ordinary job is "impossible" without a bachelor's degree), a framing critique A never fully rehabilitated. A's case remained strong on mobility, exclusion, and degree inflation, and correctly warned that B's average-lift standard could justify almost any convenient proxy, but A's rebuttals mostly reasserted earlier points rather than dismantling B's cost/liability mechanism or the tautology charge. B's closing crystallized the clash more cleanly and left the more decisive unanswered pressure point standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 point that firms run custom assessments "on top of" the degree filter precisely because it keeps applicant volume manageable, combined with the Pressure Question forcing A to define "necessary," which exposed A's task-content test as verging on tautological — a critique A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing never fully answered.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully framing "performance" as a cohort-level business metric and decisively beating Side B on the practical mechanics of alternative screening. Side A proved that Side B's alternatives (paid trials, references) are legally risky and unscalable, turning Side B's impact by showing that removing the degree screen would harm entry-level hiring.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal effectively dismantled B's proposed alternatives by pointing out that references are "legally neutered" and paid trials create "wage-and-hour liability," neutralizing B's claim that non-degree screening requires only "modest effort."
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively controlling the definitional clash over "genuinely necessary." B effectively demonstrated that A's strict task-necessity standard rendered the motion tautological for non-licensed jobs, while B's cohort-performance standard reflected actual hiring realities. B's grounding in the practical, legal, and financial friction of alternative screening methods (funnel explosion, validation costs for small firms) neutralized A's normative argument about employer laziness and cost-shifting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where B dismantled A's claim that degrees "uniquely supply" knowledge by clarifying that the degree's true value is in cheap, third-party auditing of general skills, and exposed the massive burden shifted onto small firms if they had to build custom assessment clearinghouses.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases with proper format compliance, but PRO (A) landed the more decisive sequence. A's core mechanism—test the actual performance-relevant trait directly rather than paying for a noisy, expensive proxy—was sharp and consistently pressed, and A used concrete, plausible examples (Delta, IBM, Google stripping degree lines) to anchor it. CON (B) built a genuine counter-argument (degree-as-completion-signal distinct from coursework content, network-default risk of alternative screens), but the pressure question exchange proved costly: B's answer to Q2 conceded a principled line ("drop the requirement when substitutes deliver equal or better fit at lower cost without network default") that A then exploited in closing by showing this concession essentially restates the proposition itself. B's final closing never directly answered this "that IS the proposition" catch, instead repeating the volume/cost tradeoff framing without closing the contradiction. That unrebutted capture in the last exchangeable round gives A the cleaner, more resolved path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing move (A5.4) turning B's own pressure-question answer about when degree requirements become unjustified into an admission that mirrors the proposition itself—paired with B's failure to directly rebut this in the final closing—was the most decisive exchange of the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by systematically dismantling CON's central defense of the degree as a necessary, cheap 'first-cut' screen. PRO effectively argues that ATS systems and prior job tenure already provide scalable alternatives that do not exclude two-thirds of adults. Furthermore, PRO decisively flips CON's equity argument by pointing out that a four-year degree tracks wealth far more rigidly than the interview polish CON fears.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal (A2) directly answers CON's pressure question about scalable screens by listing specific ATS filters (tenure, tool proficiency), effectively collapsing CON's core efficiency argument.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built a much stronger analytical framework by collapsing Side B's distinction between "daily tasks" and "soft skills," arguing that if persistence is genuinely necessary for performance, it can be tested directly via work history rather than through a costly degree proxy. Side A's rhetoric ("paywall for persistence," "manufacturing scarcity") was sharp and memorable, and they effectively turned Side B's own concession about substitutes into a validation of the proposition. Side B relied too heavily on repeating that degrees are a "cheap volume screen" without adequately answering Side A's points about the hidden costs of vacancies and the scalability of modern ATS filters.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they directly answered the pressure questions by showing that ATS filters for experience and short skills checks provide a scalable, cheap first cut that tests persistence directly, effectively collapsing Side B's core efficiency argument.
