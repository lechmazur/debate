### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): B won by showing that A’s theory requires escalating risk until a collision or insurance crisis forces regulation, even though that triggering damage may be irreversible. A never adequately explained why the enormous assets already in orbit provide insufficient regulatory pressure during a temporary pause. Its connectivity case also relied on unsupported claims that completion would be prohibited, affordability was about to cross a threshold, and satellite capacity was the decisive bottleneck. B’s two clipped turns were execution costs, but its commons analysis, sequencing case, and comparative weighing remained substantially stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure questions and second rebuttal exposed the central contradiction in A’s case: deployment supposedly forces governance, yet years of deployment have produced neither clear liability for abandoned constellations nor the promised binding mechanism. A conceded the present gap and answered by demanding still greater imminent danger, enabling B’s decisive “disaster doing the legislating” response.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): PRO won by brilliantly dismantling CON's theory of change. When CON argued that continued deployment and the threat of imminent disaster are the only things that force regulatory agreement, PRO decisively pointed out that in an environment where failure is irreversible (orbital debris), relying on "imminent pain" guarantees the very permanent catastrophe the rules are meant to prevent.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing distillation of CON's Rebuttal 2 admission, pointing out that "You cannot use the crash as a bargaining chip when the crash is the thing you were bargaining to prevent."
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): PRO wins the central clash on sequencing and irreversibility. CON conceded the live governance gap — nobody pays for bankrupt abandoned shells — and when pressed in A3.1/A4.4 could name no enforceable mechanism or date, falling back to waiting for insurance collapse [B4.1]. PRO narrowed harm to new constellations only [A2.1][A4.1], breaking CON's 3bn-child symmetry, and showed cost-curve harm requires a long brittle chain while debris requires one launch. That comparison sticks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO in R2 exposing that skin-in-the-game produced no rule and that CON concedes 'nobody pays' for bankruptcy — turning CON's incentive argument into evidence for the moratorium.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by showing that launch conditions use the same governmental authority as a moratorium while preserving repeated leverage through tranche-by-tranche approval. Side B established serious risks but never adequately showed why a pause would outperform conditional licensing, especially given nonparticipants, an uncertain endpoint, and the comparatively safer characteristics of modern low-orbit satellites. Side A's two clipped turns reduced its margin, while Side B's closing relied too heavily on a late and insufficiently established claim that reform becomes impossible after deployment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's enforcement-symmetry turn was decisive: if regulators can deny launch permission, they can condition that permission on deorbiting, data sharing, insurance, and brightness controls. Its seatbelt analogy then exposed Side B's response as supporting launch standards more naturally than a global stand-down.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's mechanisms and effectively turning every analogy Side A introduced. Side B's argument that a moratorium abandons continuous regulatory leverage (via tranches) in favor of an unachievable global treaty proved decisively stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 turning Side A's seatbelt analogy by pointing out that society mandates seatbelts (conditional licensing) rather than banning driving until a global seatbelt treaty is ratified (a moratorium).
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): CON kept the decisive route live: a moratorium is enforced by the same national licensing as conditional licensing (symmetry), but spends the leverage that makes conditions work. A failed the endpoint/rogue-actor pressure — conceding no global enforcer and a collective sign-off that is a decades-long treaty veto — and never answered the tranches-as-turnstile point that preserves repeated leverage. PRO's 40x scale and astronomy harm were real but neutralized by drag, maneuverability, and existing tightening (5-year, ephemeris, brightness). Conditional licensing better fits the proposition's goals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R1/R2 symmetry and leverage: same clerk who can ban can condition, and a moratorium welds shut the turnstile that gives regulators repeated chances to tighten deorbit, ephemeris and brightness rules — plus exposure of indefinite veto and vacated shells going to worse actors.
