- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): CON won by showing the motion regulates the wrong lever: duration and fines do not catch insecure design, miss fly-by-night makers, and invite bricking and token compliance. CON’s comparative path (gate design rules + disclosure/released firmware) stayed cleaner despite two clipped rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s pressure on who judges a “real” patch and the bricking concession, then closing that PRO’s own enforcement theory makes the calendar irrelevant and requires other statutes.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON wins on internal critique: PRO conceded in [B2.3] that bricking at the end is compliant, turning the floor into a kill-date and e-waste mandate, and in [B4.1] redefined enforcement as after-breach remediation of exploitable flaws, making the calendar irrelevant. PRO's externality/timing claim survives but does not distinguish its remedy from fines-after-the-fact, while CON shows design-bans are checkable at import and fines land on reachable firms, not vanishing relabelers. A-clipping costs execution but sharp pressure Qs exposed the contradiction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Q2 on bricking [A3.2] + A4/A5 tracing B2.3 to B4.3: showing compliant kill-switch and need for second ban, collapsing PRO's consumer-protection narrative into scheduled e-waste.

A demonstrated that a duration mandate regulates the calendar rather than the code, failing to address the root causes of IoT insecurity like hard-coded passwords. When pressed on enforcement and end-of-life bricking, B relied on separate policies (banning bricking separately, post-breach adjudication of patch quality) to make the motion work. A effectively weaponized these concessions to show the motion itself is inadequate, creates perverse incentives like scheduled e-waste, and that B was ultimately defending a remediation-and-recall regime rather than the calendar mandate in the text. A's alternative of point-of-sale design bans and disclosure was more targeted and enforceable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal and closing exposing that B's enforcement theory (post-breach adjudication of whether a flaw stayed exploitable) rendered the "minimum period" calendar irrelevant, proving B was defending a different statute than the one in the motion.
