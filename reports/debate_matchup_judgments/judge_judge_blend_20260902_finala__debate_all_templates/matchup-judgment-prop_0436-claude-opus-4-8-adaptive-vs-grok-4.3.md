A most effectively exploited B's concession that default prominence steers orders, showing that filtering cannot fully cure the harm if users are influenced before they see cheaper alternatives.

B's answer that only convenience-focused users are steered was plausible but did not neutralize A's point that many users may prefer cheaper options without ever seeing them.

PRO demonstrated that CON could not hold both positions—if steering is real, users are harmed; if it is not, the modest limit is harmless. CON never escaped this dilemma, repeatedly reverting to a total-ban strawman rather than engaging PRO's narrow standard of capping excessive burying. PRO's rebuttals were sharper, the weighing was cleaner, and the closing crystallized the decisive clash more effectively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal [B4], which forced CON to choose between conceding that prominence steers users—thereby proving consumer harm—or denying it—thereby undermining CON's own claim that sponsorship revenue is essential to fund the platform.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON)): A wins narrowly because its core route stayed closest to the motion: heavy sponsored default placement exploits position bias, makes cheaper nearby independents harder to find, and can be limited without banning ads. B had sharper rhetoric and strong turns on revenue and newcomer visibility, but overclaimed that prominence must remain dominant to fund the service and never fully answered A’s budget-asymmetry point that chains can outbid independents. B’s “sort is one tap away” was also weakened by conceding position bias.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s best exchange was turning position bias against A by arguing that any default ranking still allocates scarce top slots and that sponsorship gives newcomers a lever. A’s decisive answer was that paid prominence is not an equal lever when low-margin independents are structurally outbid by chains, so the turn did not neutralize the motion’s cheaper-independent harm.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): PRO’s case mapped more directly to the motion: default sponsorship dominance systematically hides cheaper nearby independents from users, and moving to a distance-and-price default solves that harm without banning ads. CON’s economic and newcomer counter-stories were rhetorically sharp—especially the chicken-and-egg trap and the turn on position bias—but they relied on speculative fee hikes and generalized concerns about incumbents that strayed from the motion’s specific focus on users finding cheaper nearby options. PRO’s answers that proximity and price provide natural visibility, and that non-dominant ad inventory still monetizes, absorbed enough of the counter-pressure to keep the core harm story live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Turn 8 rebuttal turning position bias against PRO and framing the newcomer chicken-and-egg trap was the most damaging attack, but PRO’s answers in Turn 7 that proximity and price are natural levers—without banning ads—preserved PRO’s live route to victory.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively exploiting the internal contradictions in Side A's case. When Side A conceded that position bias is decisive, Side B correctly argued that A's proposed remedy (default sorting by distance/price) simply reassigns that decisive prominence to entrenched incumbents, creating a fatal chicken-and-egg trap for new independents who need visibility to get orders but need orders to build reviews. Side B also grounded its economic argument in the realistic mechanism that ad value tracks prominence, meaning demoting sponsors would force fee hikes rather than painlessly lowering ad rates. Side A's responses relied on idealized assumptions about marketplace economics and failed to provide a viable discovery lever for newcomers under its own model.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal masterfully turned Side A's concession on position bias against its own remedy, demonstrating that mandating a distance/price default doesn't eliminate favoritism but instead locks out newcomers via a cold-start review trap, while sharply clarifying the arithmetic reality of ad pricing.
