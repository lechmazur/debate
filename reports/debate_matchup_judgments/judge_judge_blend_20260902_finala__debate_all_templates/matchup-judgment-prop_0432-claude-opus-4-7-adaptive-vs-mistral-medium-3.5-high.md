### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by keeping the debate focused on the proposition’s actual default-plus-insurance/deposit model rather than an absolute mandate. A gave a clearer comparative mechanism: deposits for damage, nuisance rules for behavior, and opt-outs for genuine building-specific constraints. B’s strongest point was that pets create nonfinancial externalities like noise, allergens, and aggression, but A answered that these are regulated in analogous contexts rather than categorically banned, and B never fully explained why default allowance with exceptions cannot handle those risks. B leaned heavily on prevention and property rights, but often overstated harms and treated pet problems as inevitable rather than exceptional.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s decisive rebuttal was that B’s prevention standard would also justify banning many ordinary tenant-related nuisances, so B needed a pet-specific reason beyond “landlords can ban pets.” This undercut B’s externality case and reframed it as overbroad compared with A’s targeted deposit, behavior-rule, and opt-out approach.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B decisively won by establishing early that a "default" model allows for genuine exceptions, neutralizing Side A's offense regarding uniquely vulnerable buildings. Furthermore, Side B's comparison of pet externalities to human externalities forced Side A to concede that pets are banned primarily because they legally can be, a point Side B masterfully exploited to show Side A had abandoned the argument from harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing turn, which weaponized Side A's admission that pets are uniquely banned because they are "discretionary property," exposing it as an argument from legal permission rather than unique harm.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built a coherent, mechanism-backed case that withstood the full exchange. Its distinction between insurance claims and held deposits neutralized Side B's primary attack on financial recovery, while its "human analogy" pressure—noise, allergens, and liability from children, guests, and perfumes—forced Side B to retreat to a "property rights/permission" frame rather than a harm-based justification. Side A crystallized this concession effectively in closing, showing the default ban rests on power, not uniquely severe risk. Side B's market-equilibrium claim was undermined by tight-market analysis, its opening was clipped, and its later speeches repeated prevention rhetoric without resolving the comparative inconsistency.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON)): B won by making the central insurance mismatch decisive: deposits cover landlord property damage, while many pet harms fall on neighbors through noise, allergens, fear, waste, and enforcement burdens. A had a plausible fairness and housing-access case, but mostly repeated that these were ordinary behavioral issues and never adequately answered the third-party consent problem or B's allergen distinction. B also turned A's preserved-discretion claim into a strong dilemma: if landlords retain broad discretion, the default does little; if not, it overrides building-specific reasons no standardized policy can price.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was against A's claim that barking, messes, and allergens are just ordinary enforceable nuisances. B showed enforcement itself is a cost, children and cooking are not elective in the same way, and allergens are not behavioral because dander can persist across tenancies and harm neighbors who are not compensated.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A cleanly wins by establishing that the proposition's insurance mechanism fails to address the externalities pets impose on non-consenting neighbors. Side A systematically dismantling Side B's analogies—showing that children are unavoidable while pets are elective, and that mold is a remediable defect while the proposition protects pet dander—cemented a dominant victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's answer to Q1 regarding dust and mold: "Mold is a defect to be eliminated; under default-allow, dander becomes a protected feature."
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON)): CON successfully showed that the proposed insurance only covers landlord unit damage, leaving third-party neighbors to bear noise, allergen, and fear externalities without compensation or choice. PRO’s repeated analogy to children and cooking failed because pets are elective amenities, and PRO never answered the persistence of dander in HVAC and drywall across tenancies. Crucially, PRO conceded that landlords retain discretion for high-risk pets, which CON weaponized to show the default does no work in hard cases and only strips discretion in easy cases where building-specific factors (thin walls, existing allergies) matter. After the exchange, PRO’s fairness and economic benefits were neutralized by the unresolved third-party cost-shifting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s response to PRO’s concession on landlord discretion (B2.8/B4.6), showing that if landlords can still require extra coverage for large or high-risk pets, the standardized default is either superfluous or actively harmful by overriding legitimate building-specific reasons in lower-risk cases.
