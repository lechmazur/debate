### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO edged this debate on three decisive points. First, the Signal Protocol response in B4.1 was the most technically grounded move in the entire exchange: it showed that diverse encrypted messaging apps already interoperate without the originating platform controlling the recipient server, directly deflating CON's central E2EE-breakage claim with a concrete real-world example CON never squarely answered. CON's pivot—"once data reaches the third-party server, the host has zero control"—is a real concern, but it is a data governance argument, not the cryptographic E2EE argument CON had been leading with, and PRO correctly noted that audited compliance standards already address post-transit storage practices. Second, PRO's point that incumbents use closed architecture primarily to block rivals rather than to improve features went largely unanswered; CON's rebuttal to this was to repeat the lowest-common-denominator claim without engaging the specific mechanism PRO described. Third, PRO maintained the cleaner weighing frame throughout—speculative and manageable harms versus concrete, observable lock-in effects—and that frame remained live at the close. CON's strongest material was the catch-22 in Rebuttal 1, which was logically tight, and the software-dynamism distinction from telecom, which was valid. PRO handled the catch-22 adequately by pointing to existing app-store audit frameworks as a template. CON's argument that "advance notice proves paralysis" is somewhat circular: it presupposes platforms cannot evolve APIs incrementally, which PRO's 5G/open-banking counterexample undermines without full rebuttal. The execution cost of B's clipped Rebuttal 1 is real and accounts for the narrow margin; PRO still won because its A1 response in Rebuttal 2 carried the critical technical load the truncated speech left unfinished.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2 (B4.1): citing the Signal Protocol as a working example of diverse encrypted messaging apps already achieving E2EE interoperability without the originating platform governing the recipient server. This was the debate's most specific, technically grounded counter and directly dismantled CON's central claim that mandated interoperability inherently breaks end-to-end encryption, a claim CON never rebuilt after this answer.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1–A2.2 and A4.1–A4.3 on the catch-22 and weakest-link problem, which B never resolved without reasserting the same audited-third-party premise.
