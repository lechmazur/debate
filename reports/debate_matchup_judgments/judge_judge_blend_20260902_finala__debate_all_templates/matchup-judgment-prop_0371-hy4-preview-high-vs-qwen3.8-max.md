### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, competently structured cases, but PRO's "honest floor" framing survived the exchange better. PRO preemptively neutralized the operational-congestion objection (bins/crew capacity already exist regardless of who pays) and gave a concrete, administrable definition of the baseline when pressed, which blunted CON's demand for specificity. CON's strongest asset—the binding pre-purchase "standardized menu" alternative—was a clever move, but it still leaves a passenger who under-declares bag needs exposed to gate-time charges, meaning it re-creates rather than dissolves the captivity dynamic PRO identified; PRO flagged this weakness in advance (B4.5) and CON's closing never fully closed that gap, instead re-asserting the subsidy/who-pays framing without engaging the "unpredictable need" scenario. CON's pressure-question answer (A1) did coherently reconcile the apparent subsidy/induced-demand tension, which was a genuine rebuttal win for CON, but PRO's closing capitalized on the unresolved captivity issue in CON's alternative, giving PRO the cleaner final position. PRO's closing did slightly overstate CON's "concession," a minor grounding cost, but not enough to offset PRO's stronger overall trajectory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure question exposing the tension between CON's subsidy claim (light packers gain nothing) and induced-demand claim (light packers will start bringing bags), paired with PRO's follow-up that CON's binding-quote alternative still leaves undeclared-need travelers exposed to gate charges—this was the exchange that most damaged CON's live route to victory, even though CON's A1 answer partially defused the contradiction.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side B won by separating surprise pricing from optional baggage pricing and offering a binding pre-purchase quote that addressed captivity without compulsory bundling. Side A repeatedly assumed a roller bag was necessary for essentially every real trip, understated scarce-bin and boarding costs, and never justified making personal-item-only travelers pay for unused overhead rights. Its alleged contradiction between cross-subsidy and induced demand also failed because different travelers can respond differently at the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it explained that committed backpackers can still subsidize roller users while marginal travelers bring more bags when the price falls to zero, defeating Side A’s contradiction claim. It then sharpened the disclosure alternative into a standardized, binding bag selection with no surprise gate fee.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO wins because it successfully neutralized CON's core offensive while keeping its own "captive necessity" argument alive. PRO caught CON in a damaging tension: CON could not consistently claim that light packers would be forced to subsidize unused bin space while also fearing that free allowances would flood bins with induced demand. PRO leveraged this to undermine CON's operational and subsidy warnings. CON's binding-menu counterplan was clever and narrowed the gap, but PRO showed it still allowed airlines to lure travelers with an artificially low seat-only fare and recover revenue on the bag any real trip requires—turning transparency into itemized captivity rather than abolishing it. PRO's closing effectively crystallized this remaining clash and gave the cleaner reason to affirm the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal exposed that CON's subsidy claim and induced-demand fear were mutually undermining—if including the baseline changed behavior, it was not a pure subsidy, and if it did not, there was no congestion. PRO then used that opening to argue that only a baseline makes the advertised fare truthful, since CON's alternative still lets airlines hook passengers with a stripped base price.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON built a tighter, more consistent case around "choice vs. coercion" and exploited a genuine concession—PRO's admission that a no-bag traveler's fare "may rise slightly"—turning it into a recurring regressive-transfer indictment that PRO never fully neutralized. CON's pressure questions forced PRO into a vague, hand-wavy quantification of "slight" and exposed unresolved operational complexity on connecting itineraries (PRO's scaled-baseline fix effectively reinvented a checked-bag service, which CON highlighted as adding complexity rather than resolving it). CON also directly undercut PRO's foundational "asymmetric information" premise by noting airlines already publish dimensions and fees, allowing comparison—a point PRO's closing never squarely answered. PRO's opening was strong (drip pricing, dignity/operations, honest-competition framing), and its rebuttals on regressivity ("current fees hurt vulnerable travelers more") were reasonable counter-narratives, but PRO's answers to CON's pressure questions were softer than CON's answers to PRO's, and PRO's closing largely reasserted normative framing rather than repairing the concession-driven damage. Both sides avoided fake citations and stayed within plausible mechanisms, though CON's "roughly $30" figure was mildly brittle precision, balanced by PRO's own honest hedging ("no universal exact figure"). Overall CON progressed the clash more effectively and left PRO's core comparative claims under more pressure at the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 exploitation of PRO's "fare may rise slightly" concession, sharpened into a concrete quantification demand in Pressure Questions (Q1), which PRO could only answer with an unquantified standard in Rebuttal 2 (A1), leaving the regressive-transfer charge largely unrebutted.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B won the central tradeoff: the mandate improves standardization only by making light packers pay for overhead-bag access they may not use. Side A conceded that their fares would rise but never convincingly explained why this transfer was necessary rather than addressing transparency through disclosure and comparison. Side B also plausibly challenged A’s operational claim by noting that included bags can increase bin demand. B’s repeated $30 figure was unsupported, but A’s claims that carry-on costs are near-universal, hidden, and unpredictable were likewise insufficiently established.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was pressing A’s concession that backpack-only travelers would pay more. A disputed the unsupported $30 estimate but answered that costs would be spread across passengers, effectively confirming B’s subsidy argument without proving that transparency required eliminating the cheaper unbundled option.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO wins by establishing that comparability and transparency matter more than preserving a stripped $49 fare. CON's best argument—the budget traveler—was neutralized by PRO's effective counter that ordinary trips require a carry-on and that unpredictable gate fees fall hardest on the same budget travelers. PRO offered a cleaner operational solution for connecting itineraries and maintained the honesty frame through the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's connecting-itinerary pressure question (A4.3), where PRO reframed the gate-check from a random lottery into a prepaid, guaranteed service right with prioritized return, exposing that CON's own system recreates the same physical constraints without the uniformity.
