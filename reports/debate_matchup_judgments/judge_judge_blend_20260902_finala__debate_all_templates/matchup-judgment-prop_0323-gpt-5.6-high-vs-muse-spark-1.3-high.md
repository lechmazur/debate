### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly and stayed on the stated motion, but Side A's central thread—"hiding the number doesn't remove the ranking, it just removes the label letting users audit it"—survived the exchange better than PRO's rebuttals to it. PRO's strongest move was reframing visible counts as a self-reinforcing bandwagon mechanism rather than genuine information, and this landed as a real critique of naive "wisdom of crowds" framing. But PRO never fully answered CON's recurring point that platforms will continue ranking by hidden engagement regardless of the default, so the proposal trades a checkable public signal for an unaccountable private one, arguably making manipulation less visible rather than eliminating it. In the Pressure Questions exchange, both sides posed sharp questions, but CON's A2 answer (opt-in destroys "common knowledge" because a signal must be public to function as shared context) more directly neutralized PRO's opt-in escape hatch than PRO's response neutralized CON's transparency/accountability claim. PRO's rebuttal that raw counts don't reveal impressions or authenticity was a fair point, but CON's polling analogy (imperfect signals still function without full data) blunted it reasonably well. Closings crystallized the clash effectively on both sides, but CON's final "labels vs. no labels, sort continues either way" framing was the cleaner, more memorable resolution of the core disagreement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2 answer to PRO's pressure question — arguing that a signal only works as common knowledge if it's public, so an opt-in cannot preserve the crowd-check PRO claims to value — most damaged PRO's key harm-reduction/opt-in architecture.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the live clash on default asymmetry and preserved functions: under PRO, liking, discovery, private analytics, and opt-in totals all remain while the compulsory public scoreboard and herd cue go; under CON, passive users absorb a status ranking unless they opt out. A’s mechanism story (counts manufacture demand via self-reinforcing visibility) and repeated demolition of CON’s accountability claim (raw totals lack impressions/authenticity baselines, so “37 likes” cannot reliably embarrass the algorithm and often legitimize fakery) did more decisive work than B’s useful-but-imperfect common-knowledge defense. B had a real live route on public checks and newcomer proof, and answered pressure directly, but never neutralized A’s choice-architecture weighing that defaults stick and the less coercive reversible baseline is therefore superior. Closings crystallized rather than reinvented; A’s was cleaner on the deciding comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 (esp. A1/A2): private disappointment ≠ public humiliation badge, and without impressions a raw count never actually audited the algorithm—collapsing B’s main accountability/newcomer route while keeping the default-asymmetry win condition intact.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully framing the debate around choice architecture and effectively neutralizing Side A's strongest argument—that public counts provide algorithmic accountability and expose astroturfing. Side B correctly points out that without impression data, a low like count cannot reliably prove algorithmic promotion, and that high fake counts serve as a fraudster's billboard for casual scrollers. Side B also maintains a cleaner comparative on the default mechanism, emphasizing that an opt-in preserves all underlying functions while removing compulsory public ranking. Side A's rhetoric was strong, particularly the "behind glass" metaphor, but Side B's logical dismantling of the accountability mechanism and harm reduction framing secured the victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantles Side A's algorithmic accountability argument by explaining that a raw count without impression data cannot reliably expose suppression or promotion, and that removing the visible herd cue forces content to persuade on its own merits.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued well, but A's central throughline — that hiding public counts changes visibility, not power, since platforms and algorithms still count and rank everything — went largely unrebutted except partially. A's Pressure Question Q2 exposed a genuine internal tension in B's "dual control" mechanism: if viewers default counts off, a newcomer's later opt-in to display high numbers provides no social proof anyway, undercutting B's own claim that opt-in protects newcomers while preserving their upside. B's rebuttal 2 explained the dual-control system but never squarely resolved this contradiction, and its closing reasserted the "tax on starting out" framing without closing the logical gap. A also successfully reframed B's "break the chain" claim as only partial, since feed placement, comments, and reputation still generate cascades — meaning B's fix is incomplete while its cost (loss of a legible collective signal) is certain. B's case remained emotionally resonant and rhetorically vivid throughout, but the decisive comparative dilemma A built and pressed was never fully neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question Q2 (viewer vs. creator control dilemma) and its follow-through in Rebuttal 2 (A4.2), which exposed that B's opt-in-once-earned mechanism is self-defeating if viewers default to hidden counts, was the most decisive exchange and went unresolved by B.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the motion live: visible-by-default is a forced public scoreboard that taxes newcomers and fuels bandwagon cascades, while hidden-by-default still preserves likes, private analytics, and other cues without drafting everyone into rank. B landed real pressure that hiding is only partial and can reduce inspectability, but overweighted “fix the algo instead” and never fully neutralized A’s cleaner burden shift on who pays for always-on display.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 A1/A2: bandwagon loop break plus dual viewer/creator control answered B’s pressure without collapsing into pure opt-in theater.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins narrowly by successfully arguing that hiding metrics by default is a cosmetic reform that obscures algorithmic influence without stopping it. While Side B (PRO) delivered highly memorable rhetoric about the "public zero" and the burden on newcomers, Side A decisively dismantled PRO's "dual control" mechanism in Rebuttal 2. A showed that selective opt-in turns metrics into advertisements for winners and strips ordinary users of transparent social context, meaning the absence of a number simply becomes a new negative signal. Side A's framing of "accountable metrics over invisible influence" provided a cleaner comparative path to victory, proving that the proposition treats the visible symptom rather than the underlying optimization disease.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which sharply dismantled PRO's "dual control" answer by demonstrating that selective opt-in creates a dynamic where displayed numbers become advertisements for winners while hidden numbers become uninterpretable, effectively neutralizing PRO's solution to the newcomer dilemma.
