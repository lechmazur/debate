- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): CON (Side A) won the three most consequential clashes. PRO's closing never engaged this mechanism. Second, the Pell Grants harm argument—that massive discharge losses force cuts to needs-based aid, directly harming the low-income borrowers PRO claims to champion—was raised in the opening, developed in both rebuttals, and crystallized as a "regressive wealth transfer" in the closing. PRO left it entirely unanswered across multiple rounds, a significant execution cost. Third, the lender-accountability pillar of PRO's case collapsed under CON's durable point that 90% of student debt is federal with no credit underwriting, so discharge cannot fix a flaw that doesn't exist for most loans. PRO's A2 response (systemic accountability via funding decisions) was thinner than the original attack and never recovered the argument. PRO's strongest counter—that courts already handle means-testing for every other consumer debt—was well-grounded and partially answered CON's definitional-vagueness charge, but CON pivoted effectively to "we already have targeted relief, so the motion only expands to mid-income strategic defaulters." PRO's "double standard" and dignity framing had rhetorical force but depended on a problem that CON showed is already substantially addressed for the most distressed borrowers. CON's closing crystallized the comparison more sharply and left PRO with fewer live routes to victory.

PRO's subsequent closing ignored this entirely, confirming it as an unanswered blow that collapsed PRO's core warrant for the motion being necessary.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins a competitive but clear exchange on the back of two moves that PRO never fully neutralized. First, the TPD redundancy argument in A4 created a genuine dilemma: if PRO's proposal really is confined to the truly permanently unable, administrative Total and Permanent Disability discharges already cover most of that category outside bankruptcy; if the proposal is broader, CON's moral-hazard and strategic-underemployment concerns become live. PRO's closing response—that TPD has gaps for displaced workers and caregivers—was a fair counter but came late and didn't defuse the strategic-underemployment concern for the non-disabled subset. Second, CON's Fannie Mae/FHA debt-to-income claim in A4.1, that mortgage underwriters use the actual $0 IDR payment rather than the full balance, directly addressed PRO's central practical-harm argument about housing access. PRO's closing restated the DTI-blocking claim without engaging this rebuttal, leaving a significant hole. PRO's strongest unrebutted argument—that the government already absorbs losses on uncollectible defaulted debt, so discharge merely ends administrative waste at no new taxpayer cost—was raised briefly but never pressed hard enough to force a CON response, and was therefore underweighted. PRO's human examples were vivid and the framing around bankruptcy's core purpose was effective, keeping the debate competitive, but on the decisive clashes CON's technical moves survived better into the final round.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.3 TPD redundancy argument: if PRO's narrow carve-out truly targets only the permanently unable, federal administrative disability discharges already exist for that population without requiring bankruptcy. This created a real dilemma PRO only partially answered in the closing, and CON combined it effectively with the Fannie Mae/FHA DTI counter, leaving PRO's two main pillars (taxpayer harm and housing harm) materially weakened.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate. A effectively narrowed the proposal to genuinely long-term inability and damaged the degree-repossession and generic moral hazard arguments. But B made the cleaner comparative case that IDR and disability discharge already cover the hardest cases without bankruptcy's credit harm, while broader discharge standards create subjectivity and risk to open-access lending. A's repeated debt-to-income claim was directly challenged and not convincingly repaired.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was the narrow-or-broad double bind: if A means permanent disability or terminal hardship, existing administrative discharge is less harmful than bankruptcy; if A means caregivers, industry collapse, or projected inability, the standard becomes subjective and invites systemic risk.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side B wins by successfully neutralizing PRO's core attacks on the status quo and exposing a structural weakness in PRO's proposal. B's rebuttal that major mortgage underwriters calculate DTI using the $0 IDR payment rather than the total loan balance directly undercut PRO's claim that IDR traps borrowers by ruining housing access. B also effectively leveraged the existing Total and Permanent Disability (TPD) discharge program to show that PRO's narrowest, most sympathetic examples are already covered without bankruptcy's credit damage, forcing PRO into a dilemma: either the plan is redundant or it opens a subjective loophole that invites moral hazard. While A provided compassionate examples and plausible administrative benchmarks, B's comparative weighing on credit impact, systemic risk to open-access lending, and the redundancy contradiction gave a cleaner, more decisive path to victory. B's responses to pressure questions were sharper and better grounded in the financial mechanics of lending and credit reporting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to PRO's claim that IDR ruins debt-to-income (DTI) ratios was the most decisive exchange. B directly countered with the specific underwriting practice that Fannie Mae and the FHA use the actual $0 monthly IDR payment for DTI calculations, not the total balance. This effectively dismantled PRO's argument that IDR is a housing trap, while reinforcing B's broader point that bankruptcy inflicts far worse, long-term credit damage than a $0/month current loan.
