### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): B wins a clear but competitive debate. A’s best material was the overbreadth challenge to “ultra-processed” foods and the regressive shame/access argument, and A expressed those points memorably. But B gave the stronger comparative reason to prefer warning labels over relying mainly on campaigns: labels operate at the point of purchase, cut through marketing and low nutrition literacy, preserve choice, and address information asymmetry in a way education campaigns alone do not. A’s education alternative never fully answered why campaigns can overcome industry advertising or rushed shopping decisions, and the access critique applied almost as much to education as to labels.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive rebuttal was that warnings complement or operationalize education at the exact moment consumer choice campaigns fail, while not banning products. A’s sharpest reply was that B narrowed the motion away from broad UPFs, but B’s point-of-purchase comparison remained the cleaner route to the motion.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by consistently exposing the proposal's overbroad category of UPFs (including staples), the regressive guilt inflicted on budget-constrained shoppers without viable alternatives, and the tobacco analogy's incompatibility with food's contextual needs. Side B narrowed scope late and left the "no alternatives" and nuance contradictions inadequately addressed, giving A the clearer route to victory across rebuttals and closings.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 on tobacco-style warnings contradicting dose-specific moderation and failing to address real-world purchase constraints for the poor.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by consistently exposing a core contradiction in Side B's mechanism: the claim that a glaring, tobacco-style hazard warning can simultaneously convey dose-specific nuance and teach moderation. Side A grounded this clash in the realistic psychology of the checkout line, effectively arguing that a prominent warning on affordable, shelf-stable staples functions as a regressive psychological tax rather than empowerment, especially when no viable alternatives exist. While Side B made a strong case about industry marketing asymmetry and the point-of-purchase gap, they struggled to resolve the tension between visibility and nuance, and their attempt to narrow the motion's broad UPF category to specific thresholds conceded ground on scope. Side A's rebuttals were sharper, their pressure questions more damaging, and their closing crystallized the comparative choice more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2 exchange targeting the tobacco analogy and the shame mechanism. By pointing out that tobacco warnings are designed to eradicate nuance and drive consumption to zero, Side A forced Side B into an unresolved contradiction: claiming their labels would be both glaringly prominent and capable of communicating dose-specific moderation. Side A's grounding of this in the checkout-line reality (noting the parent, not the CEO, faces the label) decisively shifted the weighing on low-income impact.
