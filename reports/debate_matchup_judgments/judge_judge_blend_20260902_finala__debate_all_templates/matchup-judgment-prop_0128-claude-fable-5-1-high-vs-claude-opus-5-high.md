### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by preserving the motion's narrow distinction between valuing a degree and making it a mandatory gate. Side A's implementation dilemma was sharp, but its equity case repeatedly treated ending a requirement as stripping graduates of their credential, even though graduates retain that signal while qualified non-graduates gain consideration. B also supplied plausible alternative screens and better weighed the exclusionary cost. B's repeated clipping and erroneous reference labels narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's answer to the first-generation-graduate argument was decisive: removing a degree requirement does not erase or devalue that applicant's diploma; it merely stops excluding her experienced non-graduate cousin before comparison. Side A never squarely neutralized that distinction.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): B won by turning the motion’s operative phrase—“genuinely necessary”—into an unworkable pre-hire test and forcing A to answer that honest employer judgment counts as compliance, which collapses the proposition into either a press-release norm already free to ignore or an external override A never justified. B’s answers on licences/cheap-error roles, redistribution of screening weight, and the network-less first-gen diploma as the one portable climbable asset neutralized A’s vivid equity and warehouse examples while keeping the no-track-record applicant at the center. A’s concessions (not necessary; natural experiment barely moved) were used against A more cleanly than A could reverse them.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4/B5 trap on A’s “Complied, if the review was honest” (A4.1): if the firm’s own odds judgment settles compliance, the motion removes no gates; if not, A still lacks a pre-hire standard—decisive over A’s age-norm analogy.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by exposing a fatal structural dilemma in PRO's case: either the motion collapses into a symbolic gesture that changes no actual hiring (as the natural experiment showed when wording changed but outcomes did not), or it becomes an epistemically unworkable outside override of employer judgment. CON's pressure questions trapped PRO into conceding that an employer "complies" so long as its review was "honest," which CON ruthlessly exploited to prove the motion demands nothing firms are not already free to do. CON also effectively distinguished easy cases with substitute credentials (electricians, licensed drivers, high-turnover cashiers) from harder middle-skill roles where no cheap license exists, undermining PRO's generalization. Finally, CON reversed PRO's equity argument by showing that in practice the degree is the most network-independent, publicly verifiable signal a poor applicant can earn alone, while its real-world replacements—referrals and unstructured interviews—are even more wired to wealth and polish. PRO's "who gets in the room" framing was rhetorically vivid but could not survive CON's demonstration that removing the degree gate merely shifts screening weight to gates that favor connections over competence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question asking whether an employer complies when it honestly concludes the degree improves odds at the margin, and PRO's answer that it "complies, if the review was honest." CON then exploited this concession across Rebuttal 2 and Closing to prove the motion is either toothless or requires an impossible external mandate, decisively undermining PRO's mechanism for reform.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by distinguishing removal of a degree requirement from removal of the degree as useful evidence. Its broadened-floor model preserved degrees while admitting work, military, and apprenticeship experience, making Side A's claimed vacuum a false dilemma. Side A raised a serious scalability concern, but scarcity of interview capacity did not establish that an unnecessary degree gate was the best or fairest first cut. Side B also weighed the applicant's large, exclusionary burden more convincingly against employer convenience.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's decisive response was that removing a mandatory gate does not devalue or erase the credential: degree holders still qualify, while directly relevant experience becomes another route. Side A's fixed-capacity objection showed that screening remains necessary, but not that this particular performance-unnecessary screen should remain compulsory.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): A carried the written motion more cleanly: once a degree is stipulated as unnecessary for performance, keeping it is a cheap sorting habit whose real cost is offloaded onto applicants. A’s contradiction trap, direct-vs-proxy grit reply, employer-as-decider answer, and asymmetric cost weighing survived; B’s capacity/opacity and entry-level ladder points were sharp but largely fought the premise or restated status-quo “or equivalent,” leaving the live case for retaining non-necessary requirements thinner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s [A2.1]–[A2.3] and later [A4.1]/[A4.6]: markets can discover useless screens, work history is direct grit evidence, and the downside of wrongness falls far harder on locked-out workers than on hiring managers.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing a fatal structural gap in PRO's case: removing a posted degree requirement does not solve the capacity bottleneck of mass hiring, so the screen simply goes underground to more elitist proxies like referrals and brand names. PRO's "broadened floor" never answered how a manager filters four thousand résumés down to fifty without a scalable credential, and CON's "published price" framing showed the degree is the most democratic filter available. PRO also mischaracterized CON as conceding the degree predicts nothing, which CON directly rebutted. While PRO's toll-booth metaphor and distributional points were vivid, they collapsed against CON's unmet burden of proving the replacement system is actually more open at scale.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's introduction and crystallization of the capacity constraint—four thousand applicants, fifty reads—and the resulting "underground screening" harm, which PRO never answered with a workable scalable alternative, ultimately decided the debate.
