### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): The decisive exchange was CON's museum pressure question, which forced PRO to concede that an interactive Q&A trained on a Holocaust survivor's own recorded testimony would be illegal under the motion. That bullet-bite let CON reframe the whole debate as "criminalizing teaching and grief tech over an unfiled form," a framing PRO never fully defused (its "quoting vs. improvising" and "false continuation" distinctions were coherent but did not blunt the intuitive cost of banning a paradigmatic sympathetic use). CON also cleanly closed PRO's strongest horror-story lane by committing, in direct answer to PRO's own pressure question, to an outright ban on commercial sales-bots regardless of estate consent — undercutting PRO's claim that the estate-proxy model inevitably produces branded ghosts. PRO's core thesis (death forecloses consent, so no later signature can authorize a speaking double) stayed logically intact throughout and was argued with some rhetorical force, but PRO's closing chose to restate that thesis rather than directly rehabilitate the museum concession, leaving CON's most damaging line of attack unanswered at the point where it mattered most. CON's case also offered a more concrete, workable policy synthesis (estate-consent for private/non-commercial use, categorical ban on commercial impersonation, mandatory labeling), which made the tradeoffs easier to weigh. A minor execution flaw (CON's Rebuttal 1 was clipped for length) tempers but does not erase this edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q1 pressure question forcing PRO to admit that a labeled, non-commercial Holocaust survivor Q&A would be illegal under the motion, followed by CON's closing use of that concession to frame the ban as "criminalizing teaching."
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): B won a clear but competitive debate by sustaining the crucial distinction between preserving authentic material and generating new speech in the deceased person's voice. A made the museum and family-use costs vivid, but never fully answered why estate consent can authorize unlimited new impersonated speech. A also shifted from allowing commercial use with estate approval in A2.7 to claiming an outright commercial ban in A4.1, weakening its alternative framework.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response to the museum example was decisive: searchable testimony preserves the survivor's actual words, while a mimic invents answers, emotions, and opinions the survivor never supplied. That directly reduced A's strongest example from preserved testimony to unauthorized generated performance.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by demonstrating that Side B's strict opt-in requirement would practically ban valuable educational tools (like interactive historical archives) and private grief tech, a cost Side B explicitly accepts but Side A successfully frames as an unacceptable overreach. Side A's alternative framework—allowing estate-approved, non-commercial remembrance while strictly banning commercial impersonation—neatly resolves the horror cases without destroying beneficial uses. Side B's concession that an interactive Holocaust survivor exhibit would be illegal under their rule was a decisive strategic error that cemented Side A's victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they cleanly sidestep the commercial sales-bot pressure question by banning it outright, and then hammer Side B's admission that an interactive Holocaust museum exhibit would violate their proposed law, framing it as "criminalizing teaching."

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly and executed the pressure-question format properly, but B established and sustained the more decisive substantive point: that existing legal tools (copyright, publicity rights, estate ownership of physical records) do not reach the trained model/weights themselves, especially when a company already has lawful ToS access to the data. A's answer to this (Q1) — "contract and successor control... publicity and false-endorsement limits" — gestured at solutions but never concretely explained how those tools bind a company that already possesses lawful access, leaving B's "laundering a life into weights" argument largely unanswered. B also pressed effectively on why disclosure/labeling doesn't cure the harm of a synthetic "I" issuing first-person statements, forcing A into a training/deployment distinction that is conceptually plausible but sits awkwardly against a motion specifically about training. A's strongest material — the widow/Lincoln overbreadth argument and the cruelty of a form the dead of 2015 could never sign — was rhetorically vivid and forced B into biting a real bullet ("yes, that's a feature"), but B's bullet-biting was at least a coherent, principled answer (ownership of letters isn't ownership of a first-person right to speak forever), whereas A never closed its own loophole about what stops a company that already holds the data. On balance B's case was tighter and better defended under direct questioning, giving it a clear, if not overwhelming, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q1 pressure question (Turn 5) exposing that ToS-based lawful access to data isn't blocked by copyright/estate/publicity law, and A's incomplete answer in Turn 8 relying on vague "contract and successor control" without explaining how it overrides existing lawful access.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that the proposition targets training too broadly when the central harms arise from deceptive or abusive deployment. Side B compellingly defended posthumous autonomy and exposed gaps in existing protections, but its Gmail example did not justify banning labeled memorials or historical reconstructions from published material. Its refusal to distinguish those cases left Side A with the cleaner, more tailored policy comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it used Side B’s concessions on the widow and Lincoln to show that framing makes analogous impersonation permissible, yet the proposed rule would still prohibit chatbot training solely because an impossible pre-death checkbox was absent.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully defending the core philosophical premise that first-person agency cannot be transferred to heirs or assumed by silence, effectively biting the bullet on Side A's "widow" and "Lincoln" lockout examples. Side B also decisively countered Side A's "regulate deployment" alternative by pointing out the impossibility of policing infinite future prompts and the inadequacy of existing estate laws against ToS-granted data access. Side A's arguments were intuitive but ultimately failed to overcome Side B's tight causal and philosophical framing of the weights themselves as the violation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to the pressure questions, particularly the sharp distinction between an actor saying "I am playing Lincoln" and a bot saying "I am Lincoln," and the definitive rejection of the idea that owning physical letters grants the right to generate new first-person speech.
