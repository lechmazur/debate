- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by exposing the missing step in A's case: an important interest in genetic origins does not automatically establish an absolute power to compel another person's identity, especially retroactively. B also effectively answered that leaky anonymity does not itself justify state-mandated disclosure. A's refusal to accept any supply threshold made its position principled but insufficiently balanced.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response to A's anonymity-collapse argument was decisive: the risk of private discovery does not erase the separate harm or principle involved when the state officially compels disclosure. B then linked this to A's unwilling-donor test, showing that A had established the mechanism of enforcement without adequately justifying its absolute priority.

A effectively used the reality of consumer DNA testing to show anonymity is a fiction and leveraged B's concession that future donation should be open to narrow the dispute to current adults, whom B could offer no principled reason to permanently exclude. B's reliance and privacy objections remained live but could not overcome A's framing that an identity right must bind unwilling parties, not merely request their goodwill.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where A seized on B's answer to the pressure question—admitting that under a voluntary registry the adult may not learn the donor's name—as a structural concession that CON's entire alternative fails at the exact door that matters, then crystallized the debate around the unwilling donor.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by centering the enforceable autonomy claim of actual adults and showing that voluntary registries do not provide the identifying right at issue. Side B offered a vivid supply and future-existence comparison, but repeatedly treated the rule as purely prospective despite A explicitly applying it wherever records exist, including under older anonymity promises. B also claimed its consent-based model delivered the same good while conceding identification remained donor-dependent.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest response was that no mechanism must force reluctant donors to participate: the proposition expressly accepts a smaller supply, while requiring truthful ex ante terms. This exposed B’s supply-collapse argument as a cost rather than a direct answer to why donor preference should defeat the adult’s right.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins because it successfully defends the core right to identifying information as a matter of adult autonomy that cannot be made contingent on donor consent or market convenience. While Side B landed sharp rebuttals on supply effects and import circumvention, the motion explicitly brackets supply loss as an acceptable cost. Side B’s alternative of voluntary registries and medical history disclosure ultimately offers a lesser good that evades the proposition’s demand for a legal right to identity, and Side A convincingly showed that a right dependent on the concealer’s permission is no right at all. Side B’s prospective-births framing could not overcome the motion’s framing nor fully neutralize Side A’s claim that actual adults should not bear permanent state-enforced ignorance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 answer to Side B’s pressure questions, explicitly conceding that no mechanism forces reluctant donors and that the system may become smaller, but insisting that truthful consent and enforceable rights matter more than maximizing donation volume—directly embracing the motion’s trade-off and shifting the debate back to principle.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): PRO wins because the proposition explicitly says "even if anonymity would reduce donation supply," which absorbs CON's core supply/existence cost. PRO embraces a smaller but legitimate system (A4.1-A4.2) and frames identifying info as a non-waivable interest that cannot depend on donor permission, while CON's registry alternative leaves the right contingent. CON's strongest prospective/import pressure is powerful but does not displace the rights claim baked into the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.3-B2.4 import argument that a domestic mandate just exports anonymity so donor-conceived children remain unable to identify donors, producing pure loss — met by A4.3 licensing condition and B4.5 enforcement skepticism.
