- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): A won a close, high-quality clash by preserving distinct roles for consent and substantive limits. B compellingly raised necessary audits and hidden retaliation, but repeatedly conflated scoped file-access records with keystroke capture and treated consent as though it overrode strict limits. A showed that limits can prohibit dragnets while opt-in prevents bounded but optional surveillance from becoming a condition of employment. B's two clipped turns were an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's first rebuttal most effectively broke B's dichotomy: a scoped fraud log need not be a home keylogger, strict limits govern what may never be collected, and consent governs whether otherwise bounded, optional intrusion can be imposed.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by making consent do no work. PRO conceded forbidden feeds are banned by limits alone and conceded a scoped fraud log is collected even if the trainee declines [B4.1], abandoning "only with opt-in." A mapped the motion to: forbidden = consent irrelevant, necessary = consent irrelevant, leaving opt-in only for monitoring that fails necessity - a market where the poorest sell privacy first, plus an unenforceable loyalty-test. A answered Q1 with no living-room feed, only narrow face-checks defined by law/collective agreement, while B never named jobs that get home video without consent.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Con A2/A4 showing PRO's bank-log endorsement violates its own "only with opt-in" text, plus that consent's middle-tier is unjustified surveillance for sale.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by systematically dismantling the structural logic of Side B's defense. A effectively demonstrates that "opt-in consent" either acts as a de facto ban (if refusal is truly costless) or a coercive tool (if it isn't), leaving consent to merely price unjustified surveillance for the most desperate workers. Side B struggles to defend against the "loyalty test" of soft retaliation, conceding that soft favoritism exists and offering weak grievance mechanisms that fail to capture unwritten assignment biases. While Side A suffered minor execution costs from being clipped at the very end of two early speeches, the core arguments were intact, and A's superior logical framing, sharper rebuttals, and decisive exploitation of B's concessions in the later rounds secure a clear victory.
