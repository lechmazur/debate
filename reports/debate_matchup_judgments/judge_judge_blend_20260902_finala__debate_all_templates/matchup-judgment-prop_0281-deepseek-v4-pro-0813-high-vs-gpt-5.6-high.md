### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued well, but B controlled the decisive clash over whether a workable benchmark exists. B's pressure questions forced A to concede that its "functional necessity" and "needless channel switching" standards implicitly require comparing cancellation to the enrollment channel actually used—A's own answers (A4.1's "reasonably completed online," A4.3's "genuinely required") smuggle in exactly the entry-based comparison A claims to reject. B exploited this directly in closing, showing A's standard either collapses into parity or offers no fixed ex ante test enforceable before small charges accrue. A's strongest point—that entry and exit are not functionally symmetric (family plans, data loss, financed equipment)—was real but B blunted it by conceding authentication, confirmation, and skippable warnings are compatible with parity, leaving A's safety concerns addressed rather than fatal. A's closing repeats that "parity is either literal (bad) or flexible (collapses into our standard)" but never explains why a firm-chosen-baseline parity rule with allowed functional exceptions is actually indistinguishable from mandating artificial sameness; B's rebuttal that the baseline is "the enrollment funnel the firm itself chose" plus allowed exceptions is a workable ex ante test that A never fully dismantles. B also better answered the manipulation-of-baseline worry (acquisition incentives punish deliberately slow sign-up) even if A's rebuttal that regulation changes incentives has some force. Overall B's progression across rounds was tighter and more responsive to the specific pressure it created, giving it a clear but not dominant edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Questions (Q1) forcing A to admit that its own "needless channel switching" ban is itself an entry-vs-exit comparison, which A's subsequent answer (A4.1) could not cleanly escape without effectively adopting a parity-style benchmark.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by demonstrating that cancellation inherently carries different functional requirements than enrollment, making strict parity an inappropriate regulatory tool. Side A successfully shows that Side B's necessary exceptions (allowances for warnings and confirmations) cause the parity rule to collapse into the very functional necessity standard Side A advocates.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal decisively damaged the PRO case by showing that Side B's specific allowance for "a single confirmation" was arbitrary and failed to address the complex reality of unwinding shared lines or financed equipment.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side A successfully defended the parity standard by defining it as a channel and step benchmark based on the firm's own sign-up funnel, effectively neutralizing Side B's argument that parity ignores the unique risks of cancellation. Side A also won the enforcement clash by demonstrating that Side B's "functional necessity" standard lacks a workable ex ante baseline for low-dollar subscriptions, whereas parity provides a clear, bright-line rule. Side B's argument that PRO's exceptions collapse into CON's standard was rhetorically sharp, but PRO's framing of parity as the necessary benchmark to make functional tests enforceable was more persuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal in Turn 7 and Closing, where it exposed that Side B's "functional necessity" test (e.g., whether a task can be completed online) inherently relies on a parity comparison to the available online route, thereby proving that parity is the necessary benchmark for any workable regulation.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but PRO built the more resilient case. PRO's central mechanism (post-purchase discovery of friction, technically-authorized-but-engineered delay, consent that is easy to give but costly to withdraw) survived CON's attacks better than CON's "deception-only" framework survived PRO's pressure. The decisive moment was CON's answer to Q1 in Rebuttal 2, conceding that mandatory calls/sales scripts are unjustified when an online flow could suffice — PRO correctly seized on this in closing as proof that existing deception/unfairness law was not already handling the harm, undercutting CON's core "existing law suffices" pillar. CON's strongest counter — that PRO's "functional parity" standard collapses into discretionary interface review — was real and well-pressed, but CON's own rebuttal proposed an equally discretionary "legitimate purpose vs. cost-raising" test, which PRO exploited in closing by noting CON's alternative lacks any comparative baseline while PRO's (enrollment burden as anchor) at least offers one. CON's backfire argument (fewer trials, higher prices, entrenched incumbents) was asserted with plausible mechanism but never substantiated beyond assertion, and PRO's "visible price vs. hidden friction" rejoinder blunted it. Both used the Q&A and A1/A2 format correctly; PRO's opening being clipped is a minor execution cost but did not meaningfully weaken its case given the substance carried forward. Overall PRO narrowed the clash more effectively and left CON's central concession unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question forcing CON to concede that mandatory phone calls/sales scripts are unjustified obstruction (A1 in Rebuttal 2), which PRO then leveraged in closing as the decisive admission that deception-only enforcement was insufficient, while CON's own proposed "legitimate purpose" test was shown to lack the comparative baseline PRO's rule supplies.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully trapping Side A into conceding the core premise of the debate. Side B exposes that Side A's alternative "unfairness" standard requires the exact same interface adjudication that Side A criticized Side B for, just without a baseline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing capitalized perfectly on Side A's Rebuttal 2 concessions, pointing out that if Side A agrees non-deceptive friction can be illegal, Side A must also regulate interfaces, making Side B's "equal ease" baseline the more administrable solution.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully defending a workable interpretation of the motion ("functional parity") and decisively trapping Side B on the workability of regulatory standards. Side A effectively pointed out that Side B's preferred "unfairness/legitimate purpose" standard requires the exact same case-by-case interface adjudication that Side B criticized, but without an objective baseline. In contrast, Side A's baseline—the firm's own sign-up flow—is clear and administrable. Side A also neutralized Side B's "backfire" argument (fewer free trials) by framing trials that only profit by trapping inattentive consumers as a welfare correction rather than a cost. Side B's reliance on existing deception law failed to address the core harm of engineered delay through technically truthful but exhausting sequences.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing regarding the workability of the standard, turning Side B's "continuous interface adjudication" critique back on Side B's own "legitimate purpose" test and establishing that comparing exit friction to the firm's own sign-up flow provides a neutral, objective baseline.
