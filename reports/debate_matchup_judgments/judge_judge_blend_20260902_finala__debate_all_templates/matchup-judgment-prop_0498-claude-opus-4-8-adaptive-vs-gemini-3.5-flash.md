- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO)): B won by keeping the debate focused on the core conflict of interest: the same firm ranking, charging, approving, and competing against rivals. B more cleanly separated integration from conflicted ownership and made the stronger comparative case that behavioral oversight and ecosystem competition do not neutralize self-preferencing incentives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal that A defended platform integration rather than ownership of competing apps was the decisive exchange: it blunted A's consumer-fragmentation argument and forced A onto the weaker claim that separation would be both economically ruinous and still useless.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by isolating the specific store-plus-competitor conflict and showing that behavioral oversight and ecosystem competition fail in a duopoly where both gatekeepers share the same incentive. Side A (CON) landed real pressure with the OS-level control turn, but never recovered from the dropped duopoly point or explained why surgically removing the store-level conflict would not eliminate the most direct vector of self-preferencing. B’s closing crystallization cleanly separated integration (which survives) from the conflicted ownership structure (which does not), making the comparative choice clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s duopoly rebuttal in B2.6 and B4.6: the observation that inter-ecosystem competition offers no escape when both gatekeepers share the identical conflict, which Side A never squarely answered.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): PRO wins by cleanly separating OS integration from store operation, neutralizing CON's core "breaks the phone" offense. ### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A won by making the comparative burden clearer: structural separation had to be necessary, sufficient, and worth its costs. Side B established a plausible player-referee conflict, but Side A repeatedly showed that many cited abuses flow from platform control itself, which separation leaves intact, meaning conduct rules would still be needed. A also pressed the unresolved line-drawing problem between integrated system software and commercial first-party apps, and B's monetization test made that problem worse by threatening browsers, maps, wallets, and similar integrated services. B had a clean incentive-alignment story, but it became too absolute and did not adequately answer A's point that a divested store can still self-deal through fees, rankings, APIs, and warnings.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal that malicious compliance, fees, APIs, rankings, and warning screens survive divestiture was the decisive exchange, because it directly undercut Side B's claim that structural separation is a clean substitute for behavioral enforcement.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins because it successfully demonstrated that structural separation fails its own necessity and sufficiency tests. CON exposed that PRO's distinction between "system software" and "commercial apps" collapses under scrutiny—either the test is underinclusive or it severs the very integrated products PRO promised to protect. More decisively, CON showed that the referee's power lies in platform control (fees, APIs, rankings, warning screens), not in owning first-party apps; separation merely relocates the conflict from affiliate-preferencing to fee-based self-dealing rather than removing it. Because conduct rules remain indispensable after separation, the irreversible surgery is redundant and costly. PRO's closing slogan never answered this core concession that a divested store still rigs the game for its own revenue.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 8), which used PRO's own monetization test [B4.1] to prove it would sever browsers, wallets, and maps, then showed that platform control persists after separation so self-preferencing simply becomes self-dealing for ad and fee revenue—crystallized by the "whistle, not the jersey" frame.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by systematically dismantling PRO's core mechanism: that structural separation aligns incentives and ends exploitation. CON convincingly argued that platform control itself—not first-party app ownership—is the true source of gatekeeper power, meaning a divested store would simply shift to self-dealing via fees, ad revenue, and API restrictions. CON also flipped PRO's "delay" argument by noting corporate breakups face longer litigation than conduct injunctions, and exposed PRO's "monetization test" as both overbroad and requiring the exact perpetual regulatory line-drawing PRO condemned. While PRO delivered a clean, principled case on incentive alignment, CON's necessity/sufficiency framing, sharper comparative weighing, and direct answers to pressure provided a clearer, more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Turn 8 response to PRO's Rebuttal 2 was decisive. When PRO offered a "monetization test" to separate commercial apps from system software and claimed separation would end malicious compliance, CON immediately showed the test would guillotine basic integrated apps, require perpetual regulatory classification, and fail to stop fee/API exploitation since platform control remains intact. This exchange collapsed PRO's claim that separation is a clean, one-time fix that removes the need for conduct rules.
