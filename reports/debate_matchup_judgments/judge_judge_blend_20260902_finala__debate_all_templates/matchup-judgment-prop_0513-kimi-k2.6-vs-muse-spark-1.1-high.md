### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but B's throughline was tighter and better defended under pressure. B's core distinction—that consumer price harm is final and universal while wage harm is intermediate, mediated by floors, quits, and dedicated labor institutions—was reinforced consistently across rounds. Crucially, B's pressure question and R2 rebuttal exposed a real structural problem in A's "balance not veto" framing: merger law under the Clayton Act blocks for a substantial lessening of competition in any relevant market rather than netting harms and benefits across markets, so declaring a labor market co-equal effectively does create a veto power over broad consumer benefit. A never directly engaged this doctrinal point in the final rebuttal or closing, instead reasserting the moral parity frame ("checkout receipts than paychecks") without addressing how the "any relevant market" standard would operate. A's administrability rebuttals (ad-tech analogy, cross-elasticity testing) were solid but B's concrete counter (two software firms with overlapping engineer labor pools) sharpened the overbreadth worry effectively. A scored strong points on the inadequacy of minimum wage as a monopsony remedy and on worker immobility, which B never fully neutralized, keeping A's case live but not enough to overcome B's cleaner structural attack and consistent framing across the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R2 point that merger law blocks for a substantial lessening in "any relevant market" rather than balancing gains and losses across markets, which converts A's "parity" into an effective labor veto—left largely unanswered in A's closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): A won by establishing that monopsony is a structural competition harm and that wage floors, labor rules, or quitting do not restore employer competition. B's labor-veto objection exposed some ambiguity in A's balancing model, but it overread equal seriousness as automatic blocking and never justified ignoring major labor harm when consumer benefits are absent or small.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest response was that minimum wages merely limit, rather than eliminate, monopsony harm, while worker exit depends on the very alternatives a merger removes. Comparing labor-market substitution analysis with already-complex product-market analysis also weakened B's administrability objection.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by setting a strategic trap in the Pressure Questions and flawlessly executing the penalty in Rebuttal 2. Side A relies on the premise that antitrust can balance consumer gains against worker losses, but Side B systematically dismantles this by pointing out that merger law blocks deals based on harm in *any* relevant market, thus making labor parity an absolute veto against pro-consumer mergers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5 decisively crushes A's defense by pointing out that antitrust law does not weigh across markets, turning A's "parity is balance" argument into proof of B's "labor veto" warning.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but A built the more coherent path to victory. A's core move—that market-definition and multidimensionality problems are equally present in product markets and are routinely handled with substitution evidence—directly neutralized B's administrability objection rather than merely asserting it. A also introduced a clean, largely unanswered legal principle: efficiencies or consumer gains from a different market cannot offset an anticompetitive harm in the labor market, which forecloses B's "binary remedy" complaint as a reason to discount rather than merely design remedies for the harm. Crucially, A's pressure question forced B to concede in B4.3 that a 9000 HHI plus a proven 10% wage cut "would state a statutory claim," and A's closing correctly identified this as conceding the principle at stake. B's fallback—that labor law tools (wage boards, collective bargaining) are better suited—was never squared with A's earlier, unrebutted point [A2.7] that such tools presuppose an alternative employer to bargain against and cannot fix market structure itself. B's administrability and remedy-mismatch arguments were substantial and kept the debate close, but by conceding the legal claim and failing to resolve the structural-remedy gap, B left its own closing resting on a narrower, already-weakened procedural objection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Pressure Questions exchange where A's Q2 forced B to concede in B4.3 that a merger creating 9000 HHI for ICU nurses with a proven 10% wage cut "would state a statutory claim," which A then leveraged decisively in closing to argue the principle of parity was conceded.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): A won by establishing that labor monopsony is the same competition problem on the buying side and by showing that complexity is not a principled reason to ignore it. B's strongest objection was remedy mismatch, but B overstated consumer analysis as transparent, underanswered A's evidence about measurable labor substitution, and ultimately conceded that a proven labor-market concentration and wage cut states a merger claim. A also preserved a limiting principle: ordinary substantial-lessening analysis rather than automatic blocking.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's most decisive rebuttal compared multidimensional labor compensation with differentiated product markets and identified payroll, commuting, occupation, and application evidence. B largely repeated that products have list prices, which failed to explain why negotiated and bundled product markets remain reviewable while labor markets should be discounted.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A won by successfully cornering Side B into conceding that monopsony states a statutory claim under the Clayton Act, and then demonstrating that the law forbids trading harms in one market for gains in another. Furthermore, Side A decisively neutralized Side B's proposed alternative (labor law) by pointing out that collective bargaining is structurally ineffective when workers have no alternative employers—a point Side B failed to address.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 point that labor law cannot fix monopsony because collective bargaining requires the threat of alternative employers perfectly neutralized Side B's main remedy argument.
