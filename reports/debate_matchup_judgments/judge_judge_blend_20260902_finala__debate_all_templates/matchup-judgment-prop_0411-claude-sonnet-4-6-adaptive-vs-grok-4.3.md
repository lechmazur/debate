### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the central clash about who bears the mobility burden and by using the proposition's flexible language, especially "close to" rather than identical, to answer Side A's strongest operational objections. Side A had a coherent capacity and cost case, and its fixed-infrastructure versus vehicle-dispatch distinction was a strong rebuttal. But it leaned too heavily on an asserted zero-sum driver and vehicle pool, with some unsupported claims about withdrawal and service shrinkage. Side B gave the cleaner comparative answer: current service already imposes severe capacity loss on disabled riders, and mandates can be phased, funded, and used to grow accessible supply rather than simply cannibalize existing service.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's decisive rebuttal was that Side A treated operator costs as decisive while ignoring that disabled riders already pay the cost through lost trips, then reinforced this by answering the zero-sum objection with phased fleet requirements and alternative funding mechanisms.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B successfully dismantled Side A's core premise of a static, zero-sum vehicle and driver pool. By explaining that market supply dynamically adapts to regulatory mandates, Side B neutralized Side A's mathematical objections. Side A failed to engage with this dynamic model, merely repeating its original static-capacity claims in its final two speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, which countered the "fixed driver pool" assumption by explaining that mandates alter incentive structures and fleet purchasing over time, completely undercutting Side A's primary mechanism of harm.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by framing the debate as a civil rights imperative rather than a mere efficiency calculation, consistently showing that the costs CON warned about are already borne by disabled riders under the status quo. CON raised valid capacity concerns but never justified why concentrating deprivation on disabled riders is preferable to sharing systemic costs, and PRO effectively neutralized those concerns with phased compliance and market-creation analogies. PRO’s pressure questions exposed CON’s inability to defend the moral status quo, and PRO’s closing crystallized the clash cleanly by showing CON had only described the costs of equality without defending inequality itself.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 2 answers to CON’s pressure questions, where PRO used the emissions-standards analogy to show mandates expand rather than merely reallocate supply, and demonstrated that funding mechanisms need not rely on regressive fare hikes—shifting the debate from "whether we can afford equality" to "how we implement it."

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side A won because it gave the clearer comparative mechanism: a parity mandate does not itself create wheelchair-accessible vehicles, especially in ride-hail markets with independent drivers and lower-density areas where vehicle costs and trip volume are the central barriers. Side B made a strong equal-access case and reasonably argued that mandates change operator incentives, but its answers to supply scarcity and market exit leaned too heavily on licensing pressure without showing how that pressure produces enough vehicles. Side A slightly overpressed the parity-retreat point, but its mandate-versus-supply framing remained the most decisive live route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest rebuttal was that dispatch priority only helps if accessible vehicles already exist; subsidies reduce the capital barrier and create supply, while a mandate may just create legal noncompliance.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's mechanism in non-dense markets and punishing Side B's attempt to dilute the proposition's text. Side A convincingly proved that mandates cannot magically overcome the capital barriers of acquiring accessible vehicles, framing the choice cleanly as moving paper (mandates) versus moving vehicles (subsidies).
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 surgically exposed Side B's definitional retreat ("comparable booking systems" instead of wait-time parity) and capitalized on Side B's concession regarding low-density markets to prove the mandate is unworkable at scale.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON showed that parity mandates cannot conjure vehicle supply in mid-sized and low-density markets; they simply convert shortfalls into legal violations and litigation costs while leaving disabled riders with fewer options. PRO never solved the capital-barrier problem for ride-hail fleets or overcame the concession that lower-density markets would see fewer total vehicles under the mandate, so the "moves paper, not vehicles" frame stuck.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal used PRO's own admission that low-density markets would have "fewer total vehicles" to show the mandate generates compliance failures exactly where service is most absent, pairing it with the sharp "mandate-plus-litigation versus subsidy-plus-supply" distinction.
