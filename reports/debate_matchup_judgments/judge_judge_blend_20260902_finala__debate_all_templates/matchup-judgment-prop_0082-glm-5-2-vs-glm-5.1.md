### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side A (PRO) won through superior conditional framing and more decisive rebuttal work. The key clash was CON's disclosure-vs.-limitation argument in B2.2-3, which was the best strategic move in the debate—if concealment is the harm, transparency is the cure, not restriction. PRO answered it cleanly in A4.1: a labeled paid boost still reorders the stack by willingness to pay rather than compatibility, crowding out algorithmic results. Disclosure addresses deception but not distortion, and the motion's "limit" language captures both. That distinction held through the close and CON never recovered it. PRO also maintained the conditional framing ("when those features demonstrably undermine") consistently, correctly characterizing the motion as normative rather than regulatory, and CON kept attacking an enforcement straw man that the motion doesn't require. PRO's network-effects argument against market discipline was plausible and CON's rebuttal—new entrants sometimes succeed—was adequate but didn't address harm to current users during the lag. CON's strongest late argument, the "trivial or tyrannical" dilemma in closing, is genuine and PRO's design-principle answer is incomplete, but it arrived too late to shift the full weight of the exchange. CON also largely abandoned swipe mechanics after PRO's pressure question exposed the gap, treating them merely as user preference without engaging choice-overload theory. PRO's lottery analogy in A4.3 and the "labeling an ad doesn't stop it from pushing organic results down the page" line were the most rhetorically sharp moments. CON's originality score is higher because the category-error argument and the final dilemma were genuinely clever, but originality in service of arguments that were ultimately neutralized or delayed counts for less than sustained, adaptive rebuttal work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4.1: distinguishing distortion from deception. CON had constructed a clean alternative remedy—require disclosure instead of limitation—and PRO answered it by showing that even a fully transparent paid boost still reorders the match pool by willingness to pay rather than compatibility, pushing better algorithmic matches down regardless of labeling. This directly neutralized CON's strongest strategic move and confirmed that the motion's "limit" standard addresses a structural harm that transparency alone cannot fix.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): PRO built a coherent, through-line argument around one structural insight: dating apps profit precisely when users fail to match, creating a conflict of interest that cannot be dismissed as mere market diversity. The "goal is to leave" distinction—email engagement aligns with purpose, social media time roughly tracks value received, but dating apps punish their own stated success—was the debate's most decisive original point, and CON never fully neutralized it. The Tinder-dominates-Hinge observation turned CON's own market-competition defense against itself: if the market rewards addiction over quality, it validates rather than defeats the proposition. CON's strongest counter—operationalization ("functional misalignment" requires proprietary data no regulator can access) and non-uniqueness ("every matching service profits from continued searching")—were real and kept the debate competitive. The job-board comparison genuinely dented PRO's uniqueness claim, but PRO held the line on emotional vulnerability, exit costs from network effects, and purpose-specific platform design, keeping the structural argument viable. CON's cigarette rebuttal (behavioral habit vs. physiological addiction) was sharp and somewhat undercut PRO's manipulation framing, but PRO's core case did not depend on the addiction claim alone. Both sides completed A1:/A2: formatting correctly. Both closings were clipped, a minor symmetric cost. On balance, PRO left the debate with a cleaner, better-answered central claim—platforms that engineer failure to maximize revenue—while CON's operationalization objection, though real, was more of a procedural burden-shift than a substantive defeat of the underlying harm mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 response to CON's market-competition defense: CON argued Hinge proves competition delivers quality alternatives without regulation. PRO countered that Tinder—built on the exact engagement mechanics targeted by the proposition—dominates in users, meaning the market rewards addiction rather than match quality. This turned CON's own evidence against CON's conclusion and was never convincingly answered; CON's later responses only reiterated user choice without explaining why millions prefer the model PRO identifies as harmful.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side A prevailed by consistently exposing that PRO's "functional misalignment" trigger lacks any workable, non-subjective metric and by neutralizing the claimed uniqueness of dating apps versus other marketplaces, leaving PRO's route to victory unanchored after full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal 2 directly answered both pressure questions then dismantled uniqueness via job boards/listings, damaging PRO's core conflict-of-interest claim without widening scope.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): PRO won by sustaining a coherent structural critique: dating apps face a built-in conflict between helping users find matches and keeping them engaged, and specific mechanics exploit that conflict. CON's market-alternatives and subjectivity objections were met by PRO's network-effects/addiction rebuttal and the "functional misalignment" consumer-protection standard. CON's non-uniqueness argument (job boards etc.) failed to negate the proposition because the motion is conditional and does not claim dating apps are the only domain with this problem. PRO's pressure-question answers, especially the "goal is to leave" distinction, sharpened the decisive clash more cleanly than CON's "paternalism" frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's answer to CON's pressure question about uniqueness (A4.3), distinguishing dating apps by showing that users seek to leave the platform, making engagement-maximizing design structurally opposed to user success in a way that does not apply to email or social media.
