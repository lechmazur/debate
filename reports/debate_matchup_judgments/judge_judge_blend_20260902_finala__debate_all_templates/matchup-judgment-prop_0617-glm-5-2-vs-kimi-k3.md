### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that substantial future consumer benefits are often emergent and cannot be proven through fixed pre-closing commitments, making the proposed presumption excessively restrictive. Side B established information asymmetry and irreversible competitive risks, but never adequately answered the chilling effect on startup financing or the ambiguity between a valuable target and a threatening rival. Its closing also repeated the claim that firms control disclosure despite Side A's compulsory-disclosure response.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange showed that Side B's binding commitments are either ordinary behavioral remedies available under current enforcement or a narrow escape hatch that excludes acquisitions with uncertain but potentially transformative benefits. Side B's answer identified only a backend-tool example and relied on dominance plus network effects as its rival criterion, reinforcing Side A's de facto-ban critique.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B won by successfully framing the affirmative standard as an impossible burden for emergent-value deals, effectively making it a per-se ban. Side B effectively dismantled Side A's mechanisms, showing that binding commitments are existing behavioral remedies and that punishing acquisitions shrinks the startup funding pipeline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 point that if antitrust agencies are too passive to use current tools, they cannot be trusted to rigorously administer the proposed rebuttal process.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): CON won the burden clash. PRO argued specificity and informed-party burden justifies presumption, but CON showed emergent benefits cannot be roadmapped ex ante and that PRO's only clear pass was a narrow backend tool, making the presumption a de facto ban. CON answered pressure about tools being under-used not absent and flipped PRO's competence attack, while PRO failed to name a real dominant-platform deal that would clear its test and retreated to dominance-plus-structural-features as its observable criterion, confirming CON's per-se-rule charge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4 answer to B's Q1/Q2 and its weigh of B4.1 - showing PRO's test passes only marginal vertical deals, fails its own opening examples like the photo app, and treats post-acquisition success as proof of threat - exposed the unmeetable counterfactual burden.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side A narrowly won by exposing the motion’s central overreach: failure to prove substantial consumer benefit does not establish anti-competitive harm. Side B compellingly explained information asymmetry and irreversible tipping, but repeatedly softened “prove substantial benefit” into merely naming a plausible benefit. It also insufficiently answered why competitively neutral deals should be blocked or why startup deterrence is readily reversible. Side A’s clipped opening and Side B’s sharper rhetoric kept the contest close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s final response to Side B’s “self-administering” safeguard was decisive: shareholder synergies are not proof of substantial consumer benefit, and a neutral deal can fail that affirmative test without harming competition.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a superior weighing mechanism (irreversible market tipping vs correctable VC repricing) and effectively turning Side B's arguments about "speculative" benefits against them. Side B made a strong conceptual point that the motion blocks "competitively neutral" deals, but Side A successfully argued that neutrality is indistinguishable from hidden anti-competitive intent ex ante, justifying the burden shift.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal, which weaponized Side B's claim that benefits are "speculative ex ante" by pointing out that harms are equally speculative, meaning the status quo forces the government to prove speculative harms blind.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): PRO kept three live routes - irreversible tipping, total info asymmetry, and observed status-quo failure - and answered overbreadth directly (dominance IS the theory for adjacent deals, easy proof for truly unrelated). CON's best technical hit that "no benefit != anti-competitive" and that neutral deals die was never fully neutralized but was outweighed by PRO's sharper pressure on unwritten intent and by CON's clipped opening, leaving PRO's error-cost asymmetry (repriced fund cycle vs untippable market) as the cleaner reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2 flipping A1.2's "speculative, emergent, impossible to quantify" into a symmetric-uncertainty forfeiture, paired with A4.5's Waze-survived-as-separate-product counter in Rebuttal 2 - the exchange where overbreadth vs gatekeeper foreclosure was decided.
