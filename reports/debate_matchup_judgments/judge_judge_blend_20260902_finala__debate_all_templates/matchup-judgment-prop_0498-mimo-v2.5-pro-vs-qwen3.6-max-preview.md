### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): PRO built a more coherent and internally consistent case across all five rounds. The decisive moments were: (1) PRO's Q1 exposed a genuine contradiction in CON's position—CON argued in B2.3 that "a corporate boundary does not eliminate self-preferencing," yet maintained that internal data firewalls within that same corporate boundary could do the job. CON's A1 response (B4.1) tried to reframe this by claiming a single legal entity gives regulators more oversight than a split entity, but the argument was counterintuitive and underdeveloped—CON never explained the mechanism by which being inside one company makes compliance trails more accessible rather than less. (2) PRO's handling of the reclassification pressure question (B3.2/A4.3–4) was the sharpest single argumentative move in the debate: PRO conceded the concern was real and then turned it against CON—if operators would reclassify commercial apps as system components to evade structural rules, that self-evidently proves behavioral oversight cannot contain self-preferencing. CON never answered this in Rebuttal 2 or the closing. (3) The stock-exchange analogy (A4.2) directly addressed CON's strongest remaining argument—that technical competence requires commercial first-party participation—with a plausible, well-grounded counter-example, and CON did not engage it. CON's engineering-feedback-loop argument was genuine and carried real weight, but PRO provided adequate answers (platform engineering teams, public betas, developer programs) without being required to defeat it entirely. The closing round also favored PRO: A5.1 crystallized the logical contradiction cleanly, while B5.1–4 largely repeated earlier material without sharpening the key weighing. Both sides stayed epistemically disciplined with no fake citations. CON was competent but ended up reactive and failed to resolve the core contradiction under pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4.3–4 response to CON's reclassification question was the most decisive exchange. Rather than simply defending the bright line between OS utilities and commercial apps, PRO turned the concern around: the very fact that an operator would reclassify apps to evade structural rules demonstrates exactly the kind of gamesmanship that proves behavioral oversight cannot contain self-preferencing. This converted CON's pressure question into evidence for PRO's own position, and CON never answered it in the remaining rounds.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side A wins a clear but competitive debate. A more directly tied the remedy to the motion: the platform/app conflict creates incentives and invisible micro-levers that behavioral rules struggle to monitor, while structural separation removes the core incentive. B’s best offense was that first-party apps provide real engineering feedback and that separation may shift discrimination into contracts, but B never fully explained why an independently separated store would retain the same motive to favor a former or outside app business. A also handled B’s pressure well by distinguishing commercial store apps from system components and by arguing that reclassification worries prove the need for bright-line rules. A had some execution cost from clipped turns, but its comparative framing was cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest exchange was against B’s firewall/visibility argument: A pressed the contradiction between claiming corporate boundaries cannot stop gaming while trusting weaker internal firewalls, then reframed separation as making favoritism external, visible, and litigable.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by providing highly specific, plausible mechanisms to counter Side B's pressure. While B claimed A had an unresolved contradiction regarding corporate boundaries versus internal firewalls, A effectively explained why a single legal entity provides better regulatory visibility through audit logs than opaque inter-company contracts. Furthermore, A's distinction between sterile public betas and commercial shipping products decisively won the engineering feedback loop clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 response (A4.2) comparing sterile public betas to commercial shipping products under real-world load effectively neutralized B's pressure question on the engineering feedback loop.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A wins on the strength of a cleaner, more consistently prosecuted case anchored by the toll-road metaphor. The incentive-change argument — that separation makes self-preferencing financially irrational rather than merely illegal — directly addressed CON's monitoring concern without requiring the perpetual algorithmic surveillance both sides agreed was impractical. That reframe is the most decisive move in the debate: it shifted the terrain from "who can monitor better" to "who needs to monitor at all," and CON never squarely neutralized it. PRO also landed the timing point effectively — that behavioral remedies always lag behind harm — and CON's response (bright-line prohibitions) acknowledged but did not resolve it. CON's strongest live argument was jurisdictional erosion (separation applies only to Western platforms, handing advantage to integrated rivals elsewhere), and PRO genuinely left it unanswered across five speeches; that is a real liability. However, CON's two clipped speeches (Rebuttal 2 and Closing) are execution costs that prevented full crystallization, and the jurisdictional argument is somewhat external to the motion's core question of whether the structural arrangement is principally correct. CON's integration-quality defense had real substance but was progressively weakened by PRO's "captivity vs. merit" distinction and the "innovation ceiling" reframe, which CON never cleanly dismantled. Overall, PRO built a narrower but sturdier path to victory; CON's best argument was real but orphaned in the final exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Q1 (A1 in [A4.1–A4.2]): the toll-road response directly turned CON's monitoring concern into a reason to prefer separation over behavioral remedies. By distinguishing the presence of technical capability from the financial motive to exploit it, PRO gave a clean comparative reason to prefer structural change over conduct rules — and CON's closing could only reassert the monitoring problem rather than answer the incentive logic.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): A wins a clear but competitive debate. A built the cleaner causal route: app-store gatekeepers have both the power and incentive to self-preference, conduct remedies are hard to police, and structural separation removes the financial motive at the source. B raised strong concerns about integration, consumer quality, and global competitive erosion, but those depended partly on conflating app-store separation with broader hardware-OS disintegration, which A repeatedly and effectively challenged. B's behavioral remedy alternative remained plausible but less convincing against A's incentive-level framing, and B's clipped later turns were a modest execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest rebuttal was the distinction between technical opportunity and financial motive: even if a separated platform can still tweak APIs, it no longer profits from its own downstream apps winning, making self-preferencing much less rational and much easier to deter.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B won the decisive clash on enforceability by distinguishing between technical opportunity and financial motive. Side B's analogy that a 'toll road' has no incentive to police a neutral market effectively neutralized Side A's argument about surveillance. Side A pressed a strong dropped point about international competition, but Side B's arguments about ecosystem health provided enough defense, and Side A suffered execution penalties from word count clippings.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (A1) decisively reframed Side A's monitoring paradox by explaining that separation removes the financial motive to self-preference, rendering constant surveillance unnecessary.
