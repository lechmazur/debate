### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won on the central clash for three compounding reasons. First, the asymmetry argument—a mistaken ban is reversible; developmental harm is not—anchored every round and was never neutralized. CON's answer (we don't know the harm is real, so the asymmetry assumes its conclusion) is fair, but PRO correctly noted that decades of unresolved federal deliberation make that skepticism indistinguishable from indefinite delay. The burden of "wait for certainty" ends up borne entirely by children, not regulators. Second, PRO answered the strongest CON pressure question (Q1, limiting principle) with a principled three-part test: child-focused evidence, zero nutritional role, safer alternatives already available. It isn't watertight, but it is a real limiting principle, and CON's critique—that "credible, child-focused, accumulating" can describe many food scares—landed as a risk to the principle rather than a decisive rebuttal. Third, execution costs hurt CON materially: three of its five speeches were clipped, losing argument space in both rebuttals and the opening. PRO had only one clipped turn, at the tail of the opening. CON's strongest moments—"governance by suspicion," "whack-a-mole substitution," and the concrete threshold articulation—came through but were curtailed before they could be fully pressed. PRO's patchwork reframe ("race to the top," large-market reformulation) was met with a real counter (one alarmed legislature sets national policy), but PRO's closer answer—that mistaken bans are correctable while developmental harm is not—kept winning that comparison. CON's "less extreme tools" argument (labels, school procurement) was partially rebutted by PRO's vivid point that a label does nothing for a child handed a neon treat, and CON's response that parents and institutions do the shopping, while reasonable, never regained the rhetorical high ground.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was PRO's handling of CON's pressure question Q1 (what limiting principle distinguishes a justified dye ban from any contested ingredient ban). PRO answered with a three-part principle—child-specific neurobehavioral evidence, zero nutritional function, proven-safe substitutes already available—rather than retreating to "the legislature called it credible." That answer, combined with the persistent "reversible ban vs. irreversible harm" frame, shifted the comparative weight in PRO's favor and left CON defending a threshold that looks, in practice, like indefinite waiting.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by more effectively defending a concrete evidentiary and comparative threshold for coercive state bans, exposing PRO's reliance on assumption of material harm and the governance risks of patchwork overreach; PRO's precautionary asymmetry was compelling but insufficiently answered the live burdens on actual risk reduction and reversibility costs after direct pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (direct A1/A2 answers redefining threshold and whack-a-mole risk, then weighing national policy precedent) best neutralized PRO's limiting principle and correction mechanism claims.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON wins by successfully turning PRO's strongest empirical claim—that a large state like California forces national reformulation—into a normative liability, demonstrating that it effectively allows one state legislature to bypass national scientific governance and coerce the national market. CON also maintained a clearer, more defensible policy standard: evaluate substitutes, deploy targeted exposure-reduction tools first, and require a comparative showing of material risk rather than treating disputed evidence as automatic permission for categorical bans. PRO's precautionary framing and reversible-market-versus-irreversible-harm asymmetry were rhetorically effective, but CON neutralized them by highlighting substitution whack-a-mole, the real economic and consumer costs of forced reformulation, and the weakness of PRO's proposed correction mechanism. Both sides followed format, answered pressure directly, and avoided fake precision, but CON's comparative weighing and structural critique provided the cleaner, more complete path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's claim that California's market size creates a beneficial race to the top. By pointing out that this concedes one state effectively sets national food policy without national scientific review—and asking what happens when a large state is wrong, ideological, or captured—CON flipped PRO's patchwork defense into a decisive federalism and governance liability that PRO never fully resolved.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the more coherent and durable path to victory. Three claims stayed live and were never squarely neutralized. First, the "federal delay self-justifying" argument: CON's rule meant that if evidence is disputed, states must wait, and if it is settled, federal action makes state action unnecessary — a practical veto on any state precaution. CON's only answer was to "press the FDA to accelerate," which is passive and does not engage the motion's stated premise. Second, the asymmetric-error argument in B4.2 was genuinely effective: reformulation and labeling costs are reversible; repeated child exposure during years of inertia is not. CON's response (A4.3) was that the benefit is unknown and possibly zero, which is fair but does not refute the asymmetry — it only claims one tail of the distribution. Third, PRO's proportionality framework — that the appropriate precautionary threshold for a cosmetic, nonessential additive with available substitutes should be lower than for staple foods or medicines — survived the full exchange. CON argued economic costs are certain while health benefits are speculative, but never dismantled the principle that a lower threshold applies to optional ingredients. CON's most effective contribution was the engineering-complexity rebuttal to "just reformulate" (A2.1) and the "disputed means possibly zero benefit" argument (A2.3), both of which PRO answered adequately but not crushingly. The pressure-question round was balanced, but PRO's A1 response in B4.1 provided a more workable threshold than CON's A1 in A4.1, which ultimately still deferred to federal process without specifying when, if ever, state action would be justified. CON also lost points for repetition in the closing and for never providing a credible mechanism by which "pressing FDA to accelerate" would actually protect children during the disputed period the motion describes. Both sides clipped turns, so execution costs are roughly symmetric.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's "federal delay self-justifying" critique in B5.2, paired with the reversible-vs-irreversible error asymmetry in B4.2, was the most decisive exchange. CON never answered why accepting federal delay is not itself a policy choice that preserves the status quo at families' expense, and the "press FDA to accelerate" remedy offered no timeline or mechanism, leaving that burden effectively unmet.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins: built clearer path by framing nonessential dyes as low-stakes for state precaution, weighing ongoing child exposure against reversible reformulation costs, and directly answering pressure on thresholds and error costs. Side B's federal deference and balkanization claims were neutralized without sufficient counter on vulnerability or proportionality. Clipping affected flow but did not erase clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2 directly rebutted balkanization and certainty weighting, showing reformulation feasible for cosmetics while exposing CON's error reversal favoring delay.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by establishing a cleaner comparative framework around proportionality and asymmetrical error costs. By consistently anchoring the debate to the non-essential, cosmetic nature of synthetic dyes, PRO successfully lowered the threshold for precautionary state action and reframed CON's patchwork and economic harm arguments as reversible, manageable trade-offs rather than decisive barriers. CON competently defended federal uniformity, highlighted real reformulation engineering challenges, and pressed the risk of codifying error, but never fully answered why states must remain paralyzed on optional additives when federal delay functionally acts as a veto on local health protection. PRO's direct A1/A2 responses, sharper weighing of irreversible exposure versus reversible market costs, and tighter closing crystallization made the path to victory clearer and more responsive to the motion's specific premises.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2, where they directly answered CON's pressure questions by articulating a concrete threshold for state action and weighing the asymmetry of regulatory errors. By contrasting reversible reformulation expenses with irreversible child exposure during years of federal inertia, PRO effectively collapsed CON's uniformity argument into a defense of a potentially unsafe status quo and secured the decisive proportionality clash.
