Net: A made the decisive clash easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s A1/A4.5–A4.7 answer: privacy is not exclusive; warrants/necessity tests are privacy as the controlling principle that security must meet—turning B’s “accountable security” frame into A’s content.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): The debate hinged on a means-vs-ends framing: A argued security is the structural precondition without which privacy cannot exist at all, while B argued privacy is the substantive purpose that makes a society "free" rather than merely orderly. B's early concession that "some security is a precondition" [B2.1] weakened its own case, and A exploited this repeatedly and effectively: every time B proposed a fix for a privacy crisis (warrants, targeted policing, accountable courts), A showed that fix was itself a security function, undermining B's claim that privacy "governs" security. B's attempted rebuttal—that a warrant is "security subordinated to privacy" [B4.1]—was clever wordplay but never cashed out into a structural argument as sharp as A's. B's strongest surviving point was the "well-ordered cage" framing, directly tied to the motion's "free society" language, and its A2 catch that A was redefining "security" to smuggle in privacy protections [B4.3] was a real hit A never fully answered except by reasserting its definitions. But this critique wasn't escalated into a knockout in later rounds, while A's core structural argument and rebuttal pressure remained largely intact through closing. A's closing crystallized the case more cleanly than B's, which mostly recapped the cage metaphor without resolving the "your own fix is security" problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 response to B's abuser example [A2.2-A2.3], showing that B's own proposed solution ("targeted, accountable security") was itself a security function, not privacy triumphing over security—an objection B never cleanly escaped despite the "tool vs. legitimating rule" distinction offered later.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): A won a close, high-quality clash by holding the motion’s free-society standard: security is necessary but not supreme once order exists, and bulk-surveillance “success” that kills the private sphere yields a managed cage, not freedom. A conceded B’s precondition point early, then cleanly reframed it (roof/home, lock/life, cell vs home) and treated warrants as security tools limited by privacy. B’s best move—recasting mass surveillance as security failure and calling every PRO remedy a “security function”—was sharp but partly circular, folding privacy goods into security’s definition and never fully answering why, when the two conflict inside a free society, security should rank higher. A made the decisive weighing easier: free society can endure some insecurity; it cannot remain free after the private sphere is abolished.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1–A4.2 answering B’s “targeted security is security” pressure: warrants are security tools subordinated to privacy limits (tool vs rule), not a concession that security outranks privacy.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON built the stronger path to victory by showing that PRO's own solutions to privacy crises—warrants, targeted policing, accountable courts—are security functions operating correctly, not privacy triumphing over security. CON also reframed PRO's central "well-ordered cage" by arguing that mass state monitoring is itself a security failure (the state becomes the predator), which dissolved the illusion that security can win while privacy loses. The recoverability argument—that democracies restore privacy after emergencies, but collapse without security—provided a clean comparative weighing that PRO could not squarely answer. PRO's "cage vs. home" rhetoric remained vivid, but could not overcome the analytical damage that CON landed on PRO's examples and framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal that a mass-surveillance society is not one where "security wins and privacy loses," but rather a society where security itself fails because the state becomes the coercive predator, directly neutralizing PRO's "well-ordered cage" example and undermining the premise of PRO's pressure question.
