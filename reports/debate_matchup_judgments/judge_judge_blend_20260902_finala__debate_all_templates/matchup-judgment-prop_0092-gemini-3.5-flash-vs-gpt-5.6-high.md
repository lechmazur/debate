### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): CON built a more sustained and analytically sharper case: fees are a poor proxy because size, weight, footprint, and pedestrian-hazard geometry are distinct harms that no single "oversized" label tracks, and because a surcharge lets wealthy owners buy continued risk while burdening low-income users without actually shrinking the hazard. PRO's opening was solid on mechanism (space, safety, wear) but its central adaptive move—the "tiered matrix" in Rebuttal 2—effectively conceded CON's core objection that a flat size-based category is mismatched, and CON's closing capitalized on this concession cleanly, showing the matrix still misses hood geometry, visibility, and location. PRO never fully repaired that gap in its own closing, instead repeating the "physics/pragmatism" framing without re-engaging the matrix's specific shortcomings. PRO's Pigouvian "nudge" defense was a reasonable rejoinder to the equity critique but was not pressed hard enough to overcome CON's repeated point that payment does not lower a hood or restore a sightline. CON's performance is discounted somewhat for being clipped twice (opening and Rebuttal 2), which cost some rhetorical landing and completeness, but the substantive throughline survived intact and was the more decisive comparative case by the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turning PRO's "tiered matrix" answer into an admission that no single oversized-vehicle category captures the distinct harms (space, weight, and hood-geometry/visibility risk), which PRO's closing never adequately re-defended.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON won the decisive mismatch clash. PRO showed extra size can impose costs, but CON showed a body-style surcharge neither measures nor removes them: registration weight misses urban mileage, footprint misses crash exposure, neither tracks hood geometry. PRO's tiered matrix conceded the category label fails, and the buffer/volumetric claim was answered by B's point that leftover inches don't create a bay and payment doesn't reclaim space or lower a hood. B's wealth-buys-risk and actual-obstruction framing left the cleaner voting principle.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's R1/R2 sequence that a spill should be enforced not monetized, a fit-in-bay has not displaced another user, and a surcharge lets affluent drivers pay to keep imposing risk while burdening work use.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's core mechanism. B effectively demonstrated that charging higher fees neither reclaims physical curb space nor eliminates pedestrian safety hazards, but merely commodifies them, allowing wealthy drivers to pay to maintain the risk. B's distinction between pricing a harm and preventing it through direct regulation was decisive. Furthermore, B handled A's pressure questions excellently, clarifying that their alternative was targeted spatial restriction rather than the economically paralyzing blanket bans A alleged. While A had strong rhetoric and a clear moral intuition, B's logical rigor and sharper rebuttals on the mechanics of the policy secured a clear victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.4-B4.6), which sharply rejected A's "buffer space" argument by pointing out that bay dimensions already account for maneuvering, and highlighted the fatal equity flaw in A's case: that a fee system simply allows the wealthy to purchase the right to continue imposing safety risks.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO built a coherent cost-internalization principle early (bigger vehicles impose more crash force, blind-zone risk, curb space, and road wear, so fees should track burden) and sustained it through rebuttals with concrete mechanisms (registration-linked size bands, phase-ins, narrow exemptions). Crucially, PRO turned CON's regressivity charge around: uniform pricing forces poorer pedestrians, transit riders, and small-car owners to subsidize the largest vehicles' extra costs—a reversal CON never squarely answered, instead repeating "regressive tax" and "commodifies safety" without engaging the subsidy-shifting logic. PRO also handled the pay-to-play pressure well by distinguishing a price signal (which changes marginal behavior) from a safety waiver, using the speed-cushion/congestion-pricing analogy effectively; CON's rebuttal reasserted the same "licenses risk" framing without dismantling that distinction. Both sides answered pressure questions with proper A1/A2 formatting, but PRO's answers did more actual work resolving live tensions (targeted relief vs. blanket exemption, partial-effect standard for policy success), while CON's answers largely restated prior claims. CON's administrative-complexity and street-design points were reasonable but under-developed against PRO's specific counter (bands tied to existing registration data, choice of a single instrument). Overall PRO progressed the debate more, neutralized more of CON's live routes, and left the sharper, cleaner reason to accept the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1-B4.6) reframing "who pays what is left unpriced"—arguing uniform fees are a hidden subsidy to large-vehicle owners paid by poorer non-owners—which CON never directly dismantled in Rebuttal 2 or Closing, instead reverting to restating the regressive-tax and commodification framing.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): PRO built a cleaner cost-internalization path: larger vehicles use more curb and impose greater crash/ wear costs, so equal fees are a subsidy, not neutrality. PRO answered CON's core pressures: fees don't waive safety rules, they change marginal purchase/trip decisions and fund design, and narrow commercial bands plus choice of instrument avoid both loophole collapse and massive admin. CON's commodified-safety and regressive-tax routes were repeatedly reduced to assertion without answering who pays for universal infrastructure if not those creating the burden, or why risk-reduction is acceptable for design but not pricing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Rebuttal 2 A1/A4.1 — PRO's marginal-effects reply to the pay-to-play charge (fee doesn't waive rules, some downsize, some avoid constrained curbs, revenue funds safety) plus the analogy that speed cushions also don't eliminate risk, which CON never squarely rebutted.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A built a highly cohesive case grounded in internalizing externalities and effectively neutralized Side B's main offensives. A's point that the motion allows for registration fees completely bypassed B's administrative burden argument regarding parking meters, and A's reframing of uniform pricing as a hidden subsidy from poorer non-drivers to large-vehicle owners dismantled B's regressivity claim. Side B relied on rhetorically catchy but logically brittle arguments like "commodifying safety" that failed to address marginal deterrence or the reality of price signals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, specifically pointing out that the motion allows cities to use registration fees (which are already tied to vehicle specs) to avoid the parking meter administrative nightmare, and explaining that marginal risk reduction is the standard for safety policies.
