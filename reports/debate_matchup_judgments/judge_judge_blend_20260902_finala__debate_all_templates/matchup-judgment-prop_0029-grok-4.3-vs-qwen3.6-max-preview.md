### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Both sides ran coherent, well-grounded cases, but CON (Side A) edges the debate on two structural fault lines that PRO never fully resolved. First, CON's repeated distinction between man-portable systems (minimal support requirements, immediately effective) and complex platforms (artillery, armor, networked air defense requiring maintenance echelons, spares pipelines, and integrated fire-direction networks) was a genuine asymmetry that PRO repeatedly papered over with the phrase "scaling familiar systems." PRO's causal story depended on treating MANPADS success as evidence that scaling to artillery and air defense on a compressed timeline would work the same way, which CON correctly identified as a categorical error. PRO never squarely answered it. Second, CON's pressure question about the industrial procurement mechanism—how a 2022 stockpile dump generates the predictable, sustained demand needed for multi-year factory contracts—was the weakest answered question in the debate. PRO's A1 response leaned on speculative political momentum from battlefield gains rather than an actual procurement mechanism, which was less persuasive than CON's claim that factory expansions respond to predictable long-term commitments. PRO's best unrebutted point—that the delay allowed Russia to consolidate, shorten lines, and develop glide-bomb capabilities from prepared positions—is real and concrete, and CON's response (Russian structural depth made this inevitable regardless) only partially addressed the degree question. But that live point was not enough to overcome the two structural gaps noted above. CON's "synchronization, not hesitation" framing effectively crystallized the comparison: the measured pace aligned deliveries with absorption capacity and preserved the coalition and industrial base that actually sustain Ukraine today, whereas PRO's path trades proven endurance for a speculative early spike.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 on simple versus complex system integration—"Ad hoc learning cannot substitute for crew coordination, fire direction, and electronic warfare hardening"—was the most decisive single exchange. It directly undercut PRO's central mechanism by showing that the tactical success of man-portable weapons in 2022 does not establish that artillery, armor, and integrated air defense could have been surged without the support infrastructure that takes months to build. PRO never cleanly answered this distinction across any subsequent round, which left CON's absorption-capacity argument largely intact throughout the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side B won by successfully proving that a 2022 surge would have overwhelmed Ukraine's absorption capacity and depleted Western stockpiles before production could ramp up. Side B effectively neutralized Side A's core claim that early tactical disruption would reduce long-term attrition by pointing out that Russia's industrial depth is structural, meaning Ukraine would still face a mobilized Russia but with empty Western magazines.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 answer to Q2 decisively handled Side A's argument that early strikes would lower later ammunition needs, noting that Russia's capacity for massed fires is structural and would eventually mobilize regardless of early tactical losses.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON won by proving that faster aid would have outstripped Ukraine's absorption capacity for complex systems and burned Western stockpiles, leaving Ukraine weaker in the attritional phase. PRO's window-of-vulnerability argument remained tactically plausible but could not overcome the structural link between sustainable logistics, coalition cohesion, and Ukraine's actual strength today.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's repeated distinction between immediately usable man-portable weapons and maintenance-heavy complex systems, which broke PRO's causal claim that scaling early deliveries would have produced multiplied operational disruption.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built and sustained the stronger case throughout. Its core framing — "speculative escalation risks versus documented strategic costs" — was clean, comparative, and never adequately answered. PRO's sharpest move was using the actual timeline of events (HIMARS, Leopards, deep strikes arriving later without widening the war) as direct evidence against CON's escalation thesis, turning CON's own implicit counterfactual against it. PRO then reinforced this in Rebuttal 2 with the coherent point that a chaotic, overextended Russian military in spring 2022 had fewer credible escalation options, not more — the inverse of CON's claim. CON tried to distinguish spring 2022 as a unique red line (Russian forces still in offensive posture, domestic mobilization not yet locked in), but never resolved the logical tension: if Russian forces were failing, why would the Kremlin be more willing to strike NATO supply routes at its most vulnerable moment? That distinction was asserted repeatedly but never grounded. CON's "coalition durability" argument had real merit but was undercut by PRO's point that durability is worth little if veteran cadres are destroyed and the frontline hardens irreversibly in the interim. CON's absorption-capacity argument was addressed by PRO's observation that Ukrainian crews were already adapting under fire and that the catastrophic attrition came from prolonged grinding against fortified lines, not from rapid delivery. Both sides met format requirements. PRO's pressure questions were sharper, exploiting a genuine internal contradiction in CON's position, while CON's questions restated its own claims more than they exposed a real burden on PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 [B2.1] combined with Rebuttal 2 [B4.1]: PRO argued that Russia absorbed HIMARS, Leopards, and long-range strikes later without widening the war, and that a failing, overextended Russia in spring 2022 had even less credible escalation capacity than a consolidated, mobilized Russia later. CON never resolved why spring 2022 would be uniquely dangerous rather than uniquely exploitable, leaving its core pillar standing on speculation while PRO pointed to actual events that ran counter to CON's predicted pattern.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing the debate as a choice between concrete costs (entrenchment, attrition, lost manpower) and speculative risks (escalation, coalition fracture). Side A persuasively argued that Russian weakness in early 2022 made escalation less credible, successfully neutralizing Side B's primary offensive argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized Side B's escalation argument by pointing out that escalating against NATO supply lines or using nuclear posturing would have risked regime survival while the Russian military was already overextended and stalling.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): PRO established a concrete, irreversible causal chain from delayed aid to Russian entrenchment and costly attrition, while CON’s escalation and absorption warnings remained speculative and were badly damaged by PRO’s empirical rejoinder that heavier systems arriving later caused no wider war. PRO’s weighing—documented territorial and manpower losses versus hypothetical coalition fractures—proved more persuasive across the exchange, and its closing crystallized the clash more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s first rebuttal (B2), which leveraged the later arrival of HIMARS and Leopards to dismantle CON’s escalation thesis and reframed Western delay as giving Russia the one resource it desperately needed: time to dig in.
