### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, competing causal stories, but A sustained a sharper comparative frame throughout: most fatal overdoses occur among people who never seek treatment or use alone without a witness, so population-wide supply disruption (chokepoints at precursor/wholesale/press level) protects more people than a rescue net most victims never enter. A also won the specific sub-clash on potency causation, arguing convincingly that toxic escalation stems from permissive, low-risk selling conditions rather than enforcement pressure—a point B never directly refuted, only restated. B's strongest moment was the pressure question on record seizures coinciding with record-low prices/high purity, a genuinely hard empirical challenge to A's mechanism; A's answer (seizures are a fraction of flow, "would have been worse") was serviceable but somewhat unfalsifiable. However, B's own vulnerable point—MAT/naloxone efficacy depending on retention and a present witness—drew a comparably thin rebuttal (roommates, hotlines) that didn't establish population-scale impact. On balance, A's throughline was more consistent and its rebuttals landed with slightly more precision, giving it a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over Pressure Questions was decisive: B's Q1 (record seizures coinciding with cheap, pure fentanyl) hit A's core mechanism hard, while A's Q1/Q2 (low retention and witness-dependency undermining MAT/naloxone at scale) hit B's core mechanism just as hard. Neither side fully neutralized the other's challenge, but A's follow-up reversing causation on potency escalation (driven by permissive conditions, not enforcement pressure) was the cleanest unanswered point in the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won because it successfully undermined the core feasibility of Side B's supply-side strategy. A demonstrated that synthetic opioids structurally defy interdiction: lethal doses are microgram-sized, precursors are ubiquitous, and record seizures have coincided with cheaper, purer street drugs. A then turned B's own mechanism against B by showing that enforcement-induced market disruption produces erratic, adulterated batches—the very volatility driving fatal poisonings—whereas MAT and harm reduction intervene directly at the point of death. While B landed strong blows on treatment retention and naloxone's witness problem, A answered those burdens plausibly with low-barrier expansion, social-perimeter naloxone distribution, and virtual spotting. Over the full exchange, A's structural causal argument about synthetic chemistry proved harder to neutralize than B's speculative choke-point theory, giving A a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal turned Side B's concession that enforcement creates "weaker adulterated batches" into proof that interdiction increases deadly volatility rather than reducing it, collapsing the logical foundation of B's scarcity mechanism.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by establishing a decisive comparative burden: harm reduction requires user compliance (retention, seeking care) and bystander presence (naloxone), which inherently fails the most chaotic, high-risk populations. Side A's rhetorical framing ("survival cannot depend on showing up" and "cutting supply asks nothing of them") perfectly crystallized this structural advantage. While Side B made strong, plausible points about the economics of synthetic drugs and the "iron law of prohibition," Side A effectively countered by arguing that market disruption forces supply stretching (weaker doses) and dismantles the industrial mass-pressing that drives the crisis. Side A's answers to pressure questions and its closing statements were sharper, more original, and more grounded in the practical realities of addiction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's flip of Side B's "erratic potency" argument. When Side B claimed interdiction causes toxic, unpredictable cuts, Side A countered that a permissive, low-risk market enables industrial mass-pressing of highly potent pills, whereas disruption forces dealers to stretch supply into weaker bags and operate cautiously, ultimately lowering the lethality per use. This sharply neutralized Side B's core mechanism for why interdiction increases deaths.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides followed the Pressure Questions/A1-A2 format correctly, but B built a tighter, more resilient causal story. B's core mechanism—that fentanyl's cheap, concentrated production makes interdiction subject to rapid displacement and a "waterbed effect" toward more potent analogues—was asserted early and never squarely rebutted; A's answer that "targeted" future interdiction on precursors/presses will differ from past failures was largely asserted rather than mechanistically defended, and A never answered B's pointed challenge (B4.3) about why domestic disruption would meaningfully raise costs when a year's supply fits in barrels. B also cleverly turned A's strongest point—silent, unwitnessed overdoses from counterfeit pills—into an argument for harm reduction's household/education reach, and reframed the comparative burden as "interdiction needs perfection, harm reduction needs only partial success," a framing A's closing never dislodged. A's rebuttals repeated the "silent overdose" and "fatal treadmill" claims without much evolution, and its answers to B's pressure questions (Turn 8) leaned on unsupported optimism about future enforcement efficacy. B's closing crystallized the asymmetry argument cleanly; A's closing largely restated prior points without resolving the displacement critique.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A1 in Turn 7), reframing "alone" overdoses as usually having a nearby household member reachable by naloxone/education, directly neutralized A's central "unwitnessed victim" argument while exposing that interdiction offers those same victims nothing at all.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a more durable structural framework: interdiction against synthetic opioids faces inexhaustible replacement, adaptation, and the iron law of potency, while treatment and harm reduction need only partial, repeated success to save lives. Side B landed a strong challenge with the counterfeit-pill victim, but A devastatingly turned it by showing that those isolated users are exactly whom interdiction must perfectly intercept while harm reduction can still reach. B never overcame the historical pattern of record seizures coexisting with cheap supply and rising deaths, and their answers to pressure questions about replacement and choke points remained speculative. A's closing crystallized the decisive comparison—perfection upstream versus imperfect presence at the moment of risk—that B could not answer without simply restating assertions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's turn of the counterfeit-pill scenario in Rebuttal 1, which showed that unsuspecting isolated users are precisely the cases interdiction must stop completely while harm reduction can still reach, and A's subsequent crystallization of the "perfection versus partial success" framework that persisted unanswered through the close.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by effectively turning Side A's strongest argument—the danger to unsuspecting, isolated users of counterfeit pills—into a fatal flaw for the CON side. Side B logically demonstrated that because interdiction cannot achieve the perfection required to stop every lethal envelope, those isolated users will still die unless harm reduction (like household naloxone saturation) is present. Side B's weighing of "imperfect protection present at the moment of risk" versus "perfect interdiction promised but historically failing" was decisive and rhetorically sharp. Side A's reliance on future precursor interdiction lacked the causal grounding to overcome the historical reality of supply replacement and the iron law of prohibition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they flipped the "isolated user" argument by pointing out that interdiction demands impossible perfection to stop every single pill, whereas harm reduction only requires reaching the household once with naloxone to save that same user.
