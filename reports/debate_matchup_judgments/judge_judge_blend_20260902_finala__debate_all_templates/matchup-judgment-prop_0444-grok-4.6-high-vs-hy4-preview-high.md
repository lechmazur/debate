### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a competitive debate by preserving the clearer causal case: minimum standards reduce leakage and exploitation by changing incentives for firms seeking access to a large market. Side A raised serious verification and development concerns, but repeatedly treated equivalence as identical EU pricing despite the proposition specifying minimum standards. Its concession that targeted forced-labor restrictions and due diligence are legitimate also weakened its claim that enforceable process conditions are inherently coercive or unworkable. Side B better weighed imperfect enforcement against leaving harmful production commercially rewarded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s decisive response was that Side A’s poverty argument depended on equating a minimum climate floor with matching Europe’s carbon price. The later clarification of an emissions-intensity standard was consistent with B’s original comparable-floor framing, while A never fully answered why dirty production should retain unrestricted access merely because cleaner compliance is harder for poorer producers.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively demonstrating that 'equivalent treatment' for climate standards (even if measured by emissions intensity) inherently punishes developing-world producers reliant on poor national grids. Side A successfully forced Side B into a dilemma between unworkable equivalence and narrow product bans that do not require mirror clauses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 effectively distinguished between targeted bans on forced labor (which are workable) and systemic mirror clauses (which punish poor producers for relying on developing-world infrastructure like coal grids).
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by constructing a decisive "fatal fork" in the later rounds. Side A forces Side B to define the climate standard: if it demands equivalence to EU carbon costs, it punishes developing nations for poverty; if it is redefined as a basic emissions-intensity floor, it still requires capital poor nations lack or collapses into narrow due-diligence regimes Side A already supports. Side B's retreat from "equivalent treatment" to "intensity" in Rebuttal 2 conceded the definitional high ground and failed to resolve the structural burden of how poor nations meet these standards without being penalized. Side A's distinction between narrow, definable bans (like forced labor) and broad systemic mirror clauses was highly persuasive and largely unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which crystallized the "fatal fork" by showing that Side B's redefinition of the climate floor to "emissions-intensity" either still punished poor producers on coal-heavy grids or reduced the motion to existing narrow bans, rendering the broad mirror clause unenforceable or redundant.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO won a clear but competitive debate by preserving two routes: preventing unfair undercutting inside the EU and using market access to induce compliance. CON sharply distinguished product measurement from judging labor politics and exposed possible diversion, but never showed how its targeted alternatives would address systemic labor abuses without meaningful sanctions. PRO also limited the policy to universal minima, phased support, review, and proportional enforcement, weakening the wall analogy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s second rebuttal was decisive: it directly accepted that persistent noncompliance eventually loses access while answering the feared single-bureaucrat veto with monitoring, rebuttal rights, pattern-based findings, and proportional action. This made enforcement look conditional and reviewable rather than automatically exclusionary.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively dismantling Side B's key examples and exposing a contradiction in Side B's mechanism. Side A drew a decisive distinction between metering a product (CBAM) and judging a polity (labor laws), proving that the latter results in market exclusion rather than a price adjustment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's flip of the Rana Plaza example was the decisive moment of the debate. Side B used it to show enforcement works; Side A weaponized it by pointing out that Rana Plaza succeeded because buyers stayed and funded upgrades, whereas Side B's mirror clauses enforce compliance by withdrawing buyers and shutting the door.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively distinguishing between metering physical products (like carbon or factory safety) and adjudicating foreign labor politics (like strike laws and retaliation), exposing the mechanical and political flaws in PRO's enforcement model. Side A also effectively leveraged the Rana Plaza example to demonstrate that withdrawing market access defunds the very upgrades needed to improve conditions, resulting in trade leakage rather than a raised global floor. Side B's responses were competent but relied on assertions of leverage without adequately solving the empirical reality of shifted trade flows and extraterritorial overreach.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled PRO's answers to the pressure questions by showing that independent monitors do not convert a complex political labor verdict into a simple customs reading, and that PRO's admitted end-state of exclusion inevitably causes leakage without achieving the proposed benefits.
