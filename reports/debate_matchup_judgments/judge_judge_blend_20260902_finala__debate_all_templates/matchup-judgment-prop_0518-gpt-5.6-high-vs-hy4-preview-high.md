CON conceded receipt proves expenditure not necessity, and its broad exceptions narrowed to investment-triggered exceptions collapse toward PRO. Targeted tools plus fee-shifting/public enforcement answered CON's litigation-chill pressure better than CON answered the necessity gap.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully exposing a critical timing flaw in Side B's framework. Side A consistently argued that employers need predictable protection at the time of hiring or training to justify investments, whereas Side B's remedies (like proving imminent risk of trade-secret theft) only materialize at departure. Side A's framing of this as a "timing failure" that ultimately reduces worker opportunities went largely unresolved by Side B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal decisively exposed the flaw in Side B's reliance on 'inevitable use' and 'imminent threat', noting that these counterfactuals cannot guide a startup deciding today whether to share product plans tomorrow.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON's live route - ex ante timing - survived: PRO requires a specific secret, rival role and threatened misuse to justify restraint, which cannot guide a startup deciding today whether to share product plans or fund training. B answered that courts can enjoin later, which conceded A's point that prevention arrives too late and that "inevitable use" is just a court-imposed noncompete. PRO's elasticity attack on "material" and "substantial" was partly answered by A's conjunctive verifiable criteria excluding baristas/deli workers, leaving CON with the cleaner weighing: broad, objective eligibility with notice, consideration and scope limits preserves mobility for ordinary workers while protecting embedded goodwill/research that NDAs cannot prevent after transfer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 - [A4.3]-[A4.4]: PRO's narrow-risk test needs a future rival and threatened misuse that don't exist at investment time, so garden-leave-or-sue leaves firms to withhold access; and inevitable-disclosure injunction is just a late, hurried court noncompete versus a transparent ex ante bargain.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a cleaner, more decisive framework: the party creating the business risk should internalize its cost, either by paying for exclusivity (garden leave) or proving a concrete, imminent threat. Side A effectively weaponized Side B's "broad exceptions" as elastic loopholes that shift the litigation burden onto workers, and successfully turned Side B's strongest argument (the timing and predictability problem of proving risk at departure) into a feature of their own case. Side A argued that the inability to prove a specific threat prospectively is exactly why employers shouldn't get free, preemptive bans on worker mobility. Side B argued well, particularly on the limits of after-the-fact trade-secret law, but could not overcome Side A's compelling "pay or prove" default and the practical reality that broad exceptions swallow the rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they directly answered Side B's pressure questions by flipping the "timing" critique into a defense of garden leave, crystallizing the principle that employers must internalize the cost of exclusivity rather than conscripting careers for free.
