### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON built the more durable path to victory by identifying and exploiting a structural contradiction in PRO's case: every mechanism PRO needed to make disconnection actually work (barring brokers from compiling cross-domain profiles, barring institutions from penalizing opt-outs) is itself the regulatory "fix the data/fix the use" regime CON proposed as an alternative — meaning the personal right to disconnect is either redundant (where that regime exists) or actively harmful (where it doesn't, since silent inference and "culture fit" rejections can't be legislated away). CON's "self-defeating signal" and "outsider's ladder" arguments were pressed hard from the opening through the closing and were never squarely neutralized; PRO's B4.1 answer to the pressure questions inadvertently conceded the key point, which CON immediately weaponized in Rebuttal 2 and the closing ("PRO's closing conceded the debate and called it a victory"). PRO's context-collapse and dignity arguments were substantive and vivid but ultimately didn't survive this redundancy/scarlet-letter double bind.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1–A2.2, where CON catches PRO's own credit-bureau analogy as describing regulation rather than disconnection, and A4.3–A4.4, where CON shows PRO's answer to the pressure question (B4.1) collapses the "right to disconnect" into CON's alternative regulatory remedy, making the distinctive personal right redundant or a trap.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A persuasively established context collapse and distinguished legitimate domain-specific records from omnibus scoring. However, Side B showed that uniform limits on collection and use address irrelevant cross-domain data without creating an opt-out signal. Side A never adequately resolved voluntary disclosure and hidden adverse inference, making its individual right potentially stigmatizing or redundant. Side A's repeated nonexistent C reference IDs were also an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: if strong use limits and anti-penalty rules exist, personal disconnection adds little; without them, higher-rated applicants can volunteer scores while opting out becomes a scarlet letter. Side A answered relevance exceptions well but did not escape this dilemma.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): CON won the core clash on remedy comparison. PRO identified real harms of cross-domain context collapse, but B showed irrelevance is cured by universal use-limits/permissible-purpose bans without an opt-out, and that an opt-out unravels because high scorers volunteer scores and silent inference ("culture fit") can't be policed. B's framing that PRO's own enforcement — broker barred from compiling plus anti-penalty rules — is B's regime wearing PRO's clothes makes the distinctive act of personal disconnection redundant where regulation works and a scarlet letter where it doesn't. PRO never solved that signaling trap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B R1/B R2: exposing that PRO's credit-bureau model and A4.1 anti-penalty regime concedes CON's solution, and that law can forbid the question but never the volunteering, turning "disconnect" into "door marked suspect."

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases, but PRO (B) controlled the framing throughout with the memorable "sentence, not reputation" line and consistently forced CON to defend a regulation-only remedy using CON's own best example (credit reporting) as proof that accuracy mandates and dispute rights fail even in their ideal setting. PRO's pressure questions were sharper and more decisive: Q1 exposed that CON's proposed fix already exists and already fails, and Q2 forced CON to admit that punishing disconnection amounts to coercion, which PRO then used as the crux of its closing. CON's strongest material — adverse selection, the historic exclusion analogy, and the argument that non-criminal harms (scams, harassment, reckless driving) need portable tracking because civil suits are impractical for ordinary people — was substantive and never fully neutralized, and CON effectively exploited PRO's "yes" concession on banning preference for verified histories to argue this levels honest record-builders down to bad actors. But PRO's closing directly answered that specific attack (references/credentials remain leverageable) while CON's closing mostly recapped earlier themes without directly engaging PRO's final rebuttal, a real cost under the "recap-only closing" penalty. PRO's rebuttal 2 being clipped is a genuine execution cost, but it lands after PRO had already delivered its key answers (A1/A2) and the core "coercion" and "credit-reporting failure" points, so the loss is modest. On balance PRO wins a clear but competitive decision on stronger framing, sharper pressure-question follow-through, and a closing that more precisely resolved the decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's use of credit reporting in [B2.2]/[B3.1] — turning CON's own proposed remedy (accuracy mandates, dispute rights) into proof that regulation fails even in its best-case industry — was the most decisive exchange, and CON's answer ("under-enforcement, not conceptual failure") never fully closed that gap.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that opting out shifts genuine risk onto third parties, especially where harmful patterns never produce convictions. Side A’s civil suits, references, and court records did not adequately replace cross-platform warnings, while its non-retaliation model effectively barred gatekeepers from using verified histories. The coercion framing was memorable but did not prove that legitimate risk assessment should be prohibited. Side A also incorrectly maintained that its credit-regulation question went unanswered after Side B directly answered it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response to Side A’s court-record fallback was decisive: harassment, scams, reckless conduct, and lease violations often remain non-criminal yet still predict future harm. Side A’s proposed substitutes were reactive, burdensome, and ineffective against platform hopping, leaving B’s third-party-risk argument live through the closing.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): CON won the central trade-off. PRO's best pressure on credit-reporting failure was met by B4.1's democratization + enforcement fix, and PRO's safety answer (civil suits/references) failed against B's non-conviction harms where working-class victims can't litigate. The decisive concession was A4.4: Yes, the right requires banning preference for verified histories. B4.3 turned that into punishment of honest workers who built records and into forced blind risk, shifting cost to vulnerable renters/riders via deposits and gut-instinct bias, which was better weighed than PRO's coercion frame. Clipping of A4 also cost PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 A2 + B4.3 - using PRO's Q2 admission that law must forbid preferring verified applicants to show disconnection outlaws transparency, harms honest workers' ability to leverage reputation, and forces risk onto the public for non-criminal misconduct.
