### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): PRO wins this debate, though narrowly. The decisive moment was PRO's identification of a genuine tension in CON's case: CON argued simultaneously that 18-20-year-olds almost never purchase firearms at retail [A1.1] and that closing retail access would generate a massive new black-market customer base [A2.7]. PRO labeled this the "fatal contradiction" in B4.3 and it is a real one. CON's resolution—law-abiding sport shooters go off-book, building networks that later serve dangerous people—is creative and coherent as a second-order harm theory, but PRO's closing counter ("they simply wait") is more plausible: low-risk recreational buyers blocked by a three-year gap do not typically seek federal-crime-risk criminal networks for a sporting rifle; they defer. The network-effects chain requires too many speculative links to outweigh PRO's tighter mechanism. On suicide specifically, PRO's strongest ground—impulsivity, brief crisis window, lethality differential, substitute methods genuinely less fatal—holds up across the exchange. CON's counter that committed individuals pick the next most lethal option implicitly concedes the impulsive-crisis population PRO is targeting. CON's state-level evidence claim [A2.3] (no states showed large drops) is the most damaging empirical challenge, but PRO disputes it directly in B4.2 and neither side can verify; the draw on that subpoint leaves PRO's suicide mechanism intact. CON's network-effects argument is the most original contribution in the debate and prevents a larger PRO victory, but it was not enough to flip the key clash on impulsive suicide or to fully resolve the contradiction PRO exposed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B4.3 "fatal contradiction" turn: PRO showed that CON cannot coherently argue both that retail is not the primary acquisition path for this cohort and that banning retail will generate an unprecedented new wave of black-market buyers. CON's resolution through second-order network effects was coherent but speculative, and PRO's clean rejoinder—law-abiding buyers simply wait rather than turn to criminal networks—was the most decisive single exchange in the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won by establishing a cleaner, more direct causal mechanism: legal purchase friction acts as a circuit breaker for impulsive homicides and suicides during a high-risk developmental window. While Side B offered a clever network-effect counter-story, it relied on a speculative chain (law-abiding buyers becoming illegal customers en masse) that Side A effectively undermined with common-sense rebuttals. A also landed the sharper historical point about the 1968 handgun law’s long-gun loophole, and B never recovered on suicide substitution after conceding that no immediately available alternative matches firearm lethality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 exposure of Side B’s tension—simultaneously claiming 18-20-year-olds rarely buy retail yet warning that closing retail will spawn a massive black market—followed by A’s clear defense of the friction mechanism against B’s pressure questions.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won by effectively dismantling Side B's core tradeoff. While B introduced a creative argument that banning retail purchases would push low-risk buyers into the black market, thereby building criminal networks, A successfully pressured this chain by noting that law-abiding youth are far more likely to simply wait than risk felony charges to navigate gang networks. A also cleanly neutralized B's 1968 law example by highlighting the long-gun loophole and maintained a stronger, more grounded causal story on how immediate friction disrupts impulsive suicide and violence. B's case was logical but relied on speculative downstream effects that A's behavioral rebuttal undermined.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 and 2 effectively turned B's 1968 historical example by pointing out the long-gun loophole, and sharply exposed the tension in B's claim that youth neither buy retail nor would struggle to access black markets, forcing B onto the defensive regarding network formation.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): The motion requires a comparative judgment: does this policy reduce homicide and suicide rates *more than* it increases illegal acquisition or substitution? B held that comparative frame consistently throughout the debate, while A never met it. B's core mechanism—legal purchase plus impulse window equals preventable death, and the barrier of finding an illegal source kills the impulse—was introduced in the opening and was never squarely rebutted. A's best points (household guns for suicide, self-defense necessity for young adults) were each partially answered: B conceded roughly a third of suicides involve self-purchased guns and noted the specific risk window for first-time solo buyers. A's main counter-theory—that legal restriction drives young adults into black markets, funding gangs and compounding violence—was asserted with increasing rhetorical force but never quantified. B correctly identified in R1 and again in Rebuttal 2 that A had not even attempted to show those secondary deaths would exceed the direct impulsive deaths prevented, which is the exact comparison the motion requires. A's own admission that violent offenders overwhelmingly use illegal guns was then turned against it by B [B4.3]: if those offenders already bypass retail, then the legal purchases that do occur are precisely the marginal, preventable ones this policy targets. The "asymmetric trap" framing in A's later speeches was rhetorically tidy but rested on the unquantified premise that syndicate expansion would outweigh impulse prevention—a premise A never supported and B explicitly called out at every stage. B's Rebuttal 2 was the decisive exchange, cleanly resolving the pressure questions by turning A's admissions into proof of B's comparative claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 [B4.3–B4.5]: B turned A's own repeated claim that violent offenders already use illegal guns into confirmation that legally purchased guns are exactly the marginal, preventable subset this policy targets, then landed the clean comparative indictment—A had spent the entire debate proving this policy won't stop all gun deaths, which was never the claim, while never once estimating how many additional deaths their illegal-market expansion theory would actually produce. This closed the comparative gap A needed to win the motion.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won by cleanly isolating the comparative burden and exposing Side B's failure to quantify harms. Side A demonstrated that the policy targets marginal, preventable impulsive deaths during the highest-risk developmental window, while Side B could only assert speculative black-market expansion without showing it would cause more deaths than it prevents. Side B's concession that violent offenders often already use illegal channels unintentionally reinforced PRO's argument that restricting legal purchases blocks a distinct, causally important subset of deaths. Side A's pressure questions went unanswered with any actual comparative estimate, leaving their causal pathway as the only fully defended route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4), where they turned Side B's claim that most youth gun violence already involves illegal weapons into proof that legal retail purchases represent the exact marginal, preventable subset the policy would stop, while noting Side B never offered even a rough estimate of deaths caused by increased illegal acquisition.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A wins by establishing and enforcing a clear comparative weighing framework. PRO consistently argued that the policy targets a narrow, high-risk window for impulsive violence and demanded CON quantify how many deaths the alleged black-market shift would cause to outweigh those prevented. CON never met this burden, relying instead on unquantified claims about systemic gang violence, defensive disarmament, and method substitution. PRO’s rebuttals directly exposed this evasion, particularly in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, where PRO crystallized the clash around CON’s failure to compare magnitudes. While CON raised legitimate concerns about illicit markets and household access, they repeatedly sidestepped the core comparative question of the motion, leaving PRO’s causal story and weighing framework as the cleaner, more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 and Closing sequence. PRO directly answers CON’s pressure questions, dismisses the speculative scale of the black-market shift, and forcefully highlights that CON has spent the entire debate proving the policy will not stop all deaths without ever showing it would cause more deaths than it prevents. This squarely neutralizes CON’s route to victory by exposing an unmet comparative burden and cleanly resolves the central clash.
