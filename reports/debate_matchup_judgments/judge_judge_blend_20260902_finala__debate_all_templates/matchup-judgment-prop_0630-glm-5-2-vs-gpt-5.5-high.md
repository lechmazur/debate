### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO built the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Its decisive weapon was the mutation-breeding comparison: radiation- and chemical-induced random mutagenesis is far less targeted than CRISPR yet receives no full GMO review, which cuts the legs out from CON's "irreversibility plus novelty" framework. CON responded that CRISPR's precision is itself the danger because it enables changes natural selection would never reach, but this argument is circular—mutation breeding produces more random and unpredictable changes, not fewer, so if unexpected effects are the concern, random mutagenesis is the bigger hazard. CON never resolved this tension across any round, leaving the core consistency charge alive at the close. PRO's "process superstition, not precaution" framing made the tension memorable and easy for a reader to weigh. PRO also answered its own pressure questions more cleanly: it specified concrete legal-design constraints (no foreign DNA, defined edit types, categorical exclusions, mandatory notification, escalation criteria), which was a direct and specific response to CON's slippery-slope concern. CON's counter—commercial pressure erodes all categories—is a real worry, but PRO's response that expansion requires rulemaking, not a company's preference, was never squarely neutralized. CON's strongest moment was catching PRO's concession that no pre-market review catches every emergent effect and arguing that a streamlined pathway therefore leaves more gaps. PRO's reply—staged review, field trials, monitoring—was sound but gave CON the correct rejoinder that these tools already exist in legacy GMO frameworks, leaving the question of what exactly is being streamlined. This was a genuine hit, but it didn't erase PRO's deeper structural advantage: CON never explained why the chemical/radiation mutagenesis precedent should not govern proportional regulation of gene editing. PRO's closing line—"proportional regulation is not convenience over caution; it is how caution becomes competent"—crystallized its case cleanly. CON's "made caution cheaper and called that reform" was equally sharp rhetoric, but PRO had done more argumentative work to earn its frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive rebuttal exchange was PRO's "process superstition, not precaution" charge in Rebuttal 1, built on the mutation-breeding comparison. CON's attempt to turn this around in Rebuttal 1—arguing CRISPR's precision enables changes conventional selection would never reach—actually undermines CON's own framework: if unpredictable change is the hazard, deliberate random mutagenesis should be the bigger concern, not the more targeted tool. CON never reconciled this across the remaining rounds, leaving PRO's proportionality argument structurally intact through the close.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): B won by maintaining a consistent focus on irreversibility and structural expansion risks, forcing PRO into concessions on pre-market limits that B then used to show why lowering the bar increases unmanageable exposure; PRO's proportionality argument was never fully refuted on the ground but was neutralized by the unanswered incentives problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Rebuttal 2 turning PRO's own staged-review concession into proof that lighter rules leave bigger gaps on emergent ecological effects.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by consistently framing the debate around irreversibility and the unique risks of bypassing evolutionary filters. CON effectively turned PRO’s scientific comparisons (e.g., arguing mutation breeding’s messiness is self-limiting while CRISPR’s precision efficiently commercializes novel risks) and leveraged PRO’s concession that no pre-market review catches all scale-dependent harms to argue that a lighter pathway exacerbates, rather than solves, the core safety problem. CON also directly answered pressure questions and neutralized PRO’s democratization claim by noting that lowering regulatory barriers without IP reform simply accelerates incumbent capture. PRO argued proportionality and regulatory competence well, but ultimately failed to overcome CON’s risk calculus or fully answer the structural pressures that would expand the “limited” category.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s A2.2 and A4.2, which flipped PRO’s mutation-breeding comparison by arguing precision enables efficiently commercialized novel risks, and used PRO’s own concession about the limits of pre-market review to demonstrate why a streamlined pathway dangerously magnifies irreversibility concerns.
