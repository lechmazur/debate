### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A edges this debate on three grounds. First, the motion asks whether credential inflation rose *more* than genuine opportunity expanded — a comparative burden that required Side A only to show the dominant effect, not to deny all gains. Side A consistently argued this framing while Side B too often answered the weaker question ("does a degree beat having no degree?"). Second, Side A's timing argument — that degree requirements for unchanged roles appeared after graduate supply surged, not as tasks genuinely transformed — is the most causally precise move in the debate. Side B's counter (office work now involves database tools and compliance) is plausible but cannot fully explain why the administrative-assistant archetype flipped to requiring a four-year credential rather than a brief upskilling course. Third, on epistemic discipline, Side A used appropriately cautious language ("stubbornly high," "near zero," "significant share"), while Side B stacked specific numbers throughout — 60–70% lifetime premium, 20–25% bottom-quartile gain, 10–15% vocational disadvantage — none verifiable in a closed-book setting. That brittle precision weakens Side B's credibility under the scoring criteria. Side B's strongest move was calling PRO's counterfactual "fictional" in the closing, but Side A had already established (A4.1) that the counterfactual is historical, not invented: those jobs demonstrably did not require degrees before expansion. Side A's repeated clipping is a real execution cost that kept this close, and Side B's clean formatting and the "fictional counterfactual" line were genuine strengths, but they did not neutralize the core causal story Side A built.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was on the counterfactual baseline. Side B pressed (B3.2) that a positive earnings lift versus no degree disproves the inflation claim. Side A answered (A4.3–A4.4) by reframing: the correct comparison is to the pre-expansion world where the marginal graduate's target job required no degree at all, making the added cost of the credential a pure inflationary barrier rather than a real value add. Side B's closing called this "fictional," but Side A had already grounded it historically, leaving Side B's response looking like label-switching rather than a genuine rebuttal.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won by maintaining a consistent, evidence-grounded defense that earnings premiums and new high-skill roles outweigh marginal cases, directly answering pressure questions on net returns and causation; Side B pressed a strong counterfactual but could not neutralize the persistent positive returns shown by A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 answering B's pressure questions on bottom-quartile net gains versus vocational tracks and skill evolution in routine roles.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing the debate around the marginal graduate and the correct policy counterfactual (a world without expansion). This exposed Side B's core metric (comparing graduates to non-graduates today) as circular, effectively conceding that the baseline requirement had inflated. Side A's causal mechanism (supply surge enabling cheap employer filtering) and rhetorical framing directly addressed the motion's "more than" weighing. Side B offered competent defenses on vocational trajectories and modern task complexity but failed to answer why the shifted baseline shouldn't count against expansion, ultimately losing the decisive framing clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing dismantled Side B's reliance on the aggregate graduate premium by correctly identifying the "degree vs. no degree today" comparison as the wrong counterfactual for an expansion policy, reframing the marginal graduate's forced credential purchase as a costly treadmill rather than genuine opportunity.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Both sides constructed coherent, substantive cases, but CON built the stronger path to victory on the decisive comparative question. CON's two anchor points—the persistent wage premium despite supply expansion, and the historically accurate counterfactual for first-gen and minority students—were never cleanly dismantled by PRO. PRO's "statistical artifact" rebuttal to the premium is weaker than CON's economic response: employers would not sustain a 60–80% lifetime premium for pure filtering if the work were unchanged. PRO's circularity argument (expansion caused non-college wage collapse, making the degree necessary but not genuinely beneficial) is the strongest live point PRO holds, but CON substantially neutralized it in B4.3 by identifying that the pre-expansion counterfactual for the marginal student was not a stable union job but exclusion from the knowledge economy entirely. PRO's "the gate simply moved" response to this is thin and was never properly developed. On the comparative framing the motion requires—was inflation the dominant effect over genuine opportunity?—CON's case that millions gained structural access to professional life while the premium held is a more compelling answer to that specific question. Execution costs on both sides partially offset: CON had two clipped speeches (real format cost) while PRO cited nonexistent reference IDs C1.2 and C1.3, deployed some brittle numerical precision, and left CON's strongest counterfactual claim inadequately answered through closing. CON's closing reframe around "who gets a chance at all" versus "net salary" sharpened rather than evaded the motion, because "genuine opportunity" as written does not require a purely economic definition, and the structural widening of access is a legitimate measure. The margin is competitive but real.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.3 response in Rebuttal 2 is the most decisive exchange. PRO had argued throughout that "the gate has simply moved" for first-gen students. CON countered that the pre-expansion non-college alternative for women, minorities, and first-generation workers was not the stable, high-paying union job PRO implied—it was precarious low-wage work with no ladder or outright professional exclusion. This directly undercut PRO's circularity argument by showing the counterfactual population was never in the union-job baseline PRO's net-present-value comparison assumed. PRO never recovered a clean response to this historical reframe.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A (CON) prevails: its counterfactual framing of access for first-gen/minority students and persistence of the wage premium due to real skill demand directly neutralized PRO's median-flatness and debt arguments across rebuttals and pressure answers, showing opportunity gains dominate inflation. Side B offered competent inflation points but weaker weighing of who was previously locked out.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A1/A2 answers on wage premium (employer valuation of skills, not filter) and debt trajectories (lifetime growth vs. collapsed non-degree path) cleanly rebutted B's net-present-value metric and shifted the comparison to structural enlargement.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B wins by successfully reframing the core metric of "genuine opportunity." While Side A cleanly argued that stagnant net financial returns prove credential inflation dominates, Side B effectively contextualized A's static NPV snapshot by introducing career growth, composition effects, and the structural collapse of the non-degree alternative. B's pressure questions forced A to commit to a narrow financial metric, which B then outweighed by demonstrating that access to a transformed knowledge economy—and the lifetime insurance it provides against low-skill wage collapse—constitutes a deeper, more genuine expansion of opportunity. B's closing crystallization ("PRO measures opportunity by net salary... CON measures it by who gets a chance at all") decisively resolved the clash in CON's favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over the college wage premium. A dismissed it as a statistical artifact of a shrinking non-college baseline, but B effectively dismantled this by explaining employer incentives (why pay a 60-80% premium for a mere filter?) and contextualizing the premium as proof of genuine skill demand in a transformed economy, turning A's own data point into evidence for structural opportunity.
