### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON identified a genuine two-part empirical burden in the proposition—measurable harm reduction AND no significant censorship increase—and attacked both prongs systematically throughout. The most decisive move was CON's conversion of PRO's own concession (B2.3: "the climate organizer just has to opt in") into a structural admission: if most minors never override the default, the default is not a modest friction but a baseline exclusion from public discourse. PRO tried to reframe this as "the default working," but that reframe only restated the censorship concern rather than answering it. On harm reduction, PRO's causal chain (less data → less targeting → less harm) was plausible but thinly supported; CON's objections—predator adaptation, age-gate bypass, no link-by-link evidence—remained live going into the closing. PRO's analogies (seatbelts, car seats, helmets) were effective for proportionality but CON's inversion was sharper: seatbelts protect the wearer without restricting movement, while privacy defaults restrict the minor's own reach, reversing the direction of force. PRO's rhetorical comparative weighing in B4.5-4.6 was its strongest passage, but it arrived after the critical admission had already been weaponized. CON crystallized the clash cleanly: "documented audience restriction versus speculative harm reduction" is the right frame under the proposition's own burden. PRO never escaped it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.5-A2.6 / A4.3-A4.4 sequence: CON took PRO's literal concession that most minors "just have to opt in" and showed it concedes real, measurable, admitted audience suppression—then argued PRO rebranding the chilling effect as a "safety feature" is paternalism, not analysis. This exchange converted PRO's own language into CON's strongest proof-point and was never cleanly answered.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): B won by making the decisive clash clearer: the motion required both measurable harm reduction and no significant censorship, and B consistently argued that default audience restrictions suppress minors’ public reach in a concrete, sticky way. A had plausible causal reasoning on safety, but leaned on somewhat thin evidence and repeatedly rebranded the opt-in burden as merely a “safety feature” rather than fully answering B’s functional censorship argument. B also effectively challenged A’s analogies and exposed enforcement/age-verification problems, though the debate remained competitive because A’s privacy-versus-content distinction was plausible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was turning A’s “they can still opt in” defense into evidence that defaults matter: if most minors will not override the setting, then the policy functionally restricts public reach at scale, undermining the claim that censorship does not significantly increase.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON successfully pressure-tested the proposition's dual burden. PRO was forced to concede that most minors would not opt out of restrictive defaults, which CON effectively crystallized as a significant, measurable restriction on the reach of minors' speech—fulfilling the censorship condition the proposition disclaimed. Meanwhile, PRO's harm-reduction case relied on causal-chain reasoning that never fully bridged to documented harm reduction under direct questioning. CON's burden analysis and cleaner comparative weighing in the closing gave it the decisive edge after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (A4), where it seized on PRO's admission that most minors would not opt in to public audiences, reframed the safety default as an installed baseline of censorship, and crystallized the clash as documented audience restriction versus speculative harm reduction.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins for three compounding reasons. First, the censorship definition clash resolves in PRO's favor: the ordinary meaning of censorship is content removal or suppression by authority, not reduced reach to non-consenting third parties. CON's "censorship by architecture" is conceptually interesting but too broad — under that definition every privacy law, every "friends only" setting, and every parental control is censorship, rendering the motion's own test meaningless. PRO correctly identified this and held the line. Second, PRO landed the decisive internal-consistency trap (B2.6): CON cannot simultaneously cite default-effects research to warn that marginalized teens won't opt in AND deny that powerful defaults protect minors. CON's Rebuttal 2 tried to dissolve this as "two separate failures" but never actually escaped the contradiction — if defaults are structurally decisive, default-private is decisively protective. Third, PRO's reframe of the coming-out scenario was more compelling than CON's: the greater documented risk is a closeted teen involuntarily broadcast to hostile parents or predators, not a teen who consciously wants global reach being limited to fifty friends. CON's strongest counter — that DM-blocking already exists voluntarily — was a sharp narrow point, and the "wrong harms" argument on bullying is genuinely live, but PRO correctly noted the motion only claims to measurably reduce some documented harms, not all harms. The harm reduction bar is met; the censorship bar is met under the standard the word actually carries. CON's arguments were well-developed and pressured PRO throughout, but the censorship definition and the default-effects contradiction were never resolved, which leaves PRO's core path to victory intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's internal-consistency trap at B2.6 — "the opponent cannot both cite default-effects research to claim opt-in rates will be low AND claim the policy does nothing" — was the most decisive single exchange. CON used default-effects evidence to argue marginalized teens would be trapped by private defaults, yet also argued the policy fails to reduce harm. PRO correctly identified that the same premise that drives CON's censorship argument is the premise that makes the intervention effective. CON's Rebuttal 2 tried to frame these as "two separate failures of the same policy" but that reframe still accepted the power of defaults, which surrenders the strongest anti-harm ground and never refutes the core logical trap.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): A won because it kept the debate tied to the motion: default privacy settings plausibly reduce exposure, data harvesting, targeting, and unwanted contact while leaving minors able to post and opt into wider reach. B's strongest route was censorship by reduced reach, but it depended on an overbroad definition of censorship and repeated unsupported EU claims. B also never fully answered that the motion need only measurably reduce some real harms, not solve every adolescent online harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's decisive rebuttal was the coming-out flip: B's example of reduced reach was reframed as protection against involuntary exposure, making private defaults look like audience control rather than censorship.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won by offering a narrower, more coherent reading of the motion. They successfully framed privacy-by-design as a default-setting intervention that reduces specific, measurable harms (data harvesting, predatory exposure) without suppressing content. Their rebuttal that reducing audience size is "audience selection" rather than censorship provided a clean answer to CON's strongest argument, and their involuntary-outing reframe made default-public settings look like the real harm. CON (Side A) landed solid blows on default stickiness and the "censorship by architecture" concept, but their case required stretching the definition of censorship beyond ordinary usage—a reductio PRO effectively exploited. The debate was competitive, but PRO maintained the clearer path to victory after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 reframing of default-private as protection from involuntary exposure, and their reductio that CON's definition of censorship would classify every privacy law as censorship, decisively blunted CON's architecture argument and shifted the baseline in PRO's favor.
