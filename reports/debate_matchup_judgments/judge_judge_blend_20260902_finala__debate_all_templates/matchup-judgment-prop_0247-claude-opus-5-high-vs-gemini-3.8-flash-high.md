### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that the financial burden diminishes as consumers avoid the taxed product, while reduced liquid-sugar consumption plausibly yields health benefits concentrated among the same disadvantaged households. Side A effectively established partial inelasticity and challenged earmarked revenue, but its decisive claim—that displaced soda calories would produce zero net health gain—remained an unsupported assumption. Side B also correctly made revenue a secondary benefit rather than a necessary premise. The clipped opening and some overstated binaries kept the debate competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s closing attack on Side A’s answer about a 25% consumption reduction was decisive: Side A could establish harm only by assuming complete substitution and therefore no metabolic benefit. By identifying that assumption as the load-bearing premise, Side B preserved its comparative case that an avoidable monetary cost is outweighed by reduced health harm.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won because it exposed a load-bearing tension in CON's case: soda cannot simultaneously be an unavoidable necessity for the poor and a fully substitutable good with zero health gain. A's fork on this point, combined with a sharper funding challenge showing CON's progressive investments lacked any specified revenue source, left B defending a logical contradiction. CON's closing reframe that grocery liquidity is health was rhetorically strong but could not repair the structural gap in its substitution argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal (Turn 7) where it deployed the fork that CON cannot treat soda as both a discretionary comfort and an economic necessity, paired with the funding challenge that CON offered a wish list without a wallet.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO (A) kept the decisive fork alive: a soda tax is escapable via tap/diet/smaller sizes so financial burden shrinks as consumption falls, while diabetes cost is inescapable. CON (B) asserted both continued payment and full unhealthy substitution but never quantified when a marginal family is worse off, assuming 25% less soda yields zero gain. A answered Pressure Qs by distinguishing distrust vs danger and separating price-signal from revenue, while B conceded revenue is fungible yet still relied on the unproven full-substitution claim and offered an unfunded progressive-tax alternative. That clearer comparative path to health-progressive weighing gives A the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R1 contradiction trap — if families substitute they stop paying the tax, if they keep paying they haven't substituted, so CON cannot have severe extraction and zero health gain at once — plus the discretionary-vs-necessity fork in R2.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A sharply emphasized less regressive alternatives and exposed that B’s late dental-health claim did not isolate consumer price pressure as the causal mechanism. However, A’s central fork misread “even if” as requiring the tax to burden consumers rather than conceding that such burden may occur. The British levy remained a strong example of taxation meaningfully shifting sugary-drink consumption through reformulation, while B also defended the residual checkout burden by comparing it with disproportionate health harms. A asserted that mandates would achieve the same outcome more cleanly but did not establish that they dominate or make taxation unnecessary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response that Britain’s reformulation was induced by a beverage tax was decisive. It turned A’s preferred example into support for the proposition and undercut A’s attempted separation between taxation and supply-side reformulation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing that Side A's only concrete health outcome evidence (British pediatric dental extractions) came from a manufacturer levy deliberately structured to avoid regressive consumer burdens, while Side A's admission that it would defend the regressive version "unconditionally" undermined its own equity frame. Side B successfully maintained that when supply-side alternatives can achieve the same sugar reduction without billing the poorest, the motion's explicit tolerance of disproportionate burden is unnecessary and inequitable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The British levy fork: Side B turned Side A's central example into proof that health gains came from reformulation without regressive consumer burden, then exploited Side A's "unconditional" defense to frame the motion as a preference for punishing the poor even when a costless route to the same outcome exists.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): PRO won the central equity weighing and turned CON's best evidence. CON framed Britain as a non-tax, no-burden alternative, but PRO showed the Soft Drinks Industry Levy IS the tax the motion calls for and it works precisely by forcing reformulation and shifting consumption. That leaves CON's elasticity fork and substitution claims without a decisive answer to health regressivity: a temporary till friction vs lifelong diabetes, amputations and dental surgery. B's demand for a clinical endpoint was met with dental extractions, which B could only re-describe as supply-side success — still a tax success, consistent with motion's "even if" permission.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's [A2.1]/[B2.1] flip: using CON's own British example to prove beverage taxes drive reformulation, collapsing CON's tax vs reformulation dichotomy and exposing the misreading of 'even if' as 'only if'.
