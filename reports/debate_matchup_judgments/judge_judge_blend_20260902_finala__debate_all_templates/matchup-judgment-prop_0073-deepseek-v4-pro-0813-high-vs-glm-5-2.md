### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but CON sustained the more damaging line of attack across the debate. CON's "no stopping point" and finality/discharge objections forced PRO to narrow the proposition mid-debate (invoking "treaty-recognized" claims), which CON caught as an unlicensed goalpost shift not present in the original text. PRO's rebuttal calibration answer (appraisal, negotiation, constructive trusts) addressed feasibility but never supplied a principled mechanism for when an obligation is fully discharged, and CON's closing pressed exactly this unresolved gap without reply. CON also successfully turned PRO's own analogies (tenant, mortgage) against it by noting the absence of any recorded encumbrance and the destabilizing implications of voiding all long-settled title, including titles held by Indigenous nations themselves. PRO's rebuttals were solid (especially distinguishing land/rent from improvements), but ultimately left the "how does this ever end" challenge live at the close, which is the more decisive unresolved burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response in Rebuttal 2 (B4.1–B4.4), which reframed PRO's "wrongdoer controls the records" argument as proving too much (destabilizing all title, including Indigenous titles) and reasserted that no functioning legal system can operate without a discharge mechanism—an objection PRO's closing asserted was answered but never concretely resolved.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by grounding restitution in an identifiable continuing benefit and showing that mixed investment calls for calibration rather than no remedy. Side A’s finality objection was forceful but overstated: the motion permits land return or negotiated settlement, both of which can discharge a claim, so perpetual instability does not follow. Side B also answered the infinite-regress concern with a specific claimant, seizure, and beneficiary test.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s closing answer that the remedy itself clears the title defect decisively weakened Side A’s claim that restitution necessarily creates an undischargeable lien. Crediting later public investment also preserved restitution without claiming the institution’s entire present value.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON wins because PRO never solves the finality problem. PRO imports legal analogies—unjust enrichment, liens, mortgages—that all operate within systems of statutes of limitation, evidentiary thresholds, and discharge. When CON presses what makes any settlement binding or prevents future generations from reopening the claim, PRO can only assert that "the discharge is the remedy itself," which begs the question. CON successfully shows that PRO's framework creates a perpetual, unrecorded encumbrance on public property with no principled stopping point, making democratic governance impossible. This structural objection survives the full exchange largely intact, while PRO's strongest point—the intuitive ongoing-benefit argument—is successfully met by CON's calibration-compatible responses and the decisive no-finality turn.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing framing that PRO borrows the moral force of legal doctrines while discarding the architecture—finality, statutes of limitation, evidentiary standards—that makes them governable, leaving a "perpetual unrecorded lien."

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases, but CON sustained a single decisive throughline—the absence of a limiting principle, eligibility standard, or stopping rule in the motion—and never let it drop.
