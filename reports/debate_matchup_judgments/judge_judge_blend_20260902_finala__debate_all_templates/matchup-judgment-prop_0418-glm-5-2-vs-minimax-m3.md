### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO's decisive structural move was the "layered on top" framing, established clearly in Rebuttal 2 and maintained through closing: women-only is an opt-in addition to universal service, not a replacement. This absorbed CON's entire dead-zone and thin-market attack. When the pool empties, the rider falls back to baseline — she is no worse off for having had the option fail than she would be without it. CON pressed this point in closing ("when matching fails, the benefit vanishes — and the rider is back where she started") but never answered PRO's core logic: an option that helps some riders and harms none is a net positive regardless of coverage gaps. CON's second strongest move was the threshold question — what measurable harm justifies the complexity? PRO answered with behavioral evidence: persistent workarounds (location sharing, license-plate texts) despite existing universal features are the signal that those features haven't closed the trust gap. CON's "human caution" rebuttal was the weakest moment of the debate; it treated behavioral adaptation as trivially normal rather than engaging why those behaviors persist specifically in ride-hail contexts after years of universal safety improvements. CON's universal-tools-as-alternative argument was neutralized early by PRO's "complements, not substitutes" framing, and CON never reestablished that track. The trans/non-binary cost was real but underdeveloped by CON and PRO's "implementation question" dismissal, while thin, was not squarely refuted. On balance, PRO built a more coherent causal story, adapted well to pressure, and the "layered option" reframe was the cleanest single move in the debate — converting CON's concentrated-harm arguments into self-selected tradeoffs that riders voluntarily absorb.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 in Rebuttal 2: the dead-zone scenario only bites if women-only replaces universal service; it doesn't, so in thin markets the rider simply falls back to the existing pool. This directly neutralized CON's most repeated and rhetorically forceful attack — the claim that the option fails late-shift rural workers — by showing those riders are no worse off than before the option existed.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side A wins by establishing that universal safety tools already address core risk vectors while the women-only option delivers benefits only in dense markets and leaves late-shift/rural riders with the same fallback service they already reject; B never neutralizes the concentrated-benefit vs. universal-cost comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal 2 answers on precautionary behavior and thin-market dead zones, reframing B's "layered option" claim as ineffective for the riders the motion claims to serve.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins by successfully framing the women-only option as an opt-in layer with a standard-pool fallback, which neutralized Side A's strongest offense about dead zones and halved driver pools. PRO's use of persistent rider workarounds (location sharing, plate texting) as behavioral proof of an unmet trust deficit was more convincing than CON's attempt to dismiss them as routine caution. While CON effectively highlighted thin-market limitations and universal alternatives, PRO's second rebuttal clarified that fallback means no rider is worse off, making the tradeoff strictly voluntary. PRO's weighing of manageable platform complexity against concrete trust gains directly satisfied the motion's burden, whereas CON's case relied on treating an optional feature as a mandatory market redesign.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, which directly answered CON's pressure questions by clarifying the opt-in/fallback mechanism, dismantling the dead-zone and halved-pool arguments as attacks on a replacement model that was never proposed. This pivot reframed the clash around voluntary tradeoffs and decisively blunted CON's matching-inefficiency offense.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): CON won by establishing and sustaining the "scales inversely to risk" asymmetry: the women-only product works best precisely when danger is already lowest (dense urban, peak hours) and disappears where danger peaks (late night, low-density). PRO partially conceded the off-peak/low-density problem early and never fully neutralized the framing CON built on that concession. The "degrades gracefully vs. catastrophically" comparison in B4.3 was the debate's most decisive single argument, and PRO's A4.1 response — pointing to urban density as the typical case — was plausible but did not break the asymmetry logic. PRO's strongest contribution, that monitoring structurally cannot reach intimate-space harms (locked doors, silent assault, a hand on a knee), was genuinely powerful and CON only partially answered it with the same-gender assault point and the "demographic shift, not mechanism shift" reframe. PRO also left the trust-expansion and demand-recovery argument largely unanswered by CON, which kept a live route open but was not developed enough to outweigh the supply asymmetry. Two clipped speeches were real execution costs for PRO, leaving its A4.5 argument truncated at a key moment. CON's cleaner execution, sharper framing, and tighter management of the final clash gave it a clear but competitive win on the central question of whether the gains justify the complexity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.3 "degrades gracefully vs. catastrophically" in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive exchange. PRO had conceded the off-peak/low-density case as "harder" and offered the memorable but vulnerable line "a long wait you can choose is better than a short wait that carries risk." CON converted that modest concession into a structural asymmetry: universal monitoring loses cars but keeps the safety layer; the gender filter loses the match entirely. A safety feature that vanishes when danger is highest is not a safety feature. PRO's response in A4.1 — that most trips are urban and dense, so the typical dangerous trip is covered — was reasonable but didn't break the asymmetry, and the speech was clipped before the argument could be completed.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): CON wins by showing women-only rides scale inversely to risk (worst supply exactly when danger peaks) and fail to alter underlying mechanisms, while universal monitoring scales reliably; PRO concedes key limits without neutralizing the comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1-A2.6 and A4.2-A4.5 on catastrophic vs graceful degradation under thin supply, directly answering PRO concessions.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully framing the core tradeoff around supply degradation and risk timing. While Side B (PRO) made a compelling case about trust restoration and the structural limits of monitoring, it conceded that women-only matching struggles precisely when and where danger peaks. Side A capitalized on this with the decisive "graceful vs. catastrophic degradation" comparison, arguing that a safety feature scaling inversely to risk cannot justify its operational complexity. Side A directly answered pressure questions, maintained tighter comparative weighing throughout, and crystallized the clash more effectively in the closing. Side B's responses were competent but never fully neutralized the inverse-scaling liability or proved the complexity was justified across the risk spectrum.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which introduced the "graceful vs. catastrophic degradation" framework. It directly answered Side B's pressure on thin supply while turning Side B's own off-peak concession into a decisive comparative liability, showing why the gender filter fails the proposition's justification test exactly when safety matters most.
