### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides argued competently and stayed on-topic, but B did more decisive work pinning down what "too few standard-price screenings" actually requires. B's operational test—unmet demand/usable seats rather than mere inconvenience—directly engaged the resolution's wording and exposed that A's evidence (a single inconvenient 4:15 or 11pm slot) proved inconvenience, not quantitative insufficiency. A's strongest move, seizing on B's admission that sellouts only prove "valued seeing it then over skipping it" [B4.1], was rhetorically sharp but ignored B's immediate follow-up (theaters would capture unmet standard demand themselves if it existed) [B4.2], leaving that causal-incentive argument unanswered through the closing. B's closing crystallized the decisive burden cleanly ("usable seats, not symbolic showtimes"), while A's closing mostly re-asserted its cultural framing without closing the definitional gap B had exposed in Rebuttal 2 and the pressure-question exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answer (A2/A4.3, "usable seats, not symbolic showtimes") most decisively reframed the debate's deciding question and was never fully rebutted by A's closing, which instead leaned on the earlier "manufactured scarcity" framing without addressing B's refined threshold or the self-interest/incentive counter-argument.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that premium sellouts cannot establish preference for the premium format when affordable concurrent alternatives were removed. Side A offered a useful unmet-demand threshold, but effectively conceded the proposition by acknowledging that having no standard showing in a workable window can constitute too few screenings. Its closing then reverted to characterizing the harm as mere inconvenience rather than resolving that concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s manufactured-scarcity response was decisive: willingness to pay a surcharge rather than miss the desired time demonstrates demand for timing, not preference for premium technology. Side A’s answer admitted that distinction without explaining why revenue optimization should override the motion’s affordability condition.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO's manufactured-scarcity core survived: when prime hours have zero standard options, a premium sellout cannot prove preference for the surcharge. CON answered pressure better with a workable threshold, but that threshold (no standard in a workable window / sold-out standard while premium sits available) actually validates PRO's buried-showtime example. CON's convenience-vs-availability reframe was strong, yet PRO more convincingly linked forced upsell to family cost and habit erosion under the motion's "too few" condition, giving the third-party reader the clearer reason to limit premium dominance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.4 / A4.3 clash on inconvenience vs insufficiency was decisive - B argued PRO showed only inconvenient times, not too few seats; A countered that defining access down to a single off-peak matinee renders the resolution meaningless, keeping the forced-upsell harm live.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases—PRO on access/cultural participation, CON on solvency/demand-responsiveness—and both handled the Pressure Questions format correctly with clear A1/A2 answers. The decisive exchange was PRO's Q1 ("what counts as 'enough' standard screenings?"), which forced CON into answering that "enough" is simply whatever consumer demand dictates, with no floor. That answer effectively concedes the exact scenario the motion warns against (dominance leaving too few affordable screenings) rather than refuting it, weakening CON's defense against the proposition as worded. CON's own pressure question (zero-sum capacity) landed a real hit, exposing tension in PRO's "add premium capacity while preserving standard slots" answer, but PRO's underlying claim—that balance between formats is possible without total conversion to premium—was never fully dismantled, only pressured. CON's solvency argument remained strong throughout but rested on an asserted, unproven either/or (premium dominance vs. theater closure) that PRO repeatedly identified as a false binary without CON offering a concrete refutation. On balance, PRO kept tighter focus on the motion's specific wording and forced CON into a less satisfying, evasive-sounding answer on the central limiting-principle question, giving PRO the edge in a close, competitive debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 in Pressure Questions ("what minimum share counts as 'enough' before dominance becomes exclusion?") and CON's evasive "demand decides" answer in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive exchange, exposing that CON's framework has no protective floor against the exact harm the motion targets.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by exposing the unresolved capacity tradeoff in Side B’s middle path. If peak screens are finite and premium shows are selling out, preserving additional prime-time standard slots necessarily displaces higher-demand premium screenings. Side B repeatedly denied that consequence rather than weighing it. Side A then offered the stronger comparison between temporary delay and long-term theater viability. Its unsupported economic generalizations and nonexistent C-reference IDs reduced the margin, but Side B never adequately answered the central zero-sum objection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s A4.2-A4.3 response was decisive: it showed that Side B could not simultaneously preserve prime-time standard slots, avoid turning away premium customers, and accept finite auditorium capacity. Side B’s suggestion to add premium capacity where possible did not resolve that physical constraint.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO stayed on the proposition's conditional – dominance is wrong when it leaves too few standard-price options – and exposed CON's lack of a limiting principle. CON's strongest moves were front-loaded demand and zero-sum capacity, but PRO answered that a sold-out premium screen justifies premium supply, not erasing standard prime-time, and that cultural participation requires peak-week access. CON never showed that preserving a workable number of standard evening shows would cause closure, relying on asserted solvency rather than weighed tradeoff, leaving PRO's access-based standard more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 turn that sold-out premium does not equal license for dominance and that half-empty standard shows reflect bad placement, plus the pressure question exposing CON's inability to define "enough" – crystallized in Closing as no limiting principle.
