### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent frameworks—B's "citizen's test" (know/authorize/answer before the bullet) versus A's substitution logic (contracts are the most documentable, defundable, prosecutable instrument compared to covert action or proxies). The decisive exchange was A's repeated substitution argument: banning contracted combat doesn't create honest authorization, it pushes the same missions into covert units and proxy forces with no contract, budget line, or court to sue. B's answer to this (in rebuttal 2 and closing) was to declare it "not a defense, it's the case for the ban" and assert leaders would face "no mission at all" instead—an assertion never defended against A's counter that caps-driven leaders will choose the less-visible option, not abstain. B's closing never directly re-engaged this live threat to its own accountability metric, instead pivoting to claim A's regulatory fix was a concession; A's closing cleanly rebutted that framing ("fixing a reporting rule is not the same as subpoenaing a handshake"). A also handled the Q&A pressure more concretely (forward-looking punishment via defunding, functional distinction between authorization instruments), while B's answers to A's pressure questions (the combat/support line, and the substitution question) were serviceable but left the substitution rebuttal underdeveloped. This left A with the cleaner, unrefuted comparative path: the ban's likely real-world effect is less accountability, not more, which cuts directly against the resolution's causal claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's repeated substitution point—that banning contractors pushes leaders toward covert units and proxies with "no contract, budget line, or court to sue"—which B answered only by asserting the alternative would be "no mission at all," a claim it never substantiated and did not revisit in its closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by proving that PMCs inherently function to bypass public authorization by converting war into procurement. Side B effectively exploited this concession to show that PMCs structurally undermine democratic accountability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Closing brilliantly uses Side A's proposed legislative fix (requiring full-force votes for PMCs) as definitive proof that the unregulated instrument currently functions as an unaccountable loophole.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively dismantling Side B's standard for democratic accountability. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides ran a genuinely sophisticated debate centered on the same pivot: does banning contractors return force to visible democratic control, or push it into darker, less accountable channels (drones, covert raids, proxies)? Side A's substitution-effect framing was clean and persistently applied, but its necessity scenario (embassy sieges requiring contractors because uniforms "cannot arrive in time") was directly undercut by B's pressure-question answer that emergency powers allow rapid uniformed deployment with after-the-fact ratification — and A never circled back to rebut this in Rebuttal 2, leaving a real hole in the necessity claim. B also directly contested A's core substitution claim by arguing that drones/proxies are single acts or otherwise cannot replicate the scale of sustained, budget-backed combat that contractors uniquely enable, a claim A's final rebuttal did not squarely answer. B's case also stayed more tightly tethered to the motion's actual causal claim (accountability via visible casualties, jurisdiction, and voter leverage), closing with the sharper, more memorable formulation that voters can fire a government but not a firm. A's reliance on "statutory reform will fix hidden reporting" was met with a plausible historical rebuttal (confidentiality and subcontracting have defeated reform promises) that A answered only by reasserting the ideal rather than explaining why enforcement would finally work. Both sides handled pressure questions and A1/A2 formatting correctly, but B adapted more effectively under pressure, converting a risky "retreat is accountability working" line into a workable emergency-deployment alternative without abandoning its thesis. Overall B's case proved more resilient and better answered the decisive exchanges, giving it a clear but not overwhelming edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question answer (A1 in Rebuttal 2) reframing "retreat" as legitimate emergency uniformed deployment with after-the-fact ratification, which neutralized A's strongest necessity scenario (the embassy siege) and was never adequately re-challenged by A in the final rebuttal.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully neutralizing Side A's primary offense—that a ban would simply push force into 'darker' tools like drones and proxies. Side B achieved this by distinguishing the unique capacity of contractors to scale into a parallel army that holds ground, a function drones cannot replicate without massive political risk. Side A's defense relied on the premise that transparency laws could fix contractor secrecy, but Side B effectively argued that this ignores the inherent evasive intent behind hiring them.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 expertly dismantled the substitution argument by pointing out that while drones and covert raids can kill, they cannot 'garrison an embassy, hold a city, or run a years-long ground war' the way contractors do.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by establishing a decisive structural clash: banning a regulatable, paper-trailed instrument (contractors) pushes executives toward inherently invisible, un-auditable tools (covert ops, drones, proxies). Side A successfully defended this by showing that contractor secrecy is a fixable legal failure, whereas covert secrecy is a functional necessity. Side B's philosophical argument that "retreat is accountability working" was effectively dismantled by Side A's pressure regarding embassy sieges and preventable massacres. Side A's rebuttals were sharper, more pragmatic, and better resolved the core trade-offs of the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it directly answered the pressure questions by exposing the logistical impossibility of Side B's "just send uniformed troops" emergency alternative and proving that contractor secrecy is a statutory choice that can be mandated, unlike the inherent secrecy of classified drone strikes.
