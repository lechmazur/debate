### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A built a more durable path to victory by anchoring consistently to the motion's core trade-off: cumulative, irreversible atmospheric warming against adjustable procedural steps. The irreversibility asymmetry—carbon emissions compound for centuries while review mechanisms can be restored once deployment accelerates—was introduced clearly in the opening, sharpened across rebuttals, and crystallized in the closing without late evidence dumps. PRO also effectively neutralized the status-quo as a neutral baseline, pointing out that multi-year delays are themselves generating the political reversals CON warned streamlining would cause. CON's strongest contributions—the "social license through litigation" argument and the "canceled project is worse than a delayed one" point—were genuine and partially underanswered, keeping the debate competitive. However, CON's durability argument depends on the status quo delivering durable political support, a premise PRO directly challenged with evidence of states already reversing clean-energy targets under the current regime. CON never fully closed this gap. PRO's Q1 pressure question (what share of litigation blocks actually changes final routing versus just adding years?) was the debate's sharpest unanswered burden: CON's A1 responded with examples of rerouting and mitigations but did not address how frequently litigation produces materially different outcomes rather than duplicating federal-level findings at greater delay. CON's social license framing and "true speed requires durability" slogan were rhetorically effective and showed genuine originality, preventing a wider margin, but they could not overcome the core asymmetry PRO established between permanent atmospheric harm and recoverable process steps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing irreversibility argument was the most decisive single framing: permanent atmospheric carbon versus adjustable procedural steps. CON attempted to reverse this in its closing by arguing canceled projects and political bans are equally irreversible, but this inversion was less persuasive—canceled projects are rare and capital recoverable, whereas atmospheric carbon accumulates across centuries. PRO had also pre-empted this by noting the status quo already produces reliability shortfalls driving political reversals, so CON's irreversibility counter relied on a speculation (streamlining causes worse bans than existing delays) that PRO contested throughout and CON never cleanly resolved.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side B won by making the cleaner comparative case: permitting delay causes cumulative, irreversible fossil emissions, while the motion still preserves core environmental standards and only reduces some local review and litigation pathways. Side A’s social-license and cancellation arguments were forceful and rhetorically memorable, but they leaned too heavily on speculative worst cases and repeatedly treated streamlining as total steamrolling or gutting, which overstated the proposition. B better weighed irreversibility and scale.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttal that retained core standards can still catch serious site-specific harms while redundant local litigation adds years of fossil lock-in most effectively undercut A’s claim that streamlining merely shifts delay to construction.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side B won by successfully reframing Side A's "speed vs. delay" metric into "theoretical speed vs. actual project completion." Side B effectively answered Side A's pressure with concrete examples of local litigation benefits (like noise buffers) and a sharp distinction between reversed targets and sweeping political bans. Side B's closing decisively punished Side A's claim that standards could be "tightened later."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing decisively neutralized Side A's "reversible process friction" framing by pointing out that tightening standards later cannot resurrect a project that has already burned its capital and been canceled mid-construction.
