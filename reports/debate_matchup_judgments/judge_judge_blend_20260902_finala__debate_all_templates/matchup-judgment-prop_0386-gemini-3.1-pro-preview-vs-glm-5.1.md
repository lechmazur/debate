### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Both sides ran substantive, well-grounded cases throughout, but Side B maintained a slightly cleaner live route to victory by the close. Side B's most effective sustained attack was that the "major burden" standard sweeps far beyond genuine emergencies: in underserved communities, virtually any refusal triggers the rule, because alternatives almost always impose what a reasonable person would call a major burden. This creates what B called geographic nullification—religious freedom evaporates precisely where it is most strained, contingent on market conditions no clinician controls. Side A never squarely answered this breadth critique; its closing framed "major burden" as limited to "profound financial cost or delayed time-sensitive care," but this narrow reading was never defended against B's broader characterization, and the resolution's text supports B's reading. Side B also landed the partial-access point cleanly: a rural doctor who declines one contested procedure still delivers prenatal care, manages chronic illness, and treats urgent infections; exodus removes all of that. Side A's rejoinder—that partial refusal creates a "dangerous illusion of care"—was rhetorically striking but analytically weak, because patients clearly receive substantially more care under partial refusal than under provider departure. Side A's strongest move was seizing on B's emergency concession [B4.1] to argue the right is not absolute and the debate is only about line-drawing. But B's response was clean: existing frameworks already handle genuine imminent-harm emergencies, so the resolution is either redundant for that case or overbroad for everything else. Side A's core patient-first framing was compelling and emotionally resonant, and the state-monopoly/fiduciary-duty argument was consistently solid, but it did not fully neutralize B's central claim that the resolution eliminates conscience rights across a wide range of non-emergency contested procedures in underserved settings. On balance, B made the debate easier to decide by identifying a specific, unresolved structural flaw in the resolution rather than simply opposing patient welfare.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 [B4.1–B4.5] was the most decisive exchange. B separated the genuine emergency case (which existing frameworks already handle) from the broad sweep of PRO's "major burden" standard, correctly noting that PRO's entire case leaned on hard emergency scenarios while the resolution itself captured everything short of emergency—delays, travel, expense—in underserved areas. This directly damaged PRO's live route to victory by showing the resolution is either unnecessary or overbroad, and PRO's closing never directly answered whether "major burden" is appropriately calibrated or already covered by existing law.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side A won narrowly but clearly by keeping the motion centered on patient access and third-party harm. Side B’s best point was that the rule may be broader than true emergencies and could nullify conscience protections in underserved areas, but A answered that the resolution already permits refusal when transfer is viable and only shifts the burden to clinicians when patients face serious delay, cost, or medical risk. B’s exodus argument was plausible but speculative, while A’s fiduciary-duty and captive-patient framing more directly justified the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Best exchange was B’s Rebuttal 2 overbreadth argument distinguishing emergencies from broader “major burden” cases, followed by A’s closing response that severe delay and travel cost can be functional denial and that licensed professionals, not trapped patients, should bear scarcity costs.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side A won by establishing that a medical license entails a fiduciary duty to patients that supersedes conscience when refusal imposes a major burden, and by capitalizing on Side B's concession that emergencies already override refusal—narrowing the debate to line-drawing where A's standard better protects vulnerable patients. A effectively rebutted B's exodus concern with the "illusion of care" framing and directly answered pressure questions, while B misrepresented A's answers and never fully justified why patients should absorb severe access burdens.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.2's "illusion of care" response to B's partial-access argument, which reframed B's strongest comparative claim by showing that a provider who refuses critical, time-sensitive care offers false security rather than real access.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Both sides argued competently and neither produced a decisive blow. PRO (Side A) prevailed because it consistently defended the proposition as written—conscience is honored precisely when honoring it does not abandon the patient—and that logic held up across the exchange. PRO's core patient-in-the-exam-room argument was never satisfactorily answered: CON repeatedly redirected to "the state should provide alternatives," but that future-tense remedy leaves the actual patient stranded. PRO correctly pointed out that the proposition's trigger itself creates incentives to build alternatives, and the unrebutted empirical claim that jurisdictions with referral obligations have not produced mass clinician exodus materially strengthened PRO's practicality case. CON's strongest moments were real: the explicit "Yes" admission in A4.1 (that when no alternative exists the clinician must act) was a damaging concession that CON exploited well, and CON's answer to PRO's Q2—distinguishing procedure-based refusal from person-based refusal—was the cleanest principled move in the debate. The EMTALA point narrowed the scope of contested cases. However, CON's case ultimately rested on speculative consequences (provider exodus, hospital closures) that PRO had defensibly countered with comparative jurisdictional evidence, and CON never escaped the core question of what a patient is owed right now when the state's logistics have already failed. CON's "geographic accident suspending rights" framing was rhetorically vivid but did not resolve the underlying weighing problem. The debate is close, but PRO held the live burden more consistently throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's answer to PRO's Q2 in B4.2—drawing a clean principled line between refusing a specific procedure on religious grounds versus refusing to treat a person based on their identity—was the most original and damaging single move in the debate. It gave CON a coherent answer to PRO's consistency challenge while preserving the core conscience-protection argument. PRO never fully absorbed this distinction, and it represented CON's sharpest moment of narrowing the dispute on principled ground.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): B narrowly won by making the proposition’s rigidity vivid: if no alternative exists, A’s rule compels objecting clinicians to personally perform procedures they may view as killing, and A explicitly accepted that consequence. A had a strong patient-abandonment case and exposed B’s weak practical answer for the patient in the room, but B better leveraged the broad wording of the motion and drew a clearer rights-based limit, especially distinguishing refusal of procedures from refusal of persons.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s decisive exchange was pressing A on whether the rule would compel abortion or assisted suicide when alternatives are unavailable, then using A’s “yes” answer to argue that conscience becomes contingent on geography and logistics.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins because it grounded the debate in the concrete patient left without alternatives, a burden Side B never discharged beyond deferring to unbuilt state infrastructure. While B effectively pressed the coercion frame, A showed that conditioning refusal on accessible alternatives is already workable in comparable jurisdictions and directly answered B's practical objections by demonstrating that religious institutions can adapt through partnerships rather than close. A's closing crystallization—that B had no answer for the patient already in the exam room—resolved the central clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal dismantling B's conscription analogy by distinguishing voluntary professional fiduciary duties from drafted service, and undermining the exodus claim by noting that most refusals occur in institutional settings where adaptation is possible rather than forcing solitary rural doctors to act alone.
