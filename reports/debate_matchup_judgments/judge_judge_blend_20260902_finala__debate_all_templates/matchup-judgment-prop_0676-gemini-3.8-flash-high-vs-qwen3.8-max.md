### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases, but CON more effectively engaged the precise wording of the motion ("ban or heavily restrict") by distinguishing feature-specific prohibition (facial/vocal biometric inference) from a category-wide ban, and by offering a concrete operational mechanism (validation studies, confidential audits, burden-shifting for trade secrets) that answered PRO's "black box" and "audit can't fix pseudo-science" claims. CON's counterfactual—that banning automated tools pushes hiring back to unaccountable human shortcuts (school prestige, referrals, accents, gaps) with no rubric or record—was a recurring, largely unrebutted structural advantage, since PRO's response ("a biased human can be questioned or retrained") never showed why that recourse is more accessible than an auditable algorithmic record. PRO's strongest move, the "concession collapse" argument (that CON's willingness to ban biometric features already satisfies "heavy restriction"), was clever and repeated effectively, but CON neutralized it in Rebuttal 2 and Closing by reframing it as proof that only a feature ban, not a category ban, is warranted—turning PRO's pressure into support for CON's narrower thesis. PRO's claim that all algorithmic personality assessment "inherently" enforces conformity was asserted with vivid rhetoric ("digital phrenology") but never developed beyond repetition, while CON directly labeled it "assertion, not proof" and offered a plausible counter-example (validated safety-related conscientiousness tests) that PRO did not rebut. The result is a genuinely close, high-quality debate, but CON's tighter resolution-focused argument and stronger comparative framing give it the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2/Closing turn on the "concession collapse" charge—reframing PRO's own pressure question answer as proof that PRO's real position is a feature ban rather than a category ban, undercutting PRO's central "CON's opposition collapses" line—was the most decisive exchange, because PRO never adequately answered why banning specific biometric features plus generalized "conformity" harm justified a full category ban rather than CON's validation-and-audit regime.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by exposing that Side A’s own preferred regime—banning facial and vocal inference while requiring validation, disclosure, audits, and human review—amounts to heavily restricting the named assessments. That is expressly within the proposition. Side A offered a strong counterfactual about biased human discretion and better-grounded regulatory mechanisms, but its feature-versus-category distinction never escaped the motion’s inclusion of heavy restriction. Side B also preserved legitimate competency testing while targeting psychographic inference, making its position less categorical than Side A alleged. B’s claims that personality modeling is inherently discriminatory were somewhat overstated, and its malformed reference labels were an execution flaw, but neither displaced its decisive concession argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was the repeated concession trap: once Side A endorsed prohibiting the central biometric features and imposing extensive safeguards, B argued that CON had adopted the motion’s heavy-restriction remedy. Side A answered that these were merely feature-specific rules, but that semantic distinction was unpersuasive given the proposition’s wording.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but A built a more coherent and ultimately more decisive case by narrowing the clash to a burden-allocation question: should employers have to validate video/personality tools before deployment (A's proposed rule), or is post-hoc aggregate auditing sufficient (B's proposed remedy)? A's rebuttal that "aggregate audits do not help the rejected applicant who never sees the score" directly undercut B's central "auditable and correctable" claim, and A converted this into a workable, targeted standard (job-relevant, validated, auditable, accommodating) rather than a blanket return to gut instinct. B's strongest weapon—the scale problem and the "double standard" framing (why regulate machine inference of tone/demeanor but not human interviewers doing the same)—was real pressure, but A answered it adequately by noting that human interviews already sit inside an existing accountability structure (recorded, structured, challengeable under discrimination law) that trade-secret-protected algorithmic scoring evades, and by offering concrete scale-appropriate alternatives (minimum qualifications, capped structured work samples, rubric-scored review). B's mockery of "random selection" as a "literal lottery" was rhetorically sharp but overstated, since it was only one option among several A offered, and B never fully engaged the more substantive alternatives. Both pressure-question rounds were executed correctly with proper A1/A2 answers, so no format penalty applies to either side. On balance, A's closing crystallized the debate around contestability and pre-deployment validation more persuasively than B's closing recap of the human-bias comparison, giving A a clear, if not overwhelming, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 response (A2.6, echoed in A5.1) distinguishing ex-ante validation duties from B's ex-post "aggregate audit" defense was the pivotal exchange: it exposed that B's regulatory remedy protects the system statistically but never actually empowers the individually rejected applicant, which B's closing never fully resolved.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B won by showing that the choice is not automated personality inference versus recruiter intuition. Objective qualification filters, work samples, structured review, and validated tools remain available under B’s targeted rule. Side A made a strong scalability and auditability case, but repeatedly broadened the comparison to automation generally and ultimately endorsed audits, validation, disclosure, and accommodations resembling the heavy restrictions in the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s distinction between generic aptitude or work-sample testing and automated face, voice, and personality inference was decisive. It undercut Side A’s claim that restriction necessarily restores gut hiring, while preserving B’s justification for pre-deployment validation and contestability.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO wins because it keeps focus on the specific tools in the motion — video inference of traits from faces/voices and personality scoring — with concrete failure modes (autism, accent, anxiety) and a workable validity rule (validated, auditable to candidates, accommodating). CON's strongest scale and human-bias arguments collapse into endorsing heavy restriction itself (audits, disclosure, accommodation), which affirms the proposition's disjunct, and its lottery/pedigree counter relies on a false choice PRO answered with objective filters + capped work samples. Auditability clash favors PRO: aggregate regulator review does not give the rejected individual a contestable reason, while human interviews can be structured/recorded/reviewed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's audit-contestability line in R1/R2: auditors may see aggregate distributions but the rejected applicant never sees score, weights, or validation — exposing that B's "standardized and auditable" defense does not help the person excluded, paired with Q1 forcing B to defend aggregate rather than individual accountability.
