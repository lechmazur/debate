### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): The decisive exchange was Side B's sustained pressure showing that PRO's "basic right against captivity" collapses under scrutiny into a welfare balancing test — a point PRO's A1 answer in Rebuttal 2 effectively confirmed rather than refuted. PRO described a "best-interest/least restrictive" standard that permits sanctuaries, veterinary confinement, conservation enclosures, and transport, which is precisely the welfare-calibration framework CON said direct legislation could achieve more clearly. Side B's "law is not magic words; it is enforceable duties and remedies" crystallizes this cleanly: if the operative substantive test is the same in both regimes, the personhood label does no unique structural work. PRO's strongest live point — that property status keeps the default on the owner's side regardless of how welfare rules improve, and that the historical failure of anti-cruelty law illustrates this — is real and was never fully neutralized by CON's "just write stronger statutes" response. This keeps the margin competitive. But PRO's core promise (a basic right against captivity) was the motion, and that promise evaporated in cross-examination: every case PRO condemned could still occur under their own guardian-approved best-interest test, while every permitted use CON listed is also permitted under PRO's scheme. CON's closing, which was complete, crystallized this collapse memorably. PRO's closing was clipped mid-sentence, leaving a small execution cost at the moment it mattered most. Both sides used general background knowledge responsibly without fake sourcing. Side B's rebuttal discipline — consistently narrowing to the "personhood adds nothing" comparison — gave it the cleaner path to the ballot.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 moment where it absorbed PRO's A1 answer and turned it against them: PRO's own description of the permissibility standard (least restrictive, best-interest, serves the animal's welfare, not for profit) is structurally identical to the welfare-regulation framework CON advocates, making personhood an unnecessary relabeling. This was the sharpest single exchange because it used PRO's own answer as the decisive evidence against PRO's proposition.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won by successfully arguing that Side B's practical implementation of personhood collapses into a less efficient version of the very welfare regulations Side B criticizes. Side A's strategic pressure exposed that B's "right against captivity" still requires a "best-interest" standard for custody, neutralizing B's core mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 expertly used Side B's PQ answers to demonstrate that B's proposed "right" is just welfare regulation wrapped in rights vocabulary, leaving the actual decisions to ad-hoc litigation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A (CON) successfully showed that PRO's personhood framework collapses into a best-interest welfare test that permits the same confinements it claims to abolish, while creating unstable species boundaries and unworkable cross-species litigation. CON demonstrated that direct statutory prohibitions on capture, display, and invasive experimentation could achieve the same substantive protections without the conceptual instability of non-human personhood, making PRO's core mechanism unnecessary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's repeated collapse argument: PRO's "basic right against captivity" actually permits custody whenever a guardian or judge finds it serves the animal's welfare, making it substantively indistinguishable from enhanced regulation and undermining any unique work personhood was supposed to do.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO built a coherent moral foundation—these animals have interests serious enough that the property baseline is itself the injustice—and CON never squarely disputed the interests, only the legal mechanism. That concession matters: once you grant the interests are serious enough to warrant progressive welfare reform, the remaining dispute is whether personhood is the right tool, not whether the beings deserve protection at all. PRO's guardian/fiduciary framework (least restrictive option, court oversight, analogous to incapacitated persons) was workable enough to answer CON's litigation-chaos concern; CON's "no settled norms" counter is real but not fatal, since all rights jurisprudence requires norm development over time. CON's strongest unrebutted point—that courts lack developmental benchmarks for dolphins analogous to those for children—was the debate's closest moment, but PRO's implicit response (norms emerge through case law) held the line. CON's welfare alternative suffered from PRO's best sustained argument: it leaves these beings as property until politics intervenes, making protection contingent on human convenience rather than the animal's cognizable interest. PRO's closing reframe—"property is the sledgehammer, not personhood"—was the most rhetorically effective move in the debate and crystallized the core asymmetry cleanly. CON's closing was sharper in format (no clip) and the "no settled norms" line was the most damaging pressure CON applied, keeping this result competitive rather than decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal argument that "litigation chaos is the cost of taking rights seriously, not a reason to deny them"—paired with the reframe that property law is itself the sledgehammer that gives owners presumptive control over another being's body, movement, and breeding—was the most decisive exchange. It shifted the comparative frame from "which mechanism produces less mess" to "which baseline is morally defensible," and CON never recovered a clear answer to why the owner's economic interest should remain the default once the seriousness of the animal's interests is conceded.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by effectively dismantling Side B's practical framework for personhood. While Side B offered a strong principled case and a clever analogy to the protective custody of children, Side A decisively countered this in Rebuttal 2 by pointing out the lack of legal and developmental norms for exotic animals, which would turn Side B's 'least restrictive option' standard into an endless, resource-draining expert battle for sanctuaries.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1), which masterfully answered Side B's child-custody analogy by highlighting the epistemic gap courts would face in determining an animal's best interests without established developmental norms.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): CON successfully showed that legal personhood is a structurally blunt tool that cannot reliably distinguish exploitative captivity from protective sanctuary care. PRO's core analogy to children was undermined by CON's argument that courts lack developmental norms or the animal's own testimony to adjudicate these beings' best interests, making every husbandry decision a litigation gamble that would force closures and harm the very animals it claims to shield. While PRO's moral framework was powerful, CON proved that the welfare alternative protects core interests without the self-defeating binary of personhood.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal ([A4.1]) demonstrating the epistemic gap between children—whose interests are guided by developmental psychology and eventual voice—and animals, for whom no equivalent consensus exists on optimal social grouping or enclosure standards, dismantling PRO's claim that courts could cleanly apply a "least restrictive feasible option" test.
