### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): CON built a tighter, more resilient structural argument: that the "sorting" mechanisms PRO relies on (accreditation chains, proctoring, reciprocity agreements) are themselves the verification infrastructure that makes recognition slow, so PRO cannot simultaneously promise an "easier" lane and preserve those safeguards. PRO's answers to the pressure questions (A4.1–A4.3) conceded that safety depends on accreditation bodies, reciprocity, and institutional liability — exactly the slow, jurisdiction-bound machinery CON identified as "the difficulty" itself. CON's distinction between verifying a signature/document versus verifying a continuously-checkable person (biometrics, payroll, presence) went largely unrebutted, and CON's fraud-scaling point (one credential forgery reproduces infinitely; residency fraud does not) was never squarely answered. PRO's case leaned on optimistic claims about blockchain/proctoring solving fraud without engaging CON's sharper critique that trust in issuers is a slow, human, offline decision that cannot be "legislated away." PRO also suffered a real execution cost: four of its five speeches were clipped for exceeding word limits, repeatedly cutting off its own argument before completion, while CON's speeches were cleaner and better resolved. CON's closing crystallized the clash more effectively and left the contradiction unresolved in PRO's favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.1–B4.2 exchange ("machine-readability is the mill's native language... deciding which keys to trust is human, offline, and slow") directly exposed that PRO's own safeguards (accreditation, reciprocity) are the very difficulty the motion asks to remove, and PRO never resolved this tension in its remaining (clipped) speeches.

Multiple B clippings compounded the cost of incomplete crystallisation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2 (A4.1–A4.6) trap that every safeguard PRO names is itself the difficulty “easier” removes
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): CON won the central weighing: a categorical ease must be judged on the average/weakest issuer, not showcase credentials. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO won by executing a sharper strategic sequence. In Rebuttal 1, PRO exposed that CON's opening defense of "residency" was actually a defense of embedded clinical training (supervision, mentorship), not of the bureaucratic residency-recognition process (leases, visas, entry stamps) the motion actually concerns—forcing CON to either defend bare geography (weak) or retreat into training quality (which doesn't require border-based residency). CON never cleanly resolved this substitution charge. The pressure-question exchange sharpened this: PRO's Q1 forced CON to name a mechanism unique to residency, and CON's answer ("not a documentable item, but continuous multi-observer embedding") was philosophically coherent but evasive of the concrete ask, while PRO's own answer (naming apostilles, nostrification queues, embassy attestations) was concrete and testable. The most damaging moment was CON's answer to the Manila-nurse pressure question: CON proposed "bridging programmes, supervised local practice, rigorous exams" as the safety-preserving alternative — which PRO correctly seized on in Rebuttal 2 and Closing as itself a verification-based model, undercutting CON's core claim that only physical residency secures trustworthy competence. CON's closing reasserted the "thick embedding" thesis without directly rebutting this concession-catch, leaving PRO's framing standing. CON's case remained plausible and consistently argued, especially on fraud/arbitrage risk, but PRO's rebuttals did more real work narrowing and resolving the clash, giving PRO the cleaner path to a decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (A4.4) and Closing (A5.3), turning CON's own answer to the Manila-nurse pressure question (bridging programs/competency assessment) into an admission that CON's alternative is itself a verification-based model—collapsing CON's central distinction between "thick residency" and "thin credential verification."
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by making the decisive clash clearer: CON repeatedly substituted thick supervised training for physical residency, while B showed that accreditation, proctored exams, and documented practical hours survive the motion and that residency-as-practiced relies on thinner, forgeable location proofs. B answered pressures with concrete removable barriers (nostrification queues, apostilles) and fair identity handling at licensing, while A’s equity and safety claims narrowed into slower versions of verification. A stayed coherent on embedded oversight but never fully recovered from the substitution critique or B’s comparative weighing of friction versus verification.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal (B2.1–B2.3) exposing CON’s substitution of clinical thickness for residency location proofs, which collapsed A’s three prongs and framed the rest of the debate.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): A won by exposing B's definitional substitution: residency recognition (leases, stamps, nostrification, apostilles) is not clinical supervision. A answered B's pressure with concrete barriers to remove and a licensing-level identity answer, while B's answer to Q1 conceded there is no single mechanism unique to geography and retreated to vibe of "thick embedding." A reframed the clash as verification vs friction and showed B's own fallback (bridging, exams) is A's model.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1-A2.4 plus A3 Q1: PRO names the substitution (defending training thickness to protect location thinness) and forces CON to admit no verifiable mechanism requires geography, collapsing CON's core trust argument.
