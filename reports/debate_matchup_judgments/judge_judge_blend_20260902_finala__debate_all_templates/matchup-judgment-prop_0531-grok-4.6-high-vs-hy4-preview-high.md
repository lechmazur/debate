### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and the debate sharpened cleanly around a single question: who "owns" the frame of memorial content, and can that authorship be neutralized. B's opening was strong—civic frame as collapse-of-citizenship, attributed atrocity naming, refusal of hereditary innocence—and its rebuttal 1 landed a real hit by clarifying that naming Srebrenica as crime is not the same as endorsing "we all suffered." But the pressure round exposed B's structural vulnerability: Q1 (does displaying "civil war and defense" become an official alibi?) and Q2 (who referees incompatible myths without owning the frame?) targeted the exact seam in B's case. B's answers (A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2) were adequate but essentially restated that the state already "owns the square" and that a civic frame "constrains" capture rather than fully resolving how state selection of which testimonies count differs from the verdict-authorship it disclaims. A's answers to the parallel pressure were sharper: the state owns the platform, not the verdict, via a neutral inclusion rule (any community may post attributed testimony), and denial-as-artifact is distinguished from denial-as-endorsement — a genuinely clarifying move that answers B's "second constitution" charge by turning transparency into the safeguard. A's closing crystallized this well ("authorship, not pluralism") and turned B's own "refuse hereditary innocence" language back on it as proof B cannot escape being an author/referee either. B's closing repeated the "square is not a museum" line but didn't add new leverage against A's attribution mechanism, essentially recapping rather than resolving the live tension. A's progression across rounds (privatization critique, museum/inoculation analogy, "platform not verdict" formulation) built a more resilient, adaptive structure to the core objection, giving it a modest but real edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's "state owns the platform, not the verdict" answer (A4.2) directly answering B's Q2 pressure about refereeing incompatible myths was the pivotal exchange — it gave A a mechanism (attributed plural testimony) that blunted B's core charge that any shared civic frame is inherently authored verdict, and B's closing never fully dislodged it beyond reasserting that funding the wall is itself authorship.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): A successfully defended that official memorial space must model shared citizenship rather than host incompatible ethnic founding myths. While B's attribution mechanism was creative, A's distinction between private plural memory and official civic architecture—crystallized in the critique that a memorial square is not a museum—proved decisive. A showed that separate competing narratives in official space perpetuate the conflict's grammar and enable local majority capture, whereas a narrow civic frame naming specific crimes while refusing ethnic alibis is the minimum condition for a shared political community.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal that displaying attributed denial on a state-funded memorial square constitutes official endorsement of incompatible founding myths, not honest pluralism, because a memorial square is not a museum and children inherit civic architecture as a lesson in who constitutes the people.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully distinguishing between a museum (which archives contested claims) and a public memorial (which inherently venerates and endorses). Side B effectively dismantles this by showing that state-funded attribution in a civic square is still state endorsement of an alibi, rendering Side A's "inoculation" defense unworkable in the context of memorial architecture. Side B also provides a stronger answer to the state capture burden by arguing that a minimal civic baseline constrains local majorities better than unconstrained local ethnic martyrs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing distinction that "A memorial square is not a museum," which decisively neutralized Side A's attempt to defend attributed alibis as mere archival inoculation.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly and stayed on the motion, but CON (A) maintained a more consistent, harder-to-collapse framework: true ethnic narratives (names, dates, enemies) deserve public standing, while only falsehoods (denial/glorification) should be excluded. PRO (B) had to keep narrowing its claim under pressure—first demanding a "shared civic account," then conceding that Srebrenica's victims can be named as long as the "emphasized lesson" is civic—which let CON repeatedly show that this "emphasis" is itself the demotion the motion is actually about. The decisive exchange came when B's pressure question forced A to concede the state should exclude a Serb denial memorial, and B's closing tried to leverage that concession into proof of the civic-floor thesis. A's final rebuttal cleanly defused this by distinguishing "excluding lies" from "subordinating true competing narratives to a civic frame," which is precisely what the motion asks about. B never fully answered that distinction in the closing, instead reasserting the recruitment-mechanism point without engaging the truth/denial line CON had drawn. CON's position was thus more stable across rounds and better survived the sharpest pressure point of the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 response to B's Pressure Q1 ("Exclusion does not concede a shared civic limit... Denial is not a competing ethnic narrative of violence; it is a refusal to admit the violence") was the pivotal move—it preemptively defused B's strongest closing argument that denial-exclusion proves the motion, and B never adequately rebutted this distinction in the final closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): B successfully showed that excluding denial is a truth filter distinct from the motion’s civic rewrite, and that emphasizing a shared civic account demotes ethnic truth from public narrative to subordinate caption. A’s institutional dilemma could not overcome the analytical gap between regulating lies and falsifying history through institutional parables.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s distinction between a truth filter and civic rewrite in the second rebuttal, paired with the sustained “caption vs. narrative” frame, proved decisive in showing that emphasis on civic memory necessarily demotes ethnic truth.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by delivering a sharper, more devastating critique of the proposition's core mechanism. While Side B argued persuasively that separate narratives freeze conflict and recruit for future violence, Side A successfully reframed the "shared civic account" as a form of epistemic laundering that demotes directed atrocities (like genocide) into generic parables about "institutional weather." Side A's rhetorical framing was exceptional ("ceasefire of granite," "gag dressed as citizenship"), making the stakes of subordination highly memorable. Crucially, in the late debate, Side B attempted to trap Side A by arguing that excluding genocide denial concedes the need for a civic limit. Side A brilliantly parried this by distinguishing between banning factual lies (denial) and demoting true, specific historical narratives (the actual demand of the motion). By proving that a state can ban lies without forcing victims to accept a "civic caption" for their specific catastrophes, Side A neutralized Side B's strongest late-stage pressure and secured a clear victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it successfully disentangled the "denial" trap from the core motion, proving that banning factual lies does not necessitate demoting true ethnic narratives into subordinate civic clauses.
