### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.0 (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): CON (Side B) built a more durable path to victory on two clashes that PRO never fully neutralized. First, the discriminatory documentation burden: PRO's repeated claim that documentation only requires "key creative choices, not every keystroke" never explained why AI-assisted creators must document prospectively when no other creator—including photographers using computational auto-editing that removes objects and blends exposures—faces that threshold. PRO's answer that photographers still make compositional choices actually concedes CON's parallel: AI-assisted creators also make directional and editorial choices, and there is no principled reason to demand a paper trail from one group and not the other. Second, CON's Q2—whether existing law already addresses the harm PRO identifies—was the most decisive pressure of the debate. PRO's A2 acknowledged that current law covers pure machine output but claimed the rule closes a "gray area" of minimal undocumented input. The example offered (vague prompt plus minor word swaps) was thin and was CON's original straw-man critique applied in reverse. CON's rebuttal that courts already apply substantial-creative-control analysis post-hoc when disputes arise—without requiring pre-emptive records—was never squarely answered. The corporate-vs-independent-creator inversion (that PRO's documentation rule would advantage well-resourced compliance teams over independent creators, the opposite of PRO's stated goal) also ran live through the debate without a compelling PRO response. PRO's closing was notably weaker than CON's, mostly recapping rather than sharpening. CON's clipped Rebuttal 2 is a real execution cost, but it occurred after the key arguments were already on the table, and CON's closing crystallized the two surviving clashes cleanly. PRO had a genuine foundational concern about machine-output monopolies, but the proposed remedy created more problems than it solved and PRO never closed that gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question Q2—asking what unique harm PRO's documentation rule solves that existing U.S. copyright law does not already address—was the most decisive exchange. PRO's answer in A2 conceded that current law handles fully machine-generated works and then offered a weak gray-area scenario (vague prompt plus minor word swaps potentially surviving copyright if unchallenged). CON's rebuttal that courts already apply substantial creative control post-hoc in actual disputes, without requiring pre-emptive records, directly undercut PRO's justification for a new prospective documentation burden and was never answered.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.0 (CON)): Side A (PRO) built the cleaner path to victory across the full exchange. Its constitutional anchor—copyright rewards human creative labor, not automated output—was never dislodged. On the core clash over whether "substantial creative control" is workable, PRO effectively pointed to the longstanding derivative-works standard and the focused question of what the human contributed to the final expression, regardless of the base material's origin. CON's distinction (AI outputs lack prior human authorship, making the test novel and untested) was argued repeatedly but never quite closed the gap: copyright doctrine really does focus on the human's contribution to the final expression rather than the nature of the starting point, and PRO's framing held up under pressure. The single most decisive moment was PRO's Q2, which directly challenged CON's claim that existing rules already block minimal-input AI works—CON's A2 essentially conceded the Copyright Office cannot enforce existing rules, undermining the main rationale for opposing the proposition. CON's inequality argument (corporations vs. individuals) was interesting but partially backfired: PRO's rule is explicitly designed to protect individual creators from corporate trolling, so the weighing favored PRO. CON's documentation-burden argument was plausible early but weakened when PRO noted the evidence required is exactly what active creators already retain. CON's pressure questions were structurally self-defeating: Q2 in particular invited PRO to reaffirm their own point. PRO answered both of CON's questions directly, with A1/A2 labels, and the answers were substantive. CON's rebuttal 2 was somewhat repetitive of earlier rounds rather than sharpening the choice. PRO's closing crystallized the central legal point cleanly; CON's was shorter and added little new weight. Overall, PRO controlled the decisive clash more consistently and answered the strongest pressure more directly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2—pointing to thousands of single-prompt AI works already being filed for copyright successfully—was the most decisive exchange. CON's answer conceded that the Copyright Office lacks resources to verify claims under existing rules, directly undermining CON's repeated assertion that current law is already sufficient. This concession collapsed CON's main rationale for opposing the proposition and validated PRO's need-for-the-rule argument.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.0 (CON)): Side A won by establishing a consistent legal analogy to existing derivative-work tests, directly neutralizing B’s vagueness and burden claims in Rebuttal 2 via the required A1/A2 format, and crystallizing that the rule targets only zero-contribution filings; B failed to rebut the consistency point and offered only repeated assertions of untested ambiguity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 (A4) answering the pressure questions with the public-domain analogy and existing-workflow documentation point.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.0 (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): PRO successfully established that the "substantial creative control" standard mirrors long-standing copyright doctrine applied to derivative works, public domain adaptations, and collaborative drafts, where courts evaluate the human contribution to the final expression regardless of the base material's origin. CON never supplied a coherent mechanism for why AI-generated base material makes this established test unworkable, repeatedly asserting the novelty of AI without explaining how the absence of human authorship in the underlying output prevents courts from assessing whether the final expression reflects substantial human creative choices. PRO also effectively neutralized CON's documentation-burden argument by showing that legitimate creators already retain draft files, edit histories, and work-in-progress versions as part of their ordinary workflow, meaning only bad actors with no meaningful creative input would lack evidence. PRO's pressure question exposing the current practical loophole—thousands of single-prompt AI claims getting through—sharpened the comparative need for reform, while CON's answers and closing largely recycled earlier assertions without resolving PRO's core legal analogy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 argument that the "substantial original human contribution" test is already familiar and workable for public domain, collaborative, and derivative works regardless of base material origin, directly neutralizing CON's claim that the standard is unworkably vague when applied to AI outputs.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.0 (CON)): Side A won by successfully anchoring the debate to familiar copyright principles. A's analogy to derivative works and public domain adaptations effectively neutralized B's core claim that the standard was untested or vague. A also pragmatically defused the documentation burden by framing it as standard creative workflow artifacts (drafts, edit logs) rather than a new administrative hurdle, which B failed to adequately counter beyond repetitive assertions of inequality and litigation risk. A's closing cleanly crystallized why the origin of the base material is legally irrelevant compared to the human's final contribution, leaving B with no live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2 exchange, where A dismantled the "vagueness" and "burden" offense by mapping the proposed standard directly onto existing derivative-work jurisprudence and explaining that legitimate creators already generate the required documentation through normal workflows. This reframing forced B into repetitive speculation and ceded the practical ground.
