### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B systematically dismantled Side A's core mechanisms, successfully arguing that "quiet mercy" is either arbitrary favoritism or a codified rule that algorithms can flag, and that accountability is better achieved at the institutional rather than the individual level.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing correctly identified Side A's core sleight of hand: conflating the need for a human to eventually see an error (which B supports via appeals) with a universal legal right to manual adjudication in the first instance.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON (A) won by showing that the universal human-decision right either devolves into ceremonial rubber-stamping or secretly relies on granting officials unwritten discretionary mercy—authority the proposition itself does not create and which could be exercised through targeted escalation instead. CON consistently separated the actor from the remedy, arguing that enforceable procedural safeguards (notice, correction, appeal, institutional liability) actually fix errors, whereas mandating a human consumes scarce capacity without guaranteeing lawful exceptions, accuracy, or timely relief. PRO’s appeals to dignity and accountability were rhetorically powerful but did not overcome CON’s demonstration that the proposed entitlement mandates a pulse, not a remedy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal (Turn 8), which exposed that PRO’s “human with actual authority” effectively smuggled in broad discretionary power to waive rules, noted that the proposition does not itself grant such authority, and argued that if discretion is lawfully defined, automation can route exceptions without a universal right—leaving PRO with a symbol rather than a solution.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): B wins by exposing PRO's unstable definition and cost. PRO shifted from "a human looks" to "human with authority to grant unwritten mercy" - B shows that either requires new discretion to waive law (arbitrary favoritism) or, if discretion is lawful, can be handled by targeted escalation without universal manual review. B's institutional accountability + notice/reasons/correction/appeal better answers delay, rubber-stamping, and scarcity, while A's answers on scope and queues are assertion rather than principle.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.4 dismantling A's Q1 and mercy claim: remedy for unjust rules is lawful exception plus escalation and system fix, not pulse; if law forbids exception a human can't invent it, and if discretion exists automation can flag it - so universal human mandate is unnecessary and creates arbitrary discretion.
