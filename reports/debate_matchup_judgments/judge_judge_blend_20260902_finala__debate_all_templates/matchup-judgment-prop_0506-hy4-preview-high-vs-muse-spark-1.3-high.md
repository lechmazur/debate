### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B won by consistently tying liability to a narrow combination of verified notice, deliberate inaction, and causally attributable loss. Side A raised a serious administrability and over-removal concern, especially around ex post jury judgments, but often treated all verification as inherently impossible and overstated liability as automatically covering the entire theft. Its claim that civil liability would not compensate victims was also difficult to reconcile with the remedy being debated. Side B made the cleaner case that ambiguity remains protected while knowingly ignored, confirmed scams create responsibility.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal most decisively answered the insurer framing: prompt removal means no liability, while damages cover only losses caused by the platform's post-notice delay rather than fraud occurring through unrelated apps or texts. Side A challenged practical apportionment, but did not show why ordinary causation principles could not limit recovery.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): PRO won by keeping the motion’s three-condition trigger live and decisive: liability only for ignoring repeated verified AI-impersonation reports, not for hosting speech or catching every scammer. PRO repeatedly forced the solvent-node/judgment-proof-scammer comparison and converted CON’s parody/satire worries into non-liability under the verification gate. CON landed strong points on post-hoc jury risk, delete-first incentives, multi-app hopping, and small-host burden, but never fully neutralized the narrow clean case (bank + trusted flagger + synthetic evidence) or replaced “catch the thief” with a workable victim remedy. PRO’s A1/A2 answers and closings crystallize a cleaner decision rule than CON’s insurer/streetlamp frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 A1/A2: verification as multi-step pipeline (not one flagger’s stamp) and liability limited to delay-caused losses only, not full multi-app theft.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won by demonstrating that the verification standard PRO relies upon is epistemically unworkable in practice and creates perverse incentives. CON showed that retrospective jury review of "verified" reports would pressure platforms into preemptive deletion of lawful speech, while the supposed causation-based liability collapses because courts cannot apportion damages among multiple platforms and channels. PRO's narrow framing depended on a clean verification pipeline that CON successfully undermined with the interested-party problem and the label-versus-delete incentive gap. The decisive clash resolved against PRO: liability cannot remain narrow when verification is contested, ambiguity is resolved under threat of ruinous damages, and causation among distributed clones is indivisible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (A4), especially the argument that causation is judicially unadministrable—no judge can carve which dollar of fraud came from which host's delay—directly neutralized PRO's attempt to limit liability to a temporal "slice," showing that in practice juries would treat platforms as insurers for the full theft.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the duty narrow: liability follows verified knowledge and continued distribution, not initial hosting or pre-screening. Side A’s verification dilemma created genuine chilling concerns, but its claim that delayed verification defeats the policy overlooked B’s central benefit of preventing additional post-notice victims. B also persuasively answered displacement to payment rails by distinguishing recruitment, transaction, and apportioned fault.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was its direct answer that prompt removal after verification creates no liability and that platforms answer only for victims recruited through knowing continued circulation. This undercut A’s portrayal of the proposal as strict liability for all pre-verification losses.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): B won by turning the motion’s own trigger into a live dilemma: strong verification arrives too late for viral harm (and under A’s concession of prompt post-verification removal, pre-proof victims get nothing), while report-driven “verification” forces bet-the-company adjudication and risk-averse deletion. A had the cleaner moral core—knowing retention after notice is ordinary negligence and platforms are positioned to break the chain—but overreached in pressure (Q2 blurred pre-warning amplification) and never fully solved contested/manufactured human confirmation or the broken DMCA analogy. B answered pressure more surgically, crystallized the comparison through closing, and made the proposition easier to reject on workability without needing a different motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3–B4.5: using A’s concession that prompt removal after verification owes nothing to reframe A’s amplification/ad-revenue claim as pre-screening strict liability, locking the too-late vs. censorship dilemma.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON wins by exposing a structural dilemma in PRO's mechanism: rigorous "verified" proof of AI impersonation fraud arrives too slowly to stop viral scams before victims are recruited, while weaker, report-based verification is gameable and forces platforms to over-delete satire, licensed content, and criticism to avoid bet-the-company liability. CON also turns PRO's own pressure question, showing that PRO's framework cannot compensate the pre-verification victims PRO invoked without collapsing into a pre-screening duty PRO disavows. PRO's moral examples and piracy analogies never overcome CON's demonstration that the motion would either miss the fraud or chill speech, while failing to recover stolen funds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2, which crystallized the verification dilemma and turned PRO's Q2 against PRO by showing it implicitly demanded strict liability for pre-warning amplification, undermining PRO's claim that the duty was only for knowing retention.
