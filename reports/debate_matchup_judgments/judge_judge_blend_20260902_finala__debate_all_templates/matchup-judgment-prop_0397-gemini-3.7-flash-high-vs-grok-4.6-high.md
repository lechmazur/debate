### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built a tighter, more responsive case. Its central move—distinguishing an "early snapshot" from proven long-term inability—directly defused CON's core moral-hazard worry, and PRO kept pressing CON to name any evidentiary record (disability, vanished industry, years of zero-IDR payments) that would satisfy a workable standard. CON's pressure answers (A4.1, A4.2) largely restated the existing hardship doctrine and the "conditional obligation" idea without engaging the specific case of borrowers who, by hypothesis, never recover, leaving the sunk-cost argument unrebutted. CON's IDR defense also never squarely answered PRO's repeated point that a zero-dollar payment still leaves the balance and its credit damage alive for someone who will never pay it off. CON's opening and closing were coherent and grounded in plausible economics (unsecured lending, taxpayer exposure), but the rebuttal rounds mostly re-asserted the same structural claims rather than adapting to PRO's narrowing of the dispute. PRO's closing crystallized the clash cleanly: the debate is about the already-insolvent subset, not the general borrower population, and CON never fully met that reframing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B2.1/B4.1 sequence—separating "early snapshot" moral hazard from proven long-term inability and offering a concrete evidentiary standard (disability, vanished industry, years of zero-payment IDR)—was the most decisive exchange, since CON's answers in A4.1 never rebutted it beyond restating the existing hardship test.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO kept the clash on long-term proven inability vs early-career games, answered access and taxpayer pressure with sunk-loss logic, and showed IDR leaves a live balance, interest, and credit impairment for decades. CON's moral-hazard and systemic caps arguments were strong but depended on conflating short-term suppression with years-long records and treating accounting preservation as fiscal recoupment, which PRO directly neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 reframing: early-career low earnings is not long-term inability, and a multi-year record of disability/vanished industry/zero IDR with tax returns makes strategic delay implausible; plus uncollectable debt is already lost, so discharge stops second social cost rather than creating new taxpayer loss.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by relentlessly enforcing the prompt's specific condition ("long-term inability") and exposing Side B's failure to genuinely engage with it. Side B's moral hazard and forecasting arguments relied heavily on early-career income suppression and volatility, which Side A correctly and repeatedly identified as incompatible with a long-term inability standard. Side A also provided a much stronger economic rationale for why keeping uncollectable, sunk-cost debt on the books does not protect future student access. Meanwhile, Side B's reliance on Income-Driven Repayment failed to adequately address Side A's points about the lingering credit, mobility, and tax impairments of a live zero-payment balance. Side A's rebuttals were sharper, more direct, and decisively resolved the core clashes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they decisively answered the pressure questions by defining an objective standard for long-term inability (years of zero-income tax records, disability, vanished industries) and explaining why early-career suppression inherently fails that test, effectively neutralizing Side B's core moral hazard claim.
