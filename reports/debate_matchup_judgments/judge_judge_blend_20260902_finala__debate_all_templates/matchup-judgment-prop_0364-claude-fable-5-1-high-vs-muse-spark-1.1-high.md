### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by systematically dismantling Side B's practical objections and trapping Side B on the issue of remedies. Side A effectively forces Side B to concede that verification of metrics is both possible and legitimate without exposing the algorithmic 'engine.' Side A then leverages this concession to crush Side B's proposed alternative (lawsuits and arbitration), pointing out the impossibility of suing over a number one is forbidden to see.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing point that Side B's proposed alternative remedy ("sue, arbitrate") presupposes the exact disclosure Side B is arguing against, because a performer cannot file a claim over a hidden ledger.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): PRO won by establishing that compensation tied to unauditable numbers is inherently exploitable, neutralizing CON's trade-secret fears with CON's own concession that certification is possible without exposing the algorithm, and reframing the flat-buyout threat as proof that opacity itself was the platform's advantage. CON's "bad proxy" objection lost force once PRO clarified that standardization fixes definitions rather than imposing a single metric, and CON never offered a credible remedy for performers denied the figure their pay depends on.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange in Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.6): PRO answered CON's pressure questions by unifying the Nielsen analogy with a family of fixed definitions, undermining the alleged shift and the bad-proxy objection, while exposing the flat-buyout threat as a "ransom note" and showing the data is already public to everyone except the payee.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO built a highly compelling moral and logical case that if compensation depends on a metric, the payee must be able to verify it. PRO skillfully dismantled CON's "trade secret" and "reveals the engine" defenses by pointing out that third-party auditors can verify triggers without exposing algorithms, a point CON inadvertently conceded in Rebuttal 2. PRO's closing effectively weaponized this concession to show that CON's alternative leaves independent filmmakers with no practical remedy. CON's primary defense—that mandates would cause platforms to drop performance bonuses—was persuasively framed by PRO as an admission that such bonuses rely on information asymmetry.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they seized on CON's concession that a third-party accountant can certify a completion-rate trigger without exposing the algorithm, effectively neutralizing CON's "trade secret" argument and exposing the weakness of CON's "just sue" remedy for independent filmmakers.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by meticulously dismantling Side A's core mechanisms and capitalizing on A's concessions. B proves that standardizing the data doesn't independently verify it (the server remains the sole witness), and B effectively uses A's admission that the mandate might eliminate performance pay to show that A's rule destroys its own beneficiaries.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 destruction of Side A's time-clock analogy, pointing out that an employee can independently verify their own physical hours at a plant, whereas a performer cannot independently verify server pings, proving the mandate just "notarizes the black box."
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): PRO wins narrowly because it defended the core principle that when compensation depends on reach, the worker must see the reach, and it met CON’s pressure more directly. CON’s “notarizes the black box” critique and the frame that the mandate eliminates its own beneficiaries were damaging, but CON evaded the non-union filmmaker problem, treated audit rights as ordinary contract terms despite power imbalances, and its second rebuttal was clipped, leaving PRO’s live route cleaner after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s dismantling of the timeclock analogy by highlighting the epistemic asymmetry—a worker knows her own hours, but a performer cannot independently know server counts ([B4.5])—and PRO’s counter that standardized legal duty, log preservation, and auditor penalties create verifiability without a second clock ([A4.2]).
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A built a much stronger economic and epistemic case. A's "escape hatch" argument—that streamers will drop performance pay to avoid the mandate—was devastating when paired with the point that compliance costs will kill marginal deals, not just illusory ones. A also cleanly dismantled B's wage-law analogy by pointing out that workers know their own hours, whereas creators cannot independently verify views, meaning the mandate merely "notarizes" the streamer's black box rather than opening it. B's reliance on "if it disappears, it was fake" failed to address the loss of upside for supporting talent.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of the wage-law analogy in Rebuttal 2, pointing out that an employee can contradict a timeclock because they know their own hours, but a performer cannot contradict a server's view count, perfectly neutralizing PRO's core verification mechanism.
