### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and complied with the pressure-question format cleanly. PRO's framing—personhood as a burden-of-proof shift, with friction as the motion's explicit point—was rhetorically effective and backed by real examples (Whanganui, Atrato). But CON's core structural critique was never fully neutralized: the motion grants personhood to "Indigenous or local communities" broadly, and when pressed (A's Q2) on how rival or non-traditional local factions would be prevented from weaponizing requests, PRO's answer retreated only to "recognized traditional governance structures," leaving the broader "local communities" language and scalability concern largely unaddressed. CON also sustained a plausible systemic point across rounds that personhood merely relocates political/scientific capture into courts rather than solving it, and offered a concrete alternative (strengthened standing plus co-management) that PRO dismissed rhetorically ("weaker version") but never fully dismantled on the merits. PRO's answer to CON's balancing-test question (core regenerative capacity) was a decent parry, and PRO's Q2 did expose a real tension in CON's co-management model deferring to elected governments, but that tension was consistent with CON's stated framework rather than a true contradiction. On balance, CON's unaddressed scalability/rival-claims gap and steadier systemic skepticism give it a modest edge in a closely fought, competitive debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q2 in Pressure Questions ("how do you stop rival local groups from weaponizing such requests") remained the most consequential unresolved pressure point: PRO's answer restricted itself to recognized Indigenous governance structures without addressing the motion's broader "local communities" language, leaving CON's scalability critique substantially live through the closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by making the central comparison clearer: personhood changes the legal baseline so ecological destruction must be justified rather than merely compensated after approval. A did overstate that personhood inherently shifts burdens or supplies substantive flow rights, keeping the debate competitive, but B never fully exploited that legal gap or showed why its alternatives would overcome the admitted enforcement failures.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest exchange was against B’s claim that personhood creates an absolute veto: A explained that courts could protect core regenerative capacity while permitting redesigned infrastructure, making B’s repeated paralysis framing look overstated.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): CON successfully showed that automatic personhood on request creates structural paralysis through vague rights to flow and regenerate, which collide by design with dams, flood defenses, and waterworks. PROs successful examples (Whanganui, Atrato) were shown to be carefully negotiated exceptions rather than automatic grants, and PRO never bridged that gap: their defense against rival claims and weaponization relied on optimistic, imported assumptions about state recognition frameworks not in the motion. While PROs burden-shift frame was rhetorically powerful, CON maintained that personhood merely relocates political conflict into courts without solving capture or incomplete science, and that concrete community standing plus co-management delivers enforceable protection without freezing national survival infrastructure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CONs Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 exchanges on the balancing test illusion, where CON demonstrated that a river's right to exist, flow, and regenerate structurally collides with major infrastructure by design, turning PROs touted necessary friction into litigation-biased paralysis over undefined core regenerative capacity.
