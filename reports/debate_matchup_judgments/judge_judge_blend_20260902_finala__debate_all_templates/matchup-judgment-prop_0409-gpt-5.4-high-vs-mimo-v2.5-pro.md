### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) maintained a clearer and more consistently principled distinction throughout the debate: paid optional services (rentals, concessions, events that don't displace use) are acceptable; payment that buys access to the place itself or its best parts is enclosure and is not. That line was stated in [B1.6], repeated in [B4.3], and crystallized in the closing as "funding is a management problem; enclosure is a legitimacy problem." CON's central strategic move—claiming PRO "conceded" by accepting limited paid extras—was clever but ultimately misfired. PRO correctly responded that accepting optional services inside a free commons is a different principle of ownership from making baseline or prime access negotiable, and CON never supplied a concrete limiting principle for where paid activity stops other than "regulation," which PRO correctly pointed out CON's own framework also requires. CON's practical argument (underfunded, unsupervised beaches vs. staffed hybrid ones) was the strongest live point and genuinely pressured PRO. PRO's A1 answer in Rebuttal 2—public-baseline funding via parking, visitor taxes, concession permits, philanthropy—was adequate to blunt the "no alternative" claim, and PRO correctly noted that tying maintenance to operators who monetize prime areas shifts both control and risk rather than solving the funding problem. CON had multiple clipped speeches (opening, both rebuttals, closing), which is an execution cost, though PRO also had two clipped turns. The decisive factor is that CON needed to justify the step from optional service to tiered landscape—fenced prime lawn, premium shoreline, paywalled peak hours—and offered only "it helps fund upkeep," which PRO correctly categorized as insufficient. PRO's path to the motion remained live; CON's "positions are identical" argument collapsed the debate in a way that, on closer inspection, left CON without a clear affirmative case for the kinds of enclosure the motion actually asks about.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Q2 ([B4.3]–[B4.4]) was the most decisive exchange. CON had pressed: if you accept limited paid extras, what distinguishes your position from "increasingly enclosed"? PRO's answer drew a clear line—optional service attached to an activity vs. payment that buys access to the place or its best parts—and then turned it back: "CON's line makes them negotiable whenever monetization seems useful. That is not 'just dosage.' It is a different principle of ownership." This directly neutralized CON's "positions are identical" argument, which had been the structural spine of CON's case, and left CON without a principled reply for the step toward tiered landscape that the motion actually concerns.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by maintaining a sharp, consistent distinction between optional paid extras (which they allow) and enclosure of core access or best parts (which they reject), while supplying concrete non-enclosure funding alternatives that directly responded to A's budget pressure. B's direct A1/A2 answers and closing crystallization of the unmet burden on justifying exclusion gave the cleaner comparative reason to vote PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The B rebuttal 2 exchange: directly addressed A's Qs with public-baseline mechanisms and optional-vs-enclosure principle, then showed A's own examples fit inside PRO's framework without needing to sell access.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A wins by successfully maintaining a principled distinction between optional paid services (which both sides accept) and actual enclosure of prime public land (which the motion opposes). ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins a real but competitive debate on three grounds. First, the "free does not equal equal" reframe was CON's most durable thread: by forcing PRO to defend lotteries, timed entry, and ranger limits as alternatives, CON demonstrated these systems also privilege the schedule-flexible, digitally connected, and locally situated—undermining PRO's equity core without CON needing to abandon the baseline commons. PRO never fully answered why those alternatives are structurally fairer than charging optional premium users directly. Second, CON's framing of the decisive comparison—present, verifiable degradation and budget strain versus PRO's forecast of creeping enclosure—made the debate easier to resolve. PRO's political economy mechanism (private constituencies lobby for expansion; each concession is a precedent) was the strongest sustained thread for their side and was genuinely under-answered, but CON's partial riposte ("by that standard, no lottery or permit survives politics either") kept that argument from being decisive. Third, structural execution: CON's opening and closing were both unclipped, giving it a complete frame around the debate; PRO's opening and closing were both clipped, leaving its case architecturally incomplete at the most critical structural moments. PRO's circular-argument point ("CON's model manufactures the very problem it claims to solve") was the cleverest rhetorical move in the debate but needed more development than the clipped format allowed. The closing line "A public space is not made public by a zero on a price tag" was the most memorable crystallization in the debate and captures why CON's practical stewardship framing ultimately held the stronger path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 turning PRO's crowd-management concession against them: when PRO proposed timed entry, lotteries, and ranger limits as zero-price alternatives, CON immediately showed those systems also exclude—favoring flexible schedules, digital savvy, and local proximity—thereby dismantling PRO's equity argument from within PRO's own preferred toolkit. This move ran successfully through every subsequent round and was never squarely neutralized.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built the stronger overall case through sustained focus on political economy risks and incentives created by private concessions, directly answering pressure questions with clear A1/A2 distinctions between adjustable public tools and locked-in private ones; Side A (CON) offered practical tradeoffs but left the expansionary mechanism largely unneutralized across rebuttals and closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 contrasted temporary public rationing mechanisms with private operators' expansion incentives, directly responding to A's pressure questions and undermining the "capped mixed model" claim.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully turning Side A’s equity argument and grounding the debate in practical stewardship. While Side A made a strong case about the political economy of concessions and the risk of creeping enclosure, Side B effectively demonstrated that Side A’s proposed alternatives (lotteries, timed entry) also ration access in ways that privilege time, flexibility, and digital access. Side B’s framing of the motion—distinguishing “broadly free” baseline access from optional premium zones—allowed it to absorb Side A’s democratic concerns while maintaining that mixed models fund the maintenance required to keep spaces usable. Side B also sharply exposed Side A’s reliance on idealized public funding as an evasion of real budget tradeoffs. Side A’s slippery-slope mechanism was well-articulated but ultimately weighed less heavily than Side B’s concrete account of present degradation and the comparative fairness of user-pays models for intensive amenities. Both sides incurred execution costs from repeated clipping, but Side B provided the cleaner, more actionable path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response to Side A’s proposed rationing systems, arguing they silently privilege those with schedule flexibility and digital access, effectively neutralized Side A’s equity advantage and forced the debate onto practical maintenance tradeoffs where CON held the edge.
