### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON built a tighter structural case: the "permission vs. compelled license" reframe was consistently reinforced by the fixed-record-vs-ongoing-service distinction, and the enforcement dilemma raised in Rebuttal 1 (self-policing is decorative, publisher-policing is a continuing duty) was never fully closed by PRO. PRO's answer that "the fallback is revocation, not perpetual moderation" left open exactly how abuse or license breaches would be detected pre-revocation without ongoing monitoring — CON exploited this directly in the final rebuttal and closing ("revocation is reactive... monitoring is a continuing burden"). PRO's certainty-of-harm framing in closing was rhetorically strong but didn't survive CON's counter that erasure isn't total oblivion (records remain) while the compelled-license harms are structural, not merely speculative. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions format correctly with A1:/A2: labels, but CON's questions exposed a live unresolved tension that PRO's answers papered over rather than resolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2's dilemma ("if the community polices itself, the terms are decorative; if the publisher must police them, PRO's promise of no continuing duty collapses"), later sharpened in A4/A5 by calling revocation "reactive," was the most decisive exchange — PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer never explained how a publisher detects the need to revoke without the very monitoring burden it disclaimed.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully proving that any form of allowable preservation for an online game inherently requires ongoing operational, legal, and monitoring burdens from the publisher. Side B's strongest defense—that permissions can be conditional and revocable—was cleanly dismantled by Side A's observation that enforcing conditions requires the very ongoing monitoring Side B promised to avoid, and that revocation is inherently reactive to damage already done.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal decisively damaged Side B's framework by pointing out that a conditional, revocable permission requires a monitor, trapping Side B in a continuing duty and proving that "doing nothing" is not legally or practically costless.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won by narrowing should-allow to a low-cost, disjunctive, revocable noncommercial permission — including simply not suing clean-room emulators — which directly neutralized CON's cost, third-party license, and continuing-duty claims. CON (A) repeated compelled-license and monitoring arguments but did not overcome the answer that lawful routes are optional and revocable, and left unanswered that screenshots do not preserve mechanics, state, and social play. The certainty of irreversible playable loss vs speculative, reversible harms gave B the cleaner weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2 [B4.1-B4.3] — disjunctive options avoid third-party sublicensing and revocation replaces perpetual moderation — directly answered A's strongest pressure about impossible licensing and fallback duty.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Through the middle rounds both sides argued competently: CON's service-vs-cultural-work distinction and liability/third-party-contract concerns were real, and PRO's irreversibility-vs-revocability framing and the Toontown Rewritten example gave PRO a clean, memorable throughline. PRO's pressure questions ("erasure by neglect," "riskier than the status quo") exposed a real weakness in CON's "owner-directed archiving" fallback—that it only works if the owner actually acts—and PRO's answers to CON's questions (narrow tailoring around third-party assets, light-touch pre-written licenses) were direct and format-compliant. CON's answers were reasonable but conceded the core problem still rests on unenforceable "should" language. Decisively, CON's closing speech collapsed into unreadable, corrupted text, forfeiting the chance to crystallize its case or rebut PRO's final framing, while PRO's closing cleanly restated the irreversibility asymmetry and directly addressed CON's last live objection. That execution failure, combined with PRO's generally sharper and more consistent framing, gives PRO the clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Questions in Turn 5 (Q1 on "erasure by neglect" when the owner never acts, and Q2 on regulated permission vs. the unaccountable underground status quo) forced CON to concede that its "owner-directed archiving" alternative only functions if the owner cooperates, which CON never fully repaired before its closing collapsed.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing preservation as requiring interactive playability, rather than mere documentation. Side B also provided practical, low-cost solutions to Side A's concerns over third-party IP and liability. Side A suffered a massive technical failure in its closing speech, leaving Side B's strong final framing entirely uncontested.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 answer regarding third-party IP, cleanly arguing that publishers can strip or replace expiring assets rather than using them as an excuse for total deletion.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won the central clash: for server-dependent games, no playable access = no game preserved. CON's owner-directed archive never answered erasure-by-neglect, while PRO answered CON's best pressure on licensed assets and enforcement cost with narrow tailoring (strip/replace third-party content) and a one-time pre-shutdown revocable license that converts underground risk into governed risk. The asymmetry irreversible erasure vs revocable permission was weighed cleanly and sustained through R2 and closing; CON's closing was clipped, leaving its liabilities/governance push uncrystallized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.3 answering licensed music/middleware expiry and no-live-team enforcement: PRO reframed "should allow" as granting what authority exists plus stripped archival builds under a single pre-drafted non-commercial license, directly neutralizing CON's strongest practical objections.
