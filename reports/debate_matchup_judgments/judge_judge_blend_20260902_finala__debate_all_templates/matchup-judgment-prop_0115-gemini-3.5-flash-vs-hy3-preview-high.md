### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO (Side A) built two live routes to victory that CON never fully neutralized. First, the merchant-bankruptcy scenario: if a seller fails or is uncooperative, a BNPL user remains legally obligated to the fintech lender with no statutory right to withhold payment, while a credit card user has federal dispute rights. CON's rebuttal that providers "pause automatic drafts" is a voluntary, contractual answer to a statutory gap—it concedes the underlying vulnerability. Second, PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2 made a precise and accurate distinction: credit card regulation requires a reasonable ability-to-pay assessment, not necessarily a hard credit pull. This directly cut the foundation of CON's main accessibility argument, which rested on the claim that equivalent regulation mandates hard pulls and aggregate caps. CON never clearly rebutted this distinction, continuing to assert hard-pull mandates without engaging PRO's narrowing of what "credit card-style regulation" actually requires. CON's strongest contribution—that BNPL's fixed-term, interest-free structure genuinely differs from revolving credit and serves credit-constrained borrowers—is real and was sustained throughout, but it was framed as a reason for different regulation rather than a complete defense of no affordability standards and no statutory dispute rights. PRO reframed accessibility as "access to unregulated debt is a trap, not a benefit," which CON never effectively answered. CON's reliance on the payday-loan fallback became circular, and PRO's structural counter (we don't leave sectors unregulated merely because worse options exist) held. PRO showed stronger adaptation and progression across rounds; CON's later speeches largely re-ran its opening premises.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2: by correctly distinguishing that credit card regulation mandates ability-to-pay assessment rather than necessarily hard credit pulls, PRO dismantled the factual premise of CON's central accessibility argument. Paired with PRO's Q1 pressure on the merchant-bankruptcy gap—where CON's voluntary "draft pause" answer exposed the inferiority of contractual versus statutory protection—this exchange most decisively shifted the comparative burden in PRO's favor.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built the stronger path to victory by establishing that BNPL is functionally unsecured credit and identifying concrete, unresolved consumer harms—loan stacking across non-reporting platforms and the absence of federal dispute rights when merchants fail—that structural differences do not fix. Side A (CON) raised a credible accessibility defense, but B successfully narrowed the proposition to baseline underwriting, reporting, and statutory dispute rights without accepting CON's framing that credit-card regulation necessarily mandates hard pulls and aggregate caps. B's pressure questions exposed real fragility in CON's reliance on voluntary merchant recourse and self-policing, and CON never fully answered why codifying existing soft checks would destroy the industry. By the closing, PRO's frame—consumer protection must be a statutory guarantee, not a corporate option—remained live and comparatively cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1 and B4.2, where PRO directly answered CON's pressure questions by clarifying that credit-card regulation does not inherently require hard pulls and by exposing the insufficiency of merchant-level recourse when sellers are insolvent or uncooperative, collapsing CON's accessibility-defense and voluntary-protection arguments.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B wins by consistently weighing the structural and consequential differences between BNPL and credit cards. While Side A effectively highlighted statutory dispute rights and loan stacking, Side B’s framing that revolving-credit regulations are mismatched to fixed-term, single-purchase products and would force compliance costs that eliminate interest-free access for credit-constrained users created a cleaner cost-benefit comparison. Side B handled pressure directly and maintained a tighter focus on how the proposed regulation would functionally alter BNPL’s business model, whereas Side A relied more on principled equivalence without fully resolving the access tradeoff.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over dispute resolution and underwriting standards in Rebuttal 2. Side A’s clarification that ability-to-pay checks do not require hard pulls was sharp, but Side B’s counter that codifying credit card compliance would still trigger aggregate caps and cost pass-throughs, erasing BNPL’s interest-free utility, more decisively addressed the real-world impact of the proposition.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): PRO (Side A) wins on the strength of two arguments CON never fully neutralized. First, the cross-provider stacking argument: CON's primary response was that real-time cash flow checks prevent overextension, but PRO correctly and repeatedly identified the gap—those checks see a single user's bank balance, not their total outstanding obligations across all BNPL providers. A user who owes $200 to Provider A has not yet spent that money when Provider B runs a cash flow check; the liability is invisible. CON never closed this hole. Second, PRO made a decisive structural clarification in Rebuttal 1 and carried it through the rest of the debate: credit card-style regulation means underwriting at account opening and aggregate balance reporting, not a per-transaction hard pull or per-installment trade line. This dismantled CON's most-used objection—that the regulation would make small-dollar plans economically nonviable and crush thin-file consumers—and CON's Rebuttal 2 response ("it turns BNPL into a credit card product") never grappled seriously with the account-level/aggregate-reporting distinction PRO had clearly drawn. CON's strongest contribution was the underbanked consumer harm story, which was genuine and well-sustained, but PRO partially defused it by noting that regulated BNPL would still undercut payday loans and overdraft fees, and that on-time repayment reporting would actually build credit for thin-file users—a benefit entirely absent from the current unregulated system. CON largely repeated the structural-difference argument across rounds rather than advancing it, while PRO consistently sharpened the stacking gap and regulatory mechanism throughout the exchange. The margin is clear but competitive because CON's revolving-vs-transactional distinction is a real one, and the underbanked concern deserved more direct PRO engagement than it received.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's clarification in Rebuttal 1 [A2.3] that credit card underwriting applies at account opening rather than per transaction was the most decisive single exchange. It converted CON's central objection—that requiring affordability checks would destroy small-dollar convenience—into a strawman, and PRO sustained that correction through pressure questions and the closing. CON never produced a clean answer to the resulting reality: a single upfront check with aggregate balance reporting would prevent cross-provider stacking without adding friction to individual split payments.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won by successfully dismantling CON's core objections about regulatory fit. CON's case relied on the claim that credit-card rules would impose per-transaction friction and decimate thin-file credit scores through trade-line reporting. PRO sharply corrected both points: credit-card underwriting occurs at account opening, not per purchase, and credit bureaus receive aggregate monthly balances—not individual transaction records—preserving product convenience while enabling credit-building. PRO also maintained a live, unanswered harm in cross-provider stacking: no single BNPL provider's real-time cash flow check sees total obligations across competing apps, a gap that aggregate balance reporting would close. CON's repeated framing that account-level underwriting would necessarily convert BNPL into revolving, interest-bearing debt was asserted but not substantiated, especially after PRO clarified that fee caps and zero-interest structures could remain. CON's closing largely recapped rather than resolving these live comparative burdens.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 clarification that credit-card-style regulation would require reporting aggregate monthly balances rather than individual $50 trade lines, which directly neutralized Side A's central claim that such reporting would decimate thin-file credit scores and reframed it as a potential credit-building benefit.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A wins by successfully clarifying the regulatory mechanism and neutralizing Side B’s core “friction destroys utility” offense. When Side B argued that credit-card-style rules would require burdensome per-transaction checks and ruin thin-file credit scores, Side A sharply clarified that alignment means one-time underwriting at account opening and aggregate monthly balance reporting—not per-purchase trade lines. This directly answered Side B’s pressure, preserved the pay-in-four convenience, and closed the cross-provider stacking loophole that Side B’s real-time cash flow defense never fully resolved. Side B’s category-error frame was rhetorically strong but ultimately rested on an overstated picture of regulatory burden that Side A systematically dismantled. Side A’s closing crystallized this decisive clash cleanly, giving a clearer path to vote PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2) defusing the underwriting and credit-reporting pressure. By explaining that credit card rules apply at account opening and report aggregate balances rather than individual transactions, Side A directly collapsed Side B’s main argument that regulation would destroy BNPL’s low-friction model and decimate thin-file scores, turning a major defensive liability into a comparative advantage.
