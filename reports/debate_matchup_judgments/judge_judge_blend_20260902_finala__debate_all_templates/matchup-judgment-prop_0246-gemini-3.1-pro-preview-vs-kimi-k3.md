### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): The debate converged on a single decisive clash: since both sides agreed the toxic core is "borderline" lawful-but-awful content that bans and moderation cannot reach, the only remaining lever is algorithmic downranking — which a chronological default abolishes entirely. CON (A) built this trap methodically, using concrete pre-algorithm examples (Gamergate, retweets/quote-tweets, hashtag mobs on reverse-chronological Twitter) to show that chronology's "network friction" quarantine never existed — the follower graph was already a mesh with amplification built in. PRO (B) never squarely rebutted the retweet/quote-tweet point; its closing simply reasserted that harassment "required manual effort" without addressing that retweeting requires none. PRO's answer to CON's natural-experiment pressure question (X's Following tab) — that users were "conditioned" by algorithms — was flagged by CON as unfalsifiable and self-refuting, and PRO never recovered from that exposure. PRO's opening mechanisms (doomscrolling exit cues, filter bubbles) were good material but went largely unweighed once both sides' closings converged on the brake/gas-pedal framing, where CON's "an imperfect brake beats no brake" comparison landed cleanly and unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3–A4.6): showing that retweets/quote-tweets already produced global-scale, inbound harassment (Gamergate) under pure chronology, which demolished PRO's "network friction" quarantine theory and was never adequately re-established in PRO's closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B won a solid but competitive debate by showing that chronology does not actually quarantine toxicity to followers: retweets, quote-tweets, mentions, and coordinated mobs already spread abuse across networks. Side A persuasively explained algorithmic amplification and profit incentives, but repeatedly relied on a blast-radius limitation that Side B directly disproved. Side B also supplied the stronger comparative weighing: ranked feeds are flawed, yet can suppress some lawful-but-awful content, while strict chronology structurally cannot downrank it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response to “network friction” was decisive. It explained that harassment is often inbound and that one-click sharing made the chronological follower graph a mesh rather than a wall, undercutting Side A’s central claim that toxicity would remain bounded by explicit follows.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B wins by decisively dismantling Side A's core mechanism. Side A argued that chronological feeds limit toxicity by restricting a post's "blast radius" to direct followers, creating "network friction." Side B effectively countered that features like retweets, quote-tweets, and @-mentions allowed massive out-of-network virality and inbound harassment even on purely chronological feeds, proving the follower graph was a "mesh, not a wall." Furthermore, Side B won the clash over "borderline" content by proving that downranking is the only scalable moderation tool for lawful-but-awful material, a lever that chronology entirely abolishes. Side A's rhetoric and "human vs. industrial virality" framing were strong, but Side B's structural takedown of A's causal story was more logically rigorous and convincing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's "network friction" and "blast radius" claims by pointing out that one-click retweets and quote-tweets allowed chronological mobs to reach millions without algorithmic amplification, collapsing Side A's claim that chronology inherently quarantines abuse.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): B built a tighter, more coherent framework: separate the objective function (suppress abuse vs. maximize time-on-site) from the shared machinery, and argue chronology's harms are user-correctable (unfollow/mute) while engagement ranking's harms are the product working as designed ("you cannot unfollow the algorithm"). This asymmetry was introduced early, defended through cross-examination, and crystallized cleanly in closing with the smoke-detector/slot-machine analogy. A's strongest thread—that retweets/reposts let chronological feeds cascade abuse across network lines, and that brute-force volume/flooding from followed troll accounts is a real structural vulnerability—was a genuine pressure point B never fully neutralized (B's "decay" and "bounded distribution" answers are plausible but not airtight against sustained flooding by followed accounts). However, A's central counter-move—that moderation and ranking are inseparable because they share architecture—was repeatedly asserted rather than demonstrated, and B's spam-filter/email analogy directly rebutted it without a strong counter-analogy from A. Both sides handled the pressure-question format correctly with clear A1/A2 answers, but B's answers did more work reframing the debate, while A's answers largely restated prior claims. B's closing sharpened the decisive question ("what is the system optimizing for") more effectively than A's closing, which mostly repeated the volume/spam and mute-fatigue points without resolving the moderation/ranking distinction B had pressed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2 answers plus the "moderation vs. ranking" reframe, later crystallized as smoke detector vs. slot machine) was the most decisive exchange: it directly answered A's strongest structural claim (shared architecture) and reset the debate around a clean, weighable asymmetry that A never fully dislodged.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by cleanly distinguishing chronological ordering from moderation and filtering. Side B showed that retweets, coordinated networks, and high-volume posting can remain toxic under chronology, but its central response repeatedly treated shared algorithmic infrastructure as proof that moderation and engagement-maximizing ranking are inseparable. That did not answer A’s objective-based distinction. A somewhat overstated how easily users can correct every chronological harm, yet still established the stronger comparative case that engagement ranking adds broader, persistent amplification beyond those baseline harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s A4.3-A4.6 response was decisive: a system may filter impermissible or abusive material while ordering the surviving feed by time. Side B answered that both functions use the same behavioral machinery, but common machinery does not establish a common objective or require engagement-based ordering.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won decisively by cleanly separating content moderation from engagement-optimization ranking. While Side B repeatedly tried to conflate the two—arguing that any algorithmic demotion of toxic content constitutes "ranking"—Side A effectively dismantled this with the "spam filter" and "smoke detector vs. slot machine" analogies, proving that the objective function (maximizing time-on-site vs. suppressing harm) is what drives systemic toxicity. Side A's pressure questions exposed this conflation, and their closing crystallized the core asymmetry: chronological feed failures are correctable by the user, whereas algorithmic feed failures are the product working as intended.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they answered Side B's pressure questions by distinguishing human amplification (retweets) from algorithmic broadcasting, and decisively proved that subtracting violative content (moderation) is not the same as reordering survivors to maximize engagement (ranking).
