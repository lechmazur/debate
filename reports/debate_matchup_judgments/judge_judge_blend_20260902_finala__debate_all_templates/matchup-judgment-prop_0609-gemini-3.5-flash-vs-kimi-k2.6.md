### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): PRO built the stronger cumulative case. The most pivotal moment was PRO's Rebuttal 2 decoupling move: separating the automatic, objective-trigger suspension of debt service (instant liquidity) from sovereign-led ex-post verification against a nation's own NAP (not foreign blueprints). This directly answered CON's two core objections—delay and subordination—in a single operational innovation that was not present in the opening and was never fully neutralized. CON's response that ex-post audits are "a loaded weapon" and actual cancellation stays "locked behind a compliance gate" was the strongest counter available, but PRO had already drawn a clear distinction between pausing payments (automatic) and verifying allocation (subsequent, domestic). CON's most persistent live burden was the political feasibility of unconditional debt relief. CON cited pandemic moratoria and HIPC-style cancellations as proof that no-strings relief is achievable, but never closed the gap on why creditors would reliably and systematically replicate those exceptional episodes rather than defaulting to conditionality. "It has happened before" does not rebut "creditors systematically refuse it," especially under the motion's institutional design question. PRO hammered this gap effectively across every round, and CON never produced a mechanism for getting from here to universal unconditional cancellation. CON's strongest arguments—the catastrophe bond analogy collapse, structural subordination, immediate consumption as legitimate survival need, and NAPs as donor-drafted documents—each landed real blows, but none survived PRO's decoupling response intact. The clipping of CON's Rebuttal 1 is a real execution cost. CON's justice framing was emotionally resonant but functioned more as a competing moral ideal than a direct rebuttal of the operational proposition, and PRO's "physical survival is prerequisite for sovereignty" counter matched it on its own moral terrain. On balance, PRO made the debate easier to decide by progressing from a structural claim to a concrete mechanism while CON's ideal remained aspirational.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 decoupling argument: the automatic trigger provides instant liquidity by suspending debt service on objective criteria, while climate-resilience verification happens ex-post using the debtor's own National Adaptation Plans rather than upfront bureaucratic gatekeeping. This directly answered both of CON's primary objections—delay migrating to compliance offices, and conditionality equaling foreign subordination—by separating the timing and locus of each function. CON's response that ex-post audits are still a compliance gate was the right counter but arrived too late and too briefly to neutralize the structural logic PRO had established.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): B won by making the central tradeoff clearer: automatic conditionality creates bureaucracy, external control, and potential delays that undermine the very relief A says must be urgent. A had a plausible debt-climate feedback story and a useful pragmatism argument, but B repeatedly exposed the tension between broad resilience definitions and enforceable conditions, and between instant relief and later verification. A's National Adaptation Plan answer reduced some sovereignty pressure, but B answered that ex-post audits still become a compliance gate and that urgent consumption can itself be survival. The clipped rebuttal and B's somewhat broad historical claims keep this competitive, but B had the cleaner comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was against A's catastrophe-bond analogy: B showed that triggering money on objective disaster data is different from verifying sovereign spending through procurement, audits, and compliance, so the promised automaticity likely just shifts gridlock from creditors to auditors.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): CON won by sharpening the sovereignty/coercion and bureaucracy-migration clashes across rebuttals, showing that automatic conditionality neither unlocks larger relief nor preserves real discretion, while PRO's ex-post and NAPs defenses failed to neutralize those practical burdens.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2's direct answers on immediate consumption as survival and ex-post verification as still locking relief behind compliance gates, which damaged PRO's pragmatism route.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): PRO built a durable and consistent comparative case that CON never cleanly dismantled. The decisive throughline was PRO's repeated claim that every harm CON attributed to the proposed mechanism—opportunity cost, capacity lockout, sovereignty loss, market panic—already occurs under the status quo, often in worse form. CON never squarely refuted this comparison; instead, CON kept pressing on internal weaknesses of the PRO model without defending the baseline. PRO's rebuttal to the sovereignty argument was especially strong: automatic, advance-known triggers are more sovereign-respecting than ad hoc Paris Club bargaining, and CON's "automated paternalism" response never resolved why pre-agreed rules are more coercive than secret austerity memoranda. CON's sharpest individual moment was the verification dilemma in [B4.4-B4.5]—too lax invites corruption, too rigorous locks out the fragile—which PRO's satellite-imagery answer addressed only partially. But this point, while cutting, did not anchor a stronger overall frame. CON's non-climate crisis pressure question (pandemic, food shock) was legitimate and put real stress on PRO, but PRO answered adequately by showing that freed fiscal space enables both climate investment and emergency spending simultaneously. CON's closings grew increasingly repetitive rather than crystallizing a winning comparison. PRO's closing effectively crystallized the unmanaged-mutual-ruin frame. Both sides stayed grounded in background knowledge without fake citations. CON's "capacity trap" was the most original individual argument, but it was not developed into a full systemic alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal in [A2.1–A2.2] turning the sovereignty argument: PRO correctly identified that under the current system, finance ministers beg creditor clubs for secret, ad hoc austerity terms, making that regime more paternalistic than a transparent, advance-known automatic trigger. CON's response ("automated paternalism" is still paternalism even if pre-agreed) was consistent but never resolved why rule-governed, publicly known conditions strip more self-determination than opaque bilateral bargaining. This exchange shaped the rest of the debate and PRO held the better end of it throughout.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A wins by making the debt-climate doom loop the central comparative and showing why predictable, resilience-linked relief can protect both debtor fiscal capacity and creditor asset values. Side B raised real concerns about sovereignty, urgent non-climate needs, capacity, and borrowing costs, but often overstated the model as requiring all relief to be spent on climate and did not fully answer A’s point that climate resilience protects the tax base funding hospitals and food security. A’s status quo comparison was somewhat binary, but its rebuttals were more direct and better weighed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s answer to the opportunity-cost and sovereignty attacks was most decisive: it argued that debt relief creates fiscal space for both emergency needs and resilience, while current ad hoc relief already imposes slower and harsher conditionality.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A wins by establishing that the status quo already imposes worse conditionality and delays, then neutralizing CON's autonomy, capacity, and market objections through concrete mechanisms and direct A1/A2 answers that show relief expands space for both resilience and immediate needs; B's points on sovereignty and verification remain partially live but less decisive after A's reframing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 directly answering B's pressure questions on non-climate crises and capacity verification, exposing that the status quo already delays relief while A’s model accelerates it with scaled verification.
