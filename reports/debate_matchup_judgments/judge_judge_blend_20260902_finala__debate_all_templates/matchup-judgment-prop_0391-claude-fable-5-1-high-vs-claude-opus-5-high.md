### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): A won a clear but competitive debate by distinguishing aggressive consequences from careless detection. B powerfully identified false positives, brigading, and scalable architecture, but treated bad automation as an inevitable feature of stricter moderation and never established why stronger sanctions on confirmed abuse must lower evidentiary standards. A also weighed the existing chilling effect on targets more persuasively. B’s reputation-based alternative depended on classification of offenders while claiming to avoid the same error risks, and it did not fully answer why proven abusers should face no durable consequence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s decisive response was that speedier, escalating penalties for confirmed abuse are compatible with review and appeals: aggression concerns consequences, while sloppiness concerns evidence. B’s precision-versus-aggression distinction exposed implementation risks but did not neutralize that separation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by keeping the central clash intuitive and durable: harassment is a selection mechanism that drives out vulnerable players, and a system that never punishes proven abusers is abdication dressed up as design. Side B raised sophisticated objections about automation error and architectural alternatives, but suffered real execution costs from two word-limit clips that truncated its Opening and Rebuttal 2, and it never fully answered A's pressure that the slur-spammer simply stays in the game. A's reframing of "who gets spontaneity" and its weighing of correctable errors against permanent uninstalls proved the cleaner route to victory after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which framed CON's model as "abdication with good interface design," showed CON never priced the cost of letting proven abusers remain, and turned CON's own vulnerable-user concern back on CON by noting that default-off comms taxes the closeted teenager on day one.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): PRO wins the central comparison. CON's best case - automated filters misfire, brigades weaponize reports, throwaway accounts evade bans - was turned by PRO into a case for resource-aggressive enforcement (faster consequences, review appeals, friction after a strike, not before). PRO answered Pressure cleanly with A1/A2 format: aggression = consequence speed on confirmed abuse, not lowering evidence bar; ID friction only after abuse. CON's architecture answers Q2 with "loses audience" but concedes no consequence for proven abuser and, as PRO exposed, imposes default-off voice on day one for everyone - the same spontaneity cost PRO imposes only after a strike. Clipped B speeches plus the enforcement-vs-setting semantic dodge left CON's live route weaker.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 turn [A2.1-A2.6] plus Rebuttal 2 [A4.3] where throwaway accounts, brigading, and accent-flagging are reframed as under-enforcement and PRO distinguishes post-strike friction from CON's day-one tax on all newcomers, collapsing CON's equity argument.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): A narrowly won by showing that aggressive moderation need not mean indiscriminate automation: it can mean greater investment, severity-based triage, human review for serious abuse, and reversible responses under uncertainty. B offered the sharper implementation critique and an excellent account-value asymmetry, but relied too heavily on the unsupported premise that serious abuse is already maximally enforced and that marginal enforcement must target ambiguous speech mechanically. A also weighed the silent, lasting exclusion of victims more directly against culture objections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to A’s apparent contradiction was the strongest exchange: bans can be ineffective against disposable harasser accounts yet devastating to established players because enforcement bites according to what an account has to lose. A nevertheless preserved its case by advocating triage and graduated consequences rather than automatic permanent bans.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): CON won by isolating the motion's marginal product: the proposition explicitly demands stricter enforcement despite culture objections, and CON showed that the only feasible scaled expansion hits the ambiguous middle with automation, harming invested ordinary players while bouncing off throwaway abusers. PRO's victim framing and "whose freedom" reframe were powerful, but CON's "aggression is regressive" thesis and the triage mapping turned PRO's own concession about "cheap" automated low-grade responses into a decisive argument that the motion adds precisely the harms it is asked to override.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's demonstration that PRO's triage model assigns humans to cases already enforced while handing the ambiguous middle to machines, meaning the motion's only marginal product is automated flags on ordinary speech (B4.5-B4.6).
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): PRO won the weighing. A defined aggressive as resourced triage not blind automation, defusing scale/false-positive claims, showed mute/ block shifts cost to victim and fails 50-account pile-ons/doxxing, and turned CON's own gates/quarantine/default-off as conceding the principle of overriding culture objections - the actual proposition. CON's regressive-punishment point is strong but left silent permanent exit vs reversible flag unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2/A4 reframe that "aggressive = investment + triage" and that under-resourced systems cause mass-report abuse, collapsing B1.4/B2.3 and exposing mute as cost-shifted.
