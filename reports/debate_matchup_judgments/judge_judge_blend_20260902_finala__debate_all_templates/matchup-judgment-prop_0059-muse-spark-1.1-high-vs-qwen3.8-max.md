### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won the framing battle that structured the entire debate by insisting the proposition requires a comparison to the realistic alternatives most users actually face (thin, costly, unsafe local pools), not an idealized pre-app offline market. PRO (Side B) never firmly established that this richer offline counterfactual would exist absent apps, and its key supporting claims (e.g., that campuses now show "worse conversion" than before, or that "surveys repeatedly find" majority negative outcomes) were asserted rather than grounded, which cost it on epistemic discipline. CON's "dominance requires some value, and matches are non-zero-sum unlike casino payouts" rebuttal was not fully dismantled, though PRO's casino-analogy counter (dominance ≠ value) landed as a real point that CON only partially answered in the final rebuttal. Still, CON's consistent pressure on the counterfactual burden, its answer that cheap exit is "cheap abandonment of the bad, not necessarily the good," and its tighter closing that isolated the still-unresolved "worse than what?" question gave it the cleaner, more decisive path to the decision. PRO's viable-but-awkward-middle mechanism was compelling but remained more speculative and less evidenced than CON's burden-shifting frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over whether apps "became the market" (B2.1) versus CON's reply that thinning offline institutions predate and are independent of apps (A2.1), reprised through the pressure questions and answered in A4.1/B4.1, was the pivotal clash: PRO's claim rested on an unproven counterfactual that CON kept exposing as asserted rather than demonstrated.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by keeping the debate focused on the required net comparison. Side B persuasively explained burnout, gamified replacement, and the loss of viable-but-awkward matches, but repeatedly asserted rather than established that these losses outweigh the broader pools, intent signals, and safer screening apps provide for most users. Side A also exposed the unsupported counterfactual that removing apps would restore a rich offline market. Side B was more memorable rhetorically, but Side A made the motion harder to affirm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s decisive response was that unpleasantness and ghosting do not themselves prove fewer stable relationships, and that Side B never showed its lost awkward middle exceeds the relationships apps create or the bad matches they efficiently clear.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by establishing a clear causal mechanism: the dominant swiping model systematically undermines the conditions for stable relationship formation through engagement-optimized choice architecture that prioritizes infinite replacement over repeated exposure to viable-but-awkward matches. Side A (CON) effectively challenged the pre-app counterfactual and highlighted access benefits for marginalized users, but never squarely answered B's pressure for evidence that the median user forms more stable relationships now than before, nor did they neutralize the "viable-but-awkward middle" argument. A's defense that users can still choose repetition ignored the structural incentives B identified, and their closing relied on an exhaustion-mood distinction without proving net formation gains. B's rebuttals and closing more effectively narrowed the clash and crystallized the decisive question.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over the "viable-but-awkward middle" and frictionless abandonment, where B showed that low-cost rejection systematically eliminates the repeated exposure necessary for most stable relationships, and A was unable to prove that mutual willingness to repeat survives the app's choice architecture.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON (A) won the decisive exchange. PRO's central frame—initiation vs. formation, with choice overload and low accountability degrading conversion—was clever, but CON's pressure question (A3.1) exposed a real internal tension: PRO simultaneously claimed users are harmed by "too much choice" and by "too little quality," implying constant juggling of options, while PRO's own rebuttal conceded median users don't actually juggle dozens of prospects because "chatting is work." CON's rebuttal 2 and closing pressed this contradiction hard, arguing that perceived replaceability cannot bite if users aren't actually replacing partners, and PRO's answer (B4.1) never resolved this beyond restating the claim. CON also successfully reframed the baseline (zero organic prospects vs. thin app starts) and used the widely-known fact that online meeting is now the most common path to durable couples as a direct answer to PRO's "just more starts" framing—PRO's closing dismissed this as "proving access, not formation" without engaging why data on how actual couples met doesn't count as formation evidence. CON's opening was clipped for length, a real execution cost, but the core arguments survived and were reinforced in later rebuttals, so this cost was minor relative to the strength of the rebuttal exchanges.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question (A3.1) forcing PRO to reconcile "too much choice" with "too little quality," followed by CON's rebuttal 2 (A4.3) showing PRO's own concession that "chatting is work" undercuts the replaceability mechanism—PRO's answer (B4.1) never fully resolved this tension.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A narrowly won by insisting that formation be measured per user against realistic offline access, not merely by conversion per match. Side B compellingly explained replaceability and low-accountability design, but never established that these harms outweigh expanded search and screening for most users. A's clipped opening, overstated zero-prospect baseline, and late prevalence claim kept the result close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's A1 response sharply reconciled abundant low-cost possibilities with scarce quality matches. However, Side A's broader per-person comparison remained decisive: lower conversion per start can still improve formation when the model creates substantially more viable starts.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON wins by grounding the debate in baseline reality and per-person probability. PRO’s initiation/formation distinction is analytically sharp but never overcomes CON’s core comparative: for the median adult with sparse offline options, turning zero prospects into several starts that yield a durable relationship is an improvement in formation, not a degradation. CON successfully collapses the initiation/formation split by showing formation is measured per person over time, not per chat, and leverages the widely known fact that online is now the dominant way couples meet to rebut the claim that apps only multiply fragility. PRO’s arguments about choice overload and low accountability are plausible but weakened by an unresolved tension—claiming most users are simultaneously harmed by too much choice and too little quality—and by the failure to show that offline baselines actually convert thin starts into durable relationships at a higher rate for most modern adults. CON’s rebuttals are cleaner, its weighing more direct, and its closing crystallization of the clash more memorable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s collapse of the initiation/formation distinction in Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2, showing that for adults with near-zero offline pipelines, five thin app starts yielding one durable relationship is better formation than zero starts yielding zero relationships, and that per-person probability—not per-chat conversion—is the correct metric for whether the model helps most users.
