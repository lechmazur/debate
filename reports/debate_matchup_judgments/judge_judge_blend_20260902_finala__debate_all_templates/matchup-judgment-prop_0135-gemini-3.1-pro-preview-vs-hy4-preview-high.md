### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): A won the central conditional clash: when concentrated arrivals create a genuine net loss and exceed finite physical capacity, pricing cannot necessarily repair or compensate for the harm. B convincingly weakened A’s housing link and offered better-targeted tools, but its fallback to slot rationing effectively conceded that volume limits are sometimes necessary. B’s distinction between measuring harms for full-cost pricing and measuring them for the motion’s threshold was also too thin to sustain its claim that the latter is inherently impossible. A’s nonexistent C-reference IDs were an execution flaw but not outcome-determinative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest exchange was the argument that B cannot call harms unmeasurable while advocating full-cost congestion and peak-hour pricing. B offered a proxy-based distinction, but then conceded rationing at hard physical limits, substantially validating A’s case for caps.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by demonstrating that the resolution's "outweigh" trigger is analytically fraught and that superior, more targeted alternatives exist. CON sustained the critique that crowding, pollution, and housing pressure cannot be cleanly converted into a common unit with revenue, leaving the threshold vulnerable to politicized "rubber stamp" decisions. More importantly, CON showed that day-trippers do not directly compete for housing stock, that caps scapegoat two visible modes while ignoring private cars, flights, and short-term rentals, and that staggered berth windows, slot allocation, and full-cost pricing can manage physical scarcity without destroying revenue. PRO's commercial-mutation housing argument remained plausible but speculative next to CON's direct causal mechanism, and PRO never fully answered why slot allocation could not address the finite medieval-space problem. PRO landed strong rhetorical blows and exposed tensions in CON's case, but CON's sharper weighing and preservation of multiple live routes to victory after the full exchange provided the cleaner reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's pressure question in Rebuttal 2 (A4.3), distinguishing between using observable proxies for marginal congestion pricing and the resolution's unworkable holistic "outweigh" test, which preserved CON's core incommensurability critique without collapsing into contradiction.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by exposing a fatal contradiction in Side B's case: Side B argued that the harms of crowding and pollution were unmeasurable and thus made the resolution's threshold a "rubber stamp," yet simultaneously advocated for congestion pricing and fees based on those exact harms. Side A effectively argued that if a city can quantify harm to price it, it can quantify it to cap it. Furthermore, Side A's rhetorical framing of physical limits ("you cannot buy more square footage in a medieval plaza") decisively defeated Side B's reliance on market mechanisms, making Side A's comparative case much stronger and more logically cohesive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.2-A4.3), where they explicitly called out Side B's contradiction regarding the measurability of harms for pricing versus capping, effectively neutralizing Side B's primary offensive argument about the resolution's trigger being unworkable.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): A won by using the motion's conditional threshold effectively and distinguishing a cap from a ban. B exposed a genuine weakness in linking day-trippers to housing pressure and offered fairer-seeming alternatives, but repeatedly relied on unsupported claims that overnight substitution was inevitable and that caps categorically excluded bulk-transit users. A directly answered both assumptions, preserved exemptions and complementary measures, and showed that clean vehicles or timed entry cannot always solve excessive total volume.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal was decisive: it rejected B's assumed one-for-one substitution into overnight stays and clarified that smaller accessible vehicles would serve a reduced visitor total rather than replicate every capped coach. This substantially weakened B's housing and congestion contradictions.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by grounding the debate in the motion&#x27;s trigger condition: caps apply only when crowding, pollution, and housing pressure already outweigh revenue gains, so the proportionate response is fewer peak arrivals, not a forced pivot to overnight tourism. Side A (CON) never escaped the assumption that revenue must be preserved, but the motion explicitly says revenue is outweighed by costs. CON&#x27;s concession that the answer is &quot;managing total capacity&quot; [A4.6] allowed PRO to collapse the distinction between CON&#x27;s timed-entry alternative and the motion&#x27;s targeted volume limit. PRO effectively blunted CON&#x27;s housing objection by showing the cap can pair with STR rules and that bulk volume itself feeds the transience economy, and neutralized the accessibility charge by noting that any cap excludes someone while the crush of bulk arrivals itself excludes the elderly and disabled.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO&#x27;s Rebuttal 2 and Closing seized on CON&#x27;s concession that the solution is &quot;managing total capacity&quot; to show that CON&#x27;s own timed-entry system is just another cap that sorts by booking speed and hotel proximity, while failing to solve the arithmetic of low-yield, high-volume bulk arrivals that the motion targets.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by systematically dismantling Side B's core assumption. Side B's most damaging argument—that capping day-trippers forces a substitution to overnight guests, thereby worsening the housing crisis—relies on a false equivalence that total tourist volume remains constant. Side A effectively clarifies that the motion aims for a net reduction in total arrivals when costs outweigh gains, not a one-for-one swap, and argues persuasively that overall tourism intensity and investor yield, not just overnight beds, drive housing pressure. Side A also neutralizes Side B's timed-entry alternative by pointing out it is functionally just another form of capping that still excludes visitors. Side B's rhetoric was punchy and memorable, but the logical gap in their substitution argument was ultimately fatal to their case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7), where they directly answer the pressure questions by exposing Side B's substitution and replication fallacies, clarifying that the motion cuts total arrivals rather than swapping day-trippers for hotel guests or replacing one large bus with ten small vans.
