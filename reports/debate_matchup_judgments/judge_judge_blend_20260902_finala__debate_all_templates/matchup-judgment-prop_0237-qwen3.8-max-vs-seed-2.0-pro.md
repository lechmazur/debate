### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B's central asymmetry argument—that statutory restrictions are permanent while "expanded legal pathways" are discretionary appropriations that can be starved without formally repealing anything—proved to be the debate's load-bearing clash. A offered a real mechanism (automatic statutory conditionality plus individualized pre-transfer safety review) in response to pressure questions, which was a genuine constructive answer, but B's Rebuttal 2 specifically anticipated and rebutted that mechanism (delayed funding votes, quiet narrowing of eligibility without formally cutting quotas), and A's closing never directly answered that specific counter, instead re-asserting the moral framing. B also successfully flipped A's "walk to the border" equity point by showing the current system, however cruel, is the only option left for the poorest, and the proposal removes it without a durable replacement. B's closing crystallized this unresolved asymmetry memorably ("you do not burn the last life raft"), while A's closing largely restated its opening frame rather than closing the specific gap B had opened. B's use of a fairly precise historical statistic without sourcing is a minor grounding blemish, but it does not offset the structural rebuttal advantage B sustained through the later rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1) directly countering A's "automatic sunset trigger" answer to the pressure question, showing how an administration could gut pathways through funding delays and quiet eligibility narrowing without technically triggering the suspension—paired with A's closing failing to rebut this specific mechanism.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won by presenting the proposal as a conditional policy bundle rather than an unconditional asylum bar, then explaining how automatic suspension and individualized safety review could preserve that exchange. Side B powerfully defended territorial asylum as a last backstop, but relied too heavily on asserting that guardrails would inevitably fail and on unsupported historical precision. It never adequately explained why the motion should be judged as a restriction enacted separately from its stipulated expanded pathways.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s answer to the permanence objection was decisive: it made the border restriction legally operative only while pathway minima remained available, so expiration would automatically revive eligibility. Side B raised plausible risks of administrative manipulation but did not squarely defeat that statutory design.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): PRO won by answering CON's strongest pressures directly: statutory conditionality makes the exchange enforceable, and individualized safety hearings prevent arbitrary transfer. CON's powerful "last backstop" rhetoric never overcame PRO's core point that the current territorial system itself privileges those with smugglers and money. PRO crystallized the decisive clash in closing, showing that a right dependent on surviving a dangerous journey is not equal protection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7), where they began with required A1:/A2: labels to defend statutory automatic suspension of border limits if pathways lapse and commit to individualized, evidence-based safety hearings—directly defusing CON's "fake bargain" and "exports cruelty" claims.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): B built a more durable case by identifying two structural problems early and never letting go of them: (1) "safe third country" is a definitional test the region likely cannot pass, not merely "safer than the desert," and (2) the trade is sequentially asymmetric—border limits are immediate while pathways depend on unsecured future cooperation. B's pressure questions sharpened exactly these points, asking what stops any cartel-plagued city from being labeled "safe" and what happens to a family stranded mid-transition. A's answers in Rebuttal 2 had to invent substantial new plan machinery not present in the opening—UN-verified housing, vetted anti-cartel police, monitors, and a "prospective only" cutoff—which reads as ad hoc patching rather than elaboration of an existing case, and violates the round's evidence norms against late-stage specificity. B explicitly exploited this, noting these are promised conditions, not facts, and that the prospective cutoff is arbitrary rather than principled. A's closing reasserted "less risk" and adjustability but never rebutted B's sharpest line—that wrongful returns are irreversible while delayed pathways are correctable—leaving B's asymmetry framing as the last word and the cleaner decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.3-B4.6), which reframes A's newly introduced safeguards as unenforceable promises rather than secured facts, and crystallizes the irreversibility/asymmetry argument that A's closing never fully answers.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): B won by showing that regional access and border screening need not be mutually exclusive, while A never secured protection for future applicants facing imminent threats. A introduced detailed monitoring, policing, and grandfathering safeguards only in Rebuttal 2, then B convincingly showed that these depended on uncertain implementation and did not establish individual safety. A highlighted real status quo harms, but relied on brittle numbers and an inadequately supported claim that political compromise was impossible without sharp limits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s late safeguards was decisive: prospective application merely protected those present on one arbitrary date, while the next equally endangered family could be barred, and monitored shelters could not guarantee safety from transnational threats.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side B won by showing that PRO’s tradeoff is structurally unreliable and morally asymmetric. CON demonstrated that sharp border limits would take effect immediately while expanded pathways remain uncertain future promises, and that outsourcing asylum seekers to third countries does not eliminate persecution risk—it relocates it. CON’s rebuttal of PRO’s “prospective only” safeguard as an arbitrary moral cutoff, combined with the sustained asymmetry framing, left PRO unable to prove the trade would actually deliver safety. PRO also undermined its own credibility with brittle fake precision and by introducing elaborate new factual safeguards in Rebuttal 2 that depended on unstated foreign cooperation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 demolition of PRO’s “prospective only” rule and the asymmetry framing—showing that a principle protecting refugees cannot logically expire on a cutoff date, and that if PRO’s safeguards fail the harm is irreversible whereas CON can still add pathways later.
