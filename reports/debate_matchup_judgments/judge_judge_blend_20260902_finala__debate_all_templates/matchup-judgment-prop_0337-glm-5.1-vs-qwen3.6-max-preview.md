### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins because it held the stronger live route to victory after the full exchange. Three factors decide the margin. First, PRO's default-bias argument—that most teens never change the preset and the motion is about existing-account architecture, not new surveillance—survives CON's verification objection more cleanly than CON acknowledges. PRO correctly distinguishes the motion (change a default for already-registered minors) from the straw man of building a new identity verification regime. Second, PRO's technical answer to CON's "you can't decouple addiction from utility" objection—explicit quality signals like saves, shares, and intentional clicks can replace passive dwell-time signals—is plausible and specific enough that CON never refutes it; CON asserts the decoupling is impossible but never explains why saves/shares cannot drive relevance, leaving the assertion hanging. Third, PRO's business-model indictment is a genuine turn: CON's most prominent practical argument ("engagement funds safety teams") entails that child safety must be purchased with child addiction, and PRO correctly notes this indicts the status quo rather than the reform. CON's strongest surviving argument is the displacement/fragmentation claim—determined teens bypass defaults and migrate to worse, unmoderated spaces. This is real pressure PRO never fully neutralizes; PRO's network-effects counter is plausible but insufficient. CON also scores with the "compliant vs. determined teen" analytical split in Rebuttal 2, which resolves PRO's Q2 contradiction cleanly. However, CON's other pillars—digital resilience (PRO's bourbon analogy wins that exchange), the defunding-safety argument (circular and somewhat self-defeating), and the verification alarm (overstated given what the motion actually requires)—each weaken under PRO's pressure. PRO's moral framing is sharper and more consistently sustained, and the "CON chose harm" crystallization in the closing lands harder than CON's final response. On balance PRO built the cleaner comparative case for why the default should change; CON's practical objections are real but leave too many self-defeating concessions on the table.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 on the "digital resilience" argument: the claim that exposure to addictive design builds immunity rather than dependency directly contradicts addiction psychology, and CON's counter—that digital navigation is a "learned competency" unlike toxin exposure—never explains why variable-reward slot-machine mechanics are more like a skill than a substance. PRO's bourbon analogy lands and CON drops the line entirely in later rounds, conceding the exchange by abandonment.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): A won by making the motion about safer defaults for a vulnerable majority and by showing CON's objections overreached: perfect age verification is not required for defaults, displacement is speculative, and less-addictive design need not eliminate discovery. B raised serious implementation and safety-funding concerns, but leaned on unsupported inevitabilities that engagement loss would defund protection and that recommendation utility cannot be separated from addictive mechanics.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer to CON's verification and circumvention attack was most decisive: defaults do not need airtight enforcement, and B's claim of trivial bypass sat awkwardly beside its prediction of major engagement collapse. That preserved A's route that a majority benefit justifies the policy.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B won by decisively neutralizing Side A's practical objections and turning the core clash on platform economics. Side A argued that dropping engagement would defund crucial safety moderation teams, but Side B successfully framed this as an unacceptable mandate to "addict children to fund their safety." Furthermore, Side B effectively answered Side A's technical pressure on "lifelines" by drawing a plausible distinction between passive addictive mechanics (infinite scroll, dwell time) and active relevance signals (saves, shares), proving discovery could survive without compulsion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal and subsequent pressure question on platform economics, pointing out that defending the status quo means arguing platforms must addict children to afford protecting them.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): PRO built a more coherent and better-defended case across the full exchange. The most important technical clash was CON's claim that a "less addictive" feed necessarily destroys useful curation, and PRO's [B2.1] answer—that the motion changes the algorithm's objective function rather than removing it—was the most decisive single rebuttal. It directly answered CON's chronological-firehose concern and preserved PRO's developmental-health argument intact. PRO also handled the pressure questions more cleanly: on safety evaporating, PRO correctly identified that brand liability, app-store compliance, and legal duties are independent of teen watch-time revenue, which was a direct and adequate answer to CON's floor-versus-ceiling point. On the opt-out contradiction, PRO's closing crystallized it as a feature not a bug—defaults protect the inert majority, opt-outs serve motivated exceptions—which is a coherent behavioral-design principle rather than a paradox. CON's strongest unresolved argument was the "who defines addictive" closing: if platforms themselves operationalize the standard, the motion expands rather than constrains corporate discretion. PRO's A1 answer gestured at regulatory criteria and age-appropriate design codes, but never fully explained who anchors the definition externally, leaving this burden partly open. However, CON's Rebuttal 2 was clipped mid-sentence, costing a full round of argument at a critical juncture, and CON's core objection—that engagement and meaningful discovery are inseparable—was never sustained against PRO's objective-function reframe. PRO's case was more internally consistent, better answered the live pressure, and left a cleaner reason to vote for the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's [B2.1] rebuttal distinguishing "changing the algorithm's objective function" from "stripping the algorithm entirely" was the most decisive exchange. It neutralized CON's firehose concern, absorbed CON's Instagram example, and preserved every benefit CON attributed to algorithmic curation—while keeping PRO's developmental-harm argument structurally intact. CON never recovered a satisfying answer to this distinction.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): A wins because it made the cleaner comparative case that defaults strongly shape behavior, minors deserve the protected baseline, and algorithmic usefulness can be preserved while reducing compulsive optimization. B had a strong concern about who defines addictive and possible suppression of niche communities, but relied too much on the assumption that lower addiction necessarily means weaker discovery, weaker safety, and censorship. A directly challenged that false equivalence and better weighed the vulnerable majority against edge cases.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's most decisive rebuttal was that less-addictive feeds need not mean chronological firehoses or loss of safety filtering: platforms can preserve relevance and moderation while changing ranking objectives away from watch-time extraction. That undercut much of B's communities, safety, and censorship framing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side A systematically dismantled Side B's mechanisms by demonstrating that reducing algorithmic engagement inherently reduces the curation signals that surface valuable content, and astutely pointed out that giving platforms the mandate to lower engagement effectively hands them unchecked censorship power. Side A also cleanly exposed the contradiction in Side B's reliance on both "defaults are destiny" and the availability of opt-outs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 provided an excellent answer to Side B's pressure questions, particularly the distinction that legal and brand liability only establish a "floor" for safety, meaning a drop in engagement revenue would lead platforms to deliver minimum viable compliance.
