### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B won by focusing on the proposition’s net comparison. A plausibly showed that concentration can increase markups and weaken innovation incentives, but largely asserted that alternative arrangements can reproduce merger efficiencies and never established that the additional deals blocked under stricter enforcement would be harmful overall. B directly compared markup effects with marginal-cost reductions, explained why integration can outperform contracts, and pressed A’s weakly supported assumptions about startup exits and post-clearance deals. B sometimes overstated the reliability of current screening and the conclusiveness of wireless, but its comparative case remained substantially stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response that consumers pay prices rather than markups was decisive: reduced competition can raise the markup while merger-specific cost savings still lower the final price. A’s continued-rivalry counterfactual required showing that comparable savings were available without merging, but its reliance on contracts, joint ventures, and organic growth remained asserted rather than demonstrated.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B wins on the marginal-deal comparison: A never shows deals clearing current screens are mostly harmful, leaning on "routinely" without cases, while B separates markup from price, explains why contracts/JVs don't replicate integration (hold-up, premium logic), and why blocked savings are lost forever versus conduct remedies for harmful deals. Wireless as a clean test where B's model predicted lower prices holds, A only calls it contaminated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 A1-A2 - price is markup over marginal cost so higher markup can coexist with lower price, plus surgical conduct remedies preserve savings while targeting harm, exposing A's missing evidence for marginal harms and premium-as-rent claim.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by introducing a decisive economic distinction between markups and absolute prices, effectively neutralizing Side B's core mechanism regarding pass-through. Side A also won the asymmetry of error clash by clearly explaining how surgical conduct remedies can address post-merger harms without destroying the specific efficiencies that would be permanently lost if a merger were preemptively blocked. Side B relied too heavily on bare assertions about "market power rents" and struggled to defend against the T-Mobile/Sprint empirical example, dismissing it as "contaminated" without offering a stronger counter-narrative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1), where it dismantled Side B's focus on rising markups by explaining that a sufficient drop in marginal cost will still lower the final consumer price, exposing a fundamental flaw in Side B's elasticity and pass-through argument.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate. A offered stronger concrete examples and more memorable rhetoric, but repeatedly treated removing one rival as eliminating competitive discipline altogether. B convincingly explained that residual rivalry can pass through efficiencies, while vertical integration can remove double markups and coordinate investment. A never adequately answered that vertical case and overstated the substitutability of organic growth, licensing, and contracting. Thus B retained the stronger general account of productivity and innovation losses under stricter blocking.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response that a horizontal merger does not necessarily eliminate meaningful rivalry decisively undercut A’s central pass-through argument. Its accompanying vertical-merger point was especially damaging because A’s claim that firms merely pocket savings did not answer double marginalization or coordinated investment.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): PRO kept a live asymmetry: blocking bans only buying a rival while organic, licensing, and contracting remain, whereas merging bans the rivalry that forces pass-through. A turned CON's own B1.5 concession, survived the ratchet charge with a workable presumption, answered financing with fabless chips / biotech licensing, and won the evidence ledger—measured hospital/airline price rises vs CON's generic "consumer electronics supply chains" that never named a price-lowering merger after direct pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1 + A4.2 — using CON's "pass-through fails only when rivals permanently eliminated" to define horizontal mergers, then forcing CON to finally admit a ~3-rival stopping rule, exposing the ratchet.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won by forcing Side A into a defensive position on the pass-through of efficiencies and the necessity of acquisitions for innovation. B's pressure questions exposed A's lack of concrete examples for pro-consumer mergers, and B's rebuttals effectively dismantled A's innovation argument with specific counter-examples like fabless chips and biotech licensing. A's dismissal of major sectors like hospitals and airlines as "special cases" conceded too much empirical ground, leaving B with the stronger, more grounded comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where B answered the pressure questions with concrete alternatives to acquisition (fabless chips, biotech licensing) and sharply criticized A's failure to name a specific merger that lowered prices, exposing A's "consumer electronics" answer as a vague mechanism rather than a real-world example.
