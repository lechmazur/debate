### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO's decisive live argument—that when tests disappear, wealth migrates to holistic factors (essay consultants, manufactured extracurriculars, recommendation networks) that are far more expensive and less replicable than test prep—was the central comparative claim in the debate and was never squarely defeated. CON repeatedly attacked the test as purchasable but never defended holistic review as cheaper or more equitable than a standardized exam; that asymmetry was PRO's strongest route to victory and it remained open through the closing. PRO's Q2 pressure question usefully exposed a genuine tension in CON's logic: if coarse zip-code proxies are fair stand-ins for individual ability, an individually owned test score with fee waivers is at least as fair. CON's best moments were the self-selection argument in B4.1 (the requirement itself deters qualified low-income students before any officer reviews the file) and the UC enrollment data, both of which created real pressure. PRO's responses—distinguishing application volume from seat allocation and noting that self-selection cut both ways—were partial but enough to keep the argument live. CON's repeated use of the "$1.7 billion" test-prep figure is plausible background knowledge but was deployed with false precision across multiple rounds without new support, slightly undercutting epistemic discipline. PRO's closing was clipped, a minor execution cost, but the visible content crystallized the core comparison cleanly. On balance, PRO held the more durable comparative framing across the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2.1 move in Rebuttal 1: the comparative argument that test-prep money, once tests vanish, migrates to essay consultants and extracurricular manufacturing that cost far more and cannot be replicated with a library card. This reframe—not "is the test biased?" but "biased compared to what?"—set the terms of the entire subsequent exchange and CON never directly answered the comparison, only attacking the test in isolation.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A consistently won the comparative framing by arguing that abolishing tests doesn't eliminate wealth from admissions, but merely shifts it to more opaque and expensive holistic factors. Side B effectively defended contextual GPA review but neglected to address the migration of wealth into extracurriculars and essays.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 and 2 effectively neutralize B's test-prep objections by pointing out that the wealth simply migrates to less transparent holistic factors that are even harder for low-income students to replicate.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): B maintained the decisive comparison that tests are harder to buy than holistics and handled pressure questions directly, showing CON's access claims do not resolve zero-sum displacement or neighborhood stereotypes. A leaned on UC data without neutralizing the migration argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R2 A1/A2 exchange on prep migration and enrollment interpretation.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): PRO (Side A) wins a clear but competitive debate on the strength of two moves that CON never fully neutralized. First, the UC test-blind data: CON's counter—that officers defaulted to wealth-coded signals because underlying systemic inequities were never fixed, not because tests were removed—actually reinforced PRO's central claim that admissions officers predictably discount underfunded-school GPAs when left to holistic judgment. That pivot made PRO's case stronger, not weaker. Second, and most decisively, PRO's "unenforceable wishlist" framing in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing: CON's alternative (contextualized transcript review, curbing essay coaching, eliminating legacy preferences) is exactly what holistic admissions was already supposed to deliver—and has not delivered for decades. CON never gave a concrete mechanism for how this would work differently now. When PRO pressed Q1 on what specific policy replaces the uniform benchmark, CON's A1 answer—"contextualized transcript review"—is aspirational but structurally identical to the holistic review already in use. PRO's "counterweight, not cure-all" framing in A2 was honest and effective: it conceded tests are imperfect while maintaining they are the one portable, verifiable credential a low-income student can use to offset GPA discounting. CON's best line—"a ruler that measures family income is uniform, but it is not just"—was memorable and genuine, and CON correctly identified that wealthy students can combine test scores with every other advantage. But CON's equitable alternative remained unresolved throughout, and PRO's closing crystallized the live clash cleanly rather than widening it. The debate was genuinely competitive; CON's philosophical framing was elegant, but PRO held the more actionable and better-grounded path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 framing that CON's alternative constitutes an "unenforceable wishlist" that cash-strapped public universities have failed to deliver for decades. When CON answered Q1 by offering "contextualized transcript review," PRO had already established—and CON had effectively conceded—that admissions officers routinely discount underfunded-school transcripts under current holistic review. This made CON's proposed fix structurally identical to the system already failing the students CON claimed to protect, and PRO exploited that gap cleanly in the Closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A won by successfully flipping the equity argument using the UC test-blind empirical example, demonstrating that removing tests actually harmed underrepresented students. Side A effectively framed tests as the only available counterweight to subjective wealth privileges, while successfully characterizing Side B's alternative as an unenforceable hypothetical.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing heavily damaged Side B's case by pointing out that Side B's solution (contextual transcript review and overhauling subjective metrics) relies on a utopian reform that cash-strapped universities have continually failed to implement.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by sustaining the core clash on UC outcomes and enforceable equalizers, directly answering pressure questions with scalable mechanisms while exposing CON's alternatives as unenforceable. Side A lands solid counters but leaves its reform path largely aspirational.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1/B4.1 exchange on school-based prep vs. subjective markers neutralized CON's privilege-layering rebuttal and crystallized the actionable-comparison burden.
