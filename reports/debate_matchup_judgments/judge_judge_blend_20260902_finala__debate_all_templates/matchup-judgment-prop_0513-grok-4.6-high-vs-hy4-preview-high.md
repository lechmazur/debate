### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-organized cases, but A's economic framing was more precise and harder to dislodge: monopsony wage suppression was consistently cast as genuine deadweight loss (hours/effort undersupplied), the correct textbook analog to monopoly's output restriction, whereas B's "largely a transfer" characterization never squarely answered that mechanism and instead leaned on administrability and efficiency-offset claims. A also turned B's own distinguishing points against it (e.g., "jobs are matched not shopped" implies more local, more merger-specific markets, not less) and offered a workable limiting principle (close substitutability, not shared job titles) that B's closing dismissed as "a standard, not a screen" without real engagement. The pivotal moment was A's Pressure Question forcing B to admit that even wage suppression proven to the same evidentiary standard as a price effect still would not earn an independent veto; B's honest A1 answer was consistent with CON's thesis, but it also exposed that CON's real objection is a normative hierarchy of harms rather than a pure administrability problem, and A's closing exploited that admission effectively ("hierarchy of victims," "administrability has dropped away"). B's tools/HHI-gap and two-manufacturer efficiency arguments remained live and were never fully eliminated, keeping this competitive rather than one-sided, but A's tighter causal story and sharper use of the pressure-question exchange gave it the cleaner path to the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question ("If agencies prove merger-specific wage suppression to the same standard used for a price increase, do you still refuse an independent block?") and B's direct A1 admission that it would still refuse, which A then used in closing to reframe B's administrability defense as a disguised value judgment that worker harm counts less than consumer harm.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing that merger-created buyer power is competition harm and that labor theories can face the same case-specific proof and efficiency analysis as consumer-price theories. Side A’s administrability concern remained meaningful, but its categorical refusal to block even proven wage suppression abandoned that defense. Its claim that wage suppression is mostly a transfer also failed to answer B’s point that monopsony can reduce employment and surplus, while alternative labor policies do not restore rivalry eliminated by the merger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B decisively exploited Side A’s answer that even labor harm proven to the same standard as a price effect should not independently block a merger. That concession converted the dispute from measurement difficulty into an unsupported hierarchy between seller-side and buyer-side market power.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built the decisive route: merger review stops buying market power, and buying power over workers creates deadweight loss parallel to consumer harm. Side A (CON) retreated to administrability, then in A4.1 conceded even proof to the same standard as price effects would not support an independent block because wage harm is "largely a transfer." That admission let B crystallize the clash as a hierarchy of victims and show flat prices don't certify efficiency. B also answered A's limiting-principle pressure with close-substitutability and existing evidentiary tools, while A's bespoke-markets/f false-positive claim was met by the HHI-is-a-screen-not-the-standard reply. PRO's equal-operational-status standard was more coherent with the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q1 in Pressure Questions forcing A to admit proven, merger-specific wage suppression still would not block, and B4-B5 using that concession to strip away the administrability excuse and frame tiebreaker status as half-analysis.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): A built a coherent mechanistic equivalence (destroyed alternatives → extraction, whether via higher prices or lower wages) and used the Pressure Questions round to expose the decisive weakness in B's framework: B's own A2 answer conceded that "pure, substantial wage depression with no proven price or output effect does not independently kill the deal," which functionally confirms A's central charge that CON's rule assigns zero independent weight to labor harm. A's answers to B's questions were more successful in defusing the "labor veto" charge by articulating a workable substantiality threshold analogous to how price-effect tests already work, whereas B's attempt to reframe that threshold as "co-equal treatment by another name" did not fully land because thresholds are how any equal-seriousness test would necessarily function. B's administrability and uncertainty arguments were real and unrebutted in full, but A's closing crystallized the core asymmetry (monopoly harm is condemned on its own terms; monopsony harm is waved through unless it shows up on a receipt) more memorably and left B's rebuttal largely restating priors rather than closing the gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question forcing B to admit that pure wage depression without proven price/output effect "does not independently kill the deal" (B4.3), which directly validated A's "zero weight" critique despite B's attempt to soften it as merely "refusing a second veto."
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that a merger can eliminate workers’ alternatives and extract value through lower wages just as seller power extracts value through higher prices. Side A raised legitimate administrability and efficiency concerns, but never justified why substantial, merger-specific worker harm should count only when it later affects consumers. Its claim that blocking does not restore outside options was especially weak because blocking preserves the competing employers. Side B also clarified that equal seriousness requires a substantial-harm threshold rather than making every wage change a veto.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response to the price-benefit hypothetical was decisive: a price reduction produced by destroyed labor-market alternatives may be passed-through monopsony extraction rather than productive efficiency. Side A answered by declaring the lower consumer bill controlling, which largely assumed the consumer-only standard it needed to defend.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): B won the central clash on weighting. It clarified equal seriousness as same substantial-lessening test for labor (not any dip = veto) and answered A's price-cut conflict, while A conceded in A2 that a proven, substantial, merger-specific wage depression with no price/output effect would not be blocked. That admission confirmed B's charge of zero-weighting and let B frame the hospital transfer example as preserved market power misread as efficiency. A's administrability defense was met by B's point that agencies already handle leaky markets.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 answers to A3.1/A3.2 recasting "same willingness to block" as triggered only by proven substantial labor harm, defusing the veto charge and showing modest wage effects clear, which turned A's strongest pressure into a reason to prefer the whole-market lens.
