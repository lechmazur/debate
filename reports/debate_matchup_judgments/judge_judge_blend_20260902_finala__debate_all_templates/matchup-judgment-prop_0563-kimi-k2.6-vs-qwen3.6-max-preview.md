- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): PRO built the stronger cumulative case across the exchange. This directly targeted the one thing CON offered as an alternative and showed it is functionally identical to the dysfunction CON itself criticized. CON never cleanly refuted this: its answer that multi-year contracts "lock in industrial predictability without creating a permanent entitlement" simply reasserted the distinction without explaining why those contracts would be immune to the political interruption that makes the status quo dysfunctional. PRO's second key live argument—that a Russian victory raises NATO deterrence costs above the entire price of Ukrainian aid, making the resource trade-off in the motion's own framing net-positive—was introduced early [B2.2] and consistently maintained. CON's response was "we are not advocating abandonment," which does not engage the systemic cost argument at all. CON's strongest original point—that permanent guarantees reduce Kyiv's incentive for self-sufficiency and diplomatic off-ramps—had real merit but was underdeveloped: PRO's answer that Kyiv cannot build defense industries while Russian missiles crater its power grid was never seriously rebutted. CON's "statutory straitjacket" framing was rhetorically effective and the motion's explicit "even if it reduces resources" clause gave CON a genuine hook, but PRO's response that total burden is lower with the commitment than without partially neutralized it. Both sides used grounded, plausible general knowledge without fake precision. PRO's pressure questions were sharper and more targeted; PRO also handled CON's pressure questions more directly, providing A1/A2 labels and substantive answers. On balance, PRO entered the closing with more live, unanswered arguments and a cleaner comparative story.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): CON (Side A) built the cleaner path to victory through two decisive moves that PRO never fully resolved. First, CON's "standing but conditional" dilemma—either the commitment is genuinely irrevocable (stripping leverage and creating entitlement politics) or Congress retains the power to pause it (making it functionally the same ad-hoc process PRO condemns)—was the sharpest exchange of the debate. PRO's A1 answer about "statutory baseline with oversight triggers" acknowledged the tension without escaping it: a trigger that Congress can pull is ad-hoc appropriations by another name. Second, CON's procurement-address argument ("shells fired in Donbas are not shells stockpiled in Indo-Pacific arsenals"; howitzers vs. submarines) was the most concrete and memorable distinction in the round. PRO's counter—that standing aid drives total industrial capacity—was plausible as a general claim but never addressed why guaranteed Ukrainian contracts would fund submarine pipelines rather than 155mm and tracked armor. PRO's strongest moment was the war-termination argument (guaranteed support removes Kyiv's incentive to capitulate, forcing Moscow to negotiate), but CON's counter—that open-ended assurance also removes Kyiv's incentive to accept territorial compromise—kept this live enough that neither side fully won it. PRO's opening and industrial surge framing were solid, and the structural deterrence logic was coherent throughout. But across the rebuttal and pressure exchanges, PRO was more reactive, while CON consistently narrowed the clash and forced PRO onto harder ground.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 Answer A1: the dilemma that if Congress retains the power to redirect aid the commitment is not "standing" (just the ad-hoc process PRO condemns) but if it is genuinely hard to revoke it functions as a political entitlement that raises the cost of conditionality to the point of being a dead letter. This was the most incisive exchange of the debate, and PRO never escaped it.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): B won by making the tradeoff in the motion more concrete and by pressing the contradiction in A's model of a standing but still flexible commitment. A had a plausible deterrence and industrial-base case, but relied too much on the assumption that Ukraine aid generically expands readiness. B more effectively argued that a standing Ukraine pipeline directs finite procurement toward a European land war and away from higher-priority Pacific capabilities, while also reducing leverage over Kyiv and future allied choices.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's decisive exchange was the standing-commitment dilemma: if aid can be paused or redirected, it is not meaningfully standing and loses A's deterrent uniqueness; if it is hard to revoke, it becomes an entitlement that weakens conditionality and strategic flexibility.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side A won by more convincingly establishing that standing commitment locks procurement and leverage into a secondary theater while creating a ratchet against settlement, outweighing B's deterrence and surge arguments across rebuttals and pressure exchanges.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal 1 and 2 on procurement mismatch for Pacific priorities and stripping conditionality for negotiated settlement.
