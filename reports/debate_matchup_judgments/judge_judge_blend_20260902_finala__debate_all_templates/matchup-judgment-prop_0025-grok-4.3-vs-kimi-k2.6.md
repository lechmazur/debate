### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Both sides were competent and the debate was genuinely competitive, but Side B (PRO) holds the narrower edge on the motion's central question. The decisive clash is whether CON's anti-retaliation alternative actually solves the coercion problem PRO identifies. CON's sharpest move—distinguishing a targeted anti-retaliation rule from a blanket ban—is creative and well-developed across the debate, and it genuinely pressures PRO. However, PRO's counter (B4.1) that banning only retaliation leaves the underlying social norm intact is both accurate and unmet: if the informal expectation of availability persists, workers still face career risk for non-response even without formal discipline. CON never squarely addresses this norm-persistence problem; it keeps asserting the alternative is better without explaining how it shifts the norm that generates the pressure in the first place. That gap undermines CON's central substitute proposal. On secondary clashes—surveillance and litigation—CON raises real concerns that PRO answers plausibly if not perfectly: employers already log performance data (so the right limits rather than invents monitoring), and the functional-necessity test mimics existing on-call and overtime frameworks rather than inventing novel adjudication. Neither answer is airtight, but PRO's responses are sufficient to prevent these from reversing the core verdict. PRO's Q1 was the debate's most pointed pressure—exposing the voluntariness paradox CON never fully resolved—and CON's Q1 (anti-retaliation vs. blanket ban) was the best individual argument in the round but ultimately unpersuasive because PRO's norm-persistence rebuttal stood unanswered. CON's closing crystallized the "cure outlaws the arrangements workers chose" frame memorably, but without neutralizing the norm-persistence gap, it did not change the underlying balance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B4.1 response to CON's anti-retaliation alternative: banning only retaliation leaves the social norm intact, so workers still feel compelled to offer availability to protect assignments and advancement. This directly exposed the gap in CON's substitute proposal—that informal pressure survives a formal anti-retaliation rule—and CON never produced a substantive answer to norm persistence across the remainder of the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A wins a clear but competitive debate because it kept the central clash on workplace power: without a legal default, after-hours availability becomes an implicit career requirement, especially for lower-leverage workers. Side B was sharper rhetorically and raised real concerns about flexibility, litigation, contractors, and surveillance, but too often treated the policy as a total curfew rather than a presumptive limit with explicit exceptions. A's answer that anti-retaliation alone cannot solve quiet coercion was the more decisive comparative point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response to the flexibility argument was most decisive: it argued that supposedly voluntary trades are not genuinely voluntary when refusal can quietly harm assignments or advancement, and that a default right is needed where narrower anti-retaliation rules leave the coercive norm intact.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B won by successfully highlighting the practical frictions of Side A's policy, specifically that the compliance burden would cause employers to cancel informal flexibility rather than formalize it, and that rigid cost spikes would drive offloading to contractors.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 effectively answered the pressure on contractor offloading by explaining that a statutory default imposes rigid cost spikes, uniquely manufacturing the outsourcing loophole.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won on the decisive clash. The power-asymmetry argument—that the option to ignore an after-hours message is a fiction without a legal right to refuse—was introduced cleanly in the opening and never neutralized by CON. PRO's "shield, not cage" reframe in Rebuttal 2 was the single most important argumentative move: it directly answered CON's core fear (employers defaulting to rigid no-contact rules) by noting that genuine after-hours need can be captured in formal compensated structures rather than ambient coercion. CON repeated this compliance-cost point multiple times without ever engaging PRO's reversal that clear legal boundaries reduce rather than increase administrative ambiguity. CON's answer to PRO's Q1 (how to distinguish voluntary from coerced messages) was the debate's weakest moment: "leave it to the parties" concedes the very problem without resolving it, because the parties are not equal. CON's best standalone contribution—that employers may impose blanket no-contact policies to avoid liability, removing even genuinely wanted flexibility—was addressed by PRO's point that formal on-call or scheduled arrangements capture legitimate need. CON recycled this point without engaging that rebuttal. PRO's pressure questions were sharper and more diagnostic than CON's, and PRO answered CON's pressure questions more directly. CON's closings added little beyond restatement, while PRO crystallized the core comparison cleanly. Throughout, PRO's rhetorical framing ("exploitation with better branding," "colonizing mental space," "a toll extracted by hierarchy") reinforced substantive arguments rather than substituting for them.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's "shield, not cage" response in Rebuttal 2 (B4.1): it directly answered CON's primary worry that the law eliminates beneficial voluntary exchanges by clarifying that the right protects refusal, not prohibits response, and that any genuinely time-sensitive need can be captured through formal compensated structures. CON never answered this reframe, leaving its central flexibility argument suspended on an unresolved premise.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): A won by making the central clash about whether after-hours responsiveness is genuinely voluntary under workplace hierarchy. B had a plausible flexibility and compliance-cost case, but too often treated the policy as a hard ban and did not convincingly answer A's point that refusal is meaningless without legal protection. A also used the sector-specific exceptions more effectively to contain B's urgent-client and emergency examples.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal was most decisive: it answered B's flexibility questions directly by framing the right as a shield rather than a cage, then argued that B was preserving permanent implicit on-call status for the majority to protect a minority of voluntary cases.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing the current norm of after-hours communication as inherently coercive due to power asymmetries. Side A decisively neutralized Side B's best argument—that the policy destroys personal flexibility—by pointing out that workers can still use deferred-send tools or shift their scheduled hours without needing live employer access.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal [A4.3] effectively crushed Side B's personal rhythm argument by introducing the simple, practical alternative of deferred-send tools and shifted hours.
