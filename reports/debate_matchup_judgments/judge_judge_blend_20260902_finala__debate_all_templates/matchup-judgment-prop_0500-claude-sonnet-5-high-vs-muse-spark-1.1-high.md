### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): B won by showing that A’s proposed alternatives were often category-limited: bus lanes, rehabilitation, and stormwater decentralization cannot reliably provide new utility and transport capacity where no tolerable surface corridor exists. B also supplied a plausible reason heavy, coordinated investment is superior to isolated projects through institutional capacity and standardization. A’s national-scope and opportunity-cost objections were strong, and A correctly exposed B’s unpriced lifecycle assertions, but A never fully reconciled essential urban capacity needs with its case-by-case approach. B’s exaggerations about urban concentration and predictable maintenance keep the result competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive exchange was the response that rehab-in-place and dig-once manage existing infrastructure but do not create needed capacity, while buses cannot substitute for utility corridors and elevated rail preserves permanent surface disruption. A’s later appeal to microtunneling partly conceded B’s underlying solution.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A won by effectively framing the problem around dense, growing urban centers where surface space is genuinely exhausted. When Side B pressured Side A on why this required "heavy national investment" rather than a case-by-case approach, Side A provided a decisive answer: heavy investment builds the institutional capacity and economies of scale needed to lower costs across many cities. Side B's points on geological variance were strong, but Side A won the lifecycle cost comparison by persuasively arguing that surface disruption is a compounding, recurring cost, whereas tunneling is a bounded, one-time disruption.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (A4.3) brilliantly neutralized Side B's core "scalpel vs. shovel" argument by explaining that heavy national investment creates the institutional capacity (TBM fleets, trained crews) to reduce the very cost premiums Side B was complaining about.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): B won by making the decisive comparison on scale and opportunity cost stick: the motion requires heavy national investment despite high costs, yet A's necessity case (rightly pressed and half-conceded for dense cores) never justified locking national budgets into asymmetric geological risk and inflexible debt when targeted bores plus surface alternatives serve more people per dollar. A landed cleaner early punches on undercounted surface disruption and weak alts for utilities, but B's later speeches kept narrowing the choice back to "scalpel not shovel factory" while forcing A into semantic defense of "heavy." A's clipped Rebuttal 1 and unpriced lifecycle claims left residual vulnerability that B exploited.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1-B2.3 and B4.1: turning A's dense-core necessity into a fatal mismatch with national heavy investment and asymmetric TBM risk.
