- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won on the two most important clashes. First, the administrative line-drawing dispute: A's Q2 directly exposed the contradiction that platforms already categorize custom shoutouts, personalized sessions, and high-margin upsells separately from standard subscriptions for their own revenue tracking and ad targeting—which means the line B called impossible is already drawn daily for platform profit. B's A2 response tried to pivot to "functional type vs. emotional intent," but this distinction was unpersuasive because A's point was about offer classification, not message surveillance. B never cleanly answered this. Second, the "existing controls are sufficient" argument: A's vivid and mechanistically plausible example of a time-sensitive $15 shoutout offer pushed right after a creator shares a mental health struggle demonstrated why generic budget alerts cannot intercept parasocial-exploiting push notifications; B's counter that budget alerts "establish objective financial boundaries" sidestepped A's specific mechanism entirely. B's strongest live point was the toggle failure (Q1)—if a compulsive user can simply re-enable during an emotional high, what does the default accomplish?—and A's answer was only partially satisfying, noting that removing the passive default stream of prompts is itself the key intervention rather than foolproof prevention. That partial answer kept some pressure on A, but it was enough to deflect the charge of total ineffectiveness. B's paternalism and over-censorship arguments were speculative throughout and never recovered from A's "reversal" reframe: B's position actually strips user agency by forcing accept-or-leave with no middle option. A held the opt-in ground consistently, and B's creator-harm fears rested on censorship fears that A credibly neutralized by pointing to pre-existing platform classification infrastructure. Overall, A made the debate easier to decide by sharpening the live clashes across rounds rather than widening them.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was A's Q2 in the Pressure Questions round, pressing why the line between personalized upsells and standard offerings is suddenly impossible when platforms already draw it for revenue tracking. B's A2 response attempted a pivot to "invasive policing of private messages," but this was non-responsive to A's classification argument. That exchange effectively neutralized B's central administrative-burden claim and never recovered in the remaining rounds.

Throughout, PRO crystallized the clash as user agency versus platform profit, a frame that sticks because it tracks the actual text of the motion.
