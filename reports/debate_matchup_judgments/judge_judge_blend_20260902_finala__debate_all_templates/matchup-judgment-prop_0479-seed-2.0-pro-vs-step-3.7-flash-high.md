### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO held together a coherent and structurally sound argument across all five rounds. The core case—that expert panels bake in incentive rot that systematically biases forecasts toward safe consensus, while markets reward accuracy regardless of political convenience—was never squarely refuted. PRO's manipulation comparison held up reasonably: lobbying corrupts panels invisibly and daily, while market manipulation at least leaves a detectable trace, and PRO correctly answered that policymakers retain legal and political accountability for final decisions regardless of whether a market was the input. CON's strongest move was the 72-hour freeze catch-22 in Rebuttal 2 (freeze early and you lock in a distorted price; freeze late and you miss the on-the-ground signals you promised to capture), which was a genuine tension PRO did not fully resolve. However, CON's case was undermined throughout by reliance on a suspiciously specific statistic—"70% of trading volume from users with no identifiable domain expertise" in "subsidy-augmented COVID-19 policy markets"—which reads as fabricated precision in a closed-book debate and should be discounted under the calibration rules. PRO's Q2 in the Pressure Questions round cleverly exposed the logical gap: even if 70% of volume was non-expert, did those markets actually underperform? CON's response in Rebuttal 2 was effectively a concession ("even if those markets did not underperform"), which handed PRO the core empirical ground. CON's accountability argument was partially answered—panels rarely produce real consequences for failure either, and policymakers remain accountable for the final call. PRO's closing crystallization ("accountability after disaster vs. fewer disasters") was the most memorable framing in the debate and CON's closing failed to dislodge it, retreating to the contested statistic rather than engaging the weighing frame. CON's failure to follow the A1:/A2: labeling format at the opening of Rebuttal 2 is a minor execution cost but not decisive on its own.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 in the Pressure Questions round was the pivotal moment: asking whether the COVID-policy markets actually underperformed the official forecasts despite the high non-expert volume. CON's answer in Rebuttal 2—"even if those markets did not underperform, the 70% volume proves the mechanism fails"—implicitly surrendered the central empirical claim that markets fail to produce better forecasts than expert panels, leaving CON's case resting almost entirely on accountability and manipulation concerns, both of which PRO had already partially answered.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's mechanisms. When Side B introduced a 72-hour market freeze to prevent manipulation, Side A cleanly pointed out this would block the very late-breaking, on-the-ground information Side B built its case around. Side B's closing relied on the claim that Side A never disputed that markets fail less often, which Side A had explicitly contested in the prior round.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive, effectively weaponizing Side B's 72-hour freeze safeguard by showing it cannibalizes Side B's core claim of capturing late-breaking dispersed wisdom.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): B won because its pressure questions exposed unfixable tradeoffs in A's safeguards, and B's later rebuttals showed those safeguards directly undermine the late-info advantage while leaving accountability gaps intact. A's case never neutralized the manipulation-plus-denial risk on high-stakes irreversible decisions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal 2 showing the 72-hour freeze locks out the very signals A needs while still allowing earlier distortion.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): CON won on two live arguments that PRO never fully neutralized. First, the "you can't have it both ways" dilemma in B2.1: if participants are vetted and non-anonymous, they face the same career and reputation incentives that corrupt closed panels; if anonymous, the sabotage risk returns. PRO's A4.1 response—that market rewards are tied to accuracy, not conformity—was answered effectively in B4.1 with a realistic observation that a small subsidy payout will never outweigh the dominant career incentives facing institutional insiders. PRO never closed this gap. Second, CON's "governments change futures, not forecast them" argument (B1.1–B1.5) identified a structural mismatch that PRO never squarely answered. PRO's only rebuttal was that market signals are "actionable intelligence" for drafters, but CON's response—that no minister will back a policy the formal official market says will fail, and that expert panels can publicly argue for a different future while markets can only record current expectations—remained live through the closing. PRO's concrete examples (2008, COVID) were real and rhetorically effective, and the "negligible marginal cost, massive upside" argument was never directly refuted, keeping PRO competitive. But PRO also never offered an adequate solution to the anonymity dilemma or a convincing answer to what expert panels can do that markets structurally cannot: publicly champion a bold policy against prevailing expectations. CON's closing crystallized this contrast memorably and without introducing new material, while PRO's closing was mostly recap. On the decisive clashes—incentive structure under vetted insiders and the forecasting-vs-leading distinction—CON came out ahead.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1 "you cannot have it both ways" rebuttal on anonymity was the most structurally damaging move in the debate. It showed that PRO's proposed fix (vetted, non-anonymous participants) directly undermines PRO's core advantage claim (breaking institutional groupthink), because named insiders carry the same career incentives that corrupt closed panels. PRO attempted an answer in A4.1 but CON's B4.1 response—that tiny market rewards cannot outweigh dominant institutional incentives like job security and ministerial relationships—was more realistic and was never effectively countered.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A systematically dismantled Side B's case by trapping them in a dilemma regarding participant anonymity. When Side B opted for vetted, non-anonymous insiders to avoid sabotage, Side A successfully argued this would simply recreate the career and reputational pressures that plague expert panels. Furthermore, Side A established a dominant framing that governments exist to alter the future, leaving Side B's markets as a tool that can only passively reflect the status quo.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 decisively neutralizing B's mechanism by pointing out that a mid-level official will never risk their career by betting against their minister's flagship policy just for a small market payout.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side B wins by establishing the decisive distinction between forecasting what exists versus enabling governments to change outcomes, which Side A never squarely neutralizes despite accurate rebuttals on incentives and past panel failures; B's pressure on leading vs following crystallizes the core mismatch most persuasively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.5-B2.6 and B4.3-B4.6 force the leading/following clash that A never refutes, making B's frame decisive by closing.
