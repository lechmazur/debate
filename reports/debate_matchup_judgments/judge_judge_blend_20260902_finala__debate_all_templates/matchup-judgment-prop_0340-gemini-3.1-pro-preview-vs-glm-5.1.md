### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON won by building three durable arguments that PRO never fully neutralized. First, the liability-shield argument—that "you chose this" becomes the platform's universal deflection—was introduced early and maintained consistently through the closing; PRO's formal-policy response ignored how regulatory pressure actually functions in practice. Second, PRO conceded in B2.5 that most users won't change settings, and CON hammered this throughout: if the default feed remains dominant, the reform's benefits don't materialize while the accountability risk intensifies. Third, CON's original observation in A2.4 about "overlapping" echo chambers—current algorithmic feeds fragment attention but still produce shared trending topics that make collective political action possible—was never adequately answered; PRO's rebuttal relied on the town square already being fragmented, which doesn't address whether further atomization would destroy what remnants of shared awareness remain. CON's pressure question about the corporate-designed menu (A3.1) was sharper than PRO's questions and exposed a real tension: if platforms control the menu of goals and the defaults, user "choice" is still largely corporate choice with better branding. PRO's closing made the "drowning together" rhetorical point effectively but mostly recapped earlier arguments without sharpening the clash. CON's closing crystallized the debate with precision—"same casino, better liability framework"—and identified the key question (does user control break or rebrand the attention economy?) in a way that framed the resolution cleanly. PRO's "brakes still exist" assurance and "Civic Discourse promise" accountability mechanism were genuinely interesting but remained underdeveloped relative to CON's sustained, progressive case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.4 observation that current algorithmic feeds create "overlapping" echo chambers—where millions still share trending topics and breaking news, enabling collective political action—was the most decisive rebuttal exchange. It turned PRO's own town-square-as-isolation-booths argument against them by distinguishing fragmented-but-overlapping from fully atomized discourse, and PRO never squarely answered it. Paired with CON's exploitation of PRO's concession on default behavior, this combination explained why user control could actively worsen the public sphere even if it technically offered more choice.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON wins because they established a clearer comparative edge on collective accountability: user choice atomizes harm visibility, evaporates political will, and delivers the same default casino with a liability rebrand, while PRO's autonomy case could not overcome conceded defaults and paternalism rebuttals. Clash progressed sharply on visibility and enforcement mechanisms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2's rebuttal 2 on fragmented harm losing democratic pressure (A4.1), which directly neutralized B's visibility and accountability claims after pressure questions.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON wins by showing that user control fragments harm into invisible private suffering, destroying the political will needed for regulation, while PRO conceded that most users never leave the engagement-optimized default. The proposal thus functions as a rebranded liability shield—same casino, better PR—rather than structural reform. PRO's autonomy argument never overcame the default-dominance problem or the accountability paradox.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (A4) demonstrating that atomizing feeds across millions of users makes harm politically invisible and regulation practically impossible, while the default feed still dominates—turning PRO's escape hatch into a rebranded liability shield.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): PRO won on three live routes that CON never fully neutralized. First, the advertiser infrastructure argument [B1.7]—that goal-selection tools already exist for paying customers—was never squarely answered; CON's reply [A2.5] conceded the conflict of interest rather than denying the disparity, handing PRO a clean victory on that sub-clash. Second, CON's own economic concession ("if users optimizing for less screen time would collapse the business model") was a serious own-goal that PRO's Q2 and Rebuttal 2 [B4.5] weaponized effectively: CON conceding that user and platform interests diverge is precisely the argument for user control, not against it. CON's recovery [A4.2–A4.3] was defensive and didn't close the wound. Third, on echo chambers—the most contested clash—PRO's framing of "involuntary vs. optional" [B4.3–B4.4] was more persuasive than CON's claim that engagement algorithms cross-pollinate, since CON's own Rebuttal 2 [A4.1] admitted the cross-pollination is outrage-bait designed to provoke reaction, which undercut rather than supported CON's position. CON's best arguments—stated vs. revealed preferences and the architectural difference between social platforms and curated libraries—were real and partially landed, but PRO's contextual-preference reframe and the "engagement as tiebreaker, not sole compass" answer [B4.1–B4.2] kept those points from being decisive. PRO's clipped closing is a minor execution cost but the key arguments were already on the table from Rebuttal 2 onward. CON's rhetoric was competent but the structural weaknesses in the economic and echo-chamber lines prevented a win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 in the pressure round was the debate's pivotal exchange. By pointing out that CON's own economic argument—if users could optimize for less screen time the business model would collapse—proves exactly why users cannot trust the platform to choose their optimization goal for them, PRO converted CON's strongest-seeming structural point into an indictment of the status quo. CON's response [A4.2–A4.3] never escaped this framing, retreating to "viability" rather than addressing the trust betrayal that the concession revealed.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B built a stronger case by more convincingly answering the pressure questions with feasible mechanisms like content tagging and reframing the economic concession as evidence for user control; they also maintained that the status quo manufactures echo chambers while adding optional escapes. Side A pressed hard on revealed preferences and cognitive burden but did not neutralize the interest misalignment or provide grounded support for beneficial cross-pollination.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 directly answering how goals coexist with existing signals and making echo chambers optional rather than involuntary.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B wins because it established a clearer path to victory on the core clash of platform-user conflict of interest. CON's revealed-preference and echo-chamber objections were materially damaged by PRO's arguments that engagement algorithms already flatten context and manufacture ideological rabbit holes, and that giving users goal-setting tools at least makes escape possible. CON never squarely answered PRO's pressure question showing that platforms profit from overriding user welfare, retreating instead to viability defenses that conceded the tension. PRO's sharper weighing and more direct responses to live pressure gave it the edge, though its clipped closing incurred a real execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pressure question Q2 exposing that user goals and platform revenue conflict, and its subsequent reframing that this conflict of interest is precisely why users need control—a turn CON never recovered from.
