### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that blanket unenforceability can also destroy survivor-controlled privacy, compensation leverage, and reciprocal confidentiality. Its victim-option rule plus reporting carve-outs offered a plausible less harmful alternative. Side B established serious third-party harms from secrecy, but never adequately answered why its rule was preferable to making silence voidable by the victim, nor how existing law protects against truthful but devastating employer disclosures. A overstated how fully regulator access warns future workers, and its clipped rebuttals were an execution cost, so the win remains competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s answer to B4.1 was decisive: defamation only covers falsehoods, so an employer could truthfully identify the complainant and publicize damaging details or an unsubstantiated finding. This exposed a concrete gap in B’s claim that existing privacy protections adequately replace enforceable confidentiality.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): CON won the core trade-off. PRO proved third-party harm from cover-ups, but CON showed blanket unenforceability is overbroad: it voids the survivor's own privacy shield, cuts settlement premiums, and only permits rather than compels warning. CON's narrower alternative – voidable at victim's option plus voiding any bar on police/regulator reports – meets PRO's pattern-detection goal with less cost. PRO's fallback of defamation fails because truthful, devastating disclosure is lawful, and the coercion argument collapses when PRO concedes releases stand.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4 mutual-unenforceability + defamation-requires-falsity – employer can truthfully publish name, allegation, and “unsubstantiated” file, which is the exact harm confidentiality prevented.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by decisively exposing the fatal flaw in Side B's blanket unenforceability model: it strips victims of their own privacy shield and bargaining power. Side A effectively demonstrates that without a mutually enforceable NDA, employers could lawfully leak true but devastating details about the victim, rendering Side B's reliance on defamation law inadequate. Furthermore, Side A's proposed alternative (voidable at the victim's option plus a regulator carve-out) successfully neutralizes Side B's primary "spillover" harm argument while preserving victim agency. Side B's distinction between coerced NDAs and coerced settlement releases was logically vulnerable, and Side A capitalized on it cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's claim that existing laws (like defamation) protect the victim's privacy if NDAs are unenforceable. Side A sharply points out that defamation only covers false statements, meaning an employer could lawfully and maliciously leak the victim's true identity and an "unsubstantiated" investigation file, abolishing the very shield the victim bargained for.
