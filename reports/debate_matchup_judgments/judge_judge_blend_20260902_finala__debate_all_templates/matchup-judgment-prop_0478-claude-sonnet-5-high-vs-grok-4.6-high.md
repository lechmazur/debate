### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by supplying a plausible public-account mechanism and showing that each payer can remit based only on its own payments while a public administrator aggregates eligibility. That substantially answered B's timing, surveillance, and small-platform burdens. B's final hour-rationing claim depended on platforms facing an unknown threshold-triggered liability, but A's prorated remittance model makes the cost predictable and does not require rivals to exchange data. B also offered status-neutral coverage without establishing its arrival or mechanism, leaving A's comparison against the existing benefits gap intact. B's clipped opening was an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both questions in the required format, proposed payment-cycle remittances and a trailing lookback administered publicly, and exposed that B was conflating government aggregation with cross-platform data sharing. B's probabilistic hour-capping response did not adequately explain why firms would cap work when contributions are proportional to payments rather than a surprise liability at the threshold.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by presenting a highly plausible mechanism (a UI-style public pool with continuous, prorated remittances) that systematically dismantled Side B's entire offensive strategy. Side B insisted that platforms would hit a 'compliance moat' or 'probabilistically hedge' by rationing hours to avoid crossing a threshold. However, Side A cleanly demonstrated that if platforms simply remit a continuous percentage of payouts to a public administrator, there is no cliff-liability for the firm to dodge, neutralizing the rationing argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's final point in Closing that "Unemployment systems already sum multi-payer earnings. Firms do not idle profitable work because an administrator may later add it up" decisively crushed Side B's lingering 'probabilistic hedging' argument.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully grounding its portable benefits mechanism in existing social insurance and tax frameworks (e.g., unemployment insurance, 1099s), which effectively neutralized Side B's attacks regarding surveillance moats and real-time data sharing. Side B's primary offensive lines—hour rationing and compliance moats—lost their force once Side A clarified that platforms would simply remit to a public administrator rather than share data with competitors. While Side B asked sharp pressure questions, its reliance on demanding exact drafting details (like specific day counts or exact dollar thresholds) felt like a weak veto against a structurally sound policy comparison. Side A also won the meta-comparison by forcing Side B to defend the status quo "desert" while waiting for a hypothetical decoupled system, making A's path to victory much cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it directly answered the pressure questions by comparing the aggregation mechanism to unemployment insurance and standard tax remittance, effectively dismantling Side B's "surveillance moat" and "real-time tracking" contradictions without needing to invent brittle fake precision.
