### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): PRO held the structural high ground across the full exchange. The three-way comparison (full prohibition / Nordic model / full legalization) gave PRO a clear and consistent framework that CON never dismantled. CON's core argument—that criminalized buyers demand secrecy and thus endanger workers—has genuine force, but PRO repeatedly neutralized it by distinguishing the seller's legal status as the decisive variable: a seller who cannot be arrested has unilateral access to police, healthcare, and exit services that no regime of full prohibition or full legalization can match in the same way. CON never squarely answered PRO's Q1 pressure question, which exposed the deepest flaw in CON's case: if buyer criminalization destroys screening, full prohibition (which also criminalizes the seller) must be worse by CON's own logic, yet CON's preferred alternative is full decriminalization, not full prohibition. CON answered only that "buyer paranoia still drives the encounter," which does not engage with the comparative advantage PRO claims over full prohibition. On the infrastructure point, PRO's answer in Rebuttal 2—distinguishing cooperative shared tenancy from profit-extracting pimping—was specific and legally plausible, while CON's counter (police investigating illegal purchases will surveil workspaces anyway) was real but never developed into a fatal blow. CON's trafficking and demand-expansion critique of full decriminalization was never answered with comparable force. PRO's clipped Rebuttal 1 is a real execution cost, but the remainder of PRO's performance—including clean A1/A2 answers, the memorable "she alone can enter a police station without cuffs" framing, and effective closing crystallization—maintained a clear edge. CON repeated the screening and isolation arguments across rounds without meaningfully evolving them, while PRO progressively narrowed the debate to one clean point: the seller's legal status is what reaches the woman in the room.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure question was the debate's decisive moment: if criminalizing buyers destroys screening and safety, doesn't full prohibition—which also criminalizes the seller—make screening even more impossible? CON's Rebuttal 2 answer that "buyer paranoia still drives the encounter" failed to engage with the comparative claim, leaving PRO's structural advantage over both full prohibition and full legalization intact through the closing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): CON built a decisive analytical advantage through three live clashes that PRO never fully answered. First, the "scarcity market vs. seller's market" distinction in CON's first rebuttal was the most important analytical contribution in the debate: when demand shrinks because buyers face arrest, workers competing for fewer clients cannot afford to turn anyone away, which inverts PRO's leverage narrative. PRO's response—the decriminalized seller can simply walk away—consistently ignored economic necessity, and CON drove that gap home in every subsequent round. Second, CON's pressure question exposing that buyer stings structurally require observing sellers first was genuinely damaging; PRO's answer (digital decoys and financial tracking) was only partially responsive and didn't address third-party prohibitions on brothel-keeping, which CON correctly identified as the legal architecture that strands sellers in isolation. PRO's repeated dismissal of these features as "implementation failures" was the weakest move in the debate, because CON showed enforcement cannot target buyers without surveilling sellers, making the gap structural rather than incidental. Third, CON's agriculture analogy effectively reframed the trafficking claim: we do not ban legal labor sectors because traffickers exploit them; we inspect workplaces and empower workers to report. PRO never answered that comparison. PRO's strongest contribution—that full legalization inflates demand and fuels trafficking pipelines—is a legitimate argument grounded in plausible market logic, but CON's enforcement-visibility counter was equally plausible and held its ground throughout. The closing "prohibition with better branding" framing crystallized the debate cleanly rather than widening it. CON won the central analytical clashes, landed cleaner rebuttals, and gave the sharper reason to vote against the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's first rebuttal introducing the "scarcity market" framing [A2.1–A2.2]: the argument that criminalizing buyers does not produce a seller's market but a desperation market—where fewer remaining clients hold all the cards—was the pivot of the debate. It directly inverted PRO's core leverage claim, PRO never produced a satisfying economic answer to it, and CON reinforced it successfully in every subsequent round including the closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A won because it more convincingly explained why criminalizing buyers still pushes the whole transaction underground, weakens screening, forces police surveillance of sellers, and leaves workers isolated by surrounding third-party prohibitions. Side B had a real macro argument that legalization could expand demand and trafficking, but it relied on repeated, overconfident assertions and never adequately answered the buyer-evidence-trail and seller-leverage problems.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange was its answer to Side B's leverage claim: buyer fear of arrest makes clients avoid records, screening, and safe locations, while poorer sellers with fewer clients cannot simply walk away. That directly damaged the core safety mechanism of the proposition.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B decisively won the core clash over market dynamics and worker safety. While Side A argued that sellers would gain leverage to demand screening, Side B effectively countered that buyers fearing arrest would refuse to leave a paper trail, and sellers in a shrunken "desperation market" could not afford to walk away. This thoroughly dismantled Side A's causal link for how the compromise actually protects workers on the ground.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's "leverage" argument by pointing out that an illicit buyer will never agree to leave a paper trail, and a seller in a shrunken market cannot afford to walk away.
