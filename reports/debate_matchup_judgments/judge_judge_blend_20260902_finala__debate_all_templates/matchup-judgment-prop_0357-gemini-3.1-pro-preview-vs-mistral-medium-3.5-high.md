### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built the stronger cumulative case. Its core distinction—premium connectivity services versus basic offline mechanical repair—held up across all five rounds and was never genuinely neutralized. The property-rights framing was intuitive and sustained. Side A's cybersecurity rebuttal correctly identified that local, offline diagnostic access and remote cellular exploits are different attack surfaces; forcing CON to explain why franchised-dealer access is safe while independent-mechanic access is catastrophic. CON never answered this cleanly. The answer that "dealerships use cryptographically signed tools and audit logging" actually points toward a regulatory solution, not a blanket prohibition—which Side A could have pressed harder but did not need to, because CON's own logic undercut itself. Side B's security argument depended on the 2015 Jeep Cherokee hack, but that attack was a remote cellular exploit, not an OBD-port intrusion, making the analogy weaker than CON presented it. Side B never corrected this and repeated the example through the closing, accumulating the error. Side A's Q1 pressure question was the most decisive exchange in the debate: it exposed a real double standard that CON never squarely resolved. Side A's Rebuttal 2 properly answered with A1/A2 labels, defined "core functionality" concretely (fault codes, clearing, hardware initialization), and demonstrated that authorizing a replacement part serial number discloses nothing proprietary. CON's economic argument (subscriptions fund safety infrastructure) was speculative and somewhat circular—if safety genuinely requires permanent monthly rent, that is precisely the extortion Side A warned against, and CON's framing of it as a benefit did not land. The closing on both sides was crystallizing rather than introducing new angles, but Side A's "renting the right to operate" framing was more memorable and did real argumentative work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Q1 pressure question—"Why is offline diagnostic access an existential threat when an independent mechanic uses it but perfectly safe when a franchised dealership does the same thing?"—was the debate's decisive exchange. CON's answer (dealerships use cryptographic tools and audit logging) conceded that the risk is controllable with protocols, not inherently tied to who holds the tool. This turned CON's own security apparatus into an argument for regulated independent access rather than a blanket mandate against it, and CON never recovered from the contradiction.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): B won by establishing the core clash around property rights versus monopoly control, directly answering pressure questions on dealership access and funding models, and consistently framing security risks as addressable through local offline standards without conceding on ownership degradation. A's security emphasis with the Jeep example was persistent but failed to neutralize the double-standard rebuttal or the independent repair monopoly argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 directly resolving the dealership vs independent access question and reframing subscription revenue as extortionary.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by narrowly defining the mandate as basic offline repair access while dismantling Side A's security and IP objections. Side A never recovered from the asymmetry in Side B's pressure questions: dealership diagnostic access is safe, so independent access to the same local port is not inherently catastrophic. Side A repeatedly mischaracterized Side B's position as requiring ECU re-flashing and algorithm disclosure, and its closing rehashed the Jeep hack against a strawman rather than sharpening the clash. Side B's framing of ownership versus perpetual software tenancy provided the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it distinguished hardware-serial acceptance from proprietary algorithm disclosure, exposed that Side A's security argument conflated basic local repair access with unsecured remote exploitation, and turned Side A's economic defense into evidence of extortionary manufacturer behavior.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO built a coherent and progressively tighter case across the debate. The strategic pivot in Rebuttal 2—narrowing "core repair functionality" to local, offline systems like OBD-II diagnostics that require no cloud infrastructure—was the most consequential move in the debate. It largely neutralized CON's strongest economic arguments (someone must pay for network infrastructure) because local diagnostics genuinely do not require ongoing server costs or cellular connectivity. PRO answered the pressure questions directly with A1/A2 structure and used those answers to sharpen the core clash rather than widen it. The property rights framing remained live throughout: once hardware is physically installed in a purchased vehicle, disabling its standalone operation via subscription lapse is a denial of ownership, not a service interruption. CON landed real damage in two places. The "moving the goalposts" charge had merit—PRO's early examples (heated seats, autopilot) looked more like premium features, and CON correctly noted those are different from base repairability. CON's claim that federal law already mandates OBD-II access is potentially devastating, suggesting PRO is protecting something that already exists. However, CON raised this late and did not fully exploit it; rather than explaining what genuine gap remains after existing law, CON shifted to cloud diagnostics and OTA patches, which reads as expanding the definition of "repair" past what the motion covers. PRO's safety argument (AEB lockout) was dismissed by CON as a strawman citing federal mandates—a fair counter—but CON never addressed the forward-looking principle: existing protections may erode as automakers test new subscription boundaries, which is exactly what the mandate anticipates. CON's economic arguments were consistent but never landed definitively against a motion explicitly scoped to core repair rather than premium connectivity. PRO's property rights principle remained the cleaner reason to vote, and the motion as written aligns with PRO's narrowed framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 pivot—clarifying that "core repair functionality" means local, offline OBD diagnostics requiring no cloud infrastructure—was the most decisive single move. It directly answered CON's pressure questions about who pays for network costs, stripped away CON's strongest economic objection, and reanchored the debate to the proposition's actual language. CON's counter that OBD-II is already federally protected was sharp, but CON then diluted it by pivoting to OTA patches and cloud diagnostics rather than pressing the "you're fighting a phantom threat" line cleanly to conclusion.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by maintaining focus on the proposition's economic tradeoffs and connectivity costs while exposing Side B's pivot from heated seats/safety examples to local OBD repair (already legally protected). A directly answered pressure questions, weighed manufacturing scale and subscription realities more convincingly, and crystallized why banning locks raises upfront prices without protecting new rights. B's ownership framing was strong initially but diluted by narrowing to offline systems and failing to neutralize cost arguments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 directly answering Q1/Q2 on costs and connectivity while charging B with goalpost move on "core repair."
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by exposing a fatal dilemma in Side B's (PRO) case. PRO opened with non-repair examples like heated seats and autopilot, then retreated to local OBD ports under economic pressure. CON showed that offline OBD access is already federally protected, making PRO's late case either a phantom threat or, if applied to connected repair services, an unfunded mandate that would force lifetime infrastructure costs into every vehicle's upfront price. CON's consistent economic framing, direct answers to pressure questions, and sharper crystallization in the closing resolved the decisive clash in their favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's exposure of PRO's retreat from heated seats to OBD ports, demonstrating that the threat to offline core repair is already legally protected while mandating permanent connected repair functionality would destroy flexible pricing and affordable entry-level vehicles.
