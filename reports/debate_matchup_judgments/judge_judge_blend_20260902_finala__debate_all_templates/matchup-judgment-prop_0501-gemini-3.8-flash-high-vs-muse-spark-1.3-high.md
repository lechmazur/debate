### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built a cleaner, more durable case by drawing a sharp distinction between leverage-based macroprudential tools (which it already endorses) and price-level targeting (which the motion requires). PRO (B) tried to collapse this distinction by calling A's concession on macroprudential tools a "give-away," but never explained why an explicit price target adds anything beyond what financial-stability supervision already does. This became decisive in Rebuttal 2: when PRO answered the pressure questions by defining the target as a vague "macro-stability corridor" and insisting "neither target wins" in conflict, CON turned those answers back on PRO, showing the corridor was unfalsifiable rebranding of existing tools and that PRO still had no tie-breaking rule for when housing and CPI diverge. PRO's closing reasserted the institutional-mandate narrative but never repaired this exposed gap, while CON's closing crisply crystallized why the "second master" for one interest rate is unworkable and politically dangerous. CON's grounding was also cleaner: it avoided brittle fake precision, while PRO's "corridor" language ran closer to invented operational specificity without content.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3-A4.5), where it shows PRO's own pressure-question answers reduce to CON's existing framework plus an undefined "corridor," decisively undercutting PRO's central claim that an explicit target adds real institutional force.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by distinguishing financial resilience from targeting asset prices. It showed that leverage can be restrained through mortgage standards and capital buffers without assigning a national rate a conflicting, politically vulnerable housing-price objective. Side A explained why housing risk matters but never established what the added target accomplishes beyond supervision already conceded by both sides.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B decisively turned Side A’s answer on conflicting targets: A said rates should still address consumer inflation while macroprudential tools protect solvency, allowing B to argue that this was already the CON framework and that the proposed housing corridor added no instrument, only ambiguity and political pressure.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively exposing the redundancy and internal contradictions in Side A's mechanism. When pressed on trade-offs, Side A conceded that interest rates would still prioritize consumer-price inflation while macroprudential tools handled solvency. Side B correctly identified this as its own preferred framework (financial stability and leverage targeting), demonstrating that an explicit asset-price target adds no new instruments, only conflicting mandates and political pressure to prop up falling markets. Side B's distinction between targeting leverage versus targeting asset prices was sharp, well-defended, and ultimately fatal to the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantles Side A's answers to the pressure questions, pointing out that Side A's admission that interest rates would still prioritize CPI reduces the "asset-price target" to a mere slogan that replicates Side B's system of leverage supervision.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases, but CON's structural objections proved harder for PRO to escape. CON's Tinbergen-rule framing and the "supply-choke" mechanism (rate hikes freeze builder financing and lock existing inventory, worsening the very shortage driving prices) went largely unrefuted in substance—PRO's rebuttal that boom-bust speculation is worse never engaged the direct financing-cost mechanism. More decisively, the pressure-question exchange exposed a fatal dilemma in PRO's "modest lean": when CON asked what happens if the lean fails, PRO's answer (A2 in Rebuttal 2) conceded the bank would "hold firm" and defer to regulators rather than hike further—effectively admitting the explicit mandate does no more than current implicit practice when it matters most. CON exploited this concession sharply, framing PRO's central mechanism as either toothless or recession-inducing, and PRO's closing never resolved that bind, instead re-asserting "tilt not peg" without showing why an explicit mandate adds anything beyond what CON's macroprudential-plus-normalization approach already achieves. PRO's rebuttal that macroprudential tools were "evaded" pre-2008 was also met with a plausible, ungrounded-but-reasonable counter that those frameworks simply didn't exist yet, further blunting PRO's core justification for needing monetary policy as a backstop. CON's closing crystallized this dilemma cleanly, while PRO's closing largely restated earlier claims without neutralizing the exposed concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3) turning PRO's own Pressure-Question answer (B4.2, "holds firm, works with regulators") into an admission that the explicit mandate is functionally toothless whenever speculation actually resists a modest lean—this landed the decisive blow that PRO's closing could not undo.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that housing prices can reflect supply constraints and that economy-wide rate hikes may reduce construction and employment while failing to address those constraints. Side A persuasively established that monetary policy fuels housing debt cycles, but its modest-lean model never clearly showed enough efficacy to justify an explicit stability target over targeted macroprudential regulation. B's repeated nonexistent P-reference labels were an execution flaw, but not enough to overturn its stronger instrument-fit comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal crystallized the decisive dilemma: if A's monetary response remains modest, the explicit target risks being toothless; if rates rise enough to force housing stability, the policy imposes broad recessionary and construction costs. A rejected the binary but did not fully demonstrate how its limited intervention would materially stabilize housing.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully reframing the "blunt tool" critique. While Side B argued that interest rates are too blunt to target housing without crushing the broader economy, Side A convincingly demonstrated that ignoring housing bubbles leads to devastating crashes that cause far more economic damage. Side A's mechanism of "tilting the path" and halting asset purchases early provided a plausible, moderate alternative to Side B's false dichotomy of a "toothless nudge or recession." Side A also delivered sharper, more memorable rhetoric that crystallized the stakes effectively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to the "sledgehammer" critique, arguing that the true sledgehammer is the blindness that allows bubbles to inflate, forcing a brutal, economy-crashing correction later, effectively turned Side B's primary offense into a liability.
