### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B built and sustained the stronger path to victory. Its core claim—that labor migration is driven by employer demand that enforcement cannot extinguish, only displace into ungoverned channels—was never squarely answered. Side B also converted Side A's own admission ("too low and processing is slow") into proof that the mechanism works when scaled, not that the mechanism fails. The pivotal clash over Australia is decisive: Side A repeatedly cited maritime interdiction of asylum seekers as proof enforcement works for seasonal labor migration, a category mismatch that Side B identified in Rebuttal 1 and maintained through the closing. Side A's defense—that Australia proves enforcement deters across all migrant types—did not repair the logical gap between intercepting refugee boats and preventing economic overstaying in the farming sector. Side A's asylum-seeker argument was its strongest point and it had real force, but Side B handled it adequately by noting (1) enforcement also fails asylum seekers—it makes crossings deadlier without reducing flows—and (2) the motion asks which policy reduces irregular migration *more effectively*, making the dominant economic-migrant stream the decisive metric. Side A's pull-effect claim remained live but was undercut by Side B's calibration response: matching legal supply to actual employer demand eliminates the queue rather than widening it. Side B's framing—"enforcement displaces, pathways absorb"—was cleaner and more durable across all rounds. Side A's closing was notably brief and largely repetitive, leaving the main comparative burdens unsharpened.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 [B2.3–B2.4] identified that Side A's Australia example involved asylum seekers—a population Side A itself conceded would never qualify for seasonal work visas—making it irrelevant as evidence against the labor-visa mechanism. This move neutralized Side A's single most concrete empirical anchor and forced Side A to spend the rest of the debate defending an analogy that didn't fit the policy being debated.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): A won by making the cleaner causal comparison: irregular labor migration is driven by recurring employer demand, and expanded legal channels convert that flow into governed movement, while enforcement mainly displaces it. B had a real scope argument about asylum seekers and family reunification, but leaned too heavily on the Australia example, which A persuasively narrowed as a different population and pathway. B's pull factor and overstay claims were plausible but underdeveloped and sometimes overconfident, while A better used B's concession that current legal pathways are too narrow and slow.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's first rebuttal was decisive: it turned B's claim that visas fail because they are too limited into an argument for expansion, and it sharply challenged the relevance of Australia to seasonal labor migration.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing labor migration as an economic phenomenon driven by inelastic demand. Side B brilliantly weaponized Side A's own opening argument (that current visas fail because caps are "too low") to prove that the proposition—expanding those pathways—is the exact structural fix needed. While Side A landed a strong empirical point regarding Australia's maritime deterrence, Side B effectively marginalized it by noting it targeted asylum seekers, whereas Side B's solution addresses the undisputed majority of the flow: economic migrants.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Turn 3 [B2.1] where Side B uses Side A's H-2A "caps are too low" critique as proof that expanding pathways is the correct solution.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) built the stronger comparative case across the full exchange. Three moves were decisive. First, the structural mismatch argument—that sectoral visas reach only a narrow slice of actual irregular flows, leaving asylum seekers, families, and non-sectoral workers entirely untouched—was introduced early and never adequately answered by PRO. PRO's own concession in [B2.3] that visas address only a "slice" was immediately weaponized in [A2.5] as "fatal to the comparative claim" and carried through every subsequent round. PRO never reframed or recovered from this. Second, CON's judo move on PRO's Q2 was effective: rather than conceding that enforcement alone is insufficient, CON turned the question around to show enforcement is the indispensable foundation that makes guest-worker programs function at all—"The visa gets the credit; enforcement does the work" is the most memorable and logically compact line in the debate. Third, when PRO was pressed in Rebuttal 2 to show that total undocumented stock fell in Canada and Spain (not just sector-specific entries), PRO admitted the figure is "difficult to measure precisely," which left its headline examples doing less work than advertised. PRO's genuine strength—that enforcement displaces rather than reduces migration, encouraging permanent settlement—is a real and underrebutted point, and it kept PRO competitive. But PRO never extended this argument to close the gap on the structural mismatch problem, and its reliance on sectoral examples without total-stock evidence left the comparative burden unmet. CON's closing crystallized the clash cleanly rather than widening it, whereas PRO's closing largely repeated earlier material without sharpening the comparison. On the motion's own standard—which approach reduces total irregular migration more effectively—CON held the better route to victory after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's [A2.5] weaponization of PRO's own concession in [B2.3] that sectoral visas only reach "a slice" of migration. PRO never regained footing on this: it could not show how farm visas reduce crossings by asylum seekers or non-sectoral workers, and in Rebuttal 2 admitted total undocumented stock is "difficult to measure precisely." CON's judo on PRO's Q2—reframing enforcement-dependence as proof that enforcement is the load-bearing element, not a failed complement—was the second most effective hit, landing "the visa gets the credit; enforcement does the work" as the debate's clearest evaluative frame.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B won by making the more decisive comparative case: seasonal and sectoral pathways may reduce irregularity in some targeted labor markets, but A never proved that this translates into a larger reduction in total irregular migration. B repeatedly pressed the mismatch problem, overstay risk, quota-denial spillover, and dependence on enforcement, while A mostly reasserted labor demand and sectoral substitution. A had a meaningful point that border enforcement can displace migration and encourage permanent settlement, but B’s framing that legal pathways are narrow, dependent, and potentially counterproductive was cleaner and better sustained.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was pressing A’s concession that legal pathways address only a “slice” of migration, then asking whether sectoral declines actually reduce total undocumented stock. A answered with broad claims about labor migration being significant, but did not fully solve the total-effect challenge.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully framing legal pathways as a narrow, dependent supplement to enforcement rather than a superior alternative. Side A effectively exploited Side B's concession that pathways only address a 'slice' of migration, and neutralized Side B's effectiveness claims by showing that guest-worker programs rely entirely on the coercive enforcement architecture Side B sought to dismiss.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2 effectively turning the 'return mechanism' argument by pointing out that without enforcement, an expired visa is merely a 'polite suggestion,' thus proving enforcement is the load-bearing strategy.
