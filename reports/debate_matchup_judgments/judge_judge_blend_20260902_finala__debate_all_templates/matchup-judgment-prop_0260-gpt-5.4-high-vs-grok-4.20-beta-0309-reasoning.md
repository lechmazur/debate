### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (CON)): Side A built a cleaner, more consistent case throughout the exchange. Its core distinction—blocking cross-site and cross-app trackers by default does not ban advertising, and contextual or first-party ads survive intact—was introduced early and never genuinely refuted. Side B's economic collapse argument depended on the claim that shared ad infrastructure means even contextual ads fall under mass non-opt-out, but this mechanism was asserted vaguely in Rebuttal 2 rather than demonstrated, and it never addressed PRO's repeated point that a model requiring universal silent profiling as its precondition is not a legitimate defense to regulation. The "defaults decide outcomes" pivot was the debate's sharpest exchange: B turned A's own logic into a revenue-collapse warning, but A then reframed it decisively—if defaults decide outcomes, privacy must be the default, because the current default is engineered extraction, not neutral choice. That reframing held through the closing and was never squarely answered. B's responses to vulnerable-population pressure (children, SDK/webview tracking) were weak: saying parents can apply device controls does not address the structural point A raised. B's closing recycled earlier lines without sharpening the comparison or answering A's "revenue built on silent tracking is the practice under dispute, not an answer to it." A's closing crystallized the live clash well and offered the cleaner reason to vote: some economic adaptation for firms versus universal background surveillance for ordinary users. B's rhetoric peaked with "privacy defaults sound caring until they leave less to be private about," but that line appeared twice and was never backed with a concrete mechanism strong enough to dislodge A's route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The "defaults decide outcomes" exchange across Rebuttals 1 and 2 was decisive. B correctly turned A's admission back as a revenue-collapse argument; A then answered that if defaults truly determine outcomes, the current non-neutral default of engineered extraction is itself the problem, so privacy must be the baseline. A's reframe in [A4.5]–[A4.6] and [A5.4] was clean and B never produced a mechanism showing why contextual ads—explicitly exempted from the tracker-blocking mandate—would collapse, leaving B's core economic argument without a live route through this clash.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (CON)): Side A wins because it isolated the mandate as a targeted default-setting measure that preserves choice and specifically undermined Side B's central economic-collapse argument by showing contextual and first-party advertising remain viable. Side B never provided a convincing mechanism for why non-tracking ads would become unsustainable, and Side A effectively turned Side B's own "defaults decide outcomes" logic against the surveillance status quo.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's economic harm in Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 by distinguishing behavioral tracking from contextual/first-party advertising, exposing that Side B could not explain how the latter becomes impossible under an opt-out tracker block.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (CON)): PRO wins by successfully isolating the clash around defaults and cleanly distinguishing tracker blocking from ad banning. While CON effectively argued that defaults drive outcomes and would shrink ad revenue, PRO consistently flipped this premise: if defaults dictate behavior, the status quo already forces invisible surveillance on non-technical users. PRO’s rebuttals directly answered CON’s economic warnings by explaining how contextual and first-party models survive, and provided a clearer, more grounded mechanism for why voluntary tools fail at the OS/SDK level. CON’s argument that shared ad infrastructure would collapse contextual revenue was plausible but underdeveloped, and their later speeches grew repetitive. PRO’s weighing—limited, correctable friction for ad-tech versus universal hidden profiling for users—offered the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over whether default tracker blocking would inevitably destroy contextual ad revenue. CON leveraged PRO’s admission that “defaults decide outcomes” to argue mass non-opt-out would starve publishers, but PRO effectively parried by clarifying the mandate targets cross-site profiling, not ad delivery itself, and repeatedly pressed CON for a mechanism linking tracker blocks to contextual ad failure. PRO’s A1 in Rebuttal 2 crystallized this by conceding behavioral revenue would fall (as intended) while preserving non-tracking models, neutralizing CON’s core economic harm.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): CON's decisive move was the binary dilemma introduced in Rebuttal 1 and sharpened throughout: if the mandate means blocking is default-on, then platform vendors become legally mandated referees of the web's infrastructure; if it merely means "easily enable," the legal requirement adds nothing beyond an accessible optional blocker and inertia can be addressed more precisely through direct privacy rules. PRO's answer in [A4.1] — describing the tool as something users "can easily enable" — conceded toward the opt-in reading, which CON's closing correctly identified as undermining the entire case for why this specific mandate is necessary rather than simpler targeted regulations. PRO's best live response (system-level integration bypasses mobile restrictions and can't be hidden by platforms) is genuine and partially responsive, but PRO never reconciled it with the "easily enable" language or explained why that middle-ground design survives CON's referee-of-exemptions problem — ordinary users inherit default allow-lists from filter maintainers, which preserves the upstream gatekeeping PRO claimed to eliminate. CON's "either weak or strong" crystallization in the closing was the clearest comparative framing in the debate. PRO's inertia argument and mobile-restrictions point were substantive contributions that prevented a wider margin, and CON's two clipped speeches are a real execution cost. But on the decisive clash, PRO never provided a version of the mandate that is simultaneously strong enough to solve inertia and clean enough to avoid deputizing vendors as infrastructure referees.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's binary dilemma in Rebuttal 1 [B2.1–B2.2]: "If the blocker is not default-on, then PRO's solvency vanishes because optional blockers already exist; if it is default-on, the default behavior of a few platform vendors will govern how the web monetizes." PRO's A1 answer in [A4.1] — "a tool users can easily enable" — slid toward the opt-in reading without acknowledging the concession, and CON's closing pinned this as the decisive moment where the mandate's necessity collapsed.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by collapsing Side A's solvency through a sharp double bind: if the mandate is merely an available tool, it is unnecessary because direct privacy regulation, anti-dark-pattern rules, and mobile extension parity achieve the same goal without gatekeeping; if it is robust default-on blocking, it dangerously centralizes control over exemptions and business models in a handful of platforms. Side A never recovered from admitting the blocker is not default-on, and their "community-sourced filters" answer failed to resolve who actually controls the allow-lists. Side B's closing crystallized this clash decisively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and Pressure Questions exposed that Side A could not coherently defend whether the mandate was default-on (which creates platform gatekeeping) or merely optional (which makes it redundant with less intrusive alternatives), a tension Side A never resolved through the closing.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by exposing a fatal ambiguity in Side A's mechanism. When pressed on whether the mandate is default-on (as the motion's "opt-out" language implies) or merely an accessible toggle, Side A pivoted to the latter. Side B successfully weaponized this concession into a decisive dilemma: if the tool is merely optional, it fails to solve inertia better than lighter regulatory alternatives; if it is default-on as written, it centralizes platform gatekeeper power and risks breaking functional web infrastructure. Side B's technical grounding on the blurry line between trackers and essential scripts (logins, fraud checks, payments), combined with a stronger distributional analysis showing how the mandate would insulate data-rich giants while harming small publishers, gave a cleaner path to victory. Side B's two clipping violations are weighed as execution costs, but they do not offset the structural damage inflicted on Side A's solvency and textual fidelity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing exchange, where they trapped Side A on the "opt-out" vs "easy enable" distinction. By forcing Side A to abandon default-on blocking, Side B collapsed PRO's solvency into a weak/unnecessary vs strong/dangerous dilemma that directly decided the round.
