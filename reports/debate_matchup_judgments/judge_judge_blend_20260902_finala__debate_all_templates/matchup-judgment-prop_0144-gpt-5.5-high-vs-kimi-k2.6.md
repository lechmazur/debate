### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): PRO wins a competitive but clear decision on three interlocking points. First, PRO consistently offered genuine middle-ground discovery mechanisms—searchable adult-companion sections, age-gated menus, opt-in recommendation settings, clear post-signup disclosure—and CON never directly demolished these as implausible rather than just suboptimal. CON's "placement is existence" line is powerful against a binary world of default-on vs. total obscurity, but PRO's repeated itemization of alternative pathways undermined that binary, and CON never closed the gap. Second, PRO's dependency-mechanism point—that a feature designed to form attachment through repeated use causes harm before any opt-out is triggered, making labels insufficient—was not adequately answered by CON's billboard analogy. A billboard does not adapt, escalate, personalize, or simulate intimacy over sessions; the analogy undersells what makes this category distinctive. Third, CON's selective-enforcement argument (why not infinite scroll?) is the strongest live pressure against PRO, and PRO's response—that romantic/sexual AI uniquely fuses simulated intimacy, arousal, personalization, and monetized dependency in a way scroll does not—is defensible if not airtight. CON never produced a clean refutation showing these features are truly equivalent in kind, only in degree of engagement. CON's bereaved-widower scenario is the debate's most vivid moment and genuinely pressures PRO, but PRO's A1 answer (suggest crisis support, opt-in companion category, not immediate romantic/sexual role-play) satisfies the motion's specific scope. The proposition is narrow—auto-suggestion by default—and PRO held that narrow ground consistently; CON repeatedly argued against a broader ban that PRO explicitly disclaimed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal turning CON's own "placement is existence" against CON: if default placement is that decisive, it is too powerful to spend on converting a vulnerable newcomer into a simulated intimate relationship before context or maturity is known. CON coined the phrase and PRO weaponized it cleanly, and CON never recovered a satisfying answer to why that persuasive power should be deployed at the most impressionable point of the user journey.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by effectively co-opting Side A's core premise ("placement is existence") to prove that default suggestions are too powerful a nudge to use for dependency-forming intimate AI. Side B also successfully defended the middle ground—showing that discovery can be preserved via opt-in menus without needing unsolicited intimate prompts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's turnaround of the 'placement is existence' argument was decisive, demonstrating that if defaults are truly that powerful, they are too dangerous to use for initiating vulnerable users into pseudo-romantic relationships.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by sustaining focus on unique attachment risks of default romantic/sexual prompts, using pressure responses to distinguish support options from unsolicited intimacy and turning CON's "placement is existence" against default steering while preserving opt-in access.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's A1/A2 answers to pressure questions directly addressed vulnerable-user abandonment and selective dependency claims with clear alternatives and risk distinctions, sharpening the weighing before closing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): PRO built a more consistent path through the debate on three linked claims: (1) the proposition is modest—it preserves searchability and only blocks the algorithmic nudge; (2) defaults manufacture desire rather than disclose options, because placement, sequencing, and color are optimized for uptake by a party that profits from attachment; (3) reliance on platforms to turn their highest-conversion screen into a neutral safety clinic is structurally naïve given profit incentives. CON's best move was the overbreadth point—the "can intensify" trigger sweeps in responsible, safety-forward implementations—and the argument that onboarding is precisely where safety architecture should appear. These were real and somewhat underanswered. But CON never resolved the core pressure on profit incentives: when PRO asked in Q1 why a profit-seeking platform would voluntarily build a neutral warning rather than an optimized entry point, CON replied that design rules should be enforceable—pivoting away from the incentive question rather than answering it. PRO's A2 (search requires formed intent; auto-suggestion engineers desire before intent forms) was cleaner and more direct than CON's counter (search isn't automatically safer). CON's clipped speeches (Opening and Rebuttal 1 both exceeded word limits) created minor execution costs but did not change the outcome since the core arguments were legible. PRO's casino and cigarette analogies were rhetorically effective and tracked the structural argument; CON's rhetoric was competent but less memorable. On balance, PRO's framing—defaults dress corporate preference in neutrality, and structural guards beat self-policing—held up better across the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 in Rebuttal 2: distinguishing "formed intent" search from algorithmic auto-suggestion. PRO argued that a user who types a query has bypassed the nudge and arrived on their own terms, while the auto-suggestion engineers desire before intent exists. CON's response (search is not automatically informed consent either) was valid but shallower, and PRO's point remained the cleaner answer to why the motion's line—block the default, preserve search—is coherent rather than arbitrary.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing auto-suggestion not as a neutral disclosure, but as a curated, persuasive nudge ("defaults manufacture desire"). Side A relied on an idealized vision of onboarding as a neutral safety clinic, which Side B effectively dismantled by pointing to the profit incentives of platforms and the inherently seductive nature of the welcome mat. Side B also provided a sharper distinction between AI intimacy and other features like games or dating apps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 provided an excellent, decisive answer to Side A's pressure question about the threshold of harm, clearly distinguishing the synthetic, reciprocal intimacy of AI from the human friction of dating apps or the challenge loops of games.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by establishing a clearer causal path from default suggestions to dependency via asymmetry and profit motives, directly distinguishing simulated intimacy from other features, and exposing the implausibility of CON's "safe suggestion" premise under business incentives. Side A (CON) offered solid design-focused counters but failed to neutralize the core claim that the act of auto-suggesting itself undermines informed consent, leaving their alternative less protective in practice.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's A1/A2 answers in Rebuttal 2, which crystallized why architectural intimacy simulation creates a non-arbitrary threshold and why search-based intent differs from default nudges, sharpening the overbreadth comparison.
