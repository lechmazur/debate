CON never answered this satisfactorily. CON's fallback ("one-size-fits-all" risk, "soft preference for clarity") was never anchored in a positive principle that justified non-accessible drafting. PRO consistently made the comparison clearer and the stakes more legible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 on criminal law [B2.5–B2.6]: CON had cited criminal law as a domain where precision matters most. PRO inverted this directly—when liberty and fines are at stake, the public's need for understandable law is highest, not lowest, because selective enforcement by officials precedes any appellate tidying. PRO then turned CON's admission that legislators use complexity for concealment into a direct indictment: that is exactly why a plain-language requirement is needed. This double turn—capturing CON's strongest example and converting its implicit concession into an affirmative reason—was the most decisive exchange in the debate and was never adequately answered.

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = Llama 4 Maverick (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by identifying and holding the decisive pressure point throughout the entire debate: the motion's "even if that sacrifices some legal precision" clause. From the opening, A pinpointed that this tradeoff—not the aspirational goal of clarity—is what the motion actually compels. A's core argument (vague statutory text transfers lawmaking power to prosecutors, agencies, and courts, which harms ordinary citizens more than technical language does) was never squarely neutralized by B. Meanwhile, B's rebuttals steadily retreated from the motion's hard version: by Rebuttal 2, B was arguing that plain language should be pursued "while still allowing for technical precision where necessary" and with "genuine effort" not to sacrifice "necessary precision." A correctly identified this in both Rebuttal 2 and the closing as effectively abandoning the only contested part of the motion—and B's closing did nothing to recapture that ground. A's pressure questions were sharper (exposing B's lack of a limiting principle for criminal/tax/benefits statutes), whereas B's questions were partly self-undermining: asking about jurisdictions that achieved plain language without sacrificing certainty only reinforced A's position. The alternative A offered (summaries, explanatory notes, guidance documents alongside precise enacted text) was reasonable and was never refuted. B never established why the enacted statute itself, rather than accompanying materials, must bear the accessibility burden when precision is at stake.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 [A2.1–A2.7] was the most decisive exchange. A caught B's rebuttal quietly conceding the core: by endorsing explanatory notes and definitions as the solution, B had moved onto CON's ground. This single exchange collapsed B's affirmative case and B never recovered a coherent response to it.
