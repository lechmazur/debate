### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): Both sides argued competently and consistently, but PRO (Side B) secured a narrow victory through two insights CON never adequately neutralized. First, the codes-versus-subsidies distinction (B4.3): building code mandates operate through permitting offices, not appropriations, so they do not directly cannibalize retrofit subsidy pools. CON's counter—finite skilled labor and enforcement bandwidth—was fair but speculative and never concretely demonstrated. Because this distinction undercuts CON's central "we can't do both" claim, it materially damaged CON's strongest live route to victory. Second, PRO's "physics never expires" rebuttal to the obsolescence argument was clean and largely unanswered: superior insulation and airtight envelopes deliver value regardless of future grid architecture or heat-pump specs. CON's strongest point—the scale argument (2% new builds vs. 98% existing stock)—remained rhetorically powerful throughout, and CON's closing garden-hose metaphor was the debate's most memorable line. However, PRO correctly reframed that two percent as a compounding future liability and noted the motion allows for both codes and retrofits; CON needed to show the mandate isn't worth requiring even as a baseline, not merely that retrofits matter more. PRO's pressure-question answers (A1 and A2) were structurally cleaner than CON's, which tended to re-argue rather than advance. On the regressive-cost clash, both sides partially scored: PRO's rental-market defense was real, CON's counter that most new builds are market-rate was also real, leaving this a wash. On balance, PRO held the better of the decisive resource clash and the lock-in argument, giving it a clear but not dominant edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 in Rebuttal 2 (B4.3): the distinction between building-code mandates (regulatory, enforced through permitting) and retrofit subsidy budgets (fiscal, requiring appropriations) was the sharpest single insight in the debate. It directly answered CON's most pointed pressure question about which retrofit program would be cut, and CON's response—finite skilled labor—was a reasonable counter but too vague to fully restore the resource-scarcity argument.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): Side B won because it stayed closer to the proposition: for new construction, building net-zero readiness in at the start plausibly avoids long-lived carbon lock-in and later retrofit costs. Side A made strong scale and affordability objections, but relied heavily on asserting cannibalization of retrofit resources without proving that code standards for new builds meaningfully displace retrofit subsidies or labor. B's prevention framing, operating-cost affordability answer, and rebuttal that new builds become future existing stock left the cleaner route to vote PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive rebuttal was that existing-stock retrofits and new-build standards are not mutually exclusive: even if old homes are the largest current problem, building inefficient new homes knowingly creates the next retrofit crisis.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B prevailed by sustaining a clearer core clash on scale and resource misallocation: new builds are a small annual addition with slow stock turnover, so the mandate diverts from retrofitting the dominant existing emissions source. Side A effectively defended prevention and rental impacts but did not neutralize B's point that the policy still burdens affordability for actual low-income renters while consuming finite enforcement and labor capacity. B's direct answers to pressure questions and reframing of the mopping metaphor landed more decisively across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 directly countered A's rental and "do both" claims by showing new units are mostly market-rate (passing costs along) and that code enforcement draws from the same scarce labor pool needed for deep existing retrofits, keeping the focus on actual emissions location.
