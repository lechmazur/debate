### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively defeating Side B's logic on adversarial machine learning. Side A effectively proved that pooling 30 independent detectors into one system creates a single point of failure where one leaked login allows an adversary to extract and evade the entire ensemble. Side A's distinction between sharing "facts about the adversary" (conclusions) versus "facts about us" (methods) perfectly neutralized Side B's examples.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing refutation of Side B's correlation argument, explaining that "A fine-tune does not evade countries. It evades detectors"—proving that independent hoards actually force an attacker to learn 30 separate lessons rather than just one.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): CON won the decisive clash: methods/scorers are facts about us (once known, they train better liars and cannot be revoked), while actionable platform warnings are mostly adversary indicators shareable as conclusions. PRO never neutralized black-box extraction, the permanent knowledge leak vs temporary credential fix, or the claim that pooling correlates defense failure. PRO’s transnational need and rotation story were real, but CON’s independent-detector logic and “share warnings, keep the eyes” weighing stayed cleaner after full exchange. A’s clipped R2 was a cost, not enough to flip the path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s A2/A4/A5 line: a scorer is a fact about us (extractable via queries), while hashes/IPs/payment trails are adversary facts; a Lisbon fine-tune does not beat a German detector, so pooling manufactures correlated failure.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by drawing a clean, memorable distinction between sharing conclusions (facts about the adversary, which are already shareable safely) and sharing sensitive methods (facts about our own detectors, which create irreversible exposure and correlated vulnerability). B successfully undercut A's central claim that a transnational threat requires pooled defense methods, showing that independent, diverse detectors force adversaries to solve thirty separate evasion problems, whereas a shared system turns one penetration into a single point of failure that exposes everyone. A never recovered from the mechanistic point that a fine-tune evades a specific detector, not every detector in every country, which broke the link between threat correlation and defense correlation. B crystallized this clash effectively in the closing without introducing new evidence, while A's closing simply restated the already-damaged correlation thesis.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal that pooling converts thirty independent detection problems into one target (B2.6-B2.7, B4.4-B4.5, B5.2-B5.3). By showing that a generator fine-tuned against one member's signature still trips another member's different detector, B destroyed A's argument that unilateral hoarding produces "correlated blindness," and instead framed the motion as manufacturing the very correlated failure it claimed to prevent.
