### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly, but CON built the sharper, more decisive case. CON's central move—distinguishing "preservation" (dark archives, format-shifting, orphan clearance) from "distribution" (lending in-market works)—gave a workable, judge-friendly test that PRO never neutralized. CON's pressure questions exposed the motion's real weakness: the text exempts nonprofit digital libraries "such as the Internet Archive" wholesale, not merely PRO's invented "bona fide" carve-out, and CON grounded this with the plausible, well-known example of the Internet Archive lending in-print ebooks without waitlists during the pandemic. PRO never squarely answered this overbreadth problem, instead asserting that a "preservation purpose" preamble creates an enforceable legal standard—an assertion CON directly rebutted as unenforceable ("a tax label, not a stewardship guarantee"). Conversely, when PRO pressured CON on whether its remedies (compulsory licensing, capped damages) already concede traditional enforcement is incompatible with preservation, CON handled this with a precise, disciplined concession: it conceded strict-liability/statutory damages are incompatible with good-faith archiving, but denied that the whole enforcement bundle (the power to stop substitutive copies) must fall. This nuanced answer survived scrutiny better than PRO's vaguer claims about bureaucratic slowness. Across rounds, CON progressively narrowed the clash to "who decides—the judge or the archivist," while PRO largely restated its opening frame without closing the scope gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Question ("what enforceable limit stops any self-declared nonprofit... from claiming the exemption?") combined with its later point that the motion's named exemplar, the Internet Archive, already lent in-market ebooks without waitlists—directly undercutting PRO's unenforced "bona fide" qualifier and never adequately answered by PRO.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by separating preservation from public distribution and offering targeted rights for copying, format shifting, orphan works, and unavailable works. Side B established a serious preservation problem but never justified exempting conduct that substitutes for active sales. Its claimed safeguards such as bona fide purpose, lawful acquisition, and controlled lending were not clearly supplied by the proposition and did not answer the Internet Archive counterexample.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A decisively answered the alleged concession: calibrated damages and compulsory licensing modify enforcement while retaining judicial power to stop market-substituting copies. This undercut Side B’s claim that any departure from current rules proves the need for broad exemption.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by making the decisive clash easier to judge: the motion as written is overbroad relative to genuine preservation needs. CON consistently separated dark/restricted capture and narrow orphan/compulsory tools from immunization of in-market lending, used the Internet Archive’s still-sold ebook scanning and pandemic waitlist removal as a live counterexample, and kept the enforceable market-substitute test. PRO’s corporate-veto and first-sale parity points were real, but the “bona fide purpose” limiter never became a credible textual restraint on any self-declared nonprofit, so CON’s path better preserved heritage without extinguishing incentives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 1–2 sequence: PRO’s own remedies already depart from traditional enforcement, yet the motion abolishes rather than calibrates the core power to stop sales-substituting copies; digital perfect copies are not first-sale scarce objects.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly with no fabricated sourcing, but B's pressure questions did real work that A never fully escaped. B forced A to (1) disown the resolution's own named example's most litigated conduct (in-print book lending), creating tension between defending "the Internet Archive" and defending only a narrowed subset of its activity, and (2) admit that the "exemption" is conditional on market absence, with statutory damages and injunctions returning the moment a rightsholder asserts a market. B's closing "sleight of hand" framing—that this collapses PRO's position into ordinary conditional liability rather than a genuine exemption from traditional enforcement—landed as a serious structural objection that A's closing answered only by re-asserting that the exemption "ends when its justifying purpose does," without resolving who adjudicates market existence in the first instance (a gap B explicitly flagged in B4.2 and never squarely closed by A). A's asymmetric-risk framing (irreversible deletion vs. correctable licensing) remained a strong, vivid throughline, but it was fought to a standstill rather than clearly winning out, while B's attack on the coherence and scope of A's "conditional exemption" mechanism was the more decisive, less-answered pressure point by the end of the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 point [B4.3-B4.4] that A's conditional-exemption model, once enforcement "returns" whenever a market is asserted, is functionally indistinguishable from ordinary conditional liability rather than a genuine exemption from traditional enforcement — a contradiction A's closing did not fully dissolve.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B won by defending a purpose-bound, conditional exemption rather than blanket immunity, preserving enforcement for active markets while showing why irreversible loss warrants protection where no market exists. Side A correctly identified implementation problems and offered orphan-work alternatives, but leaned too heavily on the semantic claim that any conditional exemption concedes the motion. Side B's repeated assertion that orphan-work rules require identifiable owners was weak, yet its asymmetry weighing and direct answers left the cleaner route to the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered both questions, explained forfeiture of the safe harbor as the enforcement mechanism, and separated the Internet Archive's preservation work from active-market book distribution. This substantially neutralized Side A's blanket-immunity framing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won by forcing PRO into a contradiction with the motion as written: PRO repeatedly tethered any "exemption" to conditions that restore injunctions and statutory damages the moment a market or rightsholder appears, which is ordinary conditional liability rather than exemption from traditional enforcement. CON also exploited the motion's own Internet Archive example and showed PRO had to disown the Archive's core contested lending conduct. PRO's asymmetric-risk and ownerless-crawl points landed but never escaped the motion-fidelity problem. CON's comparative weighing (targeted carve-outs already handle abandoned works without gutting creator recourse) remained cleaner after full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON Rebuttal 2 (A4.3–A4.4) exposing that PRO's "exemption evaporates / enforcement returns" answer abandons the proposition and describes retention of traditional enforcement instead.
