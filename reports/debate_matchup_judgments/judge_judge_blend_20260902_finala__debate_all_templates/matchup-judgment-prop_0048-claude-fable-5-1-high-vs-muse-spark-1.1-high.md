### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): B won by distinguishing a guaranteed headcount from staffing responsive to actual clinical need. A established the dangers and incentives behind understaffing, but its answer that nurses could not leave a quiet ward depended on surge capacity the proposed ratio law does not itself create. B also directly explained why acuity rules could produce fewer, better-targeted closures. A's retention case remained plausible, but several broad claims about jurisdictions were insufficiently grounded. B's two clipped rebuttals were execution costs, though their decisive arguments remained visible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange followed A's admission that the charge nurse could not move a nurse below the ratio. B turned this into a clear comparison: the mandate can legally tether staff to stable patients while an acuity-based duty sends attention toward septic arrivals. A invoked float and call-in staff, but never showed that ratio legislation guarantees either.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully weaponizing a specific, highly effective pressure question about rigidity (the 2 a.m. scenario) to prove that rigid ratios prioritize headcount over clinical need. Side A's defense—that hospitals should rely on a float pool—was neatly dismantled by Side B pointing out that the proposed law mandates only the rigid floor, not the float pool, effectively trapping Side A into defending a scenario where a nurse is legally forbidden from treating the sickest patients.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which masterfully capitalized on Side A's direct concession ("No - and that no is the point") to frame the ratio as a dangerous constraint on clinical judgment.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): B won a competitive debate by forcing and then owning the decisive rigidity clash: fixed headcount ratios can lock a nurse to a quiet ward while septic patients next door need attention, and A’s own “No—and that no is the point” answer plus float-pool hope did not match the actual motion. A stayed coherent on incentives, open-bed meaning, and both models rationing when short, but B’s comparative (need-based binding acuity vs headcount lock) and corridor-zero path remained live and cleaner to vote on; two clipped B turns were an execution cost, not enough to reverse the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s use of A’s A1 concession that the charge nurse may not drop a quiet ward below ratio to cover septic admissions—crystallized in B’s closing as outlawing judgment toward the sickest patients.
