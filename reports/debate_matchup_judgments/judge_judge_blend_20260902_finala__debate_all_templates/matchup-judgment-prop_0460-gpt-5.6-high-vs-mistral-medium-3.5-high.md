### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): B built a tighter, more responsive case. Its core mechanism—that transmission is a regional good trapped in a fragmented local approval system, and that non-veto protections (compensation, mitigation, review) can preserve local voice without unilateral obstruction—was stated clearly in the opening and defended consistently. Crucially, B identified and repeatedly pressed a real structural weakness in CON's case: if veto power is the safeguard for the vulnerable, why does it empirically empower the best-resourced, most litigious communities to export burdens onto weaker ones (B2.2–B2.3, B4.5)? CON never closed this gap; it kept reasserting that removing veto "entrenches" inequality without explaining why retaining veto doesn't produce the same asymmetry it condemns. B also mounted a specific, plausible rebuttal to CON's distributed-resources argument (storage duration, renters, geographic diversity for resilience) that CON never directly answered, instead repeating the same California/Texas claim nearly verbatim across three speeches—a rewording rather than a rebuttal. CON's opening was also clipped by the word limit, costing it a fully developed initial case. CON's Keystone XL analogy and emotional emphasis on irreversible harms were rhetorically effective and forced B to sharpen its "systemic reform vs. single project" distinction, but this exchange favored B analytically (no policy guarantees against political failure). B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly ("authority must match the scale of consequences") while CON's closing largely recapitulated prior assertions without resolving the burden-shifting and complementarity gaps B had exposed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's repeated point (B2.2–B2.3, reinforced in B4.5) that local veto power is wielded most effectively by well-resourced communities, shifting burdens toward the less powerful rather than protecting them, went fundamentally unanswered by CON and undercut CON's central "protects the vulnerable" framing.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO won the central coordination clash. B established scale mismatch, holdout problem, and distinction between veto and enforceable protections (review, routing, compensation). A's pressure about Keystone was answered: reform improves odds vs guarantee, oil pipeline ≠ electric transmission. A's answer that universal refusal proves non-necessity conceded B's point, and A never offered a mechanism to build multi-jurisdictional lines when each locality says elsewhere. On distributed alternatives, B's hours-vs-geography distinction beat A's overclaim that solar/batteries already replace regional grid. Opening clipped also hurt A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Q1 on the "elsewhere" problem and B4/R2 answer: PRO showed reducing fragmented veto improves buildout without guaranteeing it, while CON's reply that universal veto proves lack of necessity collapses necessity into local consent and leaves no mechanism for regional reliability.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side B built a significantly stronger and more logically sound case. Side B's crucial distinction between unilateral local veto power and enforceable procedural protections effectively neutralized Side A's claims about democratic accountability and community harm. Furthermore, Side B decisively won the clash over alternatives by explaining the physical difference between temporal energy shifting (batteries) and geographic energy shifting (transmission), exposing the flaws in Side A's reliance on distributed resources. Side A suffered from a clipped opening, repetitive use of the Keystone XL analogy (which Side B successfully contextualized as a poor comparison), and a logically weak claim that universal local refusal proves a project is unnecessary. Side B's progression and weighing were consistently superior.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it clearly dismantled the distributed resources argument by distinguishing between shifting power across hours versus across geography, and cleanly answered the Keystone XL pressure question without conceding the underlying necessity of regional transmission.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON built the more durable case by reframing the clash as "faster permits vs. faster completed lines" and consistently showing that stripping local leverage can simply displace conflict into courts, elections, and land acquisition rather than eliminating it. Its sharpest moment was A4.3's exposure of a real contradiction in PRO's safeguards: if corridor-routing and environmental-justice protections are binding, they can override the fastest route (so justice, not speed, governs); if they can be overridden for speed, they aren't real protections. PRO never answered this in its closing, instead repeating the "paralysis" framing and leaning heavily on a single TransWest example that CON had already contested as multi-causal (federal land approval, financing, cost allocation) rather than proof that vetoes specifically are the bottleneck. PRO also lost some ground procedurally: its Rebuttal 1 was clipped mid-sentence, an explicit execution cost, and its central evidentiary anchor (TransWest) was introduced only in rebuttal rather than the opening, then reused repeatedly instead of being supplemented with new argument. CON's alternative reforms (deadlines, mediation, required alternatives, narrow proportional override) gave it a constructive, non-obstructionist position that answered the motion directly rather than dodging it, while still landing the decisive unrebutted contradiction in the final rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3's "binding vs. overridable" contradiction against PRO's proposed safeguards, left unanswered through PRO's closing, was the most decisive exchange of the debate.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won the durability vs permission clash. B asserted any county = absolute indefinite veto causing TransWest delays but A showed one complex project cannot prove causation and offered non-veto fixes (firm deadlines, concurrent review, mandatory mediation, requirement to offer alternatives, narrow override). A then exposed B's safeguard contradiction - if corridor/EJ protections bind, speed isn't priority; if speed overrides them, they aren't protections - and hammered that faster permits != faster built lines, summarized as "a permit is not a power line." B clipped R1, repeated causal claim without accounting, and straw-manned CON as defending absolute veto.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2/A4 on permit vs line and contradictory safeguards: if EJ/corridor rules are binding they defeat speed priority, if not binding they don't protect, plus firm deadlines prevent indefinite blockade without stripping leverage.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by decisively separating the speed of permitting from the speed of actual construction. A's central insight—that stripping local veto power merely shifts delay downstream into litigation, land acquisition, and political resistance—fatally undermines PRO's core causal mechanism. A also effectively dismantles PRO's proposed safeguards, showing that if they are binding, they contradict the priority of speed, and if they are not, they fail to protect communities. PRO relies on a false binary between absolute local vetoes and centralized speed, failing to adequately engage with A's nuanced alternative of override as a proportionate last resort. Additionally, PRO suffered a minor execution cost with a clipped rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they expose the contradiction in PRO's safeguards (binding safeguards slow down the fastest route, meaning justice, not speed, is the priority) and reinforce the distinction between rapid permission and durable construction.
