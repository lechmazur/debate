### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B won by consistently distinguishing evidence for intensive, targeted intervention from evidence for universal provision. Side A plausibly argued that universality catches children missed by eligibility screens, but never established that this leakage is large enough to justify paying for the low-marginal-benefit majority. B therefore had the cleaner cost-benefit comparison: broad and certain costs against gains that A showed mainly for a concentrated subgroup.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to the coverage argument was decisive: enforceable quality can improve targeted classrooms, while universality still enlarges the costly denominator. A’s claim that universal coverage is the only way to catch missed children did not adequately defeat B’s cheaper automatic-enrollment alternative.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON won the core weighing: PRO’s large lifetime savings were conceded to come from small intensive programs, not universal systems. A showed real universal systems (OK, GA, Boston) show fading readiness bumps, not broad lasting drops in special-ed/retention/justice, middle-class gain is substitution (childcare subsidy, not lasting gain per proposition), and automatic roster enrollment undermines necessity. PRO never restored a population-level durable effect at universal scale, and treated political durability/coverage as a substitute for lasting educational/social gains to justify certain, broad, immediate cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.2: A turned PRO’s own quality-floor examples (Oklahoma, Georgia, Boston) against it, arguing the falsifiable test has already happened and shows only fading bumps, not the durable retention/special-ed/justice reductions needed for the proposition, breaking PRO’s extrapolation from intensive programs.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by decisively dismantling Side B's empirical foundation and exposing a fatal flaw in Side B's cost-benefit weighing. Side A correctly identifies that the high-ROI studies were small and intensive, and when Side B attempts to cite scaled universal programs (Oklahoma, Georgia, Boston), Side A effectively points out that these systems have not demonstrated the massive long-term social savings Side B relies on. Furthermore, Side A's argument that universal pre-K merely acts as a childcare subsidy for the middle-class majority—yielding no marginal lasting educational gain—forces Side B to concede in the closing that this cost is merely a "political price." Side A cleanly proves that a subsidy is not a saving, thereby defeating the proposition's requirement that the gains themselves justify the public cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they turn Side B's own examples (Oklahoma, Georgia, Boston) against them by pointing out that these specific universal systems are old enough to have shown durable results but have only produced fading readiness bumps, not the broad, lasting drops in special education or justice involvement that Side B's ROI argument requires.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the comparison focused on universal programs at scale. A offered plausible mechanisms and strong reasons to value early intervention, but its lasting-outcome evidence came mainly from intensive model programs, while its later threshold remained conditional: if universal cohorts achieved modest durable reductions, the cost would be justified. B persuasively argued that this did not establish that scaled programs actually produce broad, lasting average gains and that benefits concentrated among high-risk children better support targeted provision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s denominator rebuttal was decisive: A relied heavily on preventing rare, expensive outcomes while defending the cost of slots for every child. B’s thousand-slot illustration clarified why large benefits for a small group need not justify universal expenditure, and A’s appeal to broader readiness gains never adequately established that those gains persist at scale.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won the denominator clash. PRO shifted the scoreboard from test fadeout to rare high-cost outcomes, but conceded those outcomes are low-base-rate. CON's second rebuttal showed a few points saved among 100 at risk does not cover 1000 universal slots, and that scaled universal evidence shows fadeout of both cognitive and self-regulation gains. PRO's replies remained aspirational (quality floor, peers as mechanism) without meeting the universal-evidence burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.2 1000-slot arithmetic turning B's "few percentage points is enough" into a small average benefit that cannot justify universal cost.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by identifying and relentlessly exploiting the "denominator problem"—the arithmetic mismatch between universal costs and benefits concentrated in a high-risk "tail." Side A's pressure answers and second rebuttal effectively dismantled Side B's fiscal justification using a clear 1,000-slot arithmetic example. Side A's closing neatly neutralized Side B's late attempt to claim broad upstream savings for the non-tail majority by pointing back to the empirical record of scale-up fadeout. Side B argued well but could not overcome the structural flaw in justifying universal costs with largely targeted benefits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal, specifically the 1,000-slot arithmetic example that weaponized Side B's own admission about the low base rates of high-cost outcomes to prove the average benefit fails to cover the average universal cost.
