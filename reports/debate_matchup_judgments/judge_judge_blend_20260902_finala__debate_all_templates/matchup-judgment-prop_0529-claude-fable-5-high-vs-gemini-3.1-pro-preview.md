### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by making the central distinction sharper: auditing actual corporate models mainly requires legal access and targeted inference compute, not taxpayer-funded duplicate frontier training runs. A persuasively attacked reliance on voluntary APIs and open weights, but B answered with regulation and showed that A’s public procurement plan either fails to audit proprietary systems or funnels large sums back to the same hyperscalers. A’s guarantee-versus-goodwill frame was strong, but it overclaimed that independent verification generally requires training comparable frontier models.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive rebuttal was the pharmaceutical and Boeing analogy: regulators test finished products through mandated access rather than rebuilding the entire R&D pipeline, which directly undercut A’s claim that public frontier training capacity is necessary for verification.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won because it maintained that independent verification requires non-revocable capacity, showing that CON's framework of API access, open weights, and regulation amounts to auditing-by-permission-slip with no backstop when corporate strategy shifts. CON's inference-affordability response never fully answered how academics verify training-process claims or conduct large-scale audits without coordinated infrastructure, and PRO successfully turned the hyperscaler-entrenchment critique by demonstrating that pooled procurement creates negotiating leverage whereas scattered grants do not.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of CON's reliance on corporate voluntarism—showing that APIs get revoked and weights can be closed, while turning the "hyperscaler subsidy" critique by highlighting that CON's world sends public money to cloud giants without bargaining power—created the decisive gap on resilience and accountability.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B wins by successfully dismantling PRO's core necessity claim. B's sharp distinction between training compute (massively expensive, suited for private capital risk) and inference compute (cheap, sufficient for auditing and red-teaming) directly undercut A's argument that academics need frontier-scale training clusters for verification. B also effectively turned A's pooled procurement mechanism into a liability, framing it as a state subsidy that entrenches the very hyperscalers A warns against. While A's revocable goodwill frame was rhetorically strong and correctly pressured CON on corporate closure trends, B's risk-allocation framework and technical clarity on compute economics provided a cleaner, more fiscally plausible path to independent oversight. A struggled to justify the fiscal burden of academic training runs without conceding wasteful duplication, leaving B with the decisive comparative advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing decisively win the training versus inference clash. By separating the compute needed to build models from the compute needed to audit them, B neutralized A's central claim that massive public training capacity is required for verification. B's pharma analogy (the FDA tests finished drugs; it does not build state factories to recreate private R&D) cleanly crystallized why A's proposal was redundant, turned A's own analogy against them, and secured the fiscal and practical comparison.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): B won by making the central comparison cleaner: public hardware faces obsolescence and coordination costs, while cloud leasing deepens dependence on the same firms A says are too powerful. B also offered a narrower alternative, mandated access plus funded audit consortia, that plausibly captures much of A's safety benefit at lower cost. A had a credible public-interest science and training-time safety story, but did not fully answer the cloud dependence dilemma, allocation fights, or B's point that A's emergence argument undercuts transfer from public models to the actual frontier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive rebuttal was the cloud and emergence turn: if A rents from Big Tech, independence is weakened, and if A trains public models, its own claim that frontier risks are emergent and model-specific makes those results less relevant than mandated access to the actual frontier artifact.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): CON won by exposing two unresolved tensions in PRO's case. First, PRO's defense that countries should pursue both sovereign hardware and cloud rental failed to escape the trilemma: sovereign builds face severe obsolescence costs, while cloud rental deepens financial dependence on the very monopolies PRO claims to oppose. Second, PRO's own emergence argument—that safety risks are scale-specific—undermined their claim that alignment methodologies developed on a trailing public model would transfer to actual frontier systems. CON showed that mandated access plus targeted funding captures the relevant auditing benefits at lower cost, while PRO never satisfactorily answered the allocation-fight and speed-differential objections to multinational coordination. PRO's closing reframed the stakes eloquently but never engaged the contradiction CON crystallized, leaving CON the cleaner route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's repeated demonstration that PRO's case collapses into a trilemma on infrastructure (sovereign obsolescence versus cloud dependence) and an internal contradiction on safety transferability, most forcefully developed in Rebuttal 2 and the closing.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully identifying and exploiting a critical logical tension in Side A's case. When Side A pivoted to cloud leasing to avoid hardware obsolescence, Side B effectively argued this deepens dependence on the very monopolies PRO claims to oppose. More decisively, Side B trapped Side A on the emergence argument: if frontier risks are emergent and model-specific, safety findings from a publicly trained model that lags the commercial frontier cannot reliably transfer. Side A never resolved this contradiction, instead reframing around training-time alignment, which Side B correctly noted doesn't solve the transferability gap. Side B's alternative (mandated access plus targeted audit consortia) was consistently weighed as capturing PRO's stated benefits at a fraction of the cost and complexity. Side B's rhetorical precision and clean handling of pressure questions made the comparative choice clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2 dismantling of Side A's cloud pivot and emergence argument. B2.1 and B4.4 brilliantly framed leased compute as subsidizing the monopoly, while B2.5 and B4.5 exposed the contradiction between claiming risks are emergent and model-specific versus arguing that safety techniques from a lagging public model will transfer to frontier systems. This logical trap decisively shifted the weighing.
