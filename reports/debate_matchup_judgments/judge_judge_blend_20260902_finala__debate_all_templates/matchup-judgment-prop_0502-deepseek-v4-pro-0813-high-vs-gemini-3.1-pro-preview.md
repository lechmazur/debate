### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON successfully exposed a lethal structural dilemma in PRO's model: an Antarctic-style treaty must either override core sovereignty—making it unratifiable by the major Arctic powers—or retreat to a "regional chamber" framework that CON showed is essentially the current UNCLOS/Arctic Council order with unsubstantiated "magical enforcement powers." PRO never overcame the consent problem; repeatedly insisting that sovereignty can be "bounded" does not explain why populated coastal states would sign away EEZ jurisdiction or defense autonomy. Meanwhile, CON grounded their case in concrete, existing binding mechanisms—UNCLOS Part XII, the IMO Polar Code, the Ilulissat Declaration, and domestic Indigenous agreements—that function because they align with sovereign reality. PRO's closing crystallized neatly around "binding," but without a credible path to ratification, that binding force remained hypothetical. CON's sharper second rebuttal and closing made the comparative choice clearer: a functional, sovereignty-respecting present versus an unratifiable illusion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal [A4.1-A4.5], where it demonstrated that PRO's narrowing of the Antarctic model—excluding onshore demilitarization and limiting jurisdiction to maritime commons—had collapsed into a thinly veiled version of UNCLOS and the Arctic Council, while any stronger Antarctic analogue remained impossible to ratify. This exchange simultaneously absorbed PRO's pressure questions and reversed the burden onto PRO to explain why states would sign a treaty that PRO itself had to hollow out to make plausible.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON kept the geographic/demographic disanalogy live and turned PRO's adaptations against it. PRO's valid enforcement gap was blunted by CON's consent burden: a treaty that preserves EEZ rights adds little beyond UNCLOS/Polar Code, while a treaty that overrides EEZ/defense authority is unratifiable, creates a legal vacuum, and dilutes Indigenous leverage by shifting it to non-Arctic capitals. PRO's regional chamber answer was creative but never solved ratifiability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Con's A4.3-A4.5 paradox - that PRO's tailored treaty becomes UNCLOS/Arctic Council rebranded with "magical enforcement" and would never be signed if it truly internationalized EEZ jurisdiction - was the decisive turn.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B wins by effectively leveraging the geographic and demographic differences between the Arctic and Antarctic to demonstrate the unratifiability of the proposed treaty. Side B's decisive move in Rebuttal 2 exposed a fatal paradox in Side A's case: if the treaty overrides sovereignty, major powers will reject it; if it is watered down to respect sovereignty and only governs the high seas via regional chambers, it is functionally identical to the current UNCLOS/Arctic Council framework that Side A critiques. Side B also provided stronger, more concrete examples of existing binding mechanisms (IMO Polar Code, UNCLOS Part XII, domestic land claims) to counter Side A's claim that the current system lacks enforcement, while Side A struggled to escape the practical reality that binding treaties require the consent of the very nuclear-armed coastal states they aim to restrict.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, specifically [B4.3], which trapped Side A in a paradox by showing that their moderated treaty design was either an unratifiable illusion or just a rebranded version of the status quo.

### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON wins because it exposes a decisive feasibility gap in PRO's case. PRO convincingly argues the Arctic faces grave environmental and military risks, but it simultaneously portrays bordering states as so captured by profit and militarization that they cannot be trusted to self-regulate. CON turns this very premise into a defeater: if states are too greedy and security-obsessed to regulate themselves, they will never ratify or comply with a binding treaty that strips their sovereignty, freezes their claims, and dismantles their fleets. The Antarctic Cold War analogy fails because Antarctica was uninhabited and strategically empty, making demilitarization costless; in the populated, economically vital Arctic, the same treaty would demand existential sacrifices from Russia, the United States, and others. PRO never overcomes this consent problem or explains how enforcement would work against non-consenting great powers. CON's alternative—binding what can actually be bound through enforceable liability, shipping standards, spill response, fisheries management, and operational deconfliction—remains intact and largely unchallenged as the realistic path to Arctic protection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's turn that profit-driven, militarized bordering states will not ratify a sovereignty-surrendering treaty, converting PRO's own state-capture argument into a fatal feasibility objection.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON (A) kept a live route to victory on feasibility: the treaty needs consent of the same profit-driven powers PRO distrusts, making it a dead letter where it matters most. Its populated-ocean vs uninhabited-land disanalogy and low-cost Antarctic vs high-cost Arctic distinction neutralized PRO's Cold War precedent and enforcement claims. PRO's global-stakes urgency remained but never answered the ratification paradox.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's ratification paradox — if states are too captured to regulate they will never ratify a ban, so a paper moratorium is weaker than practical liability/shipping/spill regimes — and its cost-based distinction of Antarctic demilitarization, which B never overcame in A1.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side B built a much stronger and more realistic case, decisively exposing the fatal flaw in Side A's argument: an Antarctic-style treaty requires the consent of the very Arctic powers that Side A claims are too driven by profit and strategic interests to regulate themselves. Side B's distinction between the low-cost demilitarization of an empty Antarctica and the high-cost demilitarization of a populated, strategic Arctic was highly effective and largely unanswered. Side A's reliance on "collective economic isolation" to enforce a treaty against nuclear superpowers lacked grounding in political reality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's recurring rebuttal regarding feasibility and consent, particularly in the closing, which highlighted the contradiction in Side A's case: if bordering states are too captured by profit and security interests to regulate under current frameworks, they will never consent to a sweeping treaty that strips them of sovereignty, rendering the proposed treaty a "dead letter."
