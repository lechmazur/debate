### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO effectively reframed the debate early by clarifying the actual mechanism (no punishment for non-response, not a ban on sending or working late), which defused CON's opening strawman about destroying async flexibility. PRO then reinforced this with a concrete analogy to existing retaliation law (parental leave, whistleblower protections) to answer CON's core "unverifiable pressure forces blunt curfews" objection—an analogy CON never directly engaged with, instead re-asserting the same curfew/lose-lose claim nearly verbatim across three rounds. PRO also landed a sharp internal-tension critique (CON claims informal pressure is strong enough to persist yet too invisible for law to touch) that CON's "unverifiability" answer only partially resolved and never explicitly reconciled in later speeches. CON's points about cross-border collaboration and SME on-call costs were reasonable but underdeveloped and didn't meaningfully rebut PRO's follow-the-sun/paid-rotation proposal, which uses the motion's own "sector-specific exceptions" language. Both sides followed Q&A format correctly, but PRO's answers (A1/A2) more directly resolved the pressure than CON's, which repeated prior framing rather than sharpening it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 point (A4.3) exposing that CON cannot simultaneously claim informal pressure is real enough to drive replies yet too invisible for tribunals to police, paired with the unanswered retaliation-law analogy, was the most damaging unresolved exchange.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by keeping the proposition sharp: protect chosen after-hours work while barring punishment for non-response, with paid sector exceptions. It answered CON's flexibility and global-work attacks directly, used pressure questions to expose the voluntary-reply vs. shutdown-liability contradiction, and weighed incentive effects on staffing and overload more cleanly than CON's repeated curfew/paralysis warnings.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R2/closing on CON's contradiction: if replies are truly voluntary and unrewarded there is no tribunal liability forcing curfews; liability appears only when non-response is quietly punished.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won by exposing a decisive contradiction in CON's case: CON simultaneously argued that informal workplace pressure would survive the law (making it ineffective) and that risk-averse employers would enforce rigid server shutdowns (making it too restrictive). PRO showed these predictions cannot both be true—if after-hours replies are truly voluntary, there is no liability forcing shutdowns; if they are coerced, the law is precisely justified. PRO also effectively turned CON's cross-functional crisis argument into support for defined, paid on-call rotations with sector exceptions, and demonstrated that when after-hours contact carries a real cost, managers prioritize and staff more efficiently rather than simply compressing the same workload into daytime hours. CON's predictions of economic flight and operational paralysis were asserted rather than grounded, and CON never adequately explained why tribunals could handle retaliation in other labor contexts but not here.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's demonstration that CON's two central predictions—persistent informal pressure and employer-imposed total shutdowns—are mutually exclusive, which collapsed CON's flexibility critique and reframed the debate around whether after-hours contact is voluntary (no liability) or coerced (law needed).
