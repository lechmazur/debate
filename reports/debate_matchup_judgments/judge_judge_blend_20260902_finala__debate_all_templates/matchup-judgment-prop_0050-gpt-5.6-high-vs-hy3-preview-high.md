### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON (A) identified two structural cracks in PRO's case that were never fully closed: (1) a genuine tension between PRO's claim that motive/necessity is irrelevant to the levy while simultaneously justifying exempting the first flight on essentially motive-based fairness grounds, and (2) the private-jet loophole, where PRO's own framing of a "frequent-flyer levy" tied to commercial travel patterns leaves the highest emitters outside the core mechanism, with PRO's fix (separate aircraft-specific charges) conceded to be an add-on outside the proposition as written. PRO's dilemma framing (uniform tax either too weak to deter or too strong to preserve access) was rhetorically strong and never fully dismantled, and its administrative analogy to existing tax/booking systems was reasonable, but PRO's late reframing in Rebuttal 2 (baseline charge remains even on the "first flight") subtly walked back its own opening claim of "little or no additional levy" on first flights, weakening its signature fairness pitch under pressure. CON's fuel/emissions-tax alternative, tying cost to actual pollution rather than personal frequency, gave a coherent, simpler comparative frame that PRO's answers did not fully neutralize. Both closings crystallized the clash well, but CON's repeated hammering of the emissions-vs-frequency mismatch and the unresolved private-jet gap gave it the cleaner, more damaging line of attack overall.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 point [A2.1]-[A2.5], exposing the inconsistency between PRO's claim that motive doesn't matter and its own justification for exempting the first flight, combined with the private-jet loophole critique, which PRO never fully closed despite its Pressure Question and later rebuttal attempts.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON wins by exposing that the progressive frequent-flyer levy rests on a broken core metric: flight frequency does not track emissions or necessity. CON shows the levy would arbitrarily penalize low-emission necessary repeat travel (migrant family second visits, nurses’ work trips) while a wealthy traveler’s high-emission first long-haul flight could face little progressive charge. PRO never squarely resolves this; their fallback that a baseline emissions charge can remain undermines their own argument for shifting toward frequency, and their “employers can pay” and narrow exemption responses concede the hardship without solving it. CON’s alternative—pricing aviation fuel or emissions directly—offers a cleaner, fairer, and administratively simpler path that ties cost to actual pollution rather than travel count.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s answer to PRO’s Pressure Question 1 (Turn 8, A4.1): by showing a fuel tax ties cost directly to emissions per journey, so a migrant’s short trip pays less than a wealthy traveler’s long-haul first flight—directly reversing PRO’s fairness claim and exposing that the levy shields high-impact initial flights while punishing low-impact repeat travel.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO kept a live route: cumulative use justifies rising marginal rates and protects broad access vs the uniform-tax dilemma (too low to affect habitual flyers, too high to preserve occasional access). CON's strongest hits - second short vs first long-haul and private-jet gap - were materially answered in A4 when PRO clarified "shift toward" allows a modest baseline emissions charge under a progressive layer and that the levy counts persons not loyalty programs. CON in B4/B5 kept attacking a full first-flight exemption PRO had disavowed and stretched "frequent-flyer levy" into loyalty programs, leaving its frequency-vs-emissions frame without an answer to the dilemma.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1/A4.4 - PRO's clarification that the proposition doesn't require exempting flight one and that progression solves the low-vs-high uniform tax trade-off, which undercut CON's core fairness and emissions-proxy attacks.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built the more durable case by consistently pressing the core mismatch between flight *count* and actual harm or wealth: a wealthy traveler's first premium long-haul flight is undertaxed while a nurse's or contractor's fourth efficient short-haul flight is overtaxed. PRO never squarely resolved this, instead relying on "on average, frequent flyers are wealthy," which A correctly identified as an averages-versus-individuals problem. A also landed two rebuttals that went unanswered: the calendar-gaming loophole (splitting trips across December/January to dodge the annual threshold) and the point that an emissions-based charge can be levied per-jurisdiction at point of sale without needing a cross-border, cross-carrier lifetime ledger, whereas PRO's levy structurally requires exactly that kind of global identity reconciliation. PRO's answers to pressure questions were competent (one-way itinerary = one flight, PNR-based enforcement) and neutralized some implementation concerns, but PRO never returned to rebut the timing-gaming point or fully resolve the physics mismatch, and its own concession that emissions taxes can still burden a low-income single-flight traveler more than a wealthy multi-flight traveler was met by A's honest concession-plus-mitigation (income-based rebates), which held up better under scrutiny than PRO's untested claim that flight frequency alone tracks culpability. Both sides argued skillfully, but A's throughline ("tax the harm, not the stereotype") survived the exchange more intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 point that a rising per-flight cost can be gamed by shifting the "excessive" flight into the next calendar year, paired with A's Rebuttal 2 point that jurisdiction-based emissions taxation avoids the cross-border personal ledger problem inherent to a frequency levy — both went substantively unanswered by PRO.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won by demonstrating that a passenger's ordinal flight count is a structurally arbitrary proxy for both emissions and ability to pay. PRO never resolved the decisive mismatch: under their levy, a low-emission tenth short-haul trip can be taxed far more heavily than a carbon-intensive first long-haul premium journey, while hardship exemptions would require intrusive bureaucratic moral sorting. CON's alternative—taxing actual harm through emissions, distance, and cabin space, coupled with transparent income or remoteness support—provided a cleaner principle that survived the exchange largely intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's repeated exposure that the levy taxes the wrong variable, privileging a first high-emission long-haul flight over later low-emission trips, which PRO never answered beyond asserting such long-haul trips are "outliers."
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON won the core targeting clash. PRO argued frequency proxies wealth and waste, but CON showed a frequent-flyer levy taxes calendar order, not emissions, cabin, distance, or ability to pay – giving a break to a first long-haul premium trip while punishing a later short efficient or essential trip. CON’s pressure on connecting itineraries, year-reset gaming, and the need for a cross-carrier personal ledger vs per-ticket emissions data exposed implementation costs PRO hand-waved as loyalty tracking and PNR reuse. PRO’s answers dismissed nurses/separated parents as marginal and relied on speculative reputational effects, leaving the fairness and accuracy routes damaged.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s R1/R2 chain on proxy failure and ledger problem: tenth efficient short flight taxed more than first premium long-haul, averages don’t make individual liability fair, timing avoidance Dec->Jan, and that a jurisdiction can levy emissions from one ticket while frequency requires reconstructing a global annual history.
