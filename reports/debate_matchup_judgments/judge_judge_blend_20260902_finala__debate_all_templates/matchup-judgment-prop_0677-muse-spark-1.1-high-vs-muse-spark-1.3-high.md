### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly and answered pressure questions directly, but CON (A) built a more durable structural case. Its two central attacks — that "meaningful influence" cannot be defined ex ante without becoming a post-hoc litigation question, and that a disclosure notice gives a worker "a defendant, not data" since she already knows the underlying facts (she took leave, the scanner failed) without any mandated reversal mechanism — were never fully neutralized by PRO. PRO's best material (the credit-check analogy, the NYC law, the specific-error correction story) was each partially turned by CON: the credit analogy lacks an equivalent "report" to dispute, and CON showed the NYC example actually illustrates the vagueness problem rather than solving it. CON's late move — that regulators can audit via confidential registries and statistical disparities without individualized worker notice — offered a plausible alternative path to the accountability PRO wanted, and PRO's closing reply ("auditors can't test what they can't find") reasserted rather than dismantled it. PRO kept a clear moral throughline ("a right to know") and landed sharp pressure questions, but its rebuttals tended to repeat the same correction-and-usable-data framing without closing the remedy gap CON kept exploiting. That gives CON the cleaner, more resolved path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal that disclosure "adds a defendant, not data" (A2.4/A4.6), paired with its introduction of confidential audit/registry mechanisms as an alternative to individualized disclosure, most damaged PRO's practical case; PRO's closing reassertion that "auditors can't test what they can't find" never fully answered the registry alternative.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by decisively proving that a worker cannot correct an error (like an FMLA absence being held against them) if they do not know it was the reason for their termination. Side B's counter that the worker 'already knows their past behavior' failed to address this causal gap. Side A also effectively neutralized Side B's push for audits by arguing that audits inherently rely on transparency or statistical harms that occur too late.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's point in the closing that 'knowing isn't the same as knowing the machine counted it and the manager deferred' dismantled Side B's core claim that disclosure adds no usable data.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins because it successfully frames disclosure as the necessary precondition for both individual redress and systemic audit, while turning CON's "vendor proprietary" objection into an argument that unexplainable tools should not decide livelihoods. CON's alternative of regulator auditing remains underdeveloped—relying on "confidential tool registries" that lack comparable grounding—and its insistence that workers already know enough to contest errors fails to distinguish knowing a fact from knowing an automated system penalized it. PRO's rebuttals sharpen the clash more decisively, especially the proprietary turn and the audit-precondition argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's turn of CON's vendor-proprietary objection: if an employer cannot explain in plain English what counted toward a firing decision because the vendor calls it proprietary, that is an admission the tool should not be used—not a reason against disclosure.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON built a more resilient case by identifying two structural problems B never fully solved: (1) the vendor pass-through mechanism B relied on for compliance ("proprietary ML assesses fit" vendors won't cooperate) collapses into what A correctly reframed as a de facto product ban on undisclosed-logic tools, not the "minimal notice" B initially promised; and (2) a genuinely novel inversion — that mandatory disclosure creates a centralized, documented, aggregatable rule that is far easier to certify as a class action than diffuse individual human hunches, giving employers a rational incentive to retreat to unaccountable gut-feel decisions the law can't reach. B's closing asserted this litigation exposure is "accountability, not a bug" but never rebutted the causal mechanism of employers fleeing to worse opacity, leaving that route live for CON. CON's pressure questions and answers (A1 on existing regulatory audits, A2 on documentation-driven class certification) were sharper and more directly resolved the exchange than PRO's, which restated the value of disclosure without closing the loop on how workers get an actual remedy beyond confirming a fact they'd usually already suspect.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's turn in Rebuttal 2, using B's own vendor pass-through fix (A1) to show it amounts to banning tools lacking explainable logic, combined with the class-action/documentation asymmetry argument that reframes "sunlight" as a litigation magnet driving employers to less accountable human discretion.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully proving that the mandate would create perverse incentives, driving employers away from aggregatable (and potentially bias-reducing) automated tools toward completely opaque human discretion. Side B also successfully forced Side A to admit that non-compliant vendor tools would be effectively banned, undermining Side A's claim of a "minimal burden."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive, weaponizing Side A's admission that vendors must supply plain-language data or be barred from employment use to prove the policy acts as a small-business product ban, while sharply explaining how US litigation punishes documented algorithms over undocumented human hunches.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON demonstrated that the proposed disclosure standard is either unworkable—collapsing into vague boilerplate or a de facto product ban on common hiring tools—or counterproductive by turning documented algorithmic decisions into litigation magnets that incentivize employers to retreat to unaccountable human discretion, leaving workers with anxiety but no concrete remedy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's sustained rebuttal that disclosure mandates create class-action-ready documentation, chilling even bias-reducing tools and driving employers toward unloggable human bias, directly undermining PRO's sunlight thesis.
