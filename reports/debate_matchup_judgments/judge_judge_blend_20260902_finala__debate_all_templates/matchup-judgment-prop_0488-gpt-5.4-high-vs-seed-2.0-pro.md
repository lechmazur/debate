### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins on the central asymmetry argument: proof of consumer benefit sits inside the acquirer's control (integration roadmaps, cost data, engineering plans), while proof of lost competition requires demonstrating a future that never happened. CON never squarely answered this across the full exchange. CON's biggest liability was the repeated "unwound later" claim—PRO targeted it sharply in Q2, correctly noting that network-effect markets cannot restore the independent competitive trajectory, product choices, or rivalry pressure once a target is folded in. CON's A2 response (regulators can force divestiture of data and engineering teams) does not actually address the loss of the independent competitive path, only the assets; PRO's closing crystallized this gap cleanly. PRO also pre-empted and held the innovation/VC argument throughout: a startup ecosystem that optimizes exits around the dominant gatekeeper is incumbency protection, not competitive dynamism, and capital markets adapt to legal rules. CON's aggregate-value response to the benefit standard was plausible but never grounded a specific mechanism for why regulators would ignore aggregate welfare. CON's "90%" figure in the closing was ungrounded and somewhat undermined its epistemic discipline. PRO's answers to CON's pressure questions were direct and specific—naming the concrete conditions (merger-specific plans, documented interoperability, reasons partnership won't work) that would clear the presumption. CON's live routes never recovered from the asymmetry and reversibility attacks, while PRO's core burdens remained standing at the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 asking how harm can be "unwound later" once users, data, and engineers are folded into the incumbent was the most decisive exchange. CON's A2 (regulatory divestiture of data and engineering teams) failed to answer the underlying point—you cannot spin back an independent competitive trajectory—and PRO's closing drove this home directly. This exchange effectively neutralized CON's most-repeated claim and left PRO's error-cost framing dominant.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by establishing decisive evidence asymmetry and irreversibility of lost competitive constraints, while answering pressure questions directly and exposing Side A's overstatements on routine benefits and unwindability; Side A's clipped speeches and failure to neutralize the core burden shift weaken its path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's direct A1/A2 answers on benefit standards and funding adaptation, plus error-cost weighing in Rebuttal 2, neutralized Side A's main routes.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won because its central case—evidentiary asymmetry and the irreversibility of lost competitive constraints in network-effect markets—survived the full exchange. Side B never neutralized the burden-shifting rationale, and its error-cost story relied on unsupported exactitude ("90%") and an unexamined assumption that divestiture restores the original competitive trajectory. Side A also effectively rebutted the innovation claim by showing alternative exits exist and reframing dependence on gatekeeper acquisitions as a structural bug rather than a necessity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 ([A2.1]–[A2.8]), which distinguished the presumption from a ban, exposed the asymmetry between proving a lost future rival versus proving claimed benefits controlled by the acquirer, and flipped the error-cost analysis to show that false permission is more dangerous than false caution.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A wins by decisively capturing the evidence asymmetry and error-cost comparisons. A consistently demonstrates that the acquirer controls the integration roadmaps, engineering plans, and cost data needed to prove benefits, while regulators and outsiders face the structurally harder task of proving a counterfactual rival that was absorbed before maturing. A also effectively neutralizes B's "unwind later" defense by explaining that merged data, talent, and product trajectories cannot be cleanly separated, whereas a blocked deal can be restructured, justified with better evidence, or pursued via licensing. B's case relies heavily on an unsupported "90%" statistic and an overly optimistic view of post-merger divestiture in integrated digital ecosystems, which weakens its grounding. B never squarely answers why the gatekeeper should not bear the burden when it holds the relevant evidence. A's closing cleanly crystallizes this decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 sharply dismantle CON's error-cost framing. A turns the "VC chilling effect" into an indictment of gatekeeper-dependent startup finance, and contrasts the permanence of erased competitive trajectories with the flexibility of restructured or partnered deals. This comparative weighing directly severs CON's core route to victory on irreversibility and funding collapse.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): PRO built a coherent, emotionally resonant case around killer acquisitions, information asymmetry, and a clear weighing (permanent monopoly harm versus temporary missed synergies). It held up reasonably through the middle of the debate. However, CON identified two structural flaws that PRO never adequately resolved. First, the "limiting principle" attack: PRO's repeated answer that "substantial consumer benefit is the limiting principle" is circular—it just restates the motion's requirement without explaining which deals would be excluded, leaving the overbreadth problem live. Second, and more decisively, the "one-way ratchet" framing crystallized in CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing: PRO's framework treats uncertain future harms as sufficient to trigger a presumption but demands proof for uncertain future benefits, systematically biasing the rule against legitimate integration under ordinary uncertainty. PRO's answer—that benefits should be tied to trackable metrics—does not resolve this, because even specific projections are still forecasts, as CON correctly pressed. CON also landed the important point that false positives are not easily corrected: a startup that never scaled cannot be "revisited later." PRO's brittle specificity in Rebuttal 2 (exact percentages and user counts as illustration) was a minor credibility cost. CON suffered real execution penalties from two clipped speeches, which softened what might otherwise have been a clearer win, and the one-way ratchet argument arrived relatively late. Still, those two unanswered structural flaws—circular limiting principle and asymmetric treatment of uncertainty—leave CON with the cleaner live route to victory after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2 in Rebuttal 2, responding to PRO's claimed limiting principle: CON correctly identified that PRO's answer ("substantial consumer benefit is the limiting principle") simply repeats the motion without explaining what deals are actually excluded. This left the overbreadth objection standing and forced PRO's case to rest on the undefended assumption that complementary buys are "trivially" easy to clear—an assumption CON had been contesting since the opening.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON wins because it exposed the motion's overbreadth, one-way treatment of uncertainty, and weak limiting principle more cleanly than PRO could defend, with stronger direct answers to pressure questions and better crystallization of targeted alternatives vs blanket presumption.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2 directly neutralized PRO's claims on proof and scope, showing why burden shift favors speculation against consumers.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing that Side A&#39;s presumption is structurally overbroad and asymmetric: it treats uncertain future harms as sufficient to block deals while demanding proof of uncertain future benefits, creating a one-way ratchet against beneficial integration. B showed that the motion&#39;s &quot;substantial consumer benefit&quot; requirement is no limiting principle for the category of covered deals—it merely restates the test—so the rule sweeps in complementary acquisitions whose value is real but unprovable ex ante. B offered a clearer alternative (targeted review of nascent-rival and overlapping deals) and successfully rebutted A&#39;s weighing by showing false positives permanently kill startups and features rather than merely delaying them. A&#39;s reliance on Instagram and information asymmetry never overcame the foundational flaw that shifting the burden systematically punishes legitimate innovation under uncertainty.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B&#39;s Rebuttal 2, which exposed that Side A&#39;s &quot;limiting principle&quot; merely restated the motion text and that treating internal forecasts as &quot;proof&quot; for benefits while treating speculative harms as presumption-triggers created a structurally biased, one-way ratchet against acquisition.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively exposing the mechanical flaw in PRO's case: the motion demands ex ante "proof" of consumer benefits that are inherently uncertain until after integration, creating a one-way ratchet that treats uncertainty as guilt. CON's error-cost framing, startup exit-path analysis, and targeted-review alternative consistently outmaneuvered PRO's rigid reliance on hindsight examples and the repeatedly asserted but unsupported claim that proving substantial benefit is "trivial." PRO never adequately answered the overbreadth charge or explained how internal forecasts magically become legal proof, leaving CON's comparative case intact. Minor execution costs for CON's clipped turns do not offset the clear argumentative advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 effectively dismantled PRO's proposed limiting principle by showing that pre-merger metrics are still just forecasts, not proof. This turned PRO's standard into a stacked deck: speculative harms justify blocking deals, but speculative benefits lose by default. CON then cleanly pivoted to a narrower, evidence-based alternative that addresses PRO's entrenchment concerns without the motion's overbreadth.
