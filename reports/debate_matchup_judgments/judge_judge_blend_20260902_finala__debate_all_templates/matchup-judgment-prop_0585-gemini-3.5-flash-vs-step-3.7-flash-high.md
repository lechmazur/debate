A never resolved this tension cleanly. B also answered the pressure questions more directly than A, whose answers to B's two pressure questions were notably deflective, particularly the dodge on firm capacity during 2021-style events. Neither side used false precision or invented citations; both relied on plausible general knowledge. The margin is clear but not dominant because A's physical sequencing logic and efficiency arguments were genuine and partially unanswered.

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): PRO (Side A) wins a close but real debate on the strength of two unanswered structural advantages. First, the geographic curtailment argument is the clearest clean comparative win: rural curtailed wind still requires transmission to reach load centers even after local LDES storage, meaning LDES subsidies without transmission leaves stored energy stranded—CON never squarely addresses this. Second, PRO's regional market integration point partially neutralizes the permitting objection: SPP's market software delivered curtailment reductions and consumer savings before new physical lines were built, which directly undercuts CON's "decade-long bottleneck" framing. CON's strongest live argument—temporal resilience through advance charging before a storm—is genuinely compelling and PRO's repeated "nothing to store during a dunkelflaute" response is an inferior version of it (answering the wrong version of the argument); the real answer, geographic decorrelation of renewables across large areas, is only partially developed by PRO. CON's "redistributes scarcity" frame is rhetorically memorable but doesn't neutralize PRO's core geographic-curtailment claim. On permitting, CON correctly notes modular LDES avoids interstate siting fights, but PRO correctly notes utility-scale LDES still faces significant local permitting and interconnection hurdles. Both sides commit mild epistemic overreach—PRO's "post-mortem found significant unused capacity" is asserted without grounding; CON's "$20/kWh" target is suspiciously specific—but these roughly cancel. Progression across rounds favors PRO slightly: later speeches sharpen and narrow the comparison rather than widen it, and the closing crystallizes the geographic-versus-temporal framing clearly. The decisive margin is that PRO's primary investment comparison holds up better under the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's point that local LDES storing curtailed rural wind still leaves the energy stranded without transmission to reach load centers—meaning LDES subsidies and transmission solve genuinely different problems, and the geographic problem is the dominant daily constraint—was the most structurally damaging rebuttal CON never adequately answered.
