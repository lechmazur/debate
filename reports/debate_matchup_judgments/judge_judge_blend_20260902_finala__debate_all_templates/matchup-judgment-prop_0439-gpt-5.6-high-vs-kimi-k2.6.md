### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): PRO built a clean prima facie case (prohibition drives crime underground, dialysis vs transplant life-years, regulated oversight replaces broker predation), but its causal chain leaned heavily on unquantified assertions ("vast pool," "bulk of transactions," "a fraction" of black-market residue) that it never grounded in mechanism. CON's central move—the screening dilemma (strict eligibility rules leave the most desperate/risky sellers and rejected buyers to the black market, while loosening rules to absorb them imports coercion and concealment into the legal system)—was a genuine structural trap that PRO never resolved beyond reasserting labels. CON also sharpened the life-years comparison by distinguishing average recipient benefit from marginal payment-induced benefit and by insisting donor-side losses, crowd-out, and concealment-driven graft failure be counted rather than zeroed out. PRO's "compare to reality not utopia" framing was rhetorically effective but did not answer why aggregate harm falls rather than merely relocates. CON's case was more analytically rigorous and better exploited the exchange, though its closing being clipped is a real execution cost that keeps the margin from being larger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's screening-dilemma argument (B2.1, B4.2-B4.3: strict standards leave a concentrated underground pool while loose standards launder coercion into paperwork) was the pivotal exchange; PRO's replies (A4.1-A4.2) never offered a mechanism showing substitution dominates expansion, relying instead on unsupported "fraction/bulk" framing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): A won by consistently framing the motion as a comparative judgment against today's prohibition (not utopia), showing how regulation moves most transactions out of criminal hands and yields large recipient life-year gains while improving donor conditions relative to the black market. B landed strong challenges on substitution vs expansion, the screening dilemma, and incomplete net accounting, but its alternatives and perfectionist demands did not neutralize A's cleaner path on the proposition as written; the clipped closing was an execution cost that left the final clash less resolved for B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's screening dilemma (B2.3): legalization induces more desperate sellers while screening either rejects them (leaving them for brokers) or legitimizes coercion—directly pressuring A's substitution and agency claims.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): PRO (B) wins on burden and comparison. It correctly framed the motion as reduction vs today's prohibition, not abolition/perfection. Its mechanism — secrecy costs for brokers, bundling safety/follow-up making legal value > cash, and public purchase allocating by clinical need — answers CON's outbid/rejected-buyer critique. On life-years, it weighed large recipient gains against fraction-of-percent donor risk and improved donor safety vs black market. CON's strong screening-dilemma / induced-seller points show residual risk but never proves net black-market harms increase or net life-years fall, and its closing was clipped, costing execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.2 answering Q1: reduction not abolition, secrecy costs prevent simple outbidding, legal bundle of payment + screening + follow-up shifts bulk of transactions into light, leaving only fringe illicit.
