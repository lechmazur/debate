### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A won by building a cleaner, more adaptive case across the full exchange. The decisive move was the "smuggling" reframe in Rebuttal 2: CON consistently compared the worst version of structure (blind compliance scripts, punitive fidelity audits) against the best version of autonomy (expert diagnostic teachers who happen to know reading science). A exposed this asymmetry clearly and repeatedly, and CON never refuted it. A's mastery-based vs. calendar-based distinction directly answered both of B's pressure questions and gave a concrete policy mechanism, whereas B's answer to A's Q1 (what prevents omission of systematic decoding under autonomy?) was vague—"professional accountability and training"—which is exactly the hope-rather-than-guarantee problem A had identified from the opening. A's risk asymmetry argument also held up: structure failures are diagnosable and correctable by shifting to mastery-based audit; autonomy failures may not surface until the child is already behind. B's multilingual learner argument had real bite and was the strongest running theme—the fidelity audit concern is genuinely realistic—but A consistently answered that explicit instruction with adaptation IS the appropriate response, and that bad auditing is bad implementation rather than an indictment of structure itself. B's closing "administrative illusion" claim was asserted without engagement with A's mastery-based distinction that was already live on the table. Three of A's speeches were clipped, a modest execution cost, but the core arguments were complete before each clip. Overall, A made the debate easier to decide and left fewer live burdens unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 crystallization: "CON's entire strategy is to smuggle in the worst version of structure and the best version of autonomy. Policy cannot be built on flattering assumptions about every classroom." This single move neutralized most of B's running argument and shifted the burden directly onto CON to defend a policy that works for weak as well as excellent teachers—a burden B never squarely met.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B built the stronger case by defending mastery-based structure as a non-rigid floor that still permits adaptation, directly answering pressure questions on mechanisms and multilingual learners, and weighing the irreversible cost of missing core instruction. Side A repeatedly reframed structure as inevitably scripted compliance without neutralizing that comparison or the equity lottery argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 directly answered the pressure questions while sharpening the risk comparison between missing instruction entirely versus poor implementation of structure.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B successfully reframed structured instruction as a mastery-based floor rather than blind scripting, showing that prioritization guarantees every child systematic access to the code while still permitting teacher adaptation. Side A landed real blows on administrative reality and multilingual learners but never fully answered how autonomy alone prevents teachers from omitting decoding instruction, relying instead on training and hope. Side B's risk comparison—correctable implementation flaws versus undiscovered omission—proved decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 distinction between calendar-based and mastery-based fidelity, and the charge that CON smuggles the worst version of structure against the best version of autonomy, dissolved CON's central implementation attack and flipped the equity comparison.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): CON built the cleaner path to victory through a consistent "floor vs. hierarchy" distinction: accepting mandatory phonics screening and materials standards as a non-negotiable floor while contesting the proposition's specific claim that structured instruction should be *prioritized over* classroom autonomy in curriculum design. PRO never escaped the tension this framing created. The most decisive moment came in B's Rebuttal 2, where CON correctly identified that PRO's own A1 answer—conceding teachers may adjust time allocation, supplementary texts, and small-group interventions—conceded genuine curriculum design authority, so PRO was either accepting meaningful autonomy (CON wins) or defending rigidity that harms the same vulnerable students PRO invoked. PRO's strongest counter, the cultural-drift argument (guardrails fail because teachers revert to intuition), was plausible and somewhat underanswered by CON, but PRO repeated it across multiple rounds without developing the causal mechanism further or earning the "inevitably" framing CON correctly flagged. PRO's equity argument was strong in the opening but lost comparative force once CON established that responsiveness is also what vulnerable students need after the decoding floor is met. CON's three clipping costs are real execution penalties and kept the margin from being larger, but what was visible was argumentatively tighter and better adapted to the evolving clash. PRO was clean and disciplined across the rounds but relied on repetition rather than sharpening its decisive comparative claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.4) identifying that PRO's own A1 concession—teachers may adjust time allocation, supplementary texts, and small-group interventions—constitutes curriculum design decisions, trapping PRO in a dilemma: either meaningful autonomy over those decisions remains (CON wins the motion) or PRO defends the rigidity that CON warned about from the start.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): CON wins because it directly addressed the motion's hierarchy (structure over autonomy), accepted necessary floors on phonics while showing PRO's concessions on teacher design decisions (time, texts, interventions), and neutralized the "inevitable drift" claim without new evidence. PRO justified structure but never proved why autonomy must be subordinated once floors are met.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A1/A2 answers to B's pressure questions exposed that PRO allows meaningful design adaptations, conceding the core point on autonomy.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): CON won by exposing that PRO defended a floor of phonics instruction rather than the hierarchy demanded by the motion. CON accepted structured decoding, screening, and bans on harmful methods, but showed that prioritizing structured instruction over classroom autonomy means subordinating teacher judgment on pacing, texts, grouping, and intervention design to a central sequence. PRO's own answers conceded that teachers may adjust time allocation, supplementary texts, and small-group interventions—meaningful curriculum design decisions that PRO could not simultaneously allow and claim to have eliminated autonomy. CON's "floor is not a hierarchy" framing remained live because PRO never proved that trained teachers operating within clear literacy standards inevitably abandon phonics, and because rigidity harms vulnerable students who need responsiveness beyond decoding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (Turn 8) demonstrating that PRO's concessions on teacher adjustments to time, texts, and interventions were themselves curriculum design decisions, paired with the "floor is not a hierarchy, guardrail is not a script" framing that PRO never answered without either conceding real autonomy or defending harmful rigidity.
