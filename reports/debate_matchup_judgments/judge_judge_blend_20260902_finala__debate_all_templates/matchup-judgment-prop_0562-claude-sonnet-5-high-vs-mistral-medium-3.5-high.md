### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won the substantive comparison: cash bail sorts by wealth rather than risk, while supervised release directly addresses appearance and safety. Side B’s funding objection was sharply presented but treated the motion’s failure to specify appropriations as fatal, even though relying on supervised release reasonably entails implementing it. B also never adequately answered why retaining cash bail is preferable when wealthy high-risk defendants can pay and poor low-risk defendants cannot. Both sides used overly exact, weakly grounded figures and implementation claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s A4.2 most decisively answered the discretion attack: “most” permits higher-risk exceptions while still making supervised release the default, whereas B’s alternative preserves wealth as a determinant of freedom.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by making the live clash the resolution text itself versus the intensive infrastructure PRO needed for every claimed success metric. PRO’s wealth-test path stayed emotionally strong but could not close the practicality gap once CON repeatedly stripped the aspirational gloss. Clear comparative advantage to CON after full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R1 and R2 hammering that PRO concedes Cadillac features while the text delivers only an unfunded mandate (NY as proof).
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively attacking the implementation mechanism of the proposition. While Side A presents a strong moral case against the wealth test of cash bail and cites successful programs in D.C. and NYC, Side B effectively argues that the resolution's text does not guarantee the intensive, expensive infrastructure required to make those programs work. Side B's use of the 2019 New York bail reform as a real-world example of this exact policy failing due to underfunding is highly persuasive and directly neutralizes Side A's empirical evidence. Side B also capitalizes on Side A's concessions, pointing out that if the word "most" preserves judicial discretion, Side A is essentially defending Side B's alternative, and if it doesn't, it is a blanket elimination that ignores individualized risk. Side A's argument that funding will naturally be reallocated from pretrial detention budgets is adequately dismantled by Side B's explanation of legislative appropriations. Side B's "Cadillac vs. bicycle" framing is memorable and effectively crystallizes the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where they turn Side A's admission that supervised release requires intensive infrastructure against them, highlighting that the resolution's text mandates no such funding, and using the New York example to prove that mandates without appropriations fail in the real world.
