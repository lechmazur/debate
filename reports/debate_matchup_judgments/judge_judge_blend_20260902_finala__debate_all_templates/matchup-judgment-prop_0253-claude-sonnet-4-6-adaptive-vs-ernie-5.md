### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.0 (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by exposing a fatal contradiction in Side A's case: Side A argued both that consumers have all the information they need via static unit prices, and that manufacturers use shrinkflation precisely because consumers don't notice the unit prices and only look at headline prices. Side B used the Pressure Questions to spring this trap and crystallized it perfectly in the closing, establishing a clear market failure that justifies the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing correctly identifying Side A's concession in Rebuttal 2 (A4.1) as proving the PRO case.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.0 (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B built a stronger case by highlighting the information gap on changes rather than static data, effectively using pressure questions and exploiting CON's concession on consumer behavior to show why disclosure is needed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal to pressure questions directly addressed manufacturer's incentives and turned CON's compliance argument against them.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.0 (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by proving that shrinkflation exploits a salience gap, not merely an information gap. CON’s best objection—that unit prices are already displayed—collapsed when CON admitted in Rebuttal 2 that consumers psychologically prioritize headline prices over fine-print unit costs. PRO correctly identified this as a concession that the current format fails, and leveraged the Pressure Questions round to expose why retailers never adopt such disclosure voluntarily: transparency about size cuts functions as an advertisement of a price increase, creating a textbook market failure that mandates can fix without price controls. CON’s compliance-cost and vagueness arguments were plausible but insufficient to overcome the central concession that opacity is commercially profitable and that shoppers are systematically nudged away from noticing effective price hikes. A notable execution cost for CON was misattributing its own prior rebuttal [A2.4] to PRO in Rebuttal 2, weakening its attempt to escape PRO’s paternalism trap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Pressure Questions (B3) and CON’s mishandled answers (A4), particularly A4.1’s admission that consumers react more strongly to headline prices than unit prices even when the latter are visible, which PRO’s closing (B5) converted into a decisive concession that the current disclosure regime is practically inadequate.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.0 (CON)): Side A decisively won the core clash by distinguishing static unit pricing from disclosure of changes, and by turning Side B's admission about consumer psychology into proof that current labeling is designed to obscure true costs. Side A's rebuttals were tactically superior, especially the turn on voluntary adoption, and the closing crystallized the debate cleanly. Side B repeated that information is already available but never explained why manufacturers strategically exploit presentation gaps if the system already works.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's answer to Side B's Q2, arguing that retailers avoid voluntary shrinkflation disclosure because transparency functions as an advertisement for a price increase, proving the information gap is profitable and thus requires mandatory intervention.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.0 (PRO)): Side A won by successfully reframing the debate around enforcement and practical outcomes. When Side B admitted that existing unit price tags often fail to update, Side A decisively used this concession to argue that adding a new disclosure mandate on top of unenforced existing laws would just create a second broken rule. Side A also effectively neutralized the core harms by pointing out that the mandate would simply cause firms to raise visible prices, shifting the disguise without saving consumers money.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which brilliantly weaponized Side B's admission about failing shelf tags to prove that the core issue is lax enforcement of existing laws, not a need for new ones.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.0 (PRO)): Side A wins: it squarely neutralized PRO’s core claims by exposing that existing unit-price mandates already cover the information (with any failure being an enforcement issue, not a disclosure gap) and that the policy merely redirects rather than reduces consumer costs. PRO repeatedly conceded both points without rebuttal and failed to answer the enforcement-migration pressure. Side B offered repetitive general assertions and symbolic framing but never rebuilt a live comparative advantage after those concessions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1–A2.7 and A4.1–A4.6, which used PRO’s own admissions on migration and enforcement failure to show the mandate adds only redundant symbolism on a broken foundation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.0 (PRO)): CON won by showing that PRO's mandate was built on a cracked foundation. CON demonstrated that existing unit-pricing laws already capture the relevant information, and PRO's own admission that shelf tags often fail to update unit prices proved the problem was enforcement, not the absence of rules. Adding a separate shrinkflation disclosure on top of poorly enforced existing law would burden retailers without fixing compliance. CON also converted PRO's concession that firms would simply raise sticker prices instead of shrinking packages into a decisive migration argument: the mandate redistributes the same consumer burden into a more visible format rather than preventing it. PRO's case for transparency was coherent but never overcame the redundancy and enforcement problems CON crystallized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's first rebuttal catching PRO's admission at [B2.4] that "a visible price hike is still a price hike" and weaponizing it into the migration argument—that the mandate merely changes how cost increases are disguised rather than stopping consumer harm—which CON developed and crystallized across subsequent rounds.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.0 (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by effectively turning Side A's concessions into decisive liabilities. When A admitted that existing unit-price tags often fail to update and that firms would simply migrate to visible price hikes, B successfully framed the mandate as a symbolic layer built on a cracked enforcement foundation. A relied heavily on the normative value of transparency but never adequately answered why a new disclosure rule would succeed where current unit-pricing laws allegedly fail. B's comparative weighing on practical efficacy versus symbolic regulation was sharper, more responsive to pressure, and ultimately more persuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing masterfully leveraged A's admission that shelf tags don't update to argue that adding a new label atop poorly enforced existing rules is futile. B's framing of the mandate as merely converting hidden hikes into visible ones without solving the underlying compliance gap decisively narrowed the clash and exposed A's case as symbolic rather than substantive.
