### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A (PRO) built a stronger and more durable path to victory. Its core framing—that the decisive question is biological existence of a cure versus financial cost—was established early and never squarely neutralized. The "biological absolute vs. logistical hurdle" crystallization in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing ("perfectly equitable access to absolutely nothing") gave the argument memorable staying power and clear comparative weight. Side B's principal counter-move was the claim that alternative commitment structures (fixed-sum or milestone payments) could stimulate the pipeline without locking in high unit prices. This alternative was introduced in Rebuttal 1 and repeated through the Closing, but it was never adequately developed: CON never explained why a fixed-sum model would attract capital that had already fled the antibiotic market, why it hasn't been implemented, or how it avoids the same stewardship trade-offs. PRO's pressure questions directly exploited this gap, asking how a hypothetical alternative helps patients dying now, and CON's responses offered "diagnostics and infection prevention" as interim measures—a weak answer to a real pipeline collapse. PRO also handled CON's strongest point (global access fragmentation) more competently by pointing to AMC contract conditions that mandate tiered pricing and voluntary licensing, making the access problem a downstream manageable hurdle rather than a reason to kill the incentive mechanism altogether. CON's rebuttal to this (firms will treat AMC prices as a revenue floor) was plausible but speculative, and PRO's comparison—imperfect access to an existing drug beats perfect access to a nonexistent one—remained the stronger comparative throughout. Both sides maintained good epistemic discipline without fake citations. PRO simply executed sharper pressure questions, crisper rebuttals, and a more decisive closing crystallization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4), particularly the A1/A2 answers plus the framing that followed. PRO directly answered CON's most pointed questions, showed how AMC contract conditions can mandate tiered pricing (addressing the revenue-floor worry), and then pivoted to the cleanest comparative of the debate: a high unit price is a solvable economic hurdle; a dead pipeline is a biological absolute. This moved the clash from abstract access concerns to a concrete comparison CON never recovered from.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): A won by making the central comparison clearer: high-price AMCs may create access and stewardship problems, but no new antibiotic is a worse and more absolute failure. B's best answer was that fixed-sum or delinked commitments could stimulate innovation without high unit prices, but this remained more asserted than demonstrated and did not fully overcome A's point that governments can write AMC conditions for tiered pricing, licensing, and targeted pathogens. B exposed real risks, but A gave the cleaner reason to permit the tool rather than prohibit it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange was the response to the access objection: you cannot distribute a cure that was never invented, and contract terms can require tiered pricing or licensing. That blunted B's global-access route while keeping A's innovation route live.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B wins by establishing that the structural market failure in antibiotics—where necessary stewardship suppresses sales volume—makes high-price AMCs essential to secure any new drugs at all. Side A raised legitimate global access concerns, but never overcame Side B's core double-bind: rejecting high-price AMCs does not produce a cheaper alternative, it produces zero new antibiotics. Side B effectively answered Side A's pressure on tiered pricing by noting that governments can contractually mandate discounts and licensing. Side A's alternative of fixed-sum commitments remained underdeveloped and speculative, failing to show why capital would return to antibiotics under such models. Side B's framing of an expensive lifeline versus no lifeline proved the decisive comparative impact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2 successfully reframed the access debate by demonstrating that zero innovation is the ultimate barrier to access, and that a dead pipeline is a biological absolute while high prices are a solvable economic hurdle. This neutralized Side A's central access advantage and exposed the fatal trade-off in CON's position.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO's core market-failure argument — that the antibiotic pipeline is broken because no downstream revenue signal exists, and AMCs uniquely supply that signal — was never squarely neutralized across the debate. CON's main counter-offer, redirecting funds to "push" mechanisms such as direct public R&D and non-profit partnerships, was asserted rather than structurally argued: CON's answer to PRO's sharp pressure question (why haven't push mechanisms already worked?) reduced to "scale, not structure," which does not explain what design change would succeed where decades of grants have not. That left CON's alternative resting on a generic claim rather than a live mechanism, and PRO correctly exploited this throughout. CON did land real damage. The identification in B2.1 of the internal contradiction — PRO originally said developers recoup through "committed volumes," then pivoted to "payment decoupled from utilization" — was the sharpest rebuttal exchange of the debate. PRO's recovery (approval-based payment for a reserved stock) was plausible but represented a meaningful retreat from the opening framing, and CON correctly pressed that the motion specifies "unit prices remain high," which tethers financial reward to doses in any realistic transaction. CON's mathematical argument in B4.5 — that expenditure caps combined with high unit prices necessarily produce a tiny global stock, guaranteeing rationing — also had genuine force that PRO's Rebuttal 2 (notably brief at 190 words against a 340-word cap) did not fully resolve. CON's equity argument thus retained life throughout. The margin stays narrow because PRO's foundational comparative — no drugs at all for anyone is worse than expensive drugs with stewardship controls, and AMCs are the only mechanism that has reliably attracted private high-risk investment — remained cleaner and more directly responsive to the proposition as written than CON's underdeveloped alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's B2.1 identification that PRO had contradicted itself by claiming AMCs recoup costs through "committed volumes" while simultaneously arguing payment can be "separated from utilization." PRO's design-based response (approval-triggered stock reservation) was the pivot of the debate: plausible enough to survive, but it conceded CON's structural point and shifted PRO onto narrower ground for the rest of the round.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by keeping the debate centered on the antibiotic market failure: low expected sales make private development irrational, so a pull mechanism with high prices can be justified if paired with stewardship, capped spending, and approval/reserve-based rewards. Side B’s equity and rationing objections were serious and rhetorically strong, but they depended too heavily on the claim that high unit prices necessarily force consumption-driven volume incentives. Side A plausibly answered that governments can buy and reserve limited stock without encouraging use, and can condition rewards on novelty or availability. Side B’s push-funding alternative was plausible but more speculative and did not fully answer why permitting AMCs with safeguards should be rejected rather than combined with other tools.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to the stewardship attack was decisive: it separated financial reward from actual clinical use through reserve stock, approval-based payment, and stewardship limits, weakening Side B’s claim that AMCs inherently accelerate resistance.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON won by internalizing PRO's own concessions to expose fatal trade-offs in the proposition. PRO acknowledged that expenditure caps and limited stock were necessary to make high-unit-price AMCs fiscally tolerable, but CON demonstrated that high price plus capped spending mathematically guarantees tiny procurement volumes. This means wealthy nations hoard reserve stock while LMICs—where resistance burdens are highest—are priced out of access. CON also effectively weaponized PRO's concession that AMCs may yield only "modest improvements," showing the model drains public funds for marginal me-too drugs rather than novel classes. PRO's market-failure narrative remained plausible, but could not overcome the demonstrated reality that the proposition structurally monetizes a global health crisis and rations cures by wealth.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 argument that combining high unit prices with expenditure caps necessarily produces tiny procurement volumes, guaranteeing extreme global rationing and hoarding by wealthy nations while LMICs face treatable deaths from sepsis [A4.5-A4.6].
