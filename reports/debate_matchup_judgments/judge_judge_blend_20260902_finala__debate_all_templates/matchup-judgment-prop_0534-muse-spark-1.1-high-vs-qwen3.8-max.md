PRO's closing caught this cleanly, showing CON's proposed policy space collapses into PRO's framework rather than genuinely rejecting it. - **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side A won by showing that Side B's preventive vision either collapsed into the off-ramp regulation A already endorsed, or required banning open-source code itself to be effective. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): The decisive clash was whether self-custody is "mere possession" (PRO's key/safe analogy) or "transmission capacity" that is itself the harmful act (CON's framing). CON's central mechanism—irreversibility plus identity erasure making post-hoc evidentiary ties impossible before harm is locked in—was pressed consistently and sharpened across rounds, and PRO's answers (targeted investigation, KYC off-ramps, seized phones) implicitly conceded that enforcement depends on criminals touching custodial choke points, which CON explicitly flagged as the loophole: pure self-custody with mixers evades exactly that. PRO's rebuttal that "difficulty is not impunity" is a reasonable general principle, but never squarely resolved CON's sharper point that with self-custody the harm is irreversible before any nexus can be established, unlike cash or phones which don't combine unlimited value + instant settlement + anonymity. PRO's pressure questions were answered reasonably (ordinary policing, courts deciding exemptions), but CON's counter-question about establishing knowledge/intent without pattern monitoring was never fully defused by PRO, who fell back on "some cases are hard" without explaining how due process operates when the transfer is already irreversible. CON's closing crystallized this timing argument most cleanly: "You cannot have due process after irreversibility." PRO's case remained strong on autonomy and slippery-slope concerns about state-granted exemptions, and scored well on the dissident/survivor examples, but never fully rebutted the core mechanism CON built around irreversibility as the defining, non-analogous feature of self-custody.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal 2 (Turn 8) answering A1/A2: pinpointing that PRO's own definition of "direct tie" (control + knowingly moving funds through a mixer) requires exactly the pattern monitoring PRO rejected, and that a warrant after irreversible settlement is "theater, not prevention" — this exposed a real tension in PRO's framework that was never fully resolved.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the decisive burden clean: default legality until a specific criminal nexus, with enforcement via ordinary targeted policing rather than prior licensing of possession. B’s strongest live route—irreversibility plus the unique combo of global instant settlement and identity erasure—was real and well pressed, but never fully escaped turning every key holder into a presumptive unlicensed transmitter, or solving the exemption problem without recreating the state gatekeeping A showed self-custody exists to avoid. A answered pressure more directly, crystallized the capacity-vs-conduct clash better, and made the vote easier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s A4.1–A4.5 answers to B’s probable-cause/direct-tie pressure: ordinary post-crime leads vs universal pre-licensing, turning B’s hard-cases into punishment of capacity.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A won by defending the principled line between possession and criminal conduct. Side B landed strong points about irreversibility but never overcame the fact that self-custody is personal key storage, not third-party transmission. A’s rebuttal cleanly resolved the pressure on enforcement by showing targeted investigation remains possible without universal licensing, and B’s concession that lawful users could rely on state-granted exemptions ceded the moral high ground—turning constitutional rights into discretionary privileges. B’s closing recycled irreversibility without answering A’s due-process framing or the cash analogy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal answering B’s pressure questions (A4.1–A4.3), distinguishing targeted blockchain analysis from universal pattern surveillance and defining “direct tie” as an evidentiary link that supports warrants and forfeiture without requiring pre-crime licensure.
