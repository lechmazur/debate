### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger cumulative path to victory. Its "bait-and-switch" framing was compelling and never neutralized: platforms use originals to attract subscribers, then erase them for accounting reasons, which is the precise harm a minimum-window rule addresses. PRO's most effective move was turning CON's own claim—"streaming is a distribution channel, not an archive"—into an admission that for many originals the platform is the only realistic access point (B2.6), a point CON never squarely answered. CON's archive/library defence was then largely conceded in A4.3, where CON admitted those copies are not practically accessible to average viewers; PRO's closing (B5.1) crystallized that concession with precision, collapsing CON's cultural-preservation counter. CON's cost argument (A4.1, A5.2) relied on brittle fake precision—specific CDN per-GB rates, per-episode dollar figures, and "industry estimates" presented as verified—which undercut its credibility under the epistemic-discipline standard and was never grounded in checkable knowledge. PRO's proportionality response (storage cost for a finished title is trivially small compared with production cost; you can stop promoting without deleting) remained live throughout. CON's strongest surviving point—tax-credit window interference—was answered adequately in B4.3 (prospective compliance, reform of perverse incentives), though not perfectly. On balance, PRO had the cleaner live route: CON conceded the disappearance is real, conceded archives don't substitute for public access, and answered the harm only by asserting accounting flexibility—exactly the tradeoff PRO said regulation should override.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pivot at B2.6, turning CON's "streaming is a distribution channel, not an archive" against them to establish that the streaming platform is often the only practical access point for many originals. This set up the decisive chain: CON later conceded in A4.3 that archive and library copies are not realistically accessible to average viewers, and PRO's closing (B5.1) then used that admission to demolish CON's preservation defence entirely.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side A won because it exposed and capitalized on a decisive concession in the preservation clash. Side B’s pressure-question answer in Rebuttal 2 admitted that library and archival copies do not constitute public availability for ordinary viewers and that removed titles become effectively inaccessible to typical consumers. This validated Side A’s core harm claim and undercut Side B’s earlier argument that robust preservation alternatives exist. Side A also successfully dismantled Side B’s economic argument by showing that catalog hosting costs are not the same as production budgets, while Side B relied on brittle fake precision (specific per-episode dollar costs presented with unwarranted certainty in a closed-book setting) that the instructions discount. Side A’s framing of the rule as a modest, prospective consumer-protection measure against bait-and-switch remained intact, whereas Side B’s case collapsed into the admission that the status quo often leaves ordinary viewers with no legal access.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Pressure Question Q2 asked whether library or archive copies meant ordinary viewers could realistically stream removed originals. Side B’s answer in Rebuttal 2 (B4.3) conceded that they cannot, effectively admitting the harm Side A identified is real and that Side B’s preservation alternative fails the motion’s public-availability standard.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully defining the clash around "public availability" and forcing Side A to concede that archives and physical media do not provide ordinary viewers with real access. This validated PRO's core harm of cultural erasure and subscriber bait-and-switch. On economics, PRO effectively framed hosting costs as a minor, prospective business obligation, while CON's detailed CDN cost breakdown felt brittle and failed to prove that keeping back-catalog titles online materially chokes new production. PRO's rebuttals were sharper, directly answered pressure questions, and the closing cleanly crystallized why CON's concessions handed PRO the round.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which directly answered CON's pressure questions with a clean prospective-compliance framework and ruthlessly capitalized on CON's admission that library/archive access does not constitute true public availability for average viewers.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): Side A (CON) built a durable and consistently answered case around one decisive framing: the mandate bites hardest exactly where costs are highest and upside lowest—rights-complex and weak-performing titles—thereby chilling future investment and reducing the very ambition PRO claims to protect. This ex-ante vs. ex-post distinction was introduced early, pressed through questions, and maintained into the closing without being squarely neutralized. Side A's pressure questions were sharper than Side B's: Q2 in particular exposed the core contradiction in PRO's logic (if retention is profitable, platforms already do it; the mandate only forces retention where economics are bad). PRO never adequately answered this. Side B's two strongest claims—consumer trust/churn and cultural preservation—had real merit, but CON correctly showed that market discipline already polices abrupt removals and that a deposit/archive regime is a more targeted preservation tool. Side B's pivot to streaming as a "democratic right" was an overreach that weakened its credibility. The "30% earnings" claim in B2.2 was suspiciously precise for a closed-book debate, and the speculative "long-tail value will emerge" response to CON's pressure was weaker than CON's revealed-preference counterargument. Side B's closing largely recycled earlier points without crystallizing the clash. Side A's closing cleanly resolved the comparison: saving a subset of disappearing titles is smaller than preventing the next slate of ambitious originals from being greenlit at all.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to the pressure questions (A4.1–A4.6) was the most decisive exchange. After B tried to reframe storage as the real cost, A clarified that mandated public exhibition—not storage—is the burden, because it triggers ongoing rights renewals, residuals, and compliance obligations. A then turned B's own long-tail argument against it: if speculative future value justified retention, platforms already have incentive to keep or relicense the title. The mandate therefore forces retention precisely where the economics are worst, making the policy self-defeating as a tool for encouraging ambitious originals.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): CON won by exposing a fatal asymmetry in PRO's case: a retention mandate only binds on titles that platforms would otherwise remove—disproportionately weak performers and rights-heavy projects—while converting optional variable costs (residuals, music clearances, territorial compliance) into compulsory liabilities. PRO never convincingly answered why platforms would greenlight more ambitious, rights-complex originals after losing the flexibility to pull them, and their reliance on speculative long-tail revenue could not overcome CON's tighter ex ante/ex post framing. The upstream chilling effect on commissioning outweighs the downstream benefit of keeping a subset of marginal titles available.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's first rebuttal dismantling PRO's cost argument by showing that mandated public availability imposes ongoing rights, residual, and compliance burdens—not merely cheap storage—and that the rule therefore bites hardest on the very weak or rights-complex titles PRO claimed it would help.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = GPT-OSS-120B (PRO)): CON wins by decisively dismantling PRO's core economic assumption. PRO repeatedly claimed the cost of retention is merely "marginal storage and bandwidth," completely ignoring the industry reality of ongoing residuals, music rights renewals, territorial compliance, and legal exposure. CON consistently exposed this flaw, arguing that mandated public availability converts variable, rights-dependent costs into compulsory liabilities, which would chill upstream greenlighting of ambitious or rights-heavy projects. PRO's pressure questions were also severely misdirected, essentially asking CON to defend CON's own arguments, which CON neatly parried. CON's ex-ante vs. ex-post framing and clear separation of archival preservation from continuous public streaming provided a much stronger, more grounded path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 directly corrected PRO's confused pressure questions and crystallized the decisive economic clash: the mandate bites hardest on weak or rights-complex titles where upside is lowest, proving the policy would backfire on content creation rather than protect it.
