### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO built and sustained the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Its central structural argument — that disclosure is the precondition for the legal remedy CON itself defends — was introduced early, sharpened by pressure questions, and never squarely neutralized. CON's answer to Q2 (what forces employers to self-audit?) was the debate's weakest moment: liability risk only disciplines employers after workers successfully complete the legal process that requires the very knowledge CON opposes disclosing — a circular dependency PRO correctly identified. CON's Goodhart's Law argument was vivid but PRO turned it effectively: a system that rewards hanging up on complex calls is defective whether or not workers know it; disclosure exposes the flaw rather than creating it, and CON never answered who forces correction without external visibility. CON's proprietary-exposure argument had real force, but PRO's response — that tiered disclosure to regulators under trade-secret protections already exists across multiple regulatory frameworks — was adequately grounded and CON did not refute the mechanism, only re-labeled it a dilution of the motion. PRO's closing crystallized the contradiction cleanly: CON cannot simultaneously argue workers already know enough to trigger reactive legal remedies and that disclosure would dangerously reveal new information. CON's closing attempted to resolve this by distinguishing awareness-of-monitoring from proprietary-formula, but this distinction doesn't save the reactive framework — a worker auto-terminated by algorithm who never learns an algorithm made the decision cannot file the initial claim that activates discovery. PRO's "door with no handle" framing was the debate's most memorable and accurate image. CON fought well on metric gaming, competitive exposure, and compliance burden, and the closing was intelligent, but the central enforcement-precondition argument remained a live route to victory PRO never lost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's answer to CON's pressure question Q1 — combined with PRO's own unanswered Q2 — was the most decisive exchange. CON's Q1 tried to catch PRO in a dilution of the motion, but PRO clarified that disclosing a system's factors, data, and decision triggers is the motion as written, not a retreat from it, and that CON was the one inflating "disclose systems" into "publish exact weightings." Meanwhile PRO's Q2 — what forces an employer to audit a system that only harms workers — was never satisfactorily answered; CON said "liability risk," which depends on workers completing the very legal process that requires the withheld information, a circular trap PRO identified and CON never escaped.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's alternatives—namely, that existing legal discovery and internal audits are sufficient. Side A successfully proved that both of those mechanisms require the very visibility Side B was arguing against, making Side B's framework a "door with no handle."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to the Goodhart's Law argument in Rebuttal 1 was decisive, arguing that if an algorithm can be harmfully gamed, the metric is already defective, and disclosure exposes the flaw rather than creating the harm.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side A wins by exposing a structural contradiction in B's case and showing that disclosure is the precondition for the very legal remedies B champions. A argued that B cannot simultaneously claim workers already know enough to use existing law and that disclosure would catastrophically expose proprietary secrets. A cleanly turned B's Goodhart's Law point by showing disclosure reveals defective metrics rather than creating them, and effectively rebutted B's regulatory-swamping concern with familiar tiered-reporting analogies. B's discovery-based counter was credible but did not fully resolve the information-gap problem: workers who do not know an algorithm drove their termination cannot effectively plead around it. After the full exchange, A's path to victory—framed as "visibility with paperwork versus secrecy with hidden harm" and crystallized in a memorable closing—remained the cleaner, more live route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's identification that B's two core claims collapse into each other: B insists workers already know enough about monitoring to invoke existing legal channels, yet also insists that disclosing those same systems would destroy competitive advantage and invite gaming. A showed these cannot both be true, leaving B's case without a coherent foundation.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a competitive but clear decision. Several factors tip the balance. First, CON's opening was clipped before the judge could see its conclusion—a real execution cost that left at least one burden underdeveloped from the start. Second, PRO's Q2 in the pressure round exposed a genuine tension in CON's case: CON simultaneously claimed workers are too powerless to benefit from disclosure yet capable of ruthlessly gaming every revealed metric. CON's resolution—"gaming is what powerlessness looks like when the rules become visible"—was rhetorically sharp but incomplete; if workers demonstrably reshape their behavior in response to disclosed information, that IS a form of agency PRO only needs to establish at a minimal level. Third, PRO's causal-mechanism argument on discrimination law (B2.5-B2.6, reinforced by Q1) remained live throughout: CON's rebuttal that disparate impact works through statistical outcomes is technically correct, but it never fully addressed the individual worker's inability to identify which scoring criterion to challenge without any disclosure. PRO's concrete analogy to financial regulators using methodology documents (not codebases) offered a plausible middle-ground design answer to the IP/regulatory-capacity objection, while CON's response simply reasserted capacity gaps without engaging the design solution. CON's strongest late argument—"borrowed remedies"—was substantive and well-crystallized, but PRO's answer (existing tools work better with visible targets) was adequate and arrived before CON deployed the argument in full force. PRO's core through-line—accountability cannot operate in the dark—was never squarely dismantled, and the consent argument (B1.3) went largely unanswered across the entire debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Q2 in the Pressure Questions round ("You insist disclosure leaves workers powerless yet insist they'll ruthlessly game every revealed metric—can they act on disclosed information or not?") was the most decisive single exchange. CON's answer—that gaming and powerlessness are both symptoms of the same asymmetry—was clever but insufficient: the fact that workers demonstrably adapt behavior to disclosed criteria concedes the agency PRO needs, and CON never closed the gap between "narrow adaptation" and "no meaningful action at all."
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B won by successfully dismantling Side A's two main pillars. B accurately explained that disparate impact litigation relies on outcome data rather than algorithmic architecture, neutralizing A's claim that disclosure is legally necessary. Furthermore, B brilliantly resolved A's "agency vs. gaming" dilemma by framing gaming as "adaptation under constraint," distinct from the structural power required to force reform.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 response defining gaming as "what powerlessness looks like when the rules become visible" beautifully neutralized Side A's attempt to trap them in a contradiction.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): PRO won by exposing a decisive, unresolved tension in CON's case: workers cannot be simultaneously too powerless to benefit from transparency and yet so strategically empowered that they trigger a destructive gaming arms race. PRO effectively turned the gaming objection into proof that concealed metrics are defective, showed that existing legal remedies are blunted when workers cannot even identify the systems governing them, and crystallized the debate around the intuitive principle that accountability cannot function in the dark. CON's clipped opening and its reliance on the "borrowed remedies" frame—while sharp—could not overcome the burden of explaining why opacity helps workers or regulators.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 exchange on gaming and worker agency, where PRO converted CON's gaming arms race into evidence that revealed metrics are flawed and then trapped CON in a dilemma between powerlessness and gaming that CON never fully escaped.
