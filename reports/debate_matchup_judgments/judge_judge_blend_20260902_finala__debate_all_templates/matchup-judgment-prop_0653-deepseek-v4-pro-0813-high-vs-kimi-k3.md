### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by showing that the blanket ban sacrifices plausible beneficial uses, especially targeted retention and job-relevant predictions of future performance, while leaving undocumented managerial hunches untouched. Side B made the stronger case against individual unionization scores, but its claim that every benefit survives through aggregate or universal measures failed on scarce retention assistance. Its further assertion that AI scores derive value only from secrecy was too broad and weakened its enforcement argument. Side B's clipped second rebuttal was also a minor execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to “retention for everyone” was decisive: employers cannot universally provide raises, transfers, or schedule changes, while aggregate data cannot identify who needs timely help. Side B answered that employers could simply ask workers, but did not resolve workers' reluctance to disclose or explain how scarce interventions would be targeted.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully proving an asymmetry: legitimate benefits can be achieved through aggregate data, open communication, and past performance, while the harms specifically rely on secret individual prediction. A decisively neutralized B's 'audit trail' defense by pointing out that a logged score only provides accountability if the victim knows they were evaluated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing rebuttal dismantling B's defense of individual union scores by pointing out that true grievance remedies (like pay or safety) are inherently unit-wide, meaning individual targeting is just suppression.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a decisive asymmetry: the benefits Side B claims (retention, grievance fixing) can be achieved through aggregate or universal means, while the harms (suppression, self-fulfilling prophecies) specifically require secret, individual scoring. Side A brilliantly dismantles Side B's "audit trail" defense by pointing out the paradox that these tools only provide a preemptive advantage to employers if they remain hidden from the worker. Side B's insistence that companies will transparently log union-busting predictions for future prosecution is logically fragile, and Side A capitalizes on this with sharp, memorable rhetoric. Despite a clipped second rebuttal, Side A's structural framing and pressure questions controlled the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of the "audit trail" argument in the closing, noting that "a product that pays off only in secret cannot be defended by its transparency" and that Side B "sells the dashboard and delivers the black box."
