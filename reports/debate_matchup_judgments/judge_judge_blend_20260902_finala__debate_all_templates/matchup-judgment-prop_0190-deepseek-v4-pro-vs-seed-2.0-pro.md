### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side B won by keeping the debate focused on whether limiting actuarial fairness actually builds social solidarity or destroys it through regressive hidden costs and resentment. B successfully showed that flat community rating forces low-income workers to subsidize wealthy high-risk individuals, and that income subsidies do not erase the perceived unfairness of the headline premium structure. B’s alternative—honest progressive taxation—was a coherent answer to the solidarity problem that avoided the regressivity trap. While A landed strong points on the impossibility of distinguishing chosen from unchosen risk and the political fragility of tax-funded safety nets, A’s clipped rebuttal and closing left key burdens underdefended, and A never fully answered B’s core claim that equal premiums make the working poor feel exploited rather than bonded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s repeated rebuttal that community rating with income subsidies still forces the poor to overpay at the headline level and turns solidarity into humiliating charity, crystallized in the closing “you cannot build solidarity on a lie.”
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): PRO defended the proposition's solidarity logic while neutralizing CON's strongest attacks. The regressive "cleaner subsidizes millionaire smoker" claim was met with income-adjusted community rating at point of collection, and the evasion claim was met with guaranteed issue/renewal plus risk-adjustment – and B's Q2 forced A to concede insurers would evade anyway, undercutting his inevitability thesis. A's Q2 about limiting only unchosen risk lost when B showed the chosen/unchosen line is unworkable and that tax alternative needs the same line. CON repeated the lie/regressive frame without resolving its own concessions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure and second rebuttal on chosen vs unchosen and insurer incentives: Q2 exposed CON's self-defeat on evasion, and A2 on unworkable line-drawing plus regulatory morass turned A's demand for surgical bans into a reason to limit all risk-pricing for essential coverage.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A built a much stronger and more cohesive case, effectively using the regressivity of flat premiums to expose the flaws in Side B's model. Side A decisively attacked Side B's income subsidy patch as humiliating charity and highlighted the inevitability of insurer evasion when risk pricing is banned. Furthermore, Side B suffered severe execution costs by having three speeches clipped for exceeding the word limit, leaving key arguments incomplete. Side A's rhetoric was sharper, and its alternative of progressive taxation remained the more honest mechanism for solidarity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's income subsidy defense by framing it as setting the base price wrong on purpose and replacing solidarity with paternalistic charity, while clearly explaining why banning risk pricing makes avoiding high-risk patients profitable.

### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): A built a cohesive moral case that essential insurance is a mutual protection pact, not a betting shop. A neutralized B’s death-spiral argument by showing mandatory essential coverage prevents opt-out, and successfully framed B’s tax-and-pool alternative as stigmatizing charity rather than genuine solidarity. B raised sharp regressivity concerns but could not shake A’s core moral frame or recover from repeated clipped speeches that truncated B’s development.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2’s rebuttal that mandatory essential coverage eliminates the death spiral, paired with A5’s crystallization that B’s alternative creates charity rather than solidarity, proved decisive in shifting the moral burden back to B.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): B neutralized A's main death-spiral route by arguing essential coverage by definition implies no opt-out and a universal mandate, noting real-world universal systems that limit actuarial pricing are the most stable. A repeatedly clipped for word limit and conceded mandate/coercion is needed anyway, leaving the regressive cross-subsidy point as its best live attack. B answered the low-income pressure with the same-pool vs charity framing and kept the moral core of unchosen traits (newborn, postcode) intact, giving a cleaner reason to vote for limiting actuarial pricing for essential coverage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Rebuttal 1 [B2.1-B2.4] turning the death spiral into a product of opt-out and citing stable universal essential systems as existing proof, which directly undermined A's mechanism and forced A to rely on the mandate-is-coercion pivot.

While Side B relied on the rhetorical appeal of everyone standing in the "same pool," Side A exposed this as a regressive shell game where low-income, low-risk individuals subsidize high-income, high-risk individuals through hidden premium cross-subsidies. Side A's alternative—using progressive taxation to fund high-risk pools—provided a much cleaner, more analytically sound mechanism for true solidarity based on ability to pay. Side B struggled to answer the regressivity charge, dismissing tax-funded support as mere "charity" or "stigmatizing" without resolving the unfair burden on the working poor. Although Side A suffered minor execution costs from being clipped at the very end of several speeches, its core arguments and rebuttals were fully developed and decisively won the central clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal and closing, where it crystallized the "regressive shell game" argument, showing that community rating forces the poorest healthy workers to subsidize the wealthiest sick individuals, whereas progressive taxation actually aligns with social solidarity by tying contributions to capacity.
