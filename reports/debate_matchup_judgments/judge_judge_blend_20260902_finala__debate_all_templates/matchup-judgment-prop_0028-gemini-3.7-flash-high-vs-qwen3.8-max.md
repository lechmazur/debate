### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, competitive cases, but A's rebuttal work was sharper on the pivotal clash: the meaning of the persistent college wage premium. B repeatedly argued that if degrees were "hollow" screening devices, firms would defect and hire cheaper non-graduates, so the premium proves real productivity. A answered this directly with a signaling-equilibrium explanation (the premium can persist precisely because exclusion, not added productivity, drives it, and firms can't unilaterally deviate without adverse-selection risk). Crucially, B never engaged this counter-mechanism after it was introduced in Rebuttal 2 — B's closing simply re-asserted the same "firms would undercut" logic verbatim, which is a live, unaddressed piece of pressure that favors A. A also maintained a tighter, more consistent framing ("higher gate, not more rooms") and used the pressure-question round to sharpen the distributional point (debt and underemployment for marginal/lower-income graduates), which B's closing addressed only in vague lifetime-trajectory terms without confronting the debt/risk asymmetry specifically. B's hereditary-monopoly and sector-growth arguments remained strong and were not fully dismantled, keeping the debate competitive, but A did more of the decisive comparative work required by the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A1 answer in Rebuttal 2 (screening-equilibrium explanation of the wage premium) directly undercut B's central "if hollow, premium would collapse" argument, and B's Rebuttal 2/Closing failed to re-engage this specific mechanism, leaving it as A's strongest unanswered point.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by sustaining a tighter causal story through the full exchange. A successfully reframed B's central wage-premium evidence as the predictable result of a screening equilibrium—high average returns driven by excluding non-graduates, compatible with marginal underemployment and debt. B never squarely answered why employers rely on generic degree requirements for ordinary administrative and service roles if the need were truly about portable skills rather than cost-shifting; A's question about why firms do not simply test skills directly remained live. A's distributional point about lower-income graduates carrying the burden of devalued ordinary credentials while elite degrees retain scarcity value also survived unanswered. In the closing, A's crystallization—that CON showed who gets through the gate, while PRO showed expansion raised the gate—clarified the decisive comparison, whereas B repeated the knowledge-economy narrative without resolving the tension around credential creep for the broad labor market.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 answer to B3.1, where A reframed the resilient college wage premium as exactly what credential inflation predicts: a screening equilibrium that keeps average returns high by excluding non-graduates while the marginal graduate is underemployed. This turned B's strongest empirical claim into evidence for PRO's narrative.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won the core weighing clash: whether a resilient wage premium proves genuine opportunity or a higher gate. PRO explained how screening equilibrium sustains averages while marginal graduates face underemployment, debt, and devalued ordinary degrees, and forced CON to defend why routine clerical/admin/service roles now need generic degrees. CON's reply that work got more complex and that firms would arbitrage a pure signal was plausible but never answered the cost-shifting and elite-vs-ordinary hierarchy point. The toll-vs-rooms framing gave a cleaner comparative reason that credential inflation at scale outpaced opportunity creation at the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 A1 reinterpreted the college wage premium as the signature of a screening equilibrium where non-graduates are excluded, neutralizing B's strongest human-capital evidence and recasting premium persistence as proof of a higher gate, not more rooms.
