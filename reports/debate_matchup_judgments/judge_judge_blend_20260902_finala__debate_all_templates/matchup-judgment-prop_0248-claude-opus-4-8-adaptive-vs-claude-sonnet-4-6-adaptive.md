### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A wins a clear but competitive debate. B made a strong principled case for universal public care and against biology-based liability, but A more directly answered the motion as a cost-contribution question: if someone must pay, the able adult child with connection and benefit is a fairer first contributor than unrelated taxpayers, with exemptions and public care first. B’s repeated “biological lottery” framing was rhetorically strong but overstated the consequence, because A’s model did not require care to be withheld while family liability was sorted out.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s response that public care can be delivered first and contribution assessed afterward was the most decisive exchange, because it blunted B’s strongest implementation and “grandma in limbo” pressure while preserving A’s fairness comparison between adult children and taxpayers.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by collapsing CON's foundational consent argument and maintaining the cleaner comparative frame. CON opened on the strong moral asymmetry that children did not choose to be born, but PRO's Rebuttal 1 exposed a lethal contradiction: CON's own universal-taxation alternative compels strangers without their consent and without their having received any benefit from the parent. This forced CON to pivot to "creation" and "universality," but PRO showed in the closing that CON applies the creation principle selectively (the childless nurse did not create the need either, yet CON taxes her). CON's "equal need, equal floor" argument remained rhetorically powerful, but PRO's "public system pays first, assesses contribution after" answer meant CON never proved that care quality would differ under PRO's design; CON instead elided the difference between who funds care and what care is received. PRO also gave direct, model answers to both pressure questions, while CON's responses to PRO's questions were more diffuse. After the full exchange, PRO's core question—why the stranger who benefited nothing is fairer to bill than the child who received years of care—survived with less damage than CON's claim that biological non-choice is a dispositive barrier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's first rebuttal (Turn 3) exposed that CON's "non-consent" objection to filial duty also invalidates CON's own preferred policy of universal taxation, since taxation compels strangers who neither consented nor benefited. This trapped CON and forced a weaker pivot to "creation" and "universality" that PRO undermined in later rounds.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by successfully collapsing CON's core principled objection. CON anchored on consent and voluntary choice, but PRO sharply demonstrated that CON's own preferred alternative (universal taxation) and existing paternal child support laws also override consent. When CON pivoted to "creation of dependency," PRO effectively noted that taxation also ignores who created the need, making CON's principle look selectively applied and arbitrary. PRO also neutralized CON's practical concerns about care delays and abuse exemptions with a clear "public pays first, litigate reimbursement later" mechanism, which directly answered pressure and preserved the elderly parent's immediate safety net. CON's "biological lottery" argument was strong but ultimately could not overcome PRO's cleaner fairness framing ("connection plus benefit vs. stranger plus nothing") and PRO's tighter execution in the late rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 dismantling of CON's consent-to-creation pivot. By showing that CON's taxation model also bills people who never "created" the need, PRO exposed CON's principled line as selective, effectively collapsing CON's philosophical wall and forcing the debate onto PRO's stronger fairness comparison.

B also neutralized A's free-rider scenario with estate recovery and kept the abuse and estrangement litigation objection live.
