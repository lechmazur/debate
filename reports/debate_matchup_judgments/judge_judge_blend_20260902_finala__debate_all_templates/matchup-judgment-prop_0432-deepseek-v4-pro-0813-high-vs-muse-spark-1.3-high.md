### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON's central asymmetry—that insurance/deposits can pay for property damage but cannot compensate for sleep loss, fear, or health harms borne by third parties who never consented to the risk—was conceded by PRO ("It doesn't, and it was never supposed to," A4.3) and never fully recovered from. CON also landed two rebuttals PRO left substantially unanswered: (1) that preventive category bans for externality-heavy behaviors (smoking, candles, waterbeds) are already normal practice, undercutting PRO's claim that banning risk-sources in advance is uniquely unfair to pets; and (2) that a small landlord wanting to preserve a pet-free, low-allergen building for *future* tenants has no documentable "credible justification" under PRO's opt-out test, making the "default, not mandate" framing effectively a mandate in practice. PRO's answers to the pressure questions addressed present-tenant scenarios but never returned to this future-tenant gap, and PRO's closing pivoted to an "overbroad presumption" framing without repairing that hole. CON's vivid, consistent framing ("billing problem vs. neighbor problem," "you cannot unbark a night") also made the stakes easier to grasp and sustained across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Turn 8 point that a small landlord promising a pet-free building to *future* tenants has "nothing to document" under PRO's opt-out test was the most damaging exchange; PRO never directly answered this specific gap in its closing, leaving CON's "mandate with paperwork" charge effectively live at the end of the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing that insurance and deposits address landlord property loss but not the nonconsenting neighbors who bear noise, allergen, fear, and enforcement costs. Side A persuasively attacked blanket treatment of all pets and offered targeted conduct rules, but it never fully answered B’s point that those rules operate only after potentially irreparable harm and burdensome proceedings. B therefore preserved the clearer comparative reason for allowing genuinely pet-free buildings.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it used Side A’s admission that insurance does not compensate neighbors to expose the gap between funded property repair and delayed nuisance enforcement, while directly answering why pets can present a distinctive combination of persistent risks.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON won by making third-party neighbor externalities the decisive metric and keeping it live: insurance/deposits pay landlords, not the tenant who loses sleep, wheezes, or fears a stairwell incident, while nuisance process leaves victims living inside the harm for months. PRO’s risk-separation and overbreadth points were strong, and the admission that money does not cure neighbor harms was honest, but never neutralized CON’s core asymmetry. CON also better answered the live burden on defaults: “credible opt-outs” still force small owners into after-the-fact documentation and erase reliable pet-free sorting. PRO never restored a clean payer or faster remedy for non-consenting neighbors, so prevention over exclusion carried the final comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1–A4.2: future/preventive pet-free housing has nothing to “document,” and pets uniquely fuse continuous multi-risk harms unlike stereo/smoking controls—collapsing PRO’s opt-out and separation answers.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but CON built a cleaner, more sustained throughline: apartment harms (noise, allergens, hallway conflict) are largely non-financial, so a deposit/insurance default cannot prevent them, only compensate after the fact. CON weaponized PRO's own admission ("deposits are not meant to pay for lost sleep or an asthma attack") into a recurring "prevention vs. compensation" and "fails closed vs. fails open" frame that remained largely unrebutted in substance through to the closing. PRO's strongest counter—that bans just drive pets underground with zero recourse—is a real point, but it is an empirical assertion about compliance rates that CON directly challenged (a ban still "removes the main continuous source" even if imperfectly enforced, and discovery of a hidden pet is itself an actionable violation), and PRO never fully closed that gap. PRO also conceded that default-allow buildings can still impose pre-entry screening (vaccination, training, size fit), which muddies the motion's own "default to allow" framing and gives CON's "pay-to-play doesn't guarantee suitability" point continued traction. PRO's fairness/housing-stability arguments from the opening were strong but got largely dropped after Rebuttal 1, while CON kept returning to and sharpening its core harm-timing argument across every round, including a clean use of the pressure-question exchange. This made CON's case easier to decide by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1 turning PRO's own concession ("deposits are not meant to pay for lost sleep or an asthma attack") into the decisive "prevention vs. compensation" frame, sustained through the pressure questions and both closings, was the most damaging and clarifying exchange of the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): B won by showing that deposits address physical damage but not the recurring noise, allergen, and common-area harms central to apartment living. A offered conduct rules, yet B persuasively explained that these operate only after repeated harm, whereas discovering an animal under a ban immediately establishes a lease violation. A's housing-access benefits kept the debate competitive but received too little later weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's “bans fail closed; default-allow fails open” comparison decisively answered A's hidden-pet argument: imperfect compliance does not make bans useless, because discovery itself enables removal, while an allowed pet generally requires proof of repeated nuisance.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): PRO built the stronger comparative path by showing blanket bans deliver evasion and uninsured hidden pets rather than the promised quiet/allergen control, while default-allow plus escrow and signed conduct rules supplies clearer damage recovery and removal tools once honesty is required. CON landed real pressure on non-financial continuous harms and the timing of enforcement, but never fully neutralized that bans already leak via assistance animals, visitors, and secrecy, so their “fails closed” advantage was narrower than claimed. Across rounds PRO adapted better, answered the pressure questions directly, and crystallized why enforceable accountability beats paper purity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 answers (esp. A1/A2 plus B4.3–B4.5) turning the “money can’t fix sleep/asthma” concession into a conduct-rules comparison and showing a signed pet agreement beats hunting a secret animal.
