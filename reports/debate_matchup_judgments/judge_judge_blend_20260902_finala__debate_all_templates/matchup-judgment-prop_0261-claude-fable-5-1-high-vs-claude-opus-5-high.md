### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won a competitive debate by showing that one-successor transfer avoids replication, that an inheritance guarantee can remain waivable for privacy, and that optional labeling or portability does not provide the legally protected floor in the motion. Side B established real probate, licensing, and market costs, but its alternatives remained seller-dependent and did not fully answer the windfall from misleadingly sold libraries.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal most decisively answered Side B's dilemma: one license passes to one successor, while the buyer may opt out without negating the guarantee. The line that a guarantee is a floor while a label is a menu exposed why Side B's voluntary product alternative did not solve the motion's core problem.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO built the stronger path by anchoring the debate in a concrete consumer-protection harm: the 'Buy' button creates a reasonable expectation of ownership that vanishes at death, gifting a windfall to platforms. CON landed real analytical blows on sublicensing, perpetual service costs, and the fragility of server-dependent 'inheritance,' but never fully recovered from PRO's exposure of its alternative remedy. PRO showed that a mere 'honest label' is a menu the seller can delete, and that restitution litigation is a phantom fix no grieving family would use. The image of a widow suing title-by-title versus a single guaranteed transfer gave the cleaner comparative reason to vote. CON's privacy and cost arguments were blunted by PRO's effective answers that the guarantee is a defeasible default—like all inheritance—and that banks already run the same probate machinery.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's demolition of CON's restitution alternative as a fake remedy—'the windfall, wearing a suit'—showing that no grieving family litigates a Kindle library title-by-title and that 'cheaper for platforms' means consumers get nothing, decisively undermined CON's claim that honest labels alone fix the harm.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO turned CON's own admissions - "Buy" deception is real and a library should outlive its buyer - into a path: one-copy, one-heir transfer with opt-out by will avoids replication and privacy compulsion. CON's cost story (probate desk, sublicensing) was answered by existing bank/brokerage practice, and its restitution/label alternative left the windfall undefended and past purchasers with nothing. B's floor-vs-menu framing survived best.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 A1/A2 - clarified one-license-one-successor rule for five children, defined guarantee as platform can't strip right by boilerplate yet owner can delete/will-away, and exposed that CON's "product platforms may sell" leaves existing libraries as corporate windfall.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that A's guarantee either forces a costlier compulsory bundle or causes sellers lacking upstream heritable rights to relabel the product as a license. B also distinguished prominent point-of-sale labeling from buried terms and preserved a cheaper personal tier, while A never fully answered the risks of higher prices, catalog withdrawal, or subscription migration. A's family-continuity case was compelling, but its later concessions largely reduced the dispute to B's tiered model with a flipped default.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal most decisively used A's own answers: if unavailable heritable rights mean the seller cannot use the word Buy, the immediate result is relabeling rather than inheritance; if opting out can cost less, A has accepted priced tiers and is principally defending a different default.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by demonstrating that the proposed guarantee cannot actually guarantee transfer when upstream licenses are non-transferable or companies fail, collapsing into either honest labeling or an unwanted price hike on all buyers. PRO's concessions on opt-out pricing and upstream unavailability were exploited to show the mandate promises what it cannot deliver, while CON's alternative of transparent point-of-sale tiers remained intact. The decisive moment came when CON showed PRO's own rule merely relabels the button when rights are unavailable, leaving heirs with nothing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's argument in Rebuttal 2 [A4.5] that PRO's guarantee collapses into honest labeling when storefronts cannot obtain heritable rights upstream, because PRO's own logic requires them to stop calling it "Buy"—meaning nothing transfers and the statute merely relabels.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by turning PRO's mechanism against the word "guarantee." PRO's moral default-setting case stayed live, but A pressed the hard case and got B to admit in [B4.1] that if the storefront cannot obtain heritable rights, it "simply cannot call the button 'Buy'" – so nothing transfers. Combined with pricing (opt-out buyer "may well pay less" = priced tier), privacy (sudden-death opt-out fails), and indivisibility, A showed the mandate either evaporates when needed or becomes CON's honest, priced tiers with a flipped, more costly default. That left PRO without a live guarantee.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 / Closing: using B's own [B4.1] and [B4.3] to show the guarantee collapses to honest labeling in the hard case and to a priced tier in the easy case, exposing unenforceability and subsidy costs.
