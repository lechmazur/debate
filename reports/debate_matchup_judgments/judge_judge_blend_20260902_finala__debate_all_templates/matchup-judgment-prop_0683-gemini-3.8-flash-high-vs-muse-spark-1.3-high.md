### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): The debate turned on PRO's central move: redefining "before suspending" to mean "before permanent/final" action, allowing an instant "emergency freeze" first. CON relentlessly exploited this, arguing that a freeze IS a suspension under the motion's plain wording, so PRO had effectively conceded that platforms must act before any due process occurs — undermining the resolution as written rather than defending it. PRO never squarely rebutted this textual point, instead calling it "wordplay" in closing without dismantling CON's argument. CON also landed a sharp contradiction charge (PRO decried automated moderation as error-prone, then relied on automated triage to fix appeals) and a strong analogy (AML "tipping-off" laws) that PRO's banking rebuttal never fully answered. PRO's own pressure questions were answered adequately by CON (voluntary appeals exist; triage differs from initial detection), while CON's pressure questions exposed a durable tension in PRO's case that persisted through the final rebuttal and closing (the "two-tiered" triage critique went essentially unanswered). Both sides argued skillfully with vivid, grounded examples, but CON's sustained, escalating pressure on the definitional and consistency problems in PRO's case gave it the cleaner, more defensible path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's repeated "an emergency freeze IS a suspension" argument (first in Rebuttal 1, sharpened via the Q1 pressure question, and never fully neutralized by PRO's "final vs. freeze" distinction) was the most decisive exchange, since it kept PRO on the back foot defending a redefinition of the resolution rather than the resolution itself.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by holding Side B to the proposition’s explicit requirement of process before suspension. Side B’s emergency-freeze model was sensible policy, but it permitted immediate account suspension before an appeal and therefore defended a narrower proposition about process before permanent banning. Side A repeatedly exposed that substitution and also raised credible scale, gaming, and disclosure tradeoffs. Side B made a strong case for correctability after urgent intervention, but never reconciled that case with the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s decisive response was that an emergency freeze is substantively a suspension: the user is immediately locked out and silenced. Side B’s distinction between temporary restraint and permanent expulsion answered the policy concern but not the proposition’s requirement that appeal protections come before suspension itself.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by demonstrating that PRO functionally conceded the proposition as written. PRO's "emergency freeze" is a suspension—locking the user out instantly—yet the motion explicitly requires due-process protections, including appeal, before suspending. PRO never reconciled this textual gap, instead repeatedly defending process only before permanent bans. CON also showed that PRO's scaled triage solution would create a two-tiered system favoring established accounts, undermining PRO's own egalitarian framing, while CON's security and operational-scale objections survived mostly intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's repeated exposure that PRO's "emergency freeze" constitutes a suspension under the motion's plain text, combined with the demonstration in Rebuttal 2 that PRO's appeal-triage system would abandon new and unverified users to automated sorting—contradicting the promise of meaningful, universal due process.
