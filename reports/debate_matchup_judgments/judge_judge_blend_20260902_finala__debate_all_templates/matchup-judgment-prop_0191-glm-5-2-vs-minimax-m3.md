### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Both sides ran competent, well-structured cases and the debate narrowed cleanly across rounds. PRO's strongest sustained contributions were the circularity argument (unrepresentative trials guarantee post-market discovery, so CON's "speculative" charge is self-sealing) and the consent asymmetry (post-market exposure is unconsented). CON's strongest sustained contributions were the concrete body-count framing, the statistical-power point (proportionate enrollment doesn't change whether Phase 3 is powered to detect rare subgroup effects), and the decisive counter on zolpidem. PRO introduced zolpidem as the affirmative's only specific example of a subgroup harm that proportionate enrollment would have caught. CON's response was sharp and accurate: the sex-based dose revision came from dedicated pharmacokinetic studies and adverse-event reports, not from proportionate Phase 3 enrollment—which is exactly the targeted mechanism CON had been defending throughout. PRO's closing did not answer this counter; it pivoted to the structural point about what approval implies, leaving CON's specific rebuttal standing. The statistical-power argument also remained live: PRO acknowledged in its closing [B5.2] that Phase 3 cannot reliably detect subgroup differences but reframed it as a reason not to approve rather than a reason to require proportionate enrollment, which is not the motion. CON's "approval that honestly says 'efficacy established, subgroup refinements pending'" was a workable alternative that PRO never dismantled. PRO's rhetorical register ("managed ignorance," "one-column ledger") was slightly stronger, and the circularity argument kept PRO in contention, but CON won the most specific evidentiary exchange and kept a live route—the existing toolkit catches subgroup signals without mandated proportionate enrollment—that PRO's closing left unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's counter to PRO's zolpidem example [A4.5]: the sex-based dosing revision came from dedicated PK studies and adverse-event reports, not from proportionate Phase 3 enrollment, so the case PRO cited as evidence of proportionate enrollment's necessity actually demonstrated that CON's preferred mechanism (targeted subgroup studies, pharmacovigilance) already functions as CON described. PRO never refuted this directly.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by making the sharper comparative case that proportional representation may not actually generate the subgroup safety knowledge Side A promises, while materially delaying access. Side A had a strong ethical framing around unconsented post-market learning and known biological variation, but Side B effectively narrowed the issue to mechanism: Phase 3 trials are not necessarily powered for subgroup harms, targeted studies and labeling can address refinements, and Side A's zolpidem example was caught outside proportionate Phase 3 enrollment. Side B had some overstatement and a clipped opening, but its final weighing was cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's answer to the zolpidem example and its point that proportional enrollment does not necessarily create statistical power for subgroup safety signals was the decisive exchange.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B won by proving that proportionate representation in Phase 3 trials would not provide the statistical power needed to detect rare subgroup effects, meaning the mandate would cause the conceded delay without solving the safety issue. Side B also effectively turned Side A's Zolpidem example to validate its own preferred alternative mechanisms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal brilliantly turned the Zolpidem example, pointing out that its dose refinement came from the exact alternative tools B advocated (dedicated PK studies and adverse event reports), and that Phase 3 proportional enrollment lacks the statistical power to catch such signals anyway.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): CON's decisive late-round move was the "remediable vs irredeemable" distinction: an approved drug can be prescribed, studied, adjusted, and withdrawn; an unapproved drug can do none of those things. CON then turned PRO's own drug withdrawal examples (opioids, COX-2 inhibitors) against PRO's case, noting those harms were "detected, corrected, or pulled through a system that only functions because the drug existed in the world." This was not just clever rhetoric—it neutralized PRO's pharmacovigilance critique by showing that post-approval harm detection requires the drug to exist. PRO's evidentiary blindness argument (B2.2) was the strongest PRO move and stayed live—drugs reach unrepresented populations regardless, so the real choice is evidence versus blindness—but CON correctly answered that delay also denies benefit to the very unrepresented populations PRO claims to protect. CON also caught PRO's internal vulnerability: PRO's own B2.4 proposed adding genotype and comorbidity stratification on top of demographic categories, exposing the "escalating cascade" CON described, and PRO's rebuttal in B4.3 ("calling it insufficient is an argument for building on it") confirmed rather than denied the trajectory. PRO's "planning decision, not a years-long halt" reframe in B4.2 was plausible but CON's recruitment bottleneck reply was effective and unanswered. PRO's closing counterfactual critique—that CON must assume the drug works in untested populations—was strong, but CON's A5 response was precise: the argument never required that assumption, only that an approved drug remains a live instrument. CON made the regulatory philosophy comparison cleaner and the decisive clash easier to resolve.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.3), which established the remediable/irredeemable distinction and directly inverted PRO's drug withdrawal examples: those withdrawals prove post-market detection works, but detection only works because the drug existed. PRO never gave a clean answer to this inversion across any subsequent turn.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): B won by making the central tradeoff clearer: material approval delay costs access and patient-years now, while A did not adequately weigh the magnitude of those losses against safety gains. A had a strong case that unrepresentative trials create evidentiary blindness and post-market harms, but relied on some broad or late examples and partially minimized the stipulated delay. B's "correctable error versus permanent silence" frame directly answered A's pharmacovigilance attack and better fit the motion's acceptance of material slowing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was against A's claim that delay deaths are not certain: B reframed the issue as access to a live, studiable, adjustable drug versus an unapproved drug that cannot save anyone outside the trial, which undercut A's evidence-versus-blindness framing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): CON wins by systematically dismantling PRO's attempts to mitigate the harms of delay and cornering PRO on their own contradictions. When PRO attempts to minimize delay as a mere "planning problem," CON effectively points out that planning does not magically conjure hard-to-recruit patients. When PRO denies advocating for an escalating cascade of requirements, CON cleanly quotes PRO's own previous speech to prove otherwise. Ultimately, CON's weighing mechanism—that an approved drug allows for "correctable error" (remediation and withdrawal) while a delayed drug guarantees "permanent silence" (saving no one)—decisively resolves the core clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.6 and B4.7, which brilliantly neutralize PRO's attempt to handwave the delay as just a "planning problem" by observing that "the patients either exist in the right numbers or they don't. Planning doesn't conjure them."
