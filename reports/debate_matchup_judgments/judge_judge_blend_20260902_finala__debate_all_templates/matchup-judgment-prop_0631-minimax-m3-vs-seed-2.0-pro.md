### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A wins on balance, though the debate is genuinely competitive. Side A's constitutional foundation (Writings/Authors), the windfall-not-incentive argument, and the consistent defense of the iterative creator all remain live and largely unanswered throughout. The windfall argument is the clearest policy case for the motion, and Side B never neutralizes it—they concede one-click prompters shouldn't get copyright but never articulate an alternative rule that would draw that line without the documentation requirement. That absence weakens their entire case. Side B's strongest work is the repeated identification that Side A quietly softens "can document" into ordinary evidentiary sufficiency, which is a real tension—the motion's phrasing does create a presumption against coverage requiring affirmative proof, not just courts looking at available evidence in a dispute. Side B lands this point cleanly in Rebuttal 1 and at closing, and it is not fully neutralized. But Side A's A1 answer in Rebuttal 2 is plausible: copyright disputes already require showing who created what, and layered PSDs, RAW files, and metadata are entirely ordinary. Side B's ML-cameras rebuttal (B4.1) is a reasonable narrowing but doesn't survive Side A's underlying distinction—cameras record real scenes; generative AI synthesizes novel expression from ingested third-party work. Side A's independent-creator protection argument also holds: the iterative designer has ordinary artifacts to show; the route to harm Side B describes requires that ordinary working artists erase all trace of their process, which is implausible for most digital workflows. Side B's corporate-advantage argument is rhetorically effective but not proven—studios do benefit from version control, but the rule's evidentiary standard as Side A interprets it doesn't uniquely reward formal documentation systems over the artifacts working creators already retain.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's B2.1 catch—that Side A was silently discarding the word "document" from their own motion while defending a softer standard—was the sharpest single rebuttal. It created genuine pressure that forced Side A to spend Rebuttal 2 explaining what "can document" actually means. Side A's A1 answer (totality of ordinary evidence, not a notarized audit trail) was adequate but not fully decisive, leaving the documentation-as-new-presumption concern partially live through the close.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A won by successfully narrowing the debate to the practical effects of the word 'document' in the proposition, demonstrating that an explicit evidentiary threshold inherently favors large corporate workflows over independent creators. Side A also cleanly neutralized Side B's camera/ML distinction in the pressure rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Q1, correctly pointing out that modern digital cameras rely on computational ML models trained on millions of prior photographs, completely dismantling Side B's attempt to distinguish AI tools from cameras based on training data.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B wins because it consistently defended the human authorship requirement with constitutional and incentive grounding, directly answered pressure questions on documentation and the prompter distinction without evasion, and kept rebuttals focused on comparison rather than expansion. Side A pressed the documentation wording effectively at points but left its own burden on freelancer harm less resolved after B's clarification.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2's A1/A2 directly resolved the "document" and freelancer questions, then crystallized the prompter vs iterative creator line.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO held the better of the three central clashes. On the constitutional question, PRO's grounding in the "authors" textual requirement was more precise than CON's appeal to "progress" alone—both sides cited the same clause, but PRO's argument was structurally tighter. On the tool distinction, CON's camera analogy was rhetorically effective but ultimately weaker: a camera executes real-time directed choices; a generative AI produces a finished expressive work from a short description with no further human steering in the interval. CON never gave a satisfying answer to why that difference doesn't matter. Most importantly, PRO's reframe—that CON is the party demanding a special exception (default protection AI outputs have never had)—was never cleanly neutralized. CON's best moments were the iterative-prompting argument and the "contingent copyright is not copyright" framing, both of which had genuine force; but PRO's response that all copyright is already subject to retroactive challenge adequately parried the chilling-effect claim without inventing new doctrine. On the Pressure Questions, PRO's Q1 (one-prompt scenario) placed real burden on CON, whose A1 called it a strawman and pivoted rather than defending the scenario directly. CON's Q1 (where is the line?) was answered adequately: courts routinely apply fuzzy authorship standards, and demanding a bright numerical line misunderstands how copyright thresholds have always worked. CON's execution costs—two clipped speeches—did not decide the debate because both clipped turns made their core points before truncation, but the clips did prevent any final crystallization advantage. On balance, PRO built a cleaner path to victory grounded in existing doctrine and answered the live pressure adequately; CON mounted a competitive case but left the core "author" standard argument and the one-prompt scenario insufficiently answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's reframe in Rebuttal 1 that CON is the party asking for a special exception—AI outputs receiving default protection no other work has ever received by virtue of being challenged—was the most decisive argumentative move. CON's response (that the proposition flips the default) was logically coherent but never fully neutralized PRO's point that under existing law the "author" must be a human, so AI outputs have no default protection to flip in the first place. That asymmetry ran through the rest of the debate and PRO kept pressing it successfully.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully neutralizing Side A's main offensive push—that the proposition creates a novel, unworkable paperwork burden. Side B convincingly argues that copyright already relies on courts to draw lines on originality, and already requires creators to prove authorship if challenged. By dismantling Side A's "chilling effect" impacts, Side B leaves their core offensive principle standing: copyright is fundamentally a reward for human creative labor, and rejecting the proposition would unjustly grant 90-year monopolies for pressing a button.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal, where they answer Side A's pressure questions by pointing out that courts already draw non-numerical lines for originality and that all copyright is inherently contingent upon surviving a challenge. This effectively collapsed Side A's "trapdoor" and "unworkable standard" arguments.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): PRO (B) built the stronger case by consistently anchoring to copyright's core labor bargain, distinguishing AI's post-prompt autonomy from prior tools, and directly answering pressure questions without evasion. CON (A) raised valid burden and chilling-effect points but weakened them with clipped turns and less decisive rebuttals on the unique AI mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2 rebuttal 2 cleanly neutralized A's documentation/trapdoor claims by showing they match existing challenge rules, preserving the labor-vs-rent-seeking comparison as decisive.
