### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): CON executed more sharply in rebuttals and Pressure Questions, particularly with Q1 attempting to collapse universal co-payments into means-testing by another name, and by consistently maintaining the fiscal sustainability narrative. However, PRO's answer in A4.1 draws a textbook and legally meaningful distinction—eligibility (who gets access) versus cost-sharing (how much they contribute)—that CON never truly defeats; instead CON pivots to a different fiscal concern rather than showing the distinction is false. That distinction is central to the motion: if everyone has guaranteed access and no one is turned away for lacking assets, the poverty-trap objection CON concedes is real under means-testing is neutralized. PRO's total-societal-cost framing (wage losses, administrative overhead of means-testing, caregiver burnout) remains live throughout the debate because CON's "burden just shifts" response does not address the genuine efficiency loss of duplicating an administrative eligibility apparatus. CON's fiscal sustainability argument (rising Japan premiums, Germany bailouts) is the strongest surviving counter, but PRO correctly points out that any social insurance system adjusts contributions over time, and neither side demonstrates the total burden under the alternative is lower. CON wins the execution dimension—rebuttals and question construction—but PRO's core argumentative path (universal access plus progressive cost-sharing as equitable and comparably efficient) remains more directly responsive to the motion and survives the exchange in better shape than CON's path, which relies on a semantic collapse that the eligibility/cost-sharing distinction legitimately resists.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q1 and PRO's A1 response is the most decisive exchange. CON tries to collapse income-scaled co-payments into means-testing; PRO answers that eligibility (universal access regardless of wealth) and cost-sharing (progressive contribution after guaranteed entitlement) are genuinely distinct policy instruments. CON pivots to the fiscal-entitlement concern rather than refuting the distinction, which means PRO's equity and poverty-trap arguments remain standing even while CON lands a real blow on open-ended fiscal commitment.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A systematically dismantled Side B's defenses, particularly by arguing that "income-scaled co-payments" concedes the need for targeting while retaining the fiscal burden of universal eligibility, and by exposing that Side B's family care statistics actually represented a decline from pre-reform baselines.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which devastatingly framed Side B's 60% family care statistic as a trajectory of decline, effectively winning the crowding-out clash.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON won by demonstrating that PRO's flagship examples (Japan and Germany) actually illustrate unsustainable open-ended entitlements requiring repeated tax hikes on a shrinking workforce, not fiscal flexibility. CON also showed that PRO's "income-scaled co-payments" concede the core principle that wealthy and poor seniors should not be treated identically, effectively smuggling means-testing into a universal framework while keeping the fiscal risk of guaranteed entitlement. CON further undermined PRO's crowding-out defense by noting that family care shares fell from pre-reform baselines even in countries where absolute levels remain relatively high, and that cash-for-care allowances represent a fiscal concession rather than proof of preserved informal care. PRO's total-societal-cost framing and equity arguments remained live but could not overcome these sustained attacks on sustainability and internal coherence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's argument that PRO's income-scaled co-payments are "means-testing wearing a different hat," which exposed that PRO's model concedes the need to differentiate by ability to pay, thereby surrendering the pure universalist advantage while retaining the fiscal burden of an open-ended entitlement.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A (PRO) won by building a durable comparative framework and exploiting a critical self-contradiction in CON's case. PRO's core argument—that LTC costs exist regardless; the only question is whether they fall catastrophically on unlucky individuals or are shared universally—was stated early and never squarely neutralized. PRO's worst-case comparison (universal care: delayed home-care slots; patchwork: financial ruin and zero coverage for millions) was made repeatedly and CON never offered a clean answer to the childless senior or the saver just above any threshold. The decisive blow came from PRO's Q1 pressure question about "mandatory risk-pooling." CON's A1 in B4.1 inadvertently conceded the core PRO position by writing that "a universal public system removes the profit layer entirely and pools risk directly through taxation, which is why Japan's LTCI…still delivers a floor that private pools cannot match without a markup"—essentially arguing for universal public care while claiming to oppose it. PRO caught this contradiction explicitly in the closing. On the means-testing threshold, CON's $250,000 figure was brittle precision introduced late with no grounding, and PRO correctly showed it remains an arbitrary cliff. CON's fiscal arguments also relied on questionable specifics (Japan consuming "over 10% of GDP" on LTCI alone is implausible—Japan's LTCI is roughly 2% of GDP—and the "$300 billion / 2–3% payroll tax" figures lack grounding), which discounts their authority. PRO used appropriately cautious language with plausible mechanisms throughout, giving it better epistemic discipline. CON's rebuttals were competent but left multiple live routes intact for PRO, particularly the floor-versus-lottery framing and the moral-hazard-doesn't-apply-to-Alzheimer's argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's exploitation of CON's B4.1 answer, where CON acknowledged that universal public care "removes the profit layer entirely and pools risk directly through taxation" and "delivers a floor private pools cannot match"—the opponent inadvertently argued the PRO case while trying to distinguish mandatory private pooling from universal coverage, and PRO caught it cleanly in the closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B wins dominantly because Side A suffers a catastrophic breakdown in Rebuttal 2. After proposing "mandatory risk-pooling" in Rebuttal 1, Side A inexplicably attributes this policy to Side B, attacks it, and proceeds to argue passionately in favor of universal public systems, stating they "deliver a floor that private pools cannot match." Side B easily capitalizes on this fatal concession in the Closing. Additionally, Side A misquotes Side B in the Pressure Questions regarding whether means-testing protects or punishes the frugal, which Side B also smoothly points out and leverages.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Closing, which devastatingly points out that Side A spent its second rebuttal successfully arguing the PRO case by destroying its own private-pooling alternative.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side A suffered a catastrophic self-own in Rebuttal 2 by conceding that universal public pooling removes profit layers and delivers a floor that private mandatory pools cannot match, effectively making the PRO case. Side B immediately seized this in closing, showing that CON's own reasoning collapses into B's position. Beyond the concession, B consistently showed that CON's three alternatives each failed: private insurance has collapsed, mandatory pooling is just universal care with a surcharge, and means-testing creates arbitrary cliffs that A could not defend without producing a $250,000 threshold that is still a cliff. B framed the choice cleanly as a floor for all versus a lottery for the few, while A never answered what happens to the childless or caregivers who cannot quit their jobs. A's opening had some force, but the later exchange destroyed CON's path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question Q1 on mandatory risk-pooling versus universal coverage forced A in Rebuttal 2 to admit that universal public systems remove profit layers and underwriting inefficiencies, a concession B then weaponized to collapse CON's alternative in closing.
