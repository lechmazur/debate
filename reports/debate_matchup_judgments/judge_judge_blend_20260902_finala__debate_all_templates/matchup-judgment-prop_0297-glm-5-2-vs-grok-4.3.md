### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A built the stronger cumulative case. The decisive structural move—that relocation and replacement are not mutually exclusive, so destruction uniquely forecloses evidentiary preservation while reclaiming public space is still available to both sides—was stated early and never convincingly answered. Side B's best response was that practical resource absorption would crowd out new works, but this doesn't justify permanent artifact destruction; it's a management problem, not a reason to choose irreversibility. Side A's evidentiary-specificity argument in Rebuttal 2 (the 1925 monument's inscription, sculptural style, and dedicatory speech as specific evidence of a specific campaign) was strong and CON's answer—"new works and education" can carry history forward—was clearly weaker; new works don't replicate primary sources. Side A also turned CON's own admissions effectively: CON conceded destroyed monuments "simply disappeared from everyday life" (B2.4), and Side A's closing correctly rebranded that as amnesia rather than reckoning. The "clean public ledger" framing CON introduced was met with a sharp counter—a clean ledger in this context means shredding evidence, not resolving injustice. Side B raised legitimate worries about institutional drift and framing shifts, but Side A answered them squarely: democratic accountability works on visible objects, and curator drift is equally possible with any institution managing public memory, including those choosing what replaces a destroyed monument. Side B's closing was short, repetitive, and did not engage Side A's strongest closing arguments, which counted as a real execution cost. Side A's Rebuttal 1 clipping was also a real cost but the most important arguments survived. On balance, Side A maintained cleaner comparative pressure throughout and left more of its case standing at the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's "not mutually exclusive" rebuttal in Rebuttal 1 (A2.1–A2.3) was the most decisive exchange. CON had framed destruction as necessary to "clear space for alternatives," implying preservation sacrifices that clearing. Side A showed this is a false dilemma—the square can be reclaimed, new works installed, and the artifact preserved simultaneously. This directly collapsed the structural center of CON's case, and CON's responses (resource absorption, institutional obligation) never restored the burden because they conceded the logical point and offered only practical concerns against a principled one.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B won because it consistently attacked the most vulnerable assumption in Side A's case—that museum relocation neutralizes a monument's ideological function—and never received a satisfying answer. The "battery" framing argued that heroic scale, iconography, and design purpose persist regardless of setting, and Side A never rebutted this structurally; it kept reasserting that museums document without endorsing, which is a claim about intent, not effect. Side B also effectively turned Side A's Eastern Europe example: the physical act of destruction was itself the historical statement, not a loss of evidence. When Side B's pressure questions exposed that museums sometimes decline these objects, Side A's fallback of "archival storage" handed Side B its strongest closing material—warehoused propaganda serving no educational purpose while remaining available for future revanchist retrieval. Side A's irreversibility argument has genuine force, and the distinction between primary artifacts and secondary documentation is real, but Side B's response—that scale, composition, and iconography are thoroughly documented before demolition—was adequate to reduce the scholarly gap to marginal rather than decisive. Side A ran consistently under word count across multiple rounds, leaving the "battery" argument, the warehoused-propaganda critique, and the community-expression argument all partially unanswered. Side B's closing crystallized the debate cleanly around one question and produced the most memorable line of the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 on the Eastern Europe example [B4.3]: where Side A argued that Stalin statues were photographed precisely because they existed to photograph, Side B returned that the physical destruction was the historical statement—the community found the symbol "unbearable, not merely relocatable." This reframe converted Side A's strongest concrete example into an argument for demolition, and Side A never recovered the example as a positive asset.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by making the central clash about whether material preservation is neutral or itself continues the monument's ideological work. Side B had a coherent preservation case based on primary evidence, reversibility, and museum context, but it never fully answered the harms of institutional custody, pilgrimage, storage without context, and possible future reactivation. Side A also successfully narrowed Side B's evidence benefit to marginal scholarly gains that documentation can largely preserve, while weighing that against ongoing symbolic harm and community closure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal was decisive: it answered the pressure questions directly, then turned Side B's fallback of archival storage into warehoused propaganda and framed physical access as a thin benefit compared with keeping a domination symbol available for future activation.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side A consistently won the clash over the nature of monuments, successfully framing them as active ideological weapons rather than inert historical data. Side A neutralized Side B's 'primary evidence' impact by pointing out the sufficiency of pre-demolition documentation and exploited Side B's fallback to indefinite storage to show they abandoned the 'preservation with context' burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal decisively neutralized the 'measurable data' argument by citing pre-demolition casts and documentation, while weaponizing Side B's admission about archival storage.
