### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): PRO built a cleaner, more internally consistent case across all five rounds. Three decisions stand out. First, PRO's "harvest vs. build" framing—institutions add zero bedrooms while converting ownership opportunities into rental assets—remained unanswered throughout; CON never produced evidence that restricted firms would have added net supply to the relevant owner-occupied stock. Second, PRO's A1 answer to the LLC-compliance pressure was pointed and direct: a threshold-based rule targets large portfolios, compliance attaches to the buyer rather than the seller, and everyday family LLCs are untouched. This defused CON's best enforcement objection cleanly. Third, PRO's Q1 pressure question exposed a genuine tension CON never resolved: if institutions are only buying distressed homes that fail conventional appraisals, they cannot simultaneously be the primary cause of crowding out local buyers in bidding wars on ordinary tight-market homes. CON's A1 tried to explain both, but the explanation half-conceded that the crowding-out mechanism is narrower than their opening claimed, undermining their core critique of the proposition. CON's strongest remaining point—that local buyers are financially maxed out and won't absorb inventory—was real pressure, but PRO answered it adequately: homes in tight markets don't lack demand, they lack demand locals can compete with due to cost-of-capital asymmetries; the price a local family pays is still a market-clearing price, just not a yield-arbitrage premium. CON also repeated "performative restriction" and "static view" across multiple rounds without sharpening them into new comparative weight, while PRO consistently narrowed the clash around the proposition's explicit carve-outs. Side A's clipped opening is a small execution cost, as is the invalid [P1.3, P1.4] reference in the closing, but neither changed the outcome because the underlying arguments were already on the table. Overall, PRO gave the cleaner reason to vote for the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure question was the most decisive single exchange. By asking why institutions win bidding wars against local buyers in tight markets if they mainly renovate distressed homes that fail appraisals, PRO forced CON into an answer that created internal contradiction: CON's A1 essentially conceded institutions buy a different category of home than ordinary owner-occupied stock, which undercut CON's original crowding-out denial while simultaneously weakening their renter-protection argument. PRO then used A4.3 to follow up by showing that the inventory local buyers would absorb is precisely the homes they are currently losing bids on—closing the loop cleanly.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the debate tied to the narrow motion: existing single-family homes in tight markets where locals are crowded out. Side A had plausible concerns about supply, renters, renovation capital, and compliance, but B effectively answered that this is not a substitute for building, it is an interim restriction on extraction of finite owner-occupant stock. B’s responses on threshold rules, buyer-side certification, and local buyers absorbing inventory blunted A’s strongest implementation and liquidity objections. A’s later construction-capital argument was competitive but did not overcome B’s carveout framing for new construction and multifamily investment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it directly answered A’s pressure questions with a workable threshold and certification model, then reframed “liquidity” as Wall Street access rather than resident access, making A’s compliance and seller-harm arguments much less persuasive.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): PRO built a clearer path by keeping the narrow proposition central, directly answering pressure questions on compliance and inventory, and showing that restrictions preserve new builds while stopping extraction of existing stock. CON's liquidity and maintenance arguments were consistently neutralized without evasion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.3) on threshold rules for LLCs and resident buyers absorbing inventory, which directly met CON's questions and sharpened the ownership vs portfolio comparison.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Both sides were competent and well-grounded, but PRO holds a clear edge on the decisive clash. PRO's scale argument — that an institution buying 500 homes is categorically different from one wealthy local buying one home — was the debate's most durable claim. CON's "next highest bidder" rebuttal addresses the per-transaction outcome but never engages the aggregate math: removing a bulk buyer from a 500-home portfolio returns a large block of homes to distributed competition, even if a few still go to cash-rich locals. CON never squarely answered this. On construction financing, CON's point was initially strong, but PRO's A2 response — that genuinely tight markets should generate organic demand sufficient to de-risk construction without requiring bulk pre-sales — was more intuitive and CON's rebuttal (forward commitments versus hindsight demand) required accepting a fairly technical premise about lender behavior that PRO plausibly contested. CON's supply-side framing is structurally sound but somewhat beside the motion, which specifically targets existing inventory in already-tight markets rather than purporting to solve underlying scarcity. PRO correctly exploited that framing gap throughout. CON's closing — "a pool with no more water" — was rhetorically strong but didn't resolve the aggregate crowding-out math. PRO's closing crystallized the live clash cleanly around scale, which was the argument CON most needed to neutralize and never did.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing crystallization of the scale argument [A5.1–A5.2] was the most decisive moment: by pointing out that CON's "next highest bidder" logic only works if institutions buy one house at a time, PRO exposed the structural weakness in CON's entire second-half strategy. CON had built its final speeches around the per-transaction loss (mortgage-dependent family still loses to cash buyer), but PRO correctly reframed it as an aggregate question — removing a 500-home buyer returns 500 homes to broader competition, not just one. CON never answered this reframe, which allowed PRO's core burden to survive intact.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A had the strongest technical rebuttal on supply scarcity and construction financing, but it over-relied on the claim that restrictions do not add square footage, which did not fully answer the motion's allocative concern about local buyers being crowded out. Side B kept the debate focused on institutional scale, all-cash bulk purchasing, and the difference between ordinary competition and industrialized acquisition. Its argument that removing the highest-volume bidder class materially improves local buyers' chances remained live despite Side A's strong objections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's most decisive exchange was the response to Side A's wealthy-local point: a wealthy local may win one home, but an institution buying at scale can remove hundreds from the local buyer pool. Side A answered that scarcity still lets other deep-pocketed buyers win, but did not fully neutralize the marginal crowding-out reduction.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B won by keeping the core scarcity frame intact across rounds, showing the policy neither adds housing nor reliably converts renters to owners while neutralizing A's bulk-buyer math with direct answers on financing and remaining cash competition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.4 and B4.1 exchanges on pre-sales and lender requirements directly undercut A's construction claims.
