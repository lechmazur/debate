### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but CON built the more resilient case. CON's recurring analogy to existing discounts (non-smoker, safe-driver) forced PRO to draw a distinction between "discrete verifiable status" and "continuous biometric telemetry" that was asserted rather than deeply defended, and CON's closing rebuttal ("both reveal health... we police coercion directly") exposed that PRO's coercion logic, taken seriously, would also indict discounts nobody thinks should be banned. CON's "scalpel not sledgehammer" framing—ban punitive coverage uses directly, leave voluntary bounded discounts alone—gave a cleaner, more falsifiable alternative than PRO's repeated invocation of undefined "strict limits," which CON correctly needled as "a ban with better branding" since PRO never operationalized what the limits actually permit in practice. PRO's closing attempt to claim CON "conceded the debate" by admitting punitive uses should be barred overreached: CON's actual concession was narrow (agreeing punitive misuse should be banned) and did not concede the core dispute over voluntary discount programs, which CON's closing correctly clarified as sleight of hand. CON's pressure questions (smoker/driver parity, selective sharing) landed more damage than PRO's questions (which PRO answered adequately but without fully resolving the vagueness charge). Overall CON's case was tighter, more consistently defended across rounds, and ended with the clearer resolution of the central clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point that "we do not ban non-smoker discounts because smokers might feel pressured; we police coercion directly" (B4.4) most damaged PRO's core coercion argument, since PRO's attempted distinction (discrete status vs. continuous telemetry, A4.2) never fully explained why coercion concerns justify a categorical ban here but not elsewhere.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won by making the decisive comparison easier: the motion fuses useful voluntary pricing (step rebates, prevention funding) with genuine coverage abuses, and A repeatedly showed a narrower ban already captures the bad uses PRO fears. PRO’s coercion and socioeconomic-bias points landed cleanly and forced real concessions on risk-shedding, but never justified why government-defined “strict limits” on the whole category beat free contractual opt-in plus surgical rules. A’s non-smoker/safe-driver analogy and selective-share warehouse-worker flip survived pressure better than PRO’s market-power reply, so the vote goes against the proposition’s overbreadth.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s A4/A5 scalpel-vs-sledgehammer turn: admitting claim-denial and risk-shedding must be barred while still defending steps-only rebates, collapsing PRO’s claim that unconstrained market defense requires the full motion.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A successfully neutralized Side B's core examples by explaining how continuous biometric data captures the biological toll of manual labor and how, without strict limits, "discounts" inevitably become coercive baselines. Side A also brilliantly reframed the "strict limits" in the motion as the very mechanism that enables the selective, safe data-sharing Side B championed, trapping Side B in a contradiction regarding coverage decisions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A1 and A2), where they clarified that the motion's strict limits are what make selective "steps-only" sharing possible by legally preventing insurers from demanding full biometric feeds, completely dismantling Side B's claim that the motion bans voluntary discounts.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A built a consistent, cumulative case around one sharp distinction—capped opt-in "perk" versus dynamic "price"—and used it to defuse the auto-telematics analogy, expose that ACA protections don't reach life insurance, and explain why longitudinal averaging entrenches rather than erases structural bias (wheelchair users, night-shift workers, pregnant workers). B's strongest weapon, the "contradiction" charge (opt-in reward vs. penalty framing), landed well in Rebuttal 1 but A answered it directly and specifically in Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.3: capped, non-base-setting reward vs. price-determining discount), and B's Rebuttal 2/Closing simply re-asserted the same "contradiction" and "fixed cost is false" claims without engaging that clarification, leaving the pooling/distribution argument unrebutted. B's pressure-question answers were adequately formatted and substantively useful (naming concrete privacy limits it would accept), but its closing repeated prior points rather than progressing the clash, while A's closing crystallized the decisive comparison cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1's "contradiction" charge (opt-in rewards vs. calling discounts penalties) was the sharpest pressure point, but A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.3) neutralized it by drawing a clear line between a capped reward that cannot set the base rate/deny coverage and unconstrained dynamic pricing that functions as the price of coverage—an answer B never squarely engaged with afterward.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): B (PRO) built the clearer path on the motion as written: wearables encode circumstance more than controllable choice, default pricing use makes “opt-out” a de facto surcharge, and a capped opt-in perk that cannot raise base rates or deny coverage is not the same as underwriting by streams. A landed a sharp contradiction attack and a real preventative/adverse-selection story, but repeatedly overclaimed that meaningful discounts never transfer cost, that longitudinal trends erase bias, and that privacy lite suffices. B’s perk-vs-price distinction and pressure answers resolved the clash more cleanly than A’s restatement that risk reduction grows the pool.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1–B4.3 dissolving A’s contradiction: opt-in fitness reward stays fair only if it cannot set survival price, raise base premiums, or deny coverage—turning A’s “penalty by another name” hammer into a distinction that maps onto the proposition.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully defending the specific mechanism of the proposition against Side B's charge of contradiction, clearly distinguishing between a capped opt-in "perk" and a pricing model that penalizes opting out. Side A also grounds its case in highly plausible examples of how wearable data captures circumstances rather than choices, effectively neutralizing Side B's argument that wearables measure controllable behavior. While Side B makes a strong case about the invasiveness of traditional underwriting, its reliance on the assumption that algorithms can fairly measure personal improvement is less grounded than Side A's critique of sensor bias and surveillance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it resolves the alleged contradiction by distinguishing between a "perk" and a "price," and dismantles the claim that longitudinal tracking solves bias by pointing out that averaging months of data just certifies structural disadvantage with more precision.
