### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): PRO built the stronger cumulative case. Its three-pillar opening—inequality from family transmission, self-selection bias in electives, and critical timing—was tightly aligned with the proposition throughout. The "integration is dilution" framing [A2.3–A2.4] was the most decisive argumentative move in the debate: it converted CON's alternative into an admission that financial literacy would be fragmented, optional in practice, and subordinated to each host subject's primary demands. CON never fully neutralized this. CON's integration alternative became clearer in R2 (cross-curricular benchmarks across required courses), but PRO's crystallization—"everyone's business in theory, nobody's responsibility in practice"—exposed that enforcement mechanism as underspecified. CON's strongest ground was the teacher-shortage and compliance-theater argument, which PRO answered only with "resource the requirement like math," a somewhat hand-wavy response. However, CON's pipeline distinction (English/math have century-old infrastructure) in R2 was a genuine answer to PRO's symmetry pressure question and was the best move CON made. Still, that distinction actually reinforces PRO's mandate logic: we built those pipelines because we mandated the subjects, and a mandate for financial literacy would do the same. The diploma-denial argument was emotionally salient but logically self-undermining—by CON's own logic, addressing teacher shortages and building infrastructure is the answer, which is exactly what PRO argued. PRO's closing turned the implementation objections into affirmative arguments for the mandate, landing the cleaner final frame. CON's "shield to sword" image was memorable, but the underlying inference—that we should therefore do integration instead—left the enforcement problem unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's "everyone's business in theory, nobody's responsibility in practice" line [A4.5], responding to CON's integration alternative. It identified the enforcement gap in CON's cross-curricular benchmark proposal, showed that diffuse responsibility produces no accountability, and reframed the whole debate around which mechanism actually guarantees coverage for the most vulnerable students. CON's R2 response tried to specify that benchmarks would be mandatory across required courses, but never explained who monitors compliance or what happens when districts ignore them, leaving PRO's attack substantially live through the close.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): A won narrowly. B made a strong case that financial literacy matters and that family or elective exposure entrenches inequality, but too often treated A’s position as the failed status quo. A offered a clearer comparative alternative: required integration across existing courses gives repeated exposure without creating a new standalone graduation gate. A also pressed the implementation burden more effectively, especially teacher shortages, compliance-theater risks, and diploma denial for vulnerable students. B’s “resource the requirement” answer was rhetorically strong but underdeveloped.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was the decisive exchange: it answered B’s pressure questions directly, distinguished established English and math mandates from a new financial-literacy gate, and reframed the mandate as a potential bureaucratic sword rather than a shield.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): B wins by making the diploma-denial risk and implementation gap durable points that A never neutralizes; B's integration alternative repeatedly showed better practical equity and spaced learning while A's shield framing stayed aspirational.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.5 and B4.2 directly turn A's "shield" into a bureaucratic sword via teacher shortages and transient students, which A never resolves.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A built a cleaner, more coherent case throughout. Its equity-lottery framing—that family background functions as an inherited caste marker while electives self-select the already-privileged—was introduced early, sustained across every round, and never effectively countered by CON. CON's responses kept retreating to implementation ("schools can't hire Wall Street educators") rather than engaging the principled claim that all students deserve a baseline floor. Side A correctly labeled that as an argument for better funding, not for abandoning the requirement. Side A also landed the sharpest exchange of the debate with Q1: it exposed a genuine internal contradiction in CON's case—CON simultaneously argued that rigorous math is a better predictor of financial health and that financial decisions are too behavioral and emotional for classroom instruction. CON's A1 response was evasive, essentially restating both claims without resolving the tension. CON's strongest argument was the overconfidence/illusion-of-competency concern, which was original and real. But Side A adequately neutralized it by distinguishing shallow quizzes from a serious applied course and noting that the status quo already mass-produces dangerous overconfidence via informal channels. CON's empirical grounding for that argument ("robust educational research shows") was somewhat brittle—presented with confident specificity in a closed-book format without real sourcing. CON's zero-sum and decay arguments were both addressed cleanly by Side A with historical examples (computer literacy, civics) and the observation that financial knowledge is actually applied sooner than most mandated subjects. Side A's closing crystallized the debate memorably: "A course that is imperfect can be improved; a requirement that does not exist cannot." CON's closing was well-phrased but largely recapped earlier points without sharpening the comparative choice. On balance, Side A won more of the decisive clashes with less reliance on unsupported empirical assertions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Q1 pressure question exposed the central contradiction in CON's case: CON cannot simultaneously claim that rigorous math is a superior predictor of financial health and that financial decisions are too behavioral and emotional to be addressed in a classroom. CON's A1 response in Rebuttal 2 failed to resolve this tension, instead restating that math builds "generalized cognitive capacity" while finance is "emotional"—an answer that actually deepened the contradiction rather than dissolving it, since it implies even math cannot tame the emotional nature of financial decisions.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the debate tied to the motion’s requirement of a serious course and repeatedly showing why electives and family background preserve an inequitable lottery. Side A had plausible concerns about decay, curriculum tradeoffs, and bad implementation, but leaned heavily on unsupported empirical claims about near-zero impact and overconfidence, and often attacked shallow plug-and-play courses rather than the serious course in the proposition. B’s answers that financial concepts are used soon, durable frameworks can be taught, math complements rather than replaces domain knowledge, and implementation flaws justify rigor rather than abandonment left the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was against the implementation/overconfidence objection: A argued mandates become shallow and dangerous, but B reframed that as an indictment of bad courses, not of requiring a serious one, and weighed it against the existing inequity of leaving students dependent on family privilege.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A built a stronger case by effectively countering the decay and overconfidence arguments with comparisons to other required subjects and direct answers to pressure questions, highlighting equity and implementation feasibility.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange on overconfidence and zero-sum curriculum in Rebuttal 2, where A clarified serious course builds calibrated competence while B repeated prior points.
