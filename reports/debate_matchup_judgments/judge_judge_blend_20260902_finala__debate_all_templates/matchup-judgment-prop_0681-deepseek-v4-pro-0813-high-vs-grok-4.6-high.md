PRO's triage framework—route violence/medical emergencies to police/EMS, shift the broad nonviolent pool to clinicians—was more grounded in well-known real-world harms of police responding to psychiatric crises, and PRO used CON's concession about handcuffs as effective leverage in the final rebuttal and closing, showing sharper adaptation. PRO also gave a more concrete, non-evasive threshold answer to the pressure question (naming active violence, overdose, and imminent self-harm as exceptions), while CON's threshold answer ("zero tolerance for ZIP-code delay") was more rigid and less responsive to the tradeoff the motion explicitly poses. On balance, PRO's case was slightly better grounded and slightly more responsive under pressure, giving it a clear but not dominant edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (B4) turning CON's own admission that "fast handcuffs are a real harm" into proof that speed cannot be treated as safety, later reinforced in PRO's closing, was the most decisive single exchange, since CON never fully escaped the bind of conceding real police-caused harm while insisting any added delay was categorically worse.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively establishing that physical presence is a prerequisite for any intervention, successfully framing the delay as a fatal "empty curb." Side A conceded that police aren't perfect diagnosticians but successfully argued that they can still perform immediate physical interventions (CPR, grabbing someone off a ledge) that an empty curb cannot.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases around the core clash of "speed vs. fit of responder," but PRO more directly embraced the resolution's stipulated tradeoff and sustained a sharper comparative frame: a fast armed response can also be a failure (force, criminalization, death), so speed alone cannot be privileged as the safety metric. CON's central mitigation—co-response and staged mobile teams that "keep speed as a floor"—was never squarely defended against PRO's repeated point that co-response still puts police at first contact, thereby preserving the exact harm the motion is meant to correct. That gap let PRO's closing line ("if an officer is early, the moment can still be gone—and now the person is on the floor") stand largely unanswered as the decisive comparative image. CON's case also relied on treating the resolution's built-in tradeoff as avoidable via alternatives, which is a legitimate rebuttal strategy but never fully closed the loop on why those alternatives don't just reproduce the status quo harm PRO identifies. Additionally, CON's Rebuttal 1 was flagged as clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost per the judging rules. Pressure Questions and answers were handled competently and correctly formatted by both sides, but PRO's answers (A1/A2) more directly neutralized CON's "maximum acceptable delay" framing without conceding ground, while CON's answers restated its plan without resolving PRO's rebuttal about police remaining first contact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal that co-response "still puts police at first contact" and therefore "preserves the default the motion rejects" (B2.5), which CON never squarely answered in Rebuttal 2 or Closing, leaving PRO's core distinction between "who arrives first" and "who should arrive at all" as the more decisive framing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully exposing an asymmetry in Side A's case: while Side A effectively argued that a delayed clinician might arrive too late to stop a tragedy, Side B pointed out that a fast police officer can also fail to stop it or actively cause the harm. Side B also cleanly turned Side A's equity argument by noting that the vulnerable neighborhoods currently bear the brunt of armed, rapid-response trauma.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing crystallization that Side A 'counts only the first failure' (a delayed arrival) while giving a free pass to the harms of a fast, armed arrival.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by defending the core tradeoff more convincingly: a slower but medically appropriate response outweighs a faster armed response that often produces force, trauma, and criminalization. PRO neutralized CON's equity and time-sensitivity objections by showing that high-need neighborhoods already suffer the worst outcomes from rapid police contact, and that CON's insistence on maintaining police-speed-as-a-floor merely preserves that inequitable status quo. CON's clipped first rebuttal and reliance on a co-response alternative that sidestepped the motion's explicit condition weakened their path, while PRO handled pressure questions more directly and crystallized the decisive clash in closing: speed is not prevention, and the wrong first responder can close the window just as surely as a slow one—but leaves the person in cuffs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's flip of CON's equity argument, showing that the current system already forces the most vulnerable neighborhoods into a two-tier arrangement of fastest police contact and deepest downstream harm, making CON's speed-floor a defense of the inequity rather than a solution to it.
