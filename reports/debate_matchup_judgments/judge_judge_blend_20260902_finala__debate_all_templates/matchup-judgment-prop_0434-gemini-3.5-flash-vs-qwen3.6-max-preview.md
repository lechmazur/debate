### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): PRO edges out CON on balance, primarily because the motion is modest ("more than they currently can," not "replace all human judgment"), and PRO's central narrative—that chronic noise victims have no reliable path to relief under the status quo—was never squarely neutralized. CON's strongest technical hits (certification validates factory calibration, not apartment placement; asymmetric landlord incentives; burden inversion in nuisance law) are genuinely sharp and represent the best work in the debate, but they tend to overpredict how tribunals would mechanically apply acoustic data. CON also created a mild internal tension by arguing simultaneously that the data is unreliable (placement issues) and that it will be over-relied upon (anchoring effects), without fully reconciling how courts would both distrust the evidence's accuracy and treat it as a strict-liability trigger. PRO's pressure question Q2—noting that the status quo already allows weaponized unverified complaints against vulnerable tenants—landed a real blow that CON never fully absorbed; CON's Rebuttal 2 A1 (existing mechanisms already go beyond pure subjectivity) was the closest answer, but it didn't address the asymmetry PRO identified. PRO's speculative claim about diagnostic software detecting pipe-coupling fraud was the weakest moment on that side, left inadequately grounded. On net, PRO's rhetorical coherence, the modest framing of the motion, and the unanswered victim-empowerment argument outweigh CON's technically superior but sometimes over-extrapolating rebuttal work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 placement argument [B2.1]—distinguishing factory certification from in-apartment installation and observing that a tamper-proof log records decibels but not source or building defects—was the most decisive single exchange. It landed a concrete, verifiable point that PRO never fully resolved; the counter about diagnostic software detecting acoustic signatures was plausible but speculative rather than grounded, leaving CON's challenge substantially intact through the closing.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A wins by exposing that certification cannot guarantee placement integrity or prevent incentive-driven misuse by landlords, while showing that numbers silently invert the reasonableness burden. Side B’s shield claim and tamper-proof assurances remain underdeveloped after direct pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s A1/A2 answers to B’s pressure questions directly neutralized the “shield” and “strict placement” claims, forcing the debate onto real-world deployment asymmetries that B never fully recovered from.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): CON wins by exposing that PRO's "objective" evidence does not actually resolve the contextual problem at the heart of nuisance law and risks inverting the burden of proof. CON sustained a clear live route: certified decibel logs decontextualize noise, cannot reliably distinguish sources without placement oversight, and will anchor tribunal decisions in a way that shifts the burden to accused tenants to prove their daily living is reasonable. PRO never squarely answered why a private sensor reading would not become a strict-liability pretext for landlords seeking frictionless non-renewals, nor why complainant-generated data would not asymmetrically dominate over defensive use. PRO's answers to pressure questions relied on brittle unsupported precision about diagnostic software and acoustic signatures "easily flagging" tampering, which undermined epistemic discipline. PRO's core narrative about empowering victims remained emotionally resonant, but CON showed that expanding admissibility mechanizes disputes rather than democratizing them, and this comparative frame held through the final rebuttal and closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4), where it answered PRO's pressure questions and crystallized the "illusion of neutrality"—showing that admitting certified numbers would silently invert nuisance law by shifting the burden to the accused to explain acoustic volume, rather than requiring the complainant to prove unreasonable interference.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A built the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Its decisive move was the "admissibility is not automation" reframe, introduced in Rebuttal 1 and consistently defended: certified data gives tribunals a factual baseline; judges still apply reasonableness, exemptions, and accommodation law. CON never squarely answered this. Every time CON argued that infants or night-shift workers would be penalized, PRO correctly pointed out that CON was confusing data admission with automatic verdicts, and that darkness currently forecloses the contextual analysis CON claims to want. PRO's strongest original insight—that decibel-only logging is actually more privacy-protective than the raw audio recordings tenants currently make just to be heard—went unanswered. PRO also effectively turned the inequality argument: the status quo, where landlords dismiss complaints as subjective and poor tenants cannot afford attorneys to prove habitability breaches, already harms the vulnerable most. CON's best sustained pressure was the certification-standards dilemma (cheap devices = unreliable; expensive devices = exclusive to the wealthy), and this genuinely complicated PRO's library-loan claim. PRO's A1 response—sealed firmware, automated timestamping, read-only memory, analogized to dashboard cameras and temperature loggers—was concrete and plausible enough to blunt the attack without closing it entirely. CON's alternative (existing channels: code enforcement, police dispatch, landlord mediation) was directly attacked as already-failed systems, and CON's defense of those alternatives in A2 was thin. CON's rhetoric ("mechanical tyranny," "algorithmic prosecution") was vivid but repeatedly overstated the motion, treating expanded admissibility as if it meant automated eviction. PRO maintained accurate framing of the proposition throughout. The margin is real but not dominant: CON's practical-burden-shift argument and the cost-of-certification dilemma were genuine live pressures that PRO answered plausibly but not conclusively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was PRO's Rebuttal 1 on "context-blindness" [A2.5–A2.7]: PRO pointed out that CON was attacking a strawman—admitting evidence does not strip tribunals of discretion, it enables them. "A certified log establishes magnitude, frequency, and duration. The judge still weighs reasonableness, time of day, and necessary life sounds." CON's repeated invocation of crying infants and medical ventilators never recovered from this, because CON never showed why judicial discretion would disappear merely because better data was available. PRO then sharpened it in Rebuttal 2 by noting that victims currently record raw audio to be heard at all, which invades privacy more than decibel-only logging—a turn CON ignored entirely.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B wins by building the clearer path: their opening framed admissibility as empowering existing discretion, and they consistently rebutted inequality, context, and surveillance concerns by contrasting with the status quo while directly answering pressure questions on certification and privacy. Side A repeated practical-burden and vulnerability arguments without neutralizing those responses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2) cleanly resolved the pressure questions on chain-of-custody and distinguishing protected activity, sharpening the comparison that data activates rather than replaces human judgment.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger path to victory by showing that certified evidence gives tribunals a factual baseline without stripping judicial discretion. PRO effectively rebutted CON's inequality claim by arguing the status quo already harms low-income victims who cannot prove chronic noise, and that certified logs democratize proof rather than creating a high-tech arms race. PRO's answers to pressure questions were direct and damaging: sealed firmware and secure uploads address chain-of-custody concerns, and decibel-only logs actually protect privacy better than the raw audio recordings victims currently resort to. CON's context-blindness objection was blunted by PRO's compelling point that without verifiable data, complaints are dismissed entirely, so appeals to "human context" merely serve as impunity for chronic disruptors. CON's alternative of police and code enforcement failed to address PRO's opening argument that stretched municipal services cannot catch episodic violations. While CON's warnings about surveillance and practical burden-shifting were rhetorically sharp, they were insufficiently developed to overcome PRO's systematic rebuttals and clearer comparative weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal to the context-blindness objection, demonstrating that certified logs provide magnitude, duration, and timing so judges can still apply reasonableness standards to crying infants or medical devices, while the current evidentiary vacuum simply causes complaints to be dismissed—making CON's appeal to human context a shield for unaccountable disruption.
