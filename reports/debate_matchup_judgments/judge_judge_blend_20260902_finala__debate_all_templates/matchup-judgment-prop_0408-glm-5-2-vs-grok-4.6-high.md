### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but PRO (B) built the more coherent path to victory. B consistently tied its case to the equity mechanism (family background as the real divide) that CON itself conceded, offered concrete specifics on what a standalone course teaches that math/reading do not (locating APR, origination fees, withholding lines), and explicitly defended a "harm reduction, not perfection" standard that neutralized CON's driver's-ed disanalogy attempt. Under pressure, B answered Q1/Q2 with a clear, on-point A1/A2 structure and substantive content. CON (A) delivered punchy, memorable rhetoric ("reach without transfer," "coverage is not capability") and a genuinely sharp structural point (no "road test" gates the financial-literacy course the way driving tests gate driver's ed), but its answer to B's pressure question about the pre-income gap punted to future systemic reforms (income-sensitive credit, simpler paperwork) that schools cannot implement, leaving CON without a concrete near-term alternative to the very gap it conceded exists. CON's core claim — that recognition without a cheaper path changes nothing — is also an unfalsifiable zero-effect assertion that B explicitly called out as proving too much (it would kill every graduation requirement, including CON's own preferred algebra/civics mandate). B's case was slightly weakened by two clipped turns (opening and closing) versus CON's one, but the lost content was mostly rhetorical flourish rather than substantive argument, so the execution cost is real but modest. On balance, B's tighter linkage of equity, concrete mechanism, and appropriate burden of proof gives it a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to CON's own pressure question (A2, Turn 7) — reframing the standard as "reduced likelihood of the worst outcome" rather than guaranteed refusal, and then in B4.5 turning CON's skepticism standard against CON's own preferred requirements (algebra, civics) — was the most decisive exchange, since CON never fully recovered a concrete mechanism for the pre-income gap it had conceded.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully exposing Side B's standard of "guaranteed refusal" as an impossibly high bar that would invalidate all education. - **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO successfully defended that baseline, universal document practice is better than sorted elective exposure or reliance on unequal family background, and showed that CON’s perfectionist “theater” standard would dismiss every graduation requirement. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON built its case around a single, sustained empirical claim—that classroom financial-literacy instruction reliably fails to change real borrowing, saving, and default behavior—and used that claim consistently across rebuttals, pressure questions, and closing to argue that a mandate would be "box-checking with a syllabus." PRO never directly contested that evidentiary claim; instead it reframed the target from distant mortgages to immediate post-graduation documents (W-2s, leases, first paychecks), which was a reasonable and partially effective move, and it scored a genuine hit by noting the tension in CON's position (a semester is "too weak" but a five-minute disclosure will work). However, CON's answer—that timing/salience, not depth, makes guidance stick, and that disclosures don't require the same behavioral transfer a course promises—held up well enough to blunt that tension, and CON kept pressing that PRO's benefit had narrowed to a thin slice of cases (the parentless student facing an imminent contract) without ever rebutting the core "it doesn't change behavior" claim. PRO's equity/sorting argument is compelling as a normative frame, but it assumes the course actually works, which is precisely what went unanswered. Both sides argued cleanly within the evidence rules, executed the pressure-question format correctly, and wrote crisp, quotable closings, but CON's throughline was tighter and less contested on its central empirical premise, giving it the more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange around [B4.4] (PRO: "CON cannot hold both that a semester is too weak to change decisions and that a five-minute lender disclosure will do the same work") and CON's answer in [A4.4] (timing, not depth, is what makes guidance stick) was the sharpest clash — PRO exposed a real tension, but CON's response was just sufficient to prevent it from being fatal, keeping CON's efficacy argument the load-bearing point of the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A wins by systematically dismantling Side B's alternatives. When B relies on parental cosigners, A points out this perpetuates the exact "family luck" inequality the motion seeks to solve. When B relies on 5-minute disclosures, A exposes the contradiction of claiming a semester course is too weak to teach a student while a brief disclosure at the point of sale will succeed. A successfully shifts the timeline to immediate post-graduation decisions (W-2s, first paychecks), neutralizing B's primary offensive argument about knowledge decay.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal (A4.4) masterfully traps B in a contradiction, noting that B cannot hold both that a semester is too weak to change decisions and that a five-minute lender disclosure will do the same work.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing that the proposed mandatory course lacks proven behavioral impact, and PRO never squarely rebutted the evidence that financial-literacy requirements produce only temporary knowledge gains without improving actual borrowing, saving, or default decisions. CON successfully narrowed PRO’s benefit to a shrinking slice of students and decisions, offered a plausible just-in-time alternative, and left PRO’s universal mandate without a validated causal mechanism. PRO’s rhetoric remained polished, but its core assumption—that the course changes real financial behavior—was left undefended against CON’s sustained pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s rebuttal that classroom financial knowledge fails to transfer to real borrowing and saving decisions, exposing PRO’s progressive retreat from “all adult decisions” to “first documents next month” to a narrow subset of unsupervised graduates, and showing that a timed disclosure at the point of signing outperforms a forgotten lecture.
