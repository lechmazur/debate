### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): PRO (Side B) holds a commanding advantage on moral framing and rhetoric throughout, and its "veto over annihilation" formulation is the most memorable argument in the debate. However, CON (Side A) holds the live route to victory on the decisive practical clash. The central question the motion poses is not merely whether SIDS deserve a stronger voice—PRO wins that easily—but whether this specific weighted-voting mechanism *should be granted*, implying workability matters. CON's withdrawal/bilateral argument (major emitters would defect to bilateral deals where SIDS have zero leverage) is the core of its case, and PRO never neutralizes it. PRO's answer—that emitters threatening to leave proves they were never committed—is emotionally satisfying but logically incomplete: even imperfect multilateral participation is better for SIDS than bilateral dependency, and PRO never shows otherwise. CON then sharpens this in its second rebuttal by turning PRO's own concession ("bilateralism reduces SIDS to supplicants") against PRO, creating a genuine self-refutation. CON's metric-workability argument—that adaptive capacity makes existential risk non-purely-physical—was never fully answered; PRO's "geography doesn't negotiate" reply fails to account for varying sovereignty-via-relocation scenarios that CON highlighted. The Global South coalition-fracturing argument (SIDS weighted power vs. Bangladesh, Sahel, etc.) is also live and unanswered. PRO's rhetoric is superior, but rhetoric here amplifies a moral case that CON never disputed in principle; the practical consequence argument is where the debate was actually decided, and CON wins it on balance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal [A4.2] turns PRO's own A2 answer ("bilateral deals make SIDS supplicants") directly against PRO: since PRO admitted bilateralism strips SIDS of leverage, and CON argues weighted voting would drive emitters to bilateral deals, PRO's proposed remedy would produce the very helplessness PRO warns against. This is the sharpest piece of judo in the debate and was never adequately answered in PRO's closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully defending the objectivity of the "existential risk" metric and offering superior comparative weighing. Side B's strongest point was that giving SIDS a veto would cause major emitters to abandon multilateralism. However, Side A effectively neutralized this by arguing that if the status quo guarantees extinction, risking procedural collapse is a necessary tradeoff. Side A's decisive refutation of Side B's "adaptive capacity" argument—pointing out that relocated populations lose sovereign territory and become "refugee camps with flags"—firmly secured the conceptual core of the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's argument that "wealth to relocate" mitigates existential risk. A correctly pointed out that sovereignty requires territory, fundamentally defeating B's attempt to frame the risk metric as subjective.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): Side A prevailed by establishing a decisive trade-off: weighted veto power would trigger emitter withdrawal into bilateral deals where SIDS lose all leverage, collapsing the universal framework needed for actual emissions cuts. Side B's responses labeled this 'impunity' or 'broken system' but did not neutralize the loss of protection mechanism. Side A further showed coalition fracture among vulnerable states. Side B's existential framing and rhetoric were strong but rested on weaker answers to withdrawal and metric subjectivity after pressure rounds.
