### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): CON built and sustained a clean line of attack throughout the whole debate: if the proposition requires someone to sort "protected religious convictions" from mere "lifestyle preferences," who makes that call and by what standard? PRO's Rebuttal 2 answered Q1 by defaulting to existing civil rights law, which was honest but self-damaging — it explicitly concedes that a secular vegan family gets no institutional protection while a religiously identical dietary practice does. CON had been setting this trap since Rebuttal 1, and the closing crystallized it efficiently. PRO never recovered from this because the motion's key term ("mainly personal lifestyle preferences") demands exactly the kind of discriminatory line-drawing CON warned against. CON also handled the IEP pressure question adequately: the analogy was imperfect but the underlying point — that a verbal reminder is not administrative paralysis — was plausible and PRO's rejoinder ("IEPs have funded staff") did not fully close the gap. PRO's strongest moments were the cumulative-burden argument and the teacher-as-compliance-officer framing, both of which have intuitive merit, but these were largely neutralized by CON's observation that schools already navigate profound individual diversity. PRO's "providing the option without enforcement" distinction in Rebuttal 2 was clever on child autonomy but left the deception/family-fracture problem unresolved. Overall CON made the decisive clash clearer, answered pressure more directly, and left a live route to victory that PRO never squarely neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 pivot on the civil rights law answer: PRO's A1 response explicitly tied institutional legitimacy to existing legal protections, which concedes that religiously motivated dietary rules get accommodation while an ethically identical secular rule does not. CON immediately turned this into affirmative evidence of the discrimination it had warned about since Rebuttal 1, and PRO had no meaningful answer left in the round.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the debate tied to the motion: reducing parents' power to use schools and teachers as enforcers of highly restrictive personal preferences, not abolishing all parental values. B’s teacher-burden, equity, common-ground, and child-autonomy claims remained live. A’s pluralism and secular-conviction objection was strong, but A overextended the motion into core religious/cultural identity and never gave a workable limiting principle for when demands become too burdensome.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive rebuttal was the cumulative-burden/IEP contrast and pressure on manageability: A answered that the classroom “never” becomes unmanageable and reduced enforcement to quick lunch reminders, leaving B’s concern about endless social, dress, and dietary policing largely intact.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): CON won by exposing a structural contradiction in PRO's framework: PRO defended accommodating religious dietary restrictions under civil rights law while dismissing identical secular ethical boundaries as mere "personal lifestyle preferences." This two-tier standard undermined PRO's central claim to "neutrality" and showed their "common good" to be a state-enforced monoculture that privileges certain convictions. CON also effectively minimized PRO's administrative-paralysis scenario with concrete, low-cost examples of accommodation, and turned PRO's autonomy argument by demonstrating that schools knowingly providing forbidden options fractures family trust and encourages deception rather than healthy independence. PRO's rhetoric was polished, but the secular-vs-religious inconsistency proved fatal to their case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's sustained attack on PRO's religious-versus-secular distinction across Rebuttal 1, Rebuttal 2, and Closing, which revealed that PRO's "neutral ground" required schools to respect identical dietary restrictions for religious families while overriding them for secular families, constituting institutional bias rather than pluralism.
