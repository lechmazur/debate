### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but Con exposed and sustained the decisive weakness in Pro's proposal: the proposition's actual text ("heavy beauty retouching," "AI-generated body enhancement") is inherently vague, and Pro's repeated attempt to narrow it to "wholesale reconstruction" was itself a definitional move Con directly called out in Pressure Questions ("will you defend the text as written, or concede it sweeps in ordinary smartphone portrait mode?"). Pro's answer largely reasserted its preferred reading rather than grounding it in the actual language, leaving the vagueness charge only partially answered. Con also landed a sharp, underived-from-new-evidence distinction between the #ad analogy (a binary, checkable fact) and a "heavy"/AI-enhancement label (a subjective judgment call), which Pro never fully neutralized, and used this to build a persuasive asymmetric-burden narrative (small creators over-comply or vanish; conglomerates absorb costs easily). Con further pressed that Pro conceded the disclosure benefit is speculative ("will not turn scrollers into media critics"), while the compliance harm is concrete—an asymmetry Con kept returning to with increasing clarity across rounds. Pro's case remained emotionally resonant and causally plausible on the harm side, and its rebuttals were competent, but it spent most of its capital defending definitional boundaries rather than advancing new comparative weighing, letting Con set and control the terms of the central clash by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Con's Rebuttal 1 quoting Pro's shift from the proposition's text to "wholesale reconstruction," reinforced by the Pressure Question forcing Pro to defend the text as written—Pro's answer narrowed rather than resolved the ambiguity, and Con's follow-up distinguishing the objective #ad standard from the subjective "heavy" standard was never squarely rebutted.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing a plausible, immediate harm to vulnerable minors and presenting disclosure as a narrow response, while Side A relied heavily on speculative claims that ambiguity would price out or silence small creators. A exposed genuine line-drawing problems, especially for AI enhancement, but never showed why reasonable definitions or cautious labeling would produce costs outweighing the undisclosed illusion. Its parental-guidance alternative also failed to match disclosure at the point of exposure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A sharply distinguished binary #ad disclosure from the degree-based judgment of whether alteration is heavy. Side B nevertheless preserved its case by confining the rule to deliberate, substantial reconstruction and emphasizing that imperfect disclosure still interrupts the false presentation at the moment minors see it.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): PRO kept the proposition as a disclosure mandate, not a content ban, and anchored "heavy" to wholesale reconstruction plus existing deceptive-advertising standards and #ad practice. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-organized cases, but PRO's pressure questions did more real damage than CON's. PRO exposed a genuine internal tension in CON's case: CON simultaneously argued that minors already decode influencer perfection as fiction (so disclosure is unnecessary) and that disclosure labels become ignored fine print that still somehow chills speech. CON's rebuttal (reframing the chill as a production/compliance-stage cost rather than a viewer-reception cost) was a legitimate but only partial recovery, since it implicitly conceded the disclosure mandate would deliver little of the protective benefit PRO claims while still imposing costs — a concession PRO's closing exploited effectively by framing CON's position as internally incoherent ("too immune to need it, too fragile to survive it"). CON's own pressure question about an "exact objective threshold" was answered with a plausible if imperfect analogy to existing vague legal standards (obscenity, deceptive advertising), which is a reasonable move in a closed-book debate even though CON's spectrum objection was never fully neutralized. On balance, PRO's rebuttals were tighter, its closing crystallized the clash more sharply, and it handled the Q&A exchange with slightly more logical precision, giving it a clear but not overwhelming edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question exposing the contradiction between CON's claim that minors already decode influencer imagery as fiction and CON's claim that disclosure labels would be ignored yet still chilling — and PRO's follow-up in Rebuttal 2 turning that tension into "if labels are truly ignored noise, CON cannot simultaneously claim they chill creativity" — was the most decisive exchange, forcing CON into a narrower, less satisfying reframing of its own chilling argument.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B won by presenting disclosure as a narrow honesty requirement and showing that definitional imperfection and multifactorial harm do not justify inaction. Side A raised credible concerns about vague boundaries, compliance costs, and label fatigue, but largely asserted that children already recognize fabrication and that media literacy would work better. Those claims did not outweigh B’s plausible mechanism that explicit disclosure supplies missing context to vulnerable viewers. B also offered the clearer comparative burden: advertisers can avoid or disclose material body fabrication, whereas A shifts protection onto children and families.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest response was that disclosure need not eliminate body dissatisfaction or rewire adolescent psychology; it only needs to change an impossible body from an apparent peer comparison into identified commercial fabrication. That directly answered Side A’s label-effectiveness objection and made the limited benefit sufficient relative to the modest duty.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): PRO wins the central trade: CON claims heavy retouching is both undefinable yet inevitably over-labeled, and that kids already see it as fiction yet disclosure chills creativity. A's Pressure Questions spot both contradictions and A4 answers them cleanly — workable legal standards like "materially fabricated" plus platforms already detect manipulation, and if labels are ignored noise they cannot be a speech ban. That leaves CON's chill and multifactor-harm arguments without a live route, while PRO keeps the narrow commercial-speech-for-minors frame and harm mechanism intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Q2/A2 on CON holding that kids decode fiction so labels are noise while also claiming disclosure chills speech — crystallized in A4.3 "speech that is unnoticed is not censored" — damaged CON's effectiveness + chill pair and was never fully repaired.
