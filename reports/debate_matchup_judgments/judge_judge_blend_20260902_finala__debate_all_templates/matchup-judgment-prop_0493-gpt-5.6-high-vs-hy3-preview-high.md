### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO built a precise, textually faithful case: the motion bans corporate ownership/extraction specifically "for commercial bottling," which PRO used from Rebuttal 1 onward to cleanly separate legitimate private infrastructure services (engineering, leak repair, logistics, filtration) from the one activity being banned. This distinction directly undercut CON's central efficiency argument, which repeatedly treated the ban as equivalent to banning all private participation in water systems—a scope drift CON never fully corrected. PRO also introduced a sophisticated, underrebutted point about tradable extraction rights creating future legal leverage against regulation, and consistently weighed irreversibility (depleted aquifers) against substitutability (contracted expertise/capital), giving a clean decision rule. CON's answers to pressure questions largely reasserted that ownership is necessary for incentive alignment without engaging PRO's contract/availability-payment alternative, and CON's opening and second rebuttal were both clipped for exceeding word limits, a real execution cost per the judging rules that cost CON developed content in exactly the rounds where rebuttal work needed to land. PRO's closing crystallized the clash (necessity vs. substitutability) more sharply than CON's, which repeated prior assertions about capital flight without directly resolving PRO's scope distinction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 point [A2.1]-[A2.2] distinguishing "contracting for a service" from "alienating the source," reinforced by the pressure-question exchange (A3.1 vs A4.1), was the pivotal clash: CON's efficiency case never recovered from having to concede that expertise and capital can be procured without extraction rights.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A built the clearer path by insisting on the proposition's narrow scope and proving that capital, expertise, logistics, and emergency capacity are fully separable from private ownership/trading/extraction for commercial bottling. Side B repeatedly treated those benefits as inseparable from bottling rights, failed to answer the non-sequitur on pipe leaks and disasters, and suffered clipped turns that left key comparisons incomplete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 category-error distinction (services vs alienating the source) plus direct A1/A2 answers showing contracts fund innovation without extraction rights.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side B won decisively by exposing and exploiting Side A's core category error. Side A repeatedly conflated private contracting for water infrastructure (fixing leaks, building treatment plants) with private extraction of aquifers for commercial bottling. Side B consistently clarified that the proposition only bans the latter, dismantling Side A's argument that a ban would halt private investment in pipe repair or disaster logistics. Side A never successfully bridged the logical gap between needing private engineering and needing to sell bottled water, and suffered execution costs from being clipped in multiple rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where it cleanly separated "contracting for a service" from "alienating the asset," effectively neutralizing Side A's entire infrastructure-based offense.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON built the more resilient analytical structure: it consistently reframed the debate around measurable harm and enforceable limits rather than corporate identity, and it exploited two decisive weaknesses in PRO's case that were never squarely resolved. First, CON's pressure question (B3.2) asking what concrete harm exists in a sustainable-withdrawal scenario was never answered with anything beyond speculative appeals to "climate uncertainty," leaving PRO's categorical logic incomplete. Second, CON's Q1 (B3.1) exposed that PRO's own proposed workaround—public utilities contracting private firms for emergency bottling—likely still constitutes private "extraction... for commercial bottling" under the motion's actual text, meaning PRO's fallback solution may itself violate the proposition it is defending. PRO's answer (A4.1, distinguishing ownership from extraction) did not cleanly resolve this tension. CON also turned PRO's "regulation will always be captured by lobbying" argument back on PRO effectively: if institutions are too weak to enforce caps, they are equally too weak to enforce a ban, a point PRO never neutralized. PRO's rhetoric (rights vs. privileges, "paper barriers") was vivid but rested on unproven claims that public utilities are structurally immune to the same political and profit pressures, a claim CON directly challenged (B2.3) and PRO never substantively answered. The clipped Rebuttal 2 further cost PRO the chance to fully defend against B2.7's critique of the markup argument, leaving that point live for CON. Overall, CON's tighter comparative framing and sharper handling of pressure gave it the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Question Q1 (B3.1) asking who physically extracts water under PRO's "contracting" fallback, combined with PRO's incomplete answer (A4.1), was the most decisive exchange: it exposed that PRO's own proposed compromise likely still falls under the motion's ban on private "extraction... for commercial bottling," undermining PRO's claim that its policy preserves emergency redundancy without contradiction.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): B built the cleaner path to victory by holding the motion to its categorical wording and forcing A to defend an ownership-identity test over hydrology and enforceability. A’s structural-misalignment story (fiduciary duty, lobbying, right-to-sue) is rhetorically forceful and scores on equity/markup harms, yet never squares the sustainable-recharge case or shows why a ban is self-enforcing while metered caps, automatic drought triggers, and household-priority clauses are not. B repeatedly converts A’s “perfect regulation is fantasy” claim into a wash: any ban still needs monitoring, definitions, and resistance to capture. A’s contract-for-emergency workaround fails the motion’s text (private extracting/trading) and understates continuous capacity needs. The clipped A4 and residual overbreadth leave B’s comparative framing (manage withdrawals first; ban sacrifices low-risk cases without hydrological gain) as the decisive, still-live route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.4–B4.6: regulation-is-imperfect does not prove ban; both require the same institutional capacity, and the sustainable case remains unanswered.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by decisively controlling the text of the motion and the mechanics of enforcement. Side A repeatedly forces Side B to confront that the motion bans private extraction and trading, not just ownership, which dismantles Side B's emergency contracting workaround. Furthermore, Side A brilliantly neutralizes the perfect regulation argument by pointing out that a ban requires the exact same institutional capacity and resistance to lobbying as a permit system. Side B's rhetorical focus on corporate fiduciary duty was compelling but ultimately failed to overcome Side A's core point that hydrology responds to withdrawal volume, not corporate motive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they answer the pressure questions to expose Side B's textual evasion regarding public contracting and dismantle the paper barrier argument by showing a ban is equally vulnerable to lobbying and weak enforcement.
