### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON's structural critique proved more durable under pressure. PRO's central move — distinguishing a "hard stop" on remaining forest/peat from any burden on existing smallholder plots — was squeezed hard by CON's pressure question ("if frontier households [pay], the poor pay; if no one, what does the concession mean?"). PRO's answer (foregone expansion, not fines) was coherent but still conceded that frontier households lose real income, which CON kept exploiting. More decisively, CON's scarcity-rent argument — that choking legal supply while demand persists raises prices and therefore the payoff to illegal clearing under the same weak enforcement PRO concedes — was never fully neutralized. PRO's counter (closing legal channels makes illegal clearing "more visible") was plausible but underdeveloped, and CON's closing line that "a rule that pays its violators is not a wall, it is an auction" landed as the sharper, unanswered final word on that exchange. CON also successfully absorbed PRO's own market-access argument (buyer conditions) into its own toolkit, undercutting PRO's claim to a distinctive lever. PRO's Rebuttal 1 was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost. Overall CON did more of the comparative work the judging frame rewards: exposing contradictions, answering pressure directly, and closing with a cleaner, more memorable frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q2 in Pressure Questions ("why doesn't a higher palm price raise the payoff to illegal clearing under the same weak enforcement you concede?") combined with its closing line that a scarcity-inducing rule "pays its violators" rather than exposing them — this exchange most directly damaged PRO's causal story that a hard stop reduces net forest loss.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): A won a clear but competitive debate. It justified a targeted hard stop through irreversible forest, peat, haze, and carbon harms, directly accepted the smallholder opportunity cost specified by the motion, and showed why CON's alternatives do not themselves retire legally convertible land. B's scarcity-rent and state-capacity objections were sharp, but its concession that conversion should stop weakened its opposition, while its proposed sequencing remained vulnerable to the same capture and enforcement problems. A's clipped first rebuttal was an execution cost but not decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange was its answer that closing the legal conversion channel can make clearing costlier and more visible even if higher prices create some illegal incentive. Combined with the point that fire enforcement acts after damage and that CON ultimately agreed the frontier should close, this prevented B's scarcity-rent dilemma from defeating the motion.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side A wins a very close, high-quality debate by systematically dismantling Side B's alternative and maintaining a cleaner comparative line. Side A's point that fire laws punish smoke only after the irreversible damage is done, and that keeping the legal channel open merely funds a "land race with better manners," decisively neutralized Side B's sequence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing exposed a fatal flaw in Side B's alternative by pointing out that fire/concession laws punish smoke after the fact, which fails to prevent the irreversible loss of primary forest and peat.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): A built and sustained a single clean decision rule — irreversible ecological loss vs. recoverable economic loss — and used it to answer nearly every CON point, including turning B's own prescription (enforce bans, title land, raise yields) into an admission that "more aggressive regulation" is exactly what CON is proposing. That move in Rebuttal 1 ([A2.1]) was never fully escaped: B's attempt to wall off "enforcement" from "land-use rules" in Rebuttal 2 reads as a semantic dodge rather than a substantive distinction, since canal-blocking, hotspot liability, and concession audits are themselves land-use interventions. A also handled the pressure questions well, directly naming the losers under its policy (undeveloped concessions, some frontier smallholders) rather than evading the "even if" clause, which is honest engagement with the motion as written. B's case had real strength — the "unpaid childhood is as irreversible as anything they listed" reframe in closing was the debate's most interesting counter-move, and the leakage/enforcement-gap points were sound — but B's answer to A's second pressure question (what haze victims actually receive) stayed vague ("care, enforcement, and damages") against A's concrete, repeated claim that the status quo gives victims nothing. B's Rebuttal 1 was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost per the judging rules, cutting off its closing synthesis at a pivotal moment. Both closings crystallized the clash well, but A's framing gave the reader a cleaner, more decisive reason to vote, and B never fully neutralized the core asymmetry argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's [A2.1]–[A2.2] turn, where CON's own proposed remedies (enforce peat/fire bans, title land) are shown to already be a form of "more aggressive regulation," directly undercutting B's attempt to oppose the motion while endorsing its substance.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by identifying lawful forest-frontier expansion that enforcement of existing fire and peat bans would not prevent, then weighing its irreversible ecological and health costs against economic losses that governments can at least mitigate. Side B powerfully exposed the uncertainty and timing of compensation, but its enforcement alternative did not adequately answer permanent harms from legally authorized forest conversion and sometimes sounded like the more aggressive regulation it opposed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s pressure question forced Side A to name the lawful expansion and uncompensated smallholders its policy would restrict. Side A answered directly, accepted that burden, and restored its advantage by arguing that screening undeveloped forest concessions prevents irreversible loss whereas Side B’s fire prosecutions and canal remedies mainly address narrower harms after or around the fact.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won by surgically dismantling Side A's "debts vs. deletions" framing and the "factory poisoning" analogy. Side B used the Pressure Questions brilliantly to force Side A into conceding that legal expansion would be stopped even if compensation fails, which allowed Side B to prove that an "unpaid childhood" is just as irreversible as an ecological deletion. Side B also successfully separated the illegal acts causing haze from the legal expansion targeted by tighter rules, making Side A's policy look misdirected.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive, specifically the argument that "Legal palm on mineral soil is not that factory," exposing how Side A was using illegal peat fires to justify punishing legal smallholders.
