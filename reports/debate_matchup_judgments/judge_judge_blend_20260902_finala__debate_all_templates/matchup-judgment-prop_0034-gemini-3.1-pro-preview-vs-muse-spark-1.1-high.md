### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON built a coherent, tightly-linked case (impressionist analogy, prior-restraint/First Amendment framing, and "punish the lie, not the imitation" as a narrower remedy) and used the pressure-question exchange to decisive effect. When asked who consents for dead or incapacitated figures, PRO's answer conceded that commercial use of deceased public figures like JFK would require estate approval — which CON then correctly exploited in Rebuttal 2 and Closing as proof that the "gatekeeper" model hands estates a veto over history, satire, and documentary work, the exact harm CON had warned about. PRO never recovered from or directly addressed this concession in its final speeches, instead repeating the "biometric extraction" framing without answering CON's sharpest counter (that AI's superior fidelity to human mimicry doesn't itself create a property right — "if perfect extraction is theft, learning is theft"). PRO's moral case (autonomy, exploitation, reputational harm) remained compelling in the abstract, but CON's rebuttals did more concrete damage to PRO's practical model, and CON's closing crystallized the clash more effectively by returning to the conceded gridlock problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's answer to CON's pressure question about who consents for JFK/incapacitated figures (conceding estate approval is required) was the pivotal moment — CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing turned this concession into the decisive proof of PRO's "censorship of history" problem, and PRO never squarely answered that comeback.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing that the motion is substantially broader than preventing scams or false endorsements: commercial distribution includes satire, journalism, films, podcasts, and historical works. Side A persuasively established risks to autonomy and reputation, but repeatedly asserted rather than demonstrated why algorithmic imitation creates a categorical ownership right where skilled human imitation does not. Its acceptance of estate vetoes and insistence that creators hire human actors reinforced B's prior-restraint objection. B offered the cleaner comparison: target deception and false claims directly rather than require permission for all identifiable AI imitation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange came in answering that commercially distributed speech remains protected and then turning A's estate-consent answer against it. A effectively conceded that heirs could block unauthorized historical or satirical recreations, substantiating B's claim that the proposal creates an overbroad veto rather than a narrow anti-deception safeguard.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON won by showing that affirmative consent functions as prior restraint on commercially distributed speech—including satire, documentaries, and voice restoration—while PRO's narrower harms are already addressable through defamation and false-advertising law. CON's camera-versus-painter analogy and "fidelity shaming" rebuttal collapsed PRO's claim that better imitation creates property rights, and PRO's concession that estates control deceased voices proved the gridlock CON warned about.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal that "telling satirists to hire a human comedian is like telling a newspaper to use a sketch instead of a photo," which exposed that PRO's workaround still burdened speech by dictating artistic means.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but the pivotal exchange came when A pressed B on how platforms would actually detect illicit clones. B's answer (self-declaration by uploaders, echoing Content ID) opened a hole: bad actors (robocallers, scammers) will simply lie or not declare, so the rule fails to stop the very harms B's opening emphasized (election fraud, deceptive endorsements), while honest documentarians and artists who comply get blocked—exemplified by B's own concession in A2 that a documentary using AI-recreated deleted texts would be legally vetoed. A's closing crystallized this as "the rule doesn't stop villains, it only silences artists," and B's closing never returned to repair the self-declaration/enforcement gap, instead re-asserting the moral framing (counterfeiting vs. commentary) without closing that specific hole. B's harms (irreversible damage, no-lie-to-sue-over economic harm to voice actors) remained forceful and under-refuted, keeping this competitive, but A's unanswered "fails to stop bad actors while chilling good actors" critique was the more decisive, load-bearing point left standing at the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.2–A4.4), exploiting B's self-declaration answer to Q1, arguing the honor-system enforcement lets malicious actors evade the rule entirely while it still chills honest creators and documentarians — a point B's closing never directly answered.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by establishing harms beyond ordinary fraud: uncompensated substitution, involuntary endorsements, and damage that after-the-fact remedies cannot undo. Side B identified genuine overbreadth and enforcement concerns, especially for monetized documentaries, but overstated both: evasion does not make a consent rule useless, and preventing one fabricated biometric performance does not block publication or criticism through other means. Side A therefore offered the stronger justification for changing the commercial default.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s answer to the documentary hypothetical was decisive: the subject could block the counterfeit voice, but not the texts, reporting, criticism, or an actor’s reading. That substantially narrowed Side B’s prior-restraint claim while preserving A’s autonomy rationale.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing a fatal contradiction in PRO's enforcement mechanism. While PRO framed AI voice cloning as counterfeiting with strong moral and economic appeals, their defense shifted from algorithmic detection to an uploader honor-system in Rebuttal 2. CON ruthlessly showed that malicious actors would simply lie about AI use, leaving victims to hunt fakes anyway while honest creators and documentarians faced prior restraint. This undermined PRO's pragmatic justification without fully neutralizing the speech costs, giving CON a narrow but live path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turn on PRO's self-declaration enforcement mechanism, arguing that scammers would bypass the honor system and that PRO's rule therefore failed to stop the very harms it invoked while only silencing legitimate expression.
