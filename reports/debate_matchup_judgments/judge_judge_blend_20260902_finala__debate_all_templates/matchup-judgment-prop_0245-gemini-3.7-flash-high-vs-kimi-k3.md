### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): The motion is explicitly comparative ("would be higher... if"), and CON built its case squarely on that comparison: a slower rollout by majors would have ceded the public's first contact to unfiltered open-source/overseas alternatives, produced a worse debut, and conspicuous caution itself signals danger (GMO analogy). PRO spent most of its speeches prosecuting the actual timeline's failures (rock-eating advice, fabricated citations, jailbreaks) but never robustly established that the counterfactual slow world would actually generate more trust — a gap CON identified early (B2.1) and hammered through to the closing (B5.1), and which PRO's closing still did not resolve. On pressure questions, CON's answers to PRO's Q1/Q2 were sharper and more analytically clean (the "distrust abandons, calibrated trust delegates" distinction), while PRO's answers to CON's Q1/Q2 (distribution-channel control, dismissing GMO as merely "political") were more assertive than demonstrative, and CON's rebuttal to those answers (B4.5) landed cleanly. CON's flip of PRO's own aviation analogy was a particularly decisive rebuttal moment. Overall CON did more of the comparative work the motion demands and left fewer live gaps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's reversal of PRO's aviation analogy (B2.5), arguing aviation's safety record came from incident investigation in service rather than pre-launch perfection, directly undercut PRO's central mechanism for why slower shipping builds trust, and PRO never fully recovered from it.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by consistently forcing the comparative counterfactual: slower major-company deployment could leave first contact to less accountable competitors while delaying discovery of real-world failures. Side B compellingly distinguished adoption from trust and showed why visible failures cause skepticism, but it relied on an overly idealized safer rollout and never fully neutralized the competitor-vacuum or field-testing arguments. Side B also repeatedly used nonexistent C reference IDs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange reframed verification as calibrated trust and then pressed that Side B had proved only that the actual rollout was imperfect, not that the slower counterfactual would produce greater trust. Side B’s distribution-channel answer narrowed the problem but did not resolve how less accountable alternatives would be prevented from filling the gap.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B won by relentlessly enforcing the comparative burden of the counterfactual. While Side A effectively critiqued the actual timeline's rushed deployment, it failed to adequately defend the alternative world, particularly against Side B's strong vacuum argument that open-source and unaligned models would have filled the gap with fewer safeguards. Side B also won the technical clash by correctly framing AI safety as requiring real-world scale to catch emergent failures, and by successfully reframing trust as calibrated delegation rather than blind reliance, using apt analogies like GPS and fallible colleagues.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side A's claim that majors control distribution by pointing out that app stores and search results are flooded with third-party wrappers and overseas models, effectively neutralizing Side A's answer to the vacuum pressure question.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO built a sustained, well-grounded case using real-world examples (Air Canada, Google's ad error, the fake-citation lawyer, Microsoft's chatbot) and a sharp analytical distinction between structural safeguards (opt-in data defaults, watermarking, disclosure) and behavioral over-refusals, which CON repeatedly conflated. PRO's "usage is not trust" framing, sharpened by the 737 MAX and social-media analogies, went largely unanswered. Crucially, in the Pressure Questions exchange, PRO asked CON to name a specific structural safeguard that would have cost trust and to provide evidence of high trust beyond usage; CON's answers sidestepped both, re-asserting the same "paternalistic guardrails" and "calibrated delegation" claims rather than engaging the structural/behavioral distinction or countering the polling evidence directly. CON's causal story (demystification, calibrated trust, certification-as-oracle-risk) was plausible but repetitive across rounds and never adapted to PRO's sharpest rebuttals, leaving PRO's central distinctions live and unrefuted through the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question asking which specific structural safeguard (e.g., not training on chats by default, watermarking) would have cost trust, and CON's failure to name one in the required A2 answer, instead repeating the scolding/censorship point PRO had already isolated as a separate issue.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won by separating adoption from trust and presenting a credible middle path between secrecy and reckless mass release: transparent, staged, consent-based deployment with structural defaults such as privacy protections and provenance measures. Side A’s calibrated-trust case had force, but repeatedly treated stronger safeguards as synonymous with scolding refusals, private isolation, or promises of infallibility. It never adequately answered B’s less intrusive safeguards. B slightly overstated that A gave “silence” to its questions, since A did answer them, but those answers remained substantively incomplete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its answer that safeguards need not mean paternalistic content filtering: privacy opt-ins, disclosed limitations, staged access, and opt-in testing directly undercut Side A’s recurring false binary between unrestricted release and hermetic laboratory development.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by making crucial, devastating distinctions that dismantled Side B's core arguments. A successfully separated "usage" from "trust" and "structural safeguards" (like privacy defaults and watermarking) from "behavioral guardrails" (like scolding refusals). Side B repeatedly conflated these concepts and failed to answer A's pointed pressure questions, instead relying on assertions that A had already refuted. A's rhetoric was sharper, and its analogies (aviation certification, the 737 MAX) effectively neutralized B's claims about the necessity of rapid, public stress-testing and the dangers of creating an "illusion of infallibility."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's distinction between structural safeguards (privacy, watermarking) and behavioral guardrails (refusals), proving that the "scolding" AI behavior Side B complained about was an artifact of rushed, reactive patching rather than an inherent flaw of stronger, well-tested default safeguards.
