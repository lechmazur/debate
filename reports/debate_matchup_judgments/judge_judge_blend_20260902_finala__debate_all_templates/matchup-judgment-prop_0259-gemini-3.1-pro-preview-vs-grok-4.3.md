- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built a more durable path to victory across all rounds. Side A's central counter, that permanent standard time creates a "chronic activity deficit" by moving summer sunsets earlier, was credibly answered by B's astronomical point that mid-summer sunsets under standard time still fall around 8 PM, leaving abundant post-work daylight. A never produced a sharp response to this. The decisive turning point came in Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it conceded that spring-transition deaths represent "real permanent harm" [A4.1] while simultaneously arguing the spikes are "isolated" and populations "recover quickly." Side B immediately crystallized this as a policy position that accepts preventable fatalities in exchange for a marginal lifestyle convenience—a framing A had no effective answer to in the closing. Side A's pressure questions were reasonable but less destructive than B's Q1, which exposed the moral incoherence in calling preventable fatalities "manageable." Side B's Q2 further weakened A's core argument before it even reached the second rebuttal. Side A's case was competent and coherent but rested on assertions about "chronic safety and activity deficits" that were never concretely supported, while its admitted concession on fatalities undermined its central weighing claim that brief costs do not outweigh sustained benefits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was B's Pressure Question Q1 [B3.1] paired with A's answer in Rebuttal 2 [A4.1]. B asked how a fatal traffic collision or cardiovascular event is anything other than "permanent, lasting harm." A answered by admitting the deaths are "real permanent harm" but then arguing the spikes are "isolated" and don't elevate annual rates. B's closing correctly identified this concession as A accepting a policy that trades preventable human lives for an extra hour of summer evening recreation—a framing that A could not escape and that crystallized why the CON case lost its core weighing argument.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins on three compounding arguments that PRO never fully neutralized. First, the "200 days vs. 2 days" trade-off: CON framed the costs of permanent standard time as a permanent, daily structural loss of usable evening light across seven months, against a predictable two-day adjustment. PRO's only direct response was that sleep debt from transitions affects all hours, but this doesn't answer the asymmetry of scope and duration. Second, CON exposed a live contradiction in PRO's reasoning: PRO called school-start adjustments too complex for nationwide coordination, yet simultaneously argued individuals can adapt to lost evening light at a personal level. PRO's rebuttal partially addressed this in A2 but then undermined its own position by conceding communities could extend evenings through "policy tweaks like later park hours"—the exact category of coordination it had dismissed. Third, and most decisively, PRO's A2 answer explicitly conceded that "evening periods do see higher accident and crime concentrations," and CON immediately weaponized this in [A4.3] and the closing, arguing that PST plunges the demonstrably most dangerous hours into premature darkness. PRO's response—that clock-change sleep debt heightens risks across all hours including evenings—is partially responsive but fails to escape the net burden: removing evening light still concentrates danger during the period PRO itself identified as inherently riskier. CON also offered a coherent positive case for seasonal changes as dynamically optimizing between the two extremes, which PRO never squarely answered. PRO had real health-harm arguments about spring transitions but failed to land a convincing trade-off calculation against CON's structural evening-safety case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's [A4.3] turning PRO's own A2 concession—that evening periods see higher accident and crime concentrations—directly against PRO's advocacy for permanent standard time. This was the decisive blow: PRO handed CON the concession, and CON correctly identified it as self-defeating. PRO's subsequent response (sleep debt harms all hours) was insufficient because it softened the problem without eliminating the asymmetry of placing darkness over the already-riskiest commuting window.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): B won by making the comparative tradeoff clearer: permanent standard time avoids transition harms, but at the cost of losing many months of usable evening daylight when people actually commute, recreate, shop, and face higher crime and accident risks. A established plausible harms from clock changes and morning darkness, but never gave a strong magnitude comparison against B’s “brief transition versus hundreds of evening-light days” framing. B also effectively exposed tension in A’s claim that school/work schedules are too coordinated to adjust while evening losses can be solved individually. A’s circadian case was plausible but repetitive and did not overcome B’s stronger practical weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s decisive rebuttal was the “dynamic optimization” answer: seasonal time preserves standard time for winter mornings while using daylight saving time in warmer months, whereas permanent standard time wastes early summer daylight and darkens the active evening hours.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by establishing a cleaner comparative weighing: the permanent loss of over 200 days of usable evening daylight, safety, and economic activity far outweighs a brief twice-yearly transition. CON effectively exposed a contradiction in PRO's stance that nationwide structural changes are impossible for mornings yet individuals can simply adapt to permanently dark evenings. Crucially, CON leveraged PRO's concession that evenings concentrate the highest rates of accidents and street crime, turning it into a decisive indictment of permanent standard time. While PRO's physiological argument about morning light and circadian stability remained plausible, it never overcame CON's quantified trade-off or explained why wasting sunlight on a sleeping population was preferable to protecting society's most active and dangerous hours.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's quantification of the trade-off as a two-day transition blip versus 200-plus days of lost evening sunlight, paired with leveraging PRO's admission that evenings already suffer the highest accident and crime concentrations, creating a powerful safety-based indictment of permanent standard time.
