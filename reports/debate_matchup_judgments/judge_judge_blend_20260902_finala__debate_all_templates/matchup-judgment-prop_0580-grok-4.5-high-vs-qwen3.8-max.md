### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and followed format well, including proper A1/A2 answers to pressure questions. The decisive clash was over which metric defines "cost-effective child poverty reduction": PRO's per-dollar, current-period income-threshold framing versus CON's durability/earnings-unlock framing. PRO's coverage argument—that subsidy dollars only help families who can find, qualify for, and use care, leaving infants, rural families, and irregular-shift workers with zero benefit this period—was pressed consistently and never fully neutralized. CON's central counter (that "expanded" subsidies could fund home-based, rural, and extended-hour options) remained largely assertive rather than resolving the timing/conversion friction PRO kept raising. CON's durability reframe ("subscription to poverty") was rhetorically strong and a legitimate reading of "cost-effective," but it never overcame PRO's repeated point that subsidy benefits are speculative, delayed, and concentrated among families who already clear the care hurdle, while cash's breadth advantage was conceded rather than rebutted. PRO's closing crystallized this asymmetry more effectively, directly tying the decision back to the metric of children lifted per dollar.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.2/A4.3 vs B2.3/B4.2: PRO's repeated coverage critique (equivalent subsidy dollars still require finding, qualifying for, and converting a slot into earnings, leaving many poor children unhelped this period) was the most damaging unanswered pressure point, since CON's rebuttal only asserted expanded reach without resolving the conversion-friction problem.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully defending the coverage and efficiency of cash while neutralizing Side A's core 'durability' argument. B effectively pointed out that childcare subsidies also require continuous funding to keep parents working, meaning A's 'residual earnings' claim relies on an unrealistic scenario where the care constraint disappears after the subsidy ends.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's A1 response noting that unlocked jobs often collapse when childcare funding stops, which cleanly dismantled Side A's argument that subsidies offer a permanent exit from poverty.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO successfully defended the motion by tying cost-effectiveness to the standard income-based poverty metric, showing that monthly cash reaches nearly all poor children with minimal administrative friction while expanded subsidies inevitably leave large uncovered shares due to matching requirements, capacity lags, and thin markets. CON attempted a durable-earnings reframe, arguing that subsidies unlock lasting private income, but PRO neutralized this by showing that subsidy dollars also require renewal and that their long-term benefits are unevenly distributed rather than universal. CON’s coverage and multiplier claims relied heavily on idealized expansion assumptions that PRO repeatedly deflated with persistent implementation frictions. PRO’s sharper adherence to the motion’s comparative burden, combined with direct, damaging rebuttals on symmetry and coverage, built the cleaner route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 answering A3: PRO’s Rebuttal 2 collapsed CON’s durability advantage by demonstrating that ending subsidies collapses slots and unlocked jobs for most families, while cash was already reaching children subsidies never served, making recurring cash the more reliable per-dollar poverty reducer.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but A's "chain of assumptions" framing consistently exposed the weakest link in B's model: childcare subsidies require slots, providers, eligibility, and job-market fit to exist before any poverty reduction occurs, while cash reaches the poorest households (including those with no job, no provider, or disabled children) immediately and unconditionally. B's central rebuttal—that supply gaps are a reason to "invest in them, not abandon the tool"—never squarely answered A's pointed pressure question about how equivalent spending can create usable infant/evening slots fast enough to match cash's immediacy; B's later answers reasserted the multiplier claim rather than resolving the feasibility/timing problem. A's framing also tracked the motion's actual wording ("reduce child poverty") more directly, since income transfers close the poverty gap by definition, whereas B's "durable exits via earnings" standard required an unproven multi-step causal chain. B's case was rhetorically tidy ("temporary fill vs. durable exit") but grew somewhat repetitive across rounds without adding new resolution to the exposed vulnerability, giving A a clear, if not overwhelming, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question Q1 ("If equivalent subsidy spending must first create slots, hours, and providers before any parent can work, how is it more cost-effective than cash reaching the poorest households now?") and B's failure to directly resolve that timing/feasibility gap in the following rebuttal was the most decisive exchange, since it left A's core efficiency claim largely unrebutted.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully framing childcare subsidies as a leaky pipeline that misses the deepest poverty, such as families unable to work or those with disabled children. Side A's earnings multiplier argument is strong, but Side B effectively neutralizes it by showing that avoiding catastrophic events like eviction provides a durable poverty exit, whereas the multiplier relies on a speculative chain of assumptions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's argument that preventing an eviction with cash is a durable poverty-reduction effect directly and cleanly neutralized Side A's core attack that cash is only a temporary scoreboard fill.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger comparative case by showing that direct monthly cash reaches the poorest households immediately with fewer assumptions, while CON’s subsidy chain depended on speculative, conditional steps (slot creation, job match, sufficient wages) that leak value along the way. CON’s durable-earnings multiplier remained plausible but unverified under pressure, and PRO’s live argument about households with no earnings, infants, or disabled children—whom subsidies largely miss—went unanswered. PRO’s framing of cash as reliable poverty-gap closure, not merely a “scoreboard bump,” withstood CON’s repeated attempts to elevate future earnings over present shortfall reduction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7) exposed that CON’s subsidy chain is a speculative, leaky pipeline and reframed monthly cash as stable, immediate poverty reduction rather than a temporary fill, blunting CON’s core “durable exit” frame.
