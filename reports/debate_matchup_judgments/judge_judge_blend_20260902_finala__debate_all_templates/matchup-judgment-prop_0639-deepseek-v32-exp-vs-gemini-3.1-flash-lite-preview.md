### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but Side A (PRO) built the stronger cumulative case. PRO anchored the debate in a concrete practical failure—the French climate convention—and returned to it consistently as evidence that advisory models produce evasion rather than action. The "hope vs. guarantee" framing in the closing crystallized the key comparison cleanly. PRO also handled the A1/A2 structure correctly and answered CON's pressure questions adequately: the comparison to a legislature passing a bad law (which can still be later amended) is a fair move, and the "facilitators are less captured than lobbyists" counter is a reasonable comparative response. CON's strongest moment was B4.4's contradiction catch—if the assembly's decisions can be amended by the legislature, are they genuinely "binding"? This was a real tension PRO never fully resolved. However, CON did not press this hard enough in the closing, instead returning to the abstract ballot-box argument it had already deployed multiple times. CON's A2 answer—that assemblies are shelved because they lack consensus—also rings somewhat hollow against PRO's France example, where the proposals had broad public support but were weakened by government anyway. CON's accountability argument is philosophically serious but relies on an idealized picture of electoral feedback that PRO's practical examples repeatedly undercut. Overall, PRO maintained a more grounded, evolving comparative case, while CON's later speeches largely repeated the same abstraction without materially narrowing the core clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.4 contradiction catch—pointing out that if the assembly's decisions can be amended by the legislature they are not truly "binding," but if they cannot be amended the public is stuck with unaccountable, rigid policy—was the sharpest single argumentative move in the debate. PRO never cleanly resolved this tension, though PRO's implicit position (binding means immediate legal force, not permanent irreversibility) is defensible and CON failed to press it home in the closing.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO)): Side A won by establishing and defending the core accountability deficit of binding sortition bodies (no ballot-box recourse) and exposing the logical contradiction in Side B’s “binding but amendable” claim.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 on the binding-vs-amendable contradiction, which directly damaged B’s route to victory on implementation guarantees.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by exposing a fatal tension in the PRO model: if binding assembly decisions can be amended by elected legislatures, they are not truly binding; if they cannot, they create an unaccountable, irrevocable policy-making body. PRO's responses failed to resolve the mechanism problem, and their closing could not overcome the contradiction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal [A4.4], which seized on PRO's concession that legislatures could later amend binding assembly outputs, exposing the contradiction that rendered the proposal either non-binding or democratically unaccountable.

Side A also committed a minor execution error by citing non-existent reference IDs in its closing. The margin reflects that Side A's cherry-picking argument and expert-synthesis reframe remained live and partially unanswered, keeping this a competitive loss rather than a dominant one.

A conceded that binding decisions gone wrong would need to be corrected by politicians—the very political class A's entire case condemned. B correctly turned this into a proof point: A's model creates an unaccountable sovereign that evaporates after voting, then hands the damage to the system A attacks.
