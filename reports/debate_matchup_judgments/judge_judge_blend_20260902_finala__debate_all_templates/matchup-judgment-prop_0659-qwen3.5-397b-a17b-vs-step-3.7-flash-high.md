CON's best live route—that a ban entrenches worse human bias—was real but not enough to overcome the compounding problems PRO identified with the regulation alternative.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON)): Side A won because its core argument — that ERS measure performative emotional expression that is both irrelevant to actual job performance and systematically disadvantages autistic, Black, and culturally distinct workers — was established in the opening and never squarely neutralized by CON. CON's strongest counter (human bias is worse and ERS creates an auditable record) was plausible but insufficient: PRO distinguished that human actors are accountable under law and cross-examinable while ERS outputs, shielded by trade secrets at the pre-litigation stage, let employers launder discriminatory results as neutral fact. CON's "fix the algorithm" response never addressed PRO's more fundamental point that the core function of ERS — scoring emotional performance — is the discrimination, not just a glitch in training data. CON's protective-order counter to the trade-secret argument was legally plausible but still required a worker to first get into litigation, which assumes they already have evidence of discrimination they cannot access. PRO's alternative-tools argument (structured tests, work-sample assessments) was raised early and CON only weakly answered it by claiming ERS captures "emotional resilience" that tests cannot — a claim PRO rebutted by noting ERS detect performed emotion, not actual resilience, which was never effectively countered. Side A's clipped first rebuttal is a real execution cost, but the core arguments survived and strengthened in subsequent rounds. CON's framing was coherent and the "regulate, don't ban" structure is always rhetorically appealing, but the decisive clash — whether ERS bias is a correctable glitch or a design-level feature — went to PRO on the balance of arguments made.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's human-hiring question (A2): PRO drew a clear distinction between human hiring (accountable actors subject to anti-discrimination law, training mandates, and cross-examination) and ERS (which encode historical bias at scale, penalize neurodivergent and culturally diverse workers for normal differences, and shield employers via trade secrets). This directly blunted CON's reductio ad absurdum that banning ERS logic entails banning human hiring, and it kept the decisive clash — baked-in design flaw vs. correctable glitch — clearly resolved in PRO's favor.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the motion easier to decide: ERS in employment score performative emotional expression, which varies by culture, disability, and neurotype, and is often not job-relevant. Side A had a plausible regulation and audit-trail case, but leaned too heavily on a false binary between ERS and unrecorded human whim, while Side B repeatedly offered structured assessments and work samples as better alternatives. Side A also never fully answered the claim that ERS can launder bias behind objective-looking scores.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange was turning Side A's audit-trail argument: even if ERS create records, those records may become a shield for employers because the core score itself is biased and detached from actual job skill.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON)): Side A wins: its case that ERS inherently scores irrelevant performative expression and embeds unfixable bias was never neutralized; it directly answered pressure questions on audit access and data laundering, exposed CON's regulation as cosmetic, and kept the clash on the proposition's terms while CON's consistency claims stayed aspirational.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A2 in Rebuttal 2, which tied bias directly to ERS design and rejected "fix the algorithm" as a smokescreen.
