Those are the PRO claims; Side A was conceding the motion while labeled CON. Their pressure questions compounded the problem by asking why partial acknowledgment fuels more conflict than "a consistent, state-backed curriculum"—again the PRO argument. Side B's pressure questions exposed the logical gap in "leave it to historians" and forced Side A to answer with more PRO-aligned examples. Side B's A1/A2 answers crystallized the decisive clash: inconsistent hedging, not honest teaching, inflames diplomacy, so the corrective is consistent formal acknowledgment, not omission. Side A never recovered a coherent CON route to victory after Round 1, leaving Side B's framing essentially uncontested for the final three rounds.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = Mistral Large 3 (PRO)): CON won by consistently anchoring the debate in the motion's actual language—"should, even when doing so keeps grievances alive"—and by maintaining a coherent middle path (archives, scholarship, compensation, memorialization) that PRO never fully neutralized. PRO's repeated charge of "false dichotomy" lost force because CON clarified the alternative at every turn. The decisive clash was PRO's failure to supply a workable limiting principle when pressed. PRO answered Q1 with "framing as shared moral lesson vs. weapon of accusation," but CON correctly identified this as "teach it well"—a slogan rather than a state-action rule. The Japan/Germany contrast, PRO's best recurring weapon, actually cut both ways: CON's response—that Germany required total regime collapse and external enforcement, not just the choice to reckon honestly—was never fully refuted. PRO's "the difference is whether a nation chooses to confront its past" is rhetorical and underdeveloped as a causal mechanism. CON also correctly framed the absolute duty ("should, even under harmful diplomatic conditions") as overreach that PRO's case tacitly disputes rather than defends. PRO had stronger rhetorical lines ("blood money," "graveyards for the forgotten," "erased"), good emotional momentum, and plausible examples, which kept the debate competitive. But CON's structural discipline—narrowing the motion, defending a principled middle ground, and exposing the absence of a limiting principle—gives it the clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 response to PRO's Q1: CON accurately identified that PRO's proffered limiting condition ("framing as shared moral lesson") collapses into "teach it well"—a vague aspiration rather than a workable rule for states navigating live diplomatic conflict. This directly exposed the motion-level hole in PRO's case: the proposition asks about scenarios where formal teaching does keep grievances alive, and PRO's only answer was that good framing will prevent that outcome, which simply re-disputes the motion's premise without answering the "even when" clause.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = Mistral Large 3 (PRO)): Side A wins by maintaining a tight focus on the specific wording of the motion ('even when doing so keeps historical grievances alive') and successfully breaking Side B's Truth vs. Amnesia binary. By offering a plausible third option (archives, compensation, open scholarship), Side A provided a cleaner route to victory that avoided the diplomatic costs built into the prompt.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal effectively deconstructed the Germany analogy by pointing out the unique preconditions (regime collapse, total defeat) required for state-backed reckoning to work without fueling nationalism, cleanly answering Side B's main empirical mechanism.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = Mistral Large 3 (PRO)): CON won by exposing that PRO could not meet the motion's tough "even when" burden without collapsing into moral absolutism. CON showed that Germany's reckoning depended on exceptional post-defeat conditions and that truth could be preserved through archives, compensation, and scholarship without turning school curricula into permanent instruments of live interstate grievance. PRO's evocative examples never overcame the lack of a limiting principle.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's sustained pressure on PRO's missing limiting principle—demonstrating that PRO could not reliably distinguish when formal teaching would reconcile rather than deepen active diplomatic conflict, leaving only the slogan "teach it well."
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Mistral Large 3 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON)): Side B wins by consistently leveraging the motion's explicit stipulation ("even when doing so keeps historical grievances alive"). Side A's core argument relies on the premise that formal teaching ultimately resolves grievances and builds peace, which effectively fights the hypothetical constraint of the proposition. Side B sharply exploits this by offering a viable middle ground—preserving truth through archives, compensation, and scholarship without mandating state curricula that institutionalize diplomatic conflict. Side B also provides a more realistic contextualization of the Germany example, noting it followed regime collapse and cannot be universalized. While Side A delivers stronger rhetorical flourishes, Side B's analytical discipline, direct answers to pressure questions, and cleaner weighing of the state's dual duties to victims and the living secure a clear victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2 effectively dismantle Side A's reliance on Germany by highlighting the exceptional postwar conditions that allowed reconciliation, and sharply expose Side A's lack of a limiting principle for when formal teaching worsens rather than heals conflict. Side B's defense of "truth-preserving alternatives" successfully neutralizes Side A's moral absolutism while respecting the motion's diplomatic constraint.
