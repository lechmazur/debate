### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side B won by building and sustaining three decisive argumentative threads that Side A never fully neutralized. First, the structural incompatibility argument: securities frameworks assume T+1 settlement, market hours, and paper custodians—features that do not fit continuous, globally settled crypto markets. Side A's answer ("existing electronic surveillance already operates continuously") conceded that adaptation is required, which Side B effectively converted into a rebuttal: adapting is precisely what a distinct category does, but from correct engineering specifications rather than retrofitted ones. Second, the equivalence argument: Side B consistently demonstrated that net-capital requirements, customer-asset segregation, reserve verification, and audit obligations are general regulatory tools available under any statutory framework, not uniquely securities-law properties. Side A never explained why those protections require the securities-exchange label specifically rather than a stringent distinct category. Third, the enforcement-versus-categorization argument: FTX-style fraud was already prohibited under existing authority; the real failure was investigative will and capacity. Side A's response—that broker-dealer status creates automatic, proactive triggers rather than reactive fraud enforcement—was the strongest point they had, but Side B's closing rebuttal (commodity exchanges hold customer funds and face tailored custody rules without being called securities exchanges) provided a clean analogy that Side A left unanswered. The most decisive moment was B4.3, where Side B used Side A's own pressure-question answers to prove the core concession: if existing frameworks need substantial adaptation, the question is whether you adapt from the right starting point. Side B also properly formatted A1/A2 answers to the pressure questions, while their own pressure questions exposed the live tensions about enforcement jurisdiction and 24/7 incompatibility. Side A's arguments were competent and coherent but progressively cornered by Side B's escalating use of A's own admissions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 [B4.3]: After receiving Side A's answers to the pressure questions, Side B pointed out that A's own language—"can extend," "adapt existing infrastructure"—admitted that securities frameworks require substantial modification for crypto markets. Side B then turned this directly: a distinct regulatory category does precisely that adaptation, but starting from correct specifications rather than retrofitting T+1-era tools. This move converted A's answer into evidence for B's position and was never adequately answered in the closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): B won by making the central comparison clearer: A showed real custody, commingling, and manipulation risks, but B persuasively argued those risks justify stringent targeted rules, not necessarily securities-exchange or broker-dealer classification. B’s strongest point was that net capital, segregation, audits, and reserve checks can be imposed in a distinct framework while avoiding misclassification of Bitcoin, Ethereum, commodities, and utility-like tokens. A had a credible uniform-oversight/arbitrage argument, but did not prove the securities label was uniquely necessary rather than merely one way to impose seriousness. B slightly stretched the motion by defending a stringent distinct category rather than a lightly supervised one, but A did not exploit that enough to outweigh B’s fit-for-purpose critique.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s decisive rebuttal was that A conflated “having serious rules” with “being regulated as securities,” since a distinct regime could impose the same capital, segregation, audit, and reserve obligations while better fitting 24/7 blockchain markets.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side A won the decisive clash on enforcement mechanisms. Side B argued that recent platform collapses were reactive enforcement failures rather than a lack of regulation, but Side A expertly countered that broker-dealer status provides proactive, mandatory trips (net capital calculations and segregation audits) that prevent these collapses before they happen. Side B was forced to argue that a distinct category could simply copy these strict rules, which violated the prompt's constraint that the distinct category is 'lightly supervised.' Side A also successfully neutralized Side B's asset-mismatch argument by pointing out that custody and commingling risks are identical regardless of whether a token meets the Howey test.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Turn 7 (Side A): A cleanly answers the pressure questions by differentiating proactive broker-dealer audits from reactive fraud law, and by explaining how existing continuous surveillance for high-frequency trading resolves the 24/7 market mismatch.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A built a consistently stronger case across all rounds. The core functional equivalence argument—platforms that intermediate, custody, and profit from customer assets should face the obligations attaching to those functions—was cleanly stated, defended, and never genuinely neutralized. The decisive rebuttal in Round 1 exposed Side B's critical conflation: that blockchain settlement mechanics change the economic intermediation risk that securities regulation targets. A's FTX example (assets held in platform-controlled wallets "on-chain") directly collapsed B's blockchain-custody distinction for the platforms that matter most. A's pressure questions were sharper: Q1 cornered B on the gap between decentralized edge cases and the volume-dominant centralized platforms the motion governs; Q2 exposed proof-of-reserves as a diluted substitute for real audit and capital requirements. Side B's recurring retreat to oracle failures, smart-contract bugs, and decentralized exceptions is real for edge cases but never addressed the "most platforms" qualifier that A correctly planted in Round 2. B's A1 response—claiming users "often retain key control after execution"—didn't rebut the custody risk during the period platforms hold assets. B's targeted-safeguards alternative (proof-of-reserves, segregated wallets) was persuasively reframed by A as a lighter version of obligations securities law already imposes, which undercut rather than strengthened B's case for a distinct category. A's closing was clipped but the visible portion completed the key weighing; the execution cost is real but marginal given how cleanly A won the prior rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Round 1 rebuttal identifying that "every securities fraud involves criminal conduct—that does not make the regulatory framework under which it was detected and prosecuted irrelevant" was the most decisive exchange. It directly nullified B's repeated claim that FTX-style collapses resulted from criminal conduct rather than regulatory gaps, and A sharpened it by noting that broker-dealer capital requirements, audits, and inspections make such conduct harder to execute and easier to detect—precisely the gap crypto's light regime left open. B never recovered a clean answer to this point.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by making the central comparison clearer: most customer risk sits on centralized platforms that custody assets, control order flow, and create familiar intermediary conflicts, so securities-style duties are a better default than a lighter separate category. Side B had a coherent mismatch argument about decentralized mechanics, smart contracts, and targeted crypto rules, but it leaned too heavily on decentralized or hybrid exceptions and never convincingly answered why those should govern most platforms. Its alternative also drifted toward a more robust bespoke regime, which did not squarely defend the motion's lightly supervised category.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange was its answer that decentralized non-custodial models may deserve tailoring, but they are exceptions rather than a reason to exempt dominant custodial platforms from capital, audit, custody, and conflict rules.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A wins by effectively leveraging the 'most platforms' wording to isolate Side B's arguments as applying primarily to decentralized edge cases. A persuasively argues that dominant centralized exchanges perform traditional intermediary functions and generate traditional risks (like commingling) that require traditional securities regulation. Side B's attempt to argue that centralized exchanges lack persistent custody relies on a highly questionable description of how those platforms actually settle trades, severely weakening their core defense.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 decisively neutralizes B's focus on smart contracts and oracle failures by pointing out that rules should be calibrated to where volume and risk actually concentrate (centralized custodians), while genuine decentralized models can receive tailored accommodations within the broader framework.
