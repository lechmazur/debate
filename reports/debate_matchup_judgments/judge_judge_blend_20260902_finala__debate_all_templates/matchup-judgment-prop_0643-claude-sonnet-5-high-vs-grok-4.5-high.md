- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by keeping the governance-design burdens live. PRO's residual-risk case was coherent, but CON successfully exposed that a worker-elected director faces a unique, enforceable electoral feedback loop pointed at current-job protection, and that PRO never supplied a mechanism to sever that loop. CON also showed the mandate is one-size-fits-all where harms vary widely. PRO's responses—"statute imposes single fiduciary duty" and "shared survival aligns incentives"—were rebutted as renaming rather than resolving the conflict. The European transplant argument lacked institutional specifics for unitary boards, and by the close PRO had not materially damaged CON's core objections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's distinction between informal board pressures and the formal electoral accountability of worker-elected directors (A4.1-A4.2), which showed that PRO's "boards already balance competing interests" analogy collapses because workers can actually remove their representative, unlike traders or creditors.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.2: CON distinguished informal influence from enforceable electoral removal and showed PRO's statutory single-duty fix either fails or renders the seat symbolic, leaving incumbency and fiduciary conflict unresolved.

Side A's distinction between informal market pressures and a formal electoral feedback loop decisively neutralized Side B's defense against the "conflicting duty" and "incumbency protection" charges. Side A also effectively highlighted the mismatch between a uniform board-seat mandate and the vastly different risk profiles of various public companies, offering targeted alternatives that Side B failed to adequately refute. Side B's normative case on risk asymmetry was strong, but their inability to resolve the structural conflicts of their proposed mechanism cost them the debate.
