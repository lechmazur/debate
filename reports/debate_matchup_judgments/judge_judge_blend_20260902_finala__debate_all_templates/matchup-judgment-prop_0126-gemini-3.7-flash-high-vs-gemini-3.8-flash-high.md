### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): A built a clearer, more durable case: family contact drives recidivism reduction, high fees are a regressive tax on innocent third parties, and marginal digital costs are negligible. Crucially, A's analogy that prisons already ration scarce physical resources (yard time, showers, dining halls) via administrative timers rather than price was never squarely defeated—B's claim that phone terminals are uniquely "physically scarce" doesn't distinguish them from yard time in a way that survives scrutiny. B's strongest structural move—the "regulated nominal fee" compromise—stayed coherent throughout, but its answer to the sharpest pressure question (why should blameless families fund the state's internal infrastructure) reduced to a technical scope distinction ("fees cover only transmission/hardware, not surveillance") that never actually answered the equity objection. A's closing crystallized the moral frame ("financial tollbooth," "taxing children") more memorably, while B's closing largely restated fiscal-tradeoff assertions without fresh resolution. Both sides handled the Q&A format correctly, but A's answers landed more directly on the core clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure question ("why should innocent, low-income families fund the state's internal surveillance infrastructure") and B's technical-but-evasive answer (fees are like "utility co-pays" covering only operational costs) was the pivotal exchange—B never resolved the underlying moral objection, leaving A's regressive-tax framing as the decisive unanswered lever.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that scheduled quotas and timers can manage scarce terminals without rationing family contact by wealth. Side B also weighed the burden on innocent families and rehabilitative benefits more clearly, though its claim that funding requires no tradeoff was overstated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its answer to the congestion argument: if a nominal fee is too small to exclude poor families, it is also too small to stop hoarding or gang control, whereas enforceable time limits directly ration the scarce resource. Side A largely repeated that fees manage traffic without resolving this tension.

CON conceded predatory fees but never escaped the regressive-tax and “fees just price out the poor” pressure; its finite-budget and alert-overload claims stayed asserted rather than decisive. PRO’s later weighing made the live choice clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 plus [A4.6]: infrastructure not zero-sum vs programs, volume mostly non-threat domestic traffic, and fees ration by wealth while timers allocate equitably without creating prepaid-minute currency.
