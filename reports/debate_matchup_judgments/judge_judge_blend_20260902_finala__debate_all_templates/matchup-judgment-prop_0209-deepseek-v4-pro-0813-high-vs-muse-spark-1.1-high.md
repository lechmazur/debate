### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-organized cases, but CON's line of attack proved more decisive across the exchange. CON anchored its case in the concrete 2015 relocation precedent and used it consistently to argue that mandatory quotas produce open, legally corrosive noncompliance rather than fixing anything. PRO's attempt to distinguish "automatic, enforced" quotas from the 2015 scheme was undercut by PRO's own concession in Rebuttal 2/A1 that enforcement would require fines and blocked funds — exactly the coercive mechanism CON warned would teach member states that EU law is optional. CON's pressure question on the family-ties/capacity-formula dilemma (Q2) was the most damaging moment of the debate: PRO's answer ("priority in placement... cannot veto") never actually resolved whether the formula or the ties control, and PRO's closing dropped this thread entirely, letting CON's "formula collapses to Hamburg, or family absconds and you need internal detention" framing stand unanswered. PRO's strongest move — insisting that funding without relocating the legal claim is "subsidized containment, not burden-sharing" — was a real and clean distinction, but CON's honest concession that the claim stays local while resources move was defended coherently as a workable alternative, and PRO never showed why shifting the legal claim itself is operationally necessary rather than merely symbolically fairer. CON's rebuttal was clipped for length, a real execution cost, but the substantive content that got through was sharper and more responsive than PRO's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q2 pressure question on the family-ties vs. capacity-formula dilemma, and PRO's incomplete answer to it in Rebuttal 2/closing, was the decisive exchange — it exposed an unresolved contradiction at the heart of the quota mechanism that PRO never squarely closed.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by exposing two implementation failures that A never fully resolved: resistant member states and asylum seekers with preferred destinations. B also offered a concrete capacity-sharing alternative, weakening A’s claim that quotas were the only way to relieve frontline states. A’s fairness and legal-responsibility framing remained strong, but B made policy feasibility easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s family-ties dilemma was the most decisive exchange: either preferences override the capacity formula and quotas cease distributing burdens, or assignments override preferences and people may abscond. A’s benefits-based enforcement answer mitigated but did not eliminate that problem.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully proving that Side B's alternative (funding frontline states) only addresses processing costs and leaves the ultimate integration burden on the geographic frontline. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides built serious, well-structured cases around the central question each explicitly named: which system is actually enforceable. PRO grounded its mechanism in plausible institutional detail (QMV passage, Article 80/78, budget conditionality as a post-2015 fix) and effectively argued that leaving frontline states alone is "organized neglect." But CON's pressure questions exposed two structural cracks PRO never fully closed: (1) PRO's own "priced refusal" mechanism (pay a fine, keep no one) is functionally a financial opt-out, undercutting PRO's opening claim that there is "no free opt-out"; and (2) PRO's answer on secondary movement (the receiving state can return people to the assigned state) still depends on that assigned state's cooperation — the same collective-action failure that sank the 2015 scheme, just with better paperwork. CON named this directly in Rebuttal 2 and Closing (B4.4, B5.3) and PRO's final speech reasserted prior points without specifically neutralizing the "pay-not-receive" and "cooperation-dependent return" critiques. CON's own alternative (operational solidarity at the point of arrival) was somewhat underdeveloped and vulnerable to PRO's "billions already spent on Moria" counter, but CON's core dismantling of PRO's enforcement story was the more damaging, less-answered thread by the end of the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 / Closing point that PRO's "priced refusal" mechanism lets states pay a fine instead of taking people (B5.3), combined with the observation that PRO's return mechanism for secondary movers still depends on the assigned state's cooperation — the very compliance problem that broke the 2015 scheme (B4.4) — landed as the decisive, largely unanswered blow on the enforceability question both sides agreed was central.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): A won by distinguishing temporary frontline registration from the much larger long-term reception and adjudication burden that quotas distribute. B raised serious feasibility and compliance objections, especially against financial opt-outs, but its operational-support alternative never adequately explained how frontline states sustainably accommodate successful claimants and prolonged cases. A also offered the clearer comparison to an already-defied Dublin system, though some legal-enforcement claims were overly confident.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1 was decisive: it conceded that quotas do not eliminate initial processing, then showed that B was treating this front-end task as the entire asylum burden. B never fully neutralized A’s claim that redistribution prevents short-term arrival facilities from becoming years-long camps.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully separating the initial screening bottleneck from the burden of long-term reception, demonstrating that quotas solve the latter. Side B effectively pressured the political enforceability of relocation, but Side A's comparative weighing—that the status quo's unregistered secondary movement is worse than a legalized, trackable transfer system—provided the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's argument in Rebuttal 1 that Side B mistakes initial reception for the whole system, clarifying that quotas shrink the frontline burden from years to weeks by addressing long-term housing.
