### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and stayed on the motion, but B built the more resilient case. B's opening identified the structural problem PRO never fully solved: support is a multi-vendor bundle no single maker controls, so a legally binding packaging guarantee creates predictable legal incentives to print the minimum defensible number. The pivotal exchange came in Pressure Questions: A's Q1 answer conceded "print the date you can keep" and that "small makers print a short floor" — precisely the race-to-the-bottom mechanism B had been warning about. B seized this concession in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, quoting A's own words to show the packaging mandate normalizes disposability rather than rewarding long support. A never adequately answered why a locked, timestamped digital disclosure at checkout (B's proposed alternative) wouldn't capture the same non-erasable-record benefit without the packaging costs A relied on (reprint waste, pre-purchase invisibility for online buyers, and the misread-as-expiry risk B raised repeatedly and A never squarely rebutted). A's closing repeated the "can't be quietly erased" line without engaging that counter, leaving B's alternative unrefuted as a live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 turning A's own Pressure Question answer ("print the date you can keep," "small makers print a short floor") into direct evidence for the race-to-the-bottom mechanism was the most decisive moment; A never recovered from having its own concession weaponized against its central claim.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): CON won by exposing a structural flaw in the mandate: because smart devices rely on third-party tech stacks, a strict physical guarantee forces companies to print worst-case legal minimums. PRO conceded this by saying makers should 'print the date you can keep,' which CON successfully weaponized to prove the rule would normalize disposability rather than encourage competition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 decisively turned PRO's own answers against them, demonstrating that forcing small makers to print a 'short floor' creates a race to the bottom dictated by lawyers rather than a competitive market.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON demonstrated that mandating a physical packaging guarantee would backfire: strict liability fears and upstream dependencies would push manufacturers to print the shortest defensible date, creating a lawyer-imposed ceiling that normalizes disposability rather than informing buyers. CON also showed that physical packaging is irrelevant to online buyers and inferior to a binding, timestamped digital disclosure at checkout, which PRO eventually conceded was needed anyway. PRO's case was elegant but could not overcome the structural incentive problem or the waste/extendability trade-off.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The "ceiling versus floor" exchange in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where CON turned PRO's concession that small makers would "print a short floor" into proof that the rule forces a defensively minimal, lawyer-dictated number, undermining PRO's claim that the rule enables honest competition upward.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases, but B's mechanism-level critique proved more durable under pressure. B's "lawyer's number becomes a ceiling" argument gave a plausible causal story for why a legal guarantee would compress rather than lengthen support, and B used the Pressure Questions round decisively: when pressed on enforceability against dead chip vendors and on cheap devices, A's answers (A4.1–A4.2) had to invent supply-chain duties (escrow, contracted vendors, offline modes) not contained in the motion and openly conceded that non-compliant "smart" devices would have to disappear from the market. B correctly flagged this as scope creep — a labeling motion quietly turned into a de facto ban — and used that concession as the spine of both Rebuttal 2 and the closing ("the carton cannot keep the device alive"). A's strongest counter-punch (the empirical point that silence already produces abandonment, landed cleanly in Q1) damaged B's "hidden generosity" claim, but B's account survived by distinguishing "existing short lifespans" from "a legally frozen floor that becomes universal," and A never fully closed that gap. A's closing repeated its risk-shifting frame effectively but did not resolve the ban/scope-creep problem B had exposed. On balance B made the clash easier to decide and left the cleaner, more defensible reason to negate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question on chip-vendor liability and the "is this a ban?" question, combined with B's Rebuttal 2 response showing A's A1/A2 answers had smuggled in duties beyond the packaging label and effectively conceded a market-exit mandate for cheap devices.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's defenses of the status quo (that silence enables "generous engineers" and "living policies" provide honesty). Side A successfully framed a "living policy" as an editable bait-and-switch after purchase, making a compelling case that a printed guarantee restores consumer agency and shifts financial risk back to the manufacturer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Turn 7 [A4.6] destruction of Side B's alternative: "Your living webpage is editable after I paid. My box is a contract before I pay. That is agency."
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing that a printed box date cannot bind supply-chain dependencies like chip vendors or cloud backends, and that PRO's attempt to solve this in Rebuttal 2—introducing escrow, supplier contracts, and offline fallback modes—rewrote the motion into a supply-chain liability regime rather than a simple packaging label. CON's "lawyer's floor becomes ceiling" mechanism remained live because PRO never fully overcame the behavioral and retailer dynamics that would freeze support at the printed date. PRO's concession that devices unable to promise a year should not be sold as smart confirmed CON's charge that the policy functions as a ban disguised as disclosure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (A4), where it exposed that PRO's answers to pressure questions had invented an entire supply-chain insurance regime (escrow, contracted vendors, offline modes) that the packaging-label proposition does not contain, and that PRO had effectively conceded the motion functions as a ban on inexpensive connected devices.
