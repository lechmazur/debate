### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B won by making the clearer comparative case that governance rules about consent, authority, liability, and review can and should precede outdoor experimentation, even if technical standards are refined later. Side A’s strongest points about treaty delay, data needs, and rogue actors kept the debate competitive, but B more effectively answered that A was conflating knowledge needed for deployment with governance needed for accountable research. A also leaned on overconfident claims that tiny experiments are harmless while still uniquely necessary, which B exploited well.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response that a binding framework need not contain every technical threshold in advance, but can establish authority, consent, notification, and expert-review procedures before field data is generated, was the decisive answer to A’s “scientific vacuum” argument.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won by exposing a fatal tension in Side B's case and showing that governance can precede outdoor experimentation. Side A demonstrated that governance is fundamentally institutional and political—consent, liability, notification—not merely a set of technical thresholds, so empirical data is not a prerequisite for a binding framework. More damagingly, Side A relentlessly pressed the contradiction that Side B simultaneously called field experiments 'harmless' and justified them by citing atmospheric complexity that models cannot capture. Side B never resolved this: if the system is truly too uncertain to model, safety cannot be assumed by scale alone, and the political demonstration of unilateral atmospheric capability becomes an independent harm. Side A successfully turned Side B's 'rogue actor' argument by showing that open experimentation normalizes unilateral modification and hands capability to bad actors, whereas a moratorium strips the 'responsible research' pretext. Side B's 'co-evolution' frame was persuasive in the abstract but crumbled against Side A's 'capture' argument: once experiments create institutional stakes and demonstrated capability, governance chases facts on the ground rather than authorizing them. Side A's closing crystallized these live clashes cleanly, while Side B's closing largely repeated claims that had already been undermined. The margin reflects a clear but competitive win: Side B raised legitimate concerns about monitoring and treaty timelines, but failed to answer the core comparative pressure on whether governance should authorize or retroactively manage planetary manipulation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's sustained attack on Side B's contradictory assertions that outdoor experiments are both physically harmless and urgently necessary because atmospheric complexity defies modeling. This pressure began in the Question round, was sharpened in Rebuttal 2 by linking unpredictability to the impossibility of guaranteed safety, and was crystallized in the closing as a resolved contradiction that collapsed Side B's risk-assessment and necessity claims simultaneously.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A wins by successfully exposing and exploiting a core tension in Side B’s case: the simultaneous claim that small-scale experiments are predictably harmless due to scale while also arguing that atmospheric complexity makes modeling insufficient and empirical data strictly necessary. Side A’s distinction between structural governance (consent, liability, notification regimes) and technical thresholds effectively neutralized B’s “data-first” demand. A also turned B’s rogue-actor argument by showing how open field trials normalize unilateral capability and hand strategic knowledge to adversaries, whereas a moratorium establishes a clear legal line that delegitimizes non-compliant action. Side B delivered a competent case but relied on repetitive framing and never fully resolved the contradiction A pressed across multiple rounds. A’s closing crystallized the clash around political leverage and path dependency, providing the cleaner comparative path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 and Closing sequence, where it directly answers B’s pressure questions while hammering the logical contradiction in B’s safety claims (“If we genuinely cannot predict effects, we cannot also guarantee safety”). This forced B onto the defensive and allowed A to reframe the entire debate around political precedent and leverage rather than technical data collection.
