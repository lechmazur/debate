- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): B won by establishing the collective-action mechanism and showing that prohibition need not eliminate every payment to reduce reliable, scalable ransomware revenue. B's repeated clipping and unsupported claims that attacks would plummet narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to underground evasion was decisive: imperfect enforcement does not make prohibition futile if it removes lawful and insurable payment channels. A showed that sophisticated attackers could adapt, but did not neutralize the prospect of meaningful market contraction.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side A successfully reframed the opponent's strongest example (Colonial Pipeline) to show that ransoms often fail to provide rapid recovery while actively funding future attacks.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal, which flipped Side B's Colonial Pipeline example to demonstrate that the decryption key was sluggish and the company relied on backups anyway, shattering the illusion of ransom as a reliable emergency brake.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON (Side A) kept the cleaner comparative: immediate, irreversible harm to hospitals/pipelines versus speculative, partial market shrinkage. ### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing the cleaner comparative mechanism: support reduces victim desperation, prohibition removes reliable demand, and residual evasion cannot preserve ransomware at current scale. Side A persuasively showed that support cannot undo data exposure, but repeatedly assumed catastrophic victims would keep paying in sufficient numbers to sustain the global market. Its intelligence-loss argument was also weakened by B's point that victims would still report attacks to obtain aid. B better weighed immediate hard cases against market-wide deterrence, while A's repeated clipping imposed an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal most decisively answered the secrecy and proxy objections: attack reporting could continue as a condition of support, while intermediaries merely reroute payments and cannot recreate demand once major victims are less desperate to pay.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by effectively leveraging the 'victim support' clause to neutralize Side B's best arguments, while Side B suffers from significant execution errors (clipping in three speeches). Side A's nuanced explanations that support includes 'regulatory shielding' to blunt double-extortion, and that victims will still report attacks (preserving forensics) even if they hide payments, went largely unrefuted by Side B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they explain that victims will report the attack to unlock funds (preserving forensics) and that state support (like regulatory shielding) collapses the payment value of a data leak rather than blocking it technologically.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): B wins on the decisive double-extortion and intelligence clash. B conceded it cannot block a dump but argued funded legal/liquidity support collapses the value of paying for silence, shrinking conversion and overall market volume, while reporting for aid preserves forensics. A asserted catastrophic harm would keep high-value payments large and hidden via proxies but was clipped three times, never showing how proxies preserve scale when major-market paydays dry up or why secret payments would remain dominant rather than residual.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's A1/A2 in R2: admitting support doesn't stop dump but collapses payment value, and that attack reporting for aid preserves forensics while only a small hidden minority of payments remains.
