### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger cumulative case by consistently anchoring arguments to the motion's operative language—"mainly personal lifestyle preferences"—while CON spent most of the debate trying to slide the terrain toward religious mandates and disability accommodations that are already protected by separate legal frameworks. PRO correctly identified this maneuver early and kept calling it out. The decisive structural advantage came in PRO's dilemma formulation at [B4.6]: either some parental rules genuinely are lifestyle preferences, in which case schools need a principle for declining them (which PRO provides), or none are, in which case CON must defend enforcing every parental demand no matter how idiosyncratic. CON never cleanly escaped this trap. The closest CON came was the informal-partnership-vs-codification distinction [A4.2], which was a genuine insight but was underdeveloped and left hanging. PRO's pressure questions also outperformed CON's: the "schools already make these calls" question directly exposed CON's core claim as internally inconsistent, and the A1 answer citing IEPs and 504 plans was more concrete than CON's parallel answer. CON's "majoritarian bias" framing had some rhetorical force and the "Trojan horse for state assimilation" line was memorable, but these were not backed by a clean argument that survived PRO's response that declining to enforce a preference is neutrality rather than indoctrination. CON's repeated re-introduction of religious and disability examples that fell outside the motion's scope counted as an execution cost across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's dilemma at [B4.6] was the most decisive exchange: forcing CON to choose between accepting that some parental demands are lifestyle preferences (requiring a declining principle PRO already provides) or defending unlimited institutional enforcement of every parental directive. Combined with [B4.1]'s concrete answer that IEPs and 504 plans already operationalize the distinction CON called impossible, this neutralized CON's central "who draws the line" objection without CON ever producing a clean counter-answer.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): B won by keeping the debate tied to the motion's qualifier: mainly personal lifestyle preferences. A's parental-rights and pluralism case had force, but it repeatedly relied on religious, medical, disability, and cultural obligations that B persuasively showed were outside the motion or already protectable. B gave the cleaner principle: schools should enforce safety, education, and legally grounded accommodations, not become surveillance arms for idiosyncratic household rules. A's line-drawing objection was the best CON route, but A never supplied a workable alternative short of making schools enforce nearly every parental demand.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's dilemma in Rebuttal 2 was decisive: either some demands really are personal lifestyle preferences, in which case schools need discretion to decline them, or A must defend institutional enforcement of every idiosyncratic rule. A's answer repeated majoritarian-risk concerns but did not escape the dilemma.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A won by successfully leveraging the motion's text to carve out medical and religious exceptions, forcing Side B to defend the enforcement of arbitrary preferences. Side A's framing of non-enforcement as 'neutrality' rather than 'indoctrination' decisively neutralized Side B's primary offense.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 effectively answered the pressure questions by pointing to existing objective frameworks (IEPs, civil rights law) and highlighting the absurdity of treating non-enforcement as active indoctrination.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): CON (Side A) built the more durable structural argument: the framework PRO needs to implement this motion—sorting "idiosyncratic lifestyle preferences" from "protected accommodations"—requires a decision-maker who will inevitably favor mainstream, institutionally recognized practices over marginal ones. CON grounded this with the kosher/halal/hijab examples and then exploited PRO's own inconsistency: PRO's opening called the hijab "isolating clothing that restricts physical movement," but by Rebuttal 1 it had become a "widely recognized religious accommodation." CON caught that shift sharply in both the pressure questions and the closing, and PRO's response ("used hypothetically") was evasive rather than clarifying. CON's Q2 in the pressure round was particularly sharp—challenging PRO to name a restrictive lifestyle preference schools should override that isn't already prohibited by existing law—and PRO's answer (beige-food diets, gender isolation during recess) was the debate's most effective PRO moment, partially answering that pressure. But PRO never stabilized the line-drawing framework that their entire case depends on, and CON crystallized this failure cleanly in the closing. PRO's harm arguments (developmental harm, administrative burden) had genuine weight but were not decisively comparative against CON's structural concern. CON's Rebuttal 1 contained garbled mid-sentence characters that disrupted readability and count as an execution cost, but the surrounding arguments remained discernible. Overall, CON made the debate easier to decide in their favor by sustaining one precise, repeatable pressure point that PRO never squarely neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The hijab category-shift exchange was decisive. CON caught PRO moving the hijab from "isolating clothing restricting physical movement" (Opening) to "widely recognized religious accommodation" (Rebuttal 1) within a single debate, then hammered this instability in the pressure questions and closing. PRO's defense—that the opening was "hypothetical"—was evasive. CON correctly used this as direct evidence that PRO's framework for distinguishing protected practices from idiosyncratic preferences cannot be applied stably, undermining the entire mechanism PRO needed to make the motion work.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the motion focused on active use of teachers and schools to enforce highly restrictive personal rules, rather than ordinary parental boundary-setting or protected religious accommodation. Side A raised a serious gatekeeping concern, but overextended it and repeatedly relied on a misdescribed hijab inconsistency that was not actually in Side B's opening. Side B's framing of teacher burden, classroom mission, and child social development remained the cleaner route to the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's decisive rebuttal was distinguishing passive respect for a family boundary from active enforcement of isolating rules, and arguing that the motion targets the latter. That answered much of Side A's pluralism objection while preserving concrete cases like enforced social isolation.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side B won by successfully proving that the distinction between 'lifestyle preference' and 'legitimate accommodation' is inherently subjective and risks majoritarian bias against unorthodox families. B effectively weaponized A's own examples—such as gender separation and dress codes—to demonstrate that what A considers an 'idiosyncratic lifestyle' is often a minority religious tenet. A compounded this by fumbling the defense of their own opening examples, allowing B to decisively win the core clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal, which challenged A's shifting definitions and reframed A's example of forbidden mixed-gender group work as an orthodox religious tenet, perfectly illustrating the dangers of institutional bias.
