### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by making the platform’s causal contribution, superior ability to prevent harm, and the rights holder’s otherwise inadequate remedy the decisive comparison. Its due-care safe harbor also provided a credible middle path between immunity and blanket strict liability. Side B raised serious overblocking and market-concentration risks, but its neutral-tool analogy and reliance on user volition did not fully address cases where the platform’s model supplies the infringing expression.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s most effective response was that shared liability could be conditioned on reasonable safeguards and safe harbors. This blunted Side B’s censorship and startup-collapse impacts while preserving liability for platforms that generate predictable infringements without reasonable precautions. Side B’s reply that this made the platform an intermediary did not establish why intermediaries should receive complete immunity.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by locking the decisive clash on manufacture/causation and the remedy vacuum: platforms generate fresh copies from model parameters rather than hosting third-party uploads, so traditional notice-and-takedown and pure user-volition framing leave rights holders without a workable recovery path. CON landed real pressure on policing/fair-use difficulty and open-source cartelization, and exposed tension in PRO’s negligence/safe-harbor softening, but never neutralized the co-creation and “recover from no one” problems as cleanly as PRO answered CON’s due-care and licensing questions. PRO’s moderate “shared liability with earned harbors” path made the proposition easier to affirm than CON’s toolmaker-immunity alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s manufacture/“where did the expression come from” line (through R1, pressure Qs, and closing use of A’s latent-parameters answer) against A’s neutral-tool and volition defense.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won by establishing that platform-generated copies fall outside existing DMCA safe harbors designed for user-uploaded content, and by dismantling CON’s "neutral tool" analogy. A showed the platform’s model—not the user’s prompt—supplies the protected expression, making shared liability with earned safe harbors a coherent policy to close a real remedial gap. CON’s volition defense and monopoly warnings were plausible but failed to answer where rights holders recover when anonymous users prompt on-demand infringement, and their attempt to absorb PRO’s proposal into existing law was decisively rebutted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 dismantling of the "neutral tool / existing law" frame, showing that DMCA safe harbors cover storage at user direction while generative AI involves platform-manufactured copies, leaving CON without a doctrine to shield on-demand generation and exposing the practical emptiness of "sue the user" remedies.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing the proposition's lack of a limiting principle. Side A embraced strict liability and relied on platform indispensability, but never adequately distinguished culpability from mere causal involvement or answered the deliberate-user case, fair-use overblocking, and incumbent-entrenchment concerns. Side A established that AI platforms are more active than passive hosts, but did not show why existing direct and secondary liability doctrines were insufficient or why every infringing output should trigger shared liability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to A4.1-A4.2 was decisive: it turned Side A's strict-liability concession and indispensability test into an overbreadth objection, contrasting genuinely platform-caused regurgitation with users deliberately engineering infringement.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by forcing the decisive clash onto strict liability versus existing fault doctrines, then exploiting PRO's shift from "duty of care" to automatic shared liability. CON's deliberate-infringer and indispensability critiques (utility/ISP) were never cleanly answered, while PRO's strongest regurgitation scenario was neutralized as already actionable without the motion. PRO kept a coherent co-producer frame but lost comparative pressure on limiting principle, fair-use/censorship costs, and why secondary liability fails.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 turning PRO's A1/A2 answers into a concession of faultless strict liability plus but-for causation without a limiting principle.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): CON won by demonstrating that the motion imposes strict liability without a limiting principle, sweeps in user-deliberate infringement while redundantly covering cases already addressed by existing law, and would force censorship-inducing filters that only incumbents could afford. PRO was unable to defend a coherent liability standard after being pressed, conceded strict liability, and never recovered from the rebuttal that indispensability is merely but-for causation rather than culpability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question exposing the strict liability tension (B3.1), followed by the rebuttal that indispensability is not culpability and the electric utility comparison (B4.4-B4.6), which decisively undermined PRO's theory of shared responsibility.
