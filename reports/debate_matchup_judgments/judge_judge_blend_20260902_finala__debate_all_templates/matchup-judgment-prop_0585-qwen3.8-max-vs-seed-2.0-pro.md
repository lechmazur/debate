### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but B's central "decade-urgency" framing—transmission's long lead times versus rapidly retiring coal and gas orders being placed today—landed as the more decisive and less-answered clash. A's systemic "pooling beats duplication" argument was strong and original, but A never squarely rebutted B's core claim that gas turbines being financed now cannot be stopped by transmission that won't exist for a decade; A's closing gestured at "before they become excuses for new gas" without engaging the timing mechanics B repeatedly stressed. The pressure-question exchange also favored B: B's question forcing A to name a fast-completed transmission/market reform exposed a real gap, and A's answer (A1 in Rebuttal 2) never named one, instead retreating to a generic "portfolio" claim—an evasion under the judging criteria. B's answers to A's questions, by contrast, engaged directly with numbers and reasoning (5-7 days of storage doing "double duty"), even if those figures were somewhat overprecise. That overprecision is a real ding against B's grounding score, but it did not offset the structural advantage B held on the least-resolved point of the debate: the opportunity cost of committing primary capital to slow infrastructure while irreversible fossil decisions are made this decade. A's case remains a strong, coherent systemic vision, but B did the sharper comparative work in the middle rounds and left the more damaging point unanswered at the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 argument that storage does "double/triple duty" (peak shaving, frequency regulation) 90% of the year, directly countering A's "duplication trap" framing, combined with B's unanswered closing point that gas turbines being ordered now cannot be stopped by transmission that won't be built for a decade.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B won by establishing the stronger system-level comparison: regional pooling reduces duplicated reserves, moves scarce clean power, unlocks renewables, and improves the value of later storage. Side A created serious timeline pressure, but repeatedly relied on unsupported exact timelines, deployment figures, weather claims, and categorical assertions that transmission would cancel no gas orders. It also never demonstrated that five to seven days of storage at every locality would be affordable, scalable, or sufficient. B answered the speed objection by distinguishing greenfield lines from grid upgrades and market integration, making its broader platform case survive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to the rare-event challenge was decisive: transmission need not be built solely for a one-percent crisis because it also provides everyday congestion relief, renewable delivery, and reserve sharing, whereas A’s alternative requires rarely needed multi-day reserves to be duplicated across many localities. A’s claim that those reserves would perform daily services did not resolve the cost, readiness, or recharge problem.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): A wins because B conceded that every locality would need roughly 5–7 days of firm storage, allowing A to expose a crippling duplication trap: local reserves must be replicated across thousands of nodes, remain fragile if events last longer, and still invite gas backfill. While B’s timeline urgency and gas-lock-in narrative were potent, B never showed that building a week of multi-day storage per node is actually faster or cheaper than the portfolio of grid enhancements A described, nor did B overcome A’s core systemic frame that pooling scarce clean resources through transmission beats fragmenting the grid into isolated storage islands.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s pressure question forcing B to specify local reserve requirements (5–7 days), which A then weaponized into the decisive “duplication trap” argument showing B’s model is prohibitively expensive and still unreliable compared to pooled regional reserves.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and followed the pressure-question format correctly. PRO's foundation-first logic (curtailment, "two-thirds" storage reduction, transmission unlocks storage) was clear but rested on an unhedged precise statistic and, crucially, PRO's own fallback to "small, targeted backup" for residual multi-day scarcity events conceded exactly the gap CON was pressing on. CON exploited this well, reframing the debate around "firmness" versus mere distance, and caught a real inconsistency: PRO's opening mechanism required continent-spanning integration, but PRO's rebuttal retreated to "boring, incremental interties," which CON flagged directly ([B4.5]) and PRO never squarely resolved in closing. CON's own answers were somewhat evasive on how storage gets charged when transmission-driven curtailment (not just timing mismatch) is the bottleneck, but this was a partial gap rather than a full concession. Overall CON's reframe was more resilient under pressure and CON's closing ("pipes move energy; storage guarantees it") crystallized the clash more decisively than PRO's restated "order" framing, which did not answer the gas-backup/decarbonization critique CON had raised.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's [B4.5] catching PRO's shift from "connect three time zones" (large-scale integration) to "boring incremental interties" as an implicit concession that continent-spanning integration does less work than PRO's opening claimed, combined with turning PRO's "small targeted backup" answer into a gas-reliance critique that undercut PRO's decarbonization claim.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side B won the priority comparison: transmission is deployable now, addresses system-wide congestion and geographic balancing, and reduces the amount of storage subsequently required. Side A effectively exposed B’s zero-output standard as a strawman and showed that correlated scarcity remains, but it did not establish that subsidizing immature long-duration storage would address more reliability and decarbonization needs than the broader transmission foundation. B’s unsupported exact claims about curtailment, storage reductions, and event frequency substantially narrowed the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest rebuttal was that Side A’s fast, modular deployment examples resembled short-duration storage rather than the long-duration technology at issue. Side A answered that subsidies could accelerate maturation, but never resolved the comparative uncertainty over cost, scale, and deployment speed.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side A won by showing that transmission solves a proven, immediate bottleneck—curtailment—that permanently wastes clean power regardless of storage capacity, while cutting total long-duration storage needs by roughly two thirds before any storage is purchased. Side B made a strong case for temporal firmness during multi-day lulls, but relied on subsidies maturing a technology that does not yet exist at grid scale and never fully overcame the structural inefficiency of building storage before the wires needed to charge it or utilize it regionally. A's "foundation first" framing created the cleaner comparative rationale for primary investment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 (Turn 3) exposing that Side B conflated short-duration and long-duration storage, and establishing that 100+ hour grid-scale storage is not currently deployed at scale, materially undermining CON's claims that storage is presently faster and easier to site than transmission.
