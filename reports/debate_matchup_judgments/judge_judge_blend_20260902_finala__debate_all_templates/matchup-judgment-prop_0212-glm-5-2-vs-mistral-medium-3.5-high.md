### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by consistently exploiting the structural tension between the motion's language ("much more broadly") and PRO's repeated reliance on safeguards borrowed from the restricted-access model the motion asks to move beyond. This was not a trivial debating trick—CON maintained it precisely throughout all five turns and it genuinely damaged PRO's case. PRO's best answer, the "default-shift" argument (changing the presumption from secrecy to access while keeping narrow exceptions), was substantive and real, but CON answered it effectively: a presumption shift makes preemptive proof of harm nearly impossible in small communities, so the promised exceptions collapse in practice. The Argentina rebuttal was the most decisive single exchange: PRO cited Argentina repeatedly as a success model for broad access, but CON accurately showed that Argentina's Abuelas process was targeted, case-by-case inquiry—exactly the restricted model CON was defending. PRO never recovered from that inversion. CON's small-community redaction point (street, date, unit identifies a person even without a name) was sharp and largely unanswered. CON also converted PRO's "speculative harms" charge by pointing to Stasi informant outing as documented, not hypothetical. PRO's human-stakes case (Algeria, Rwanda, Argentina) carried genuine emotional weight and grounded the debate in concrete injustice, which prevented a blowout. But PRO's safeguards-versus-breadth tension remained live and unresolved through the closing, while CON crystallized it cleanly. CON's closing phrase—"a second violence performed in justice's name"—was rhetorically effective and substantively grounded, not mere oratory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal that Argentina's Abuelas process was a targeted, case-by-case inquiry—not broad access—was the most damaging single exchange. PRO had cited Argentina as the flagship example of successful broad opening; CON showed it actually vindicated the restricted model CON was defending. PRO never adequately answered this inversion across any subsequent round, leaving their strongest positive example working against them.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by demonstrating that Side B's defense depended on safeguards—redaction, mediation, targeted review, and confidentiality agreements—that the motion's call for "much more broadly" access structurally undermines. Side A successfully turned Side B's Argentina example into evidence for controlled, case-by-case access rather than broad opening, and maintained a consistent meta-argument that Side B was quietly borrowing from the restricted model the proposition asks voters to move beyond. Side A also exposed a decisive burden shift: a presumption of access requires victims to prove specific future harm in advance, which is nearly impossible in small communities where retaliation is subtle and delayed. Side B's moral case about ongoing injustice remained forceful, but never resolved the core tension that the safeguards they needed to prevent harm were incompatible with the broader default they were defending.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's sustained attack across Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 showing that "much more broadly" cannot coherently mean "broad but also heavily safeguarded," turning Side B's Argentina example against them, and demonstrating how redaction collapses in small communities where contextual details identify individuals regardless of whether names are printed.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively exposing the contradiction at the heart of Side A's case: PRO defends "much broader" access by repeatedly importing safeguards (redaction, mediation, confidentiality agreements) that belong to the careful, restricted model the motion explicitly asks states to move beyond. B's structural critique—that a presumption of broad access unfairly shifts the burden onto vulnerable individuals to prove specific future harm, and that redaction routinely fails in small, fractured communities where contextual details identify people anyway—materially collapses A's route to victory. B also successfully neutralizes A's flagship Argentina example by noting it succeeded precisely because it was a targeted, case-by-case process, not a broad disclosure regime. A's later speeches retreat into redefining the motion rather than answering the textual clash, while B consistently sharpens the comparison and crystallizes why the proposition as written lacks the protections PRO's own moral case depends on.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2 effectively dismantled PRO's safeguard reliance, demonstrating that "much more broadly" structurally undermines the redaction and review PRO needs, and showing how historian publication norms and tight community ties make any proposed firewalls porous in practice.
