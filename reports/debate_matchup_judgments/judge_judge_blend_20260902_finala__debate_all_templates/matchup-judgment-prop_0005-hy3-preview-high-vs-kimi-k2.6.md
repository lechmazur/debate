### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Both sides ran coherent, substantive cases, but CON (Side B) emerged with a cleaner comparative path to victory on three decisive exchanges. First, CON's redirection mechanism argument (B4.1–B4.2) is the most unexplored gap in PRO's case: PRO repeatedly assumed a ban would free up billions for neutral public health messaging, yet never supplied a statutory or economic mechanism by which prohibition converts private marketing budgets into public grants. CON correctly identified this as a fantasy, and PRO's closing still relied on the same unsupported assumption without repairing the gap. Second, CON maintained throughout that physicians remain the prescription gatekeeper, making the harm PRO describes filtered rather than automatic; PRO's strongest rejoinder (ad-driven appointments waste clinical time) is real but narrower than the structural claim it was meant to rebut. Third, the underdiagnosis argument — that the ad is the knock at the door for people who have normalized symptoms — was never fully neutralized. PRO's counter that ads push branded products rather than condition awareness is partially right but ignores CON's concrete HIV PrEP and depression examples, where brand awareness and condition awareness are functionally identical. PRO's strongest moment was the structural incompatibility argument (A2.3) and the useful distinction between government and corporate spots (A4.1), but CON's response — that the 30-second format works fine as an awareness signal if the physician contextualizes it — held together across rounds. PRO also used nonexistent reference IDs (C1.3, C2.5) in Rebuttal 2, a minor epistemic cost. CON's closing was rhetorically sharper and better crystallized the weighing: "filtered distortion vs. absolute silence" gave readers a memorable decision rule. The margin is clear but not dominant because PRO's structural and vulnerable-populations arguments remained live and only partially answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO's Q1 (B4.1–B4.2): CON identified that the proposition contains no statutory redirection mechanism, so a ban would more plausibly shift pharma spending to physician detailing and shareholder returns than to neutral public health campaigns. PRO's entire closing still rested on the redirection premise without addressing this gap, making CON's answer the most decisive single exchange in the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): B won by making the clearer comparative case: ad harms are filtered through physician gatekeeping, while a ban creates a real loss of awareness for undiagnosed patients. A had plausible concerns about distortion, costs, and vulnerable consumers, but leaned too heavily on neutral public campaigns and the unsupported idea that banned ad spending would be redirected there. B directly exploited that missing mechanism and framed the tradeoff more decisively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal was decisive: it answered A's pressure questions by separating market awareness from physician override and by showing that prohibition does not fund neutral public health messaging. That undercut A's main answer to the underdiagnosis benefit.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won decisively by exposing a critical flaw in Side B's case: the assumption that banning corporate ads would somehow redirect that private spending into underfunded public health campaigns. Side A logically demonstrated that without a statutory mechanism, this money would simply go to other corporate channels, leaving a complete vacuum in patient awareness. Side A also successfully weighed the filtered harms of ad-debunking against the absolute harms of underdiagnosis.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (A4.1/A4.2) completely dismantled Side B's claim that a ban would free up corporate money for public health campaigns, correctly noting that expecting Wall Street to voluntarily fund neutral health education is a fantasy.
