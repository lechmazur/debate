### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): PRO won primarily by anchoring the debate around a comparative claim that CON never fully neutralized: that every holistic alternative—essays, extracurriculars, letters of recommendation—correlates with family income more tightly than standardized test scores do. CON's strongest counter was the algorithmic-filter argument (applications below a threshold never reaching human eyes), which is genuinely powerful, but PRO's Pressure Question 2 correctly identified that this is an argument against how scores are misused, not against collecting the data. CON's rebuttal—"collecting the data guarantees it will be used as a filter"—was circular and speculative, and the same institutional-incentive logic would equally condemn GPA cutoffs. PRO's answer to CON's Q2 [B4.3] was the debate's clearest analytical moment: transcript contextualization requires grading distributions, course-rigor data, and institutional knowledge across thousands of unfamiliar high schools, while a test score provides one standardized coordinate that makes that contextualization possible rather than redundant. CON's A1 answer to PRO's scaling question was more substantive than expected (regional recruiters, school profiles, batch geography), but PRO correctly pressed that these systems still cannot supply the missing baseline knowledge for thousands of schools at volume. CON also produced memorable rhetoric ("numerical turnstile calibrated to privilege," "automates inequality behind a decimal point") and the debate was genuinely competitive throughout. PRO's two clipped turns are a real execution cost that limited its scoring in grounding, but the core comparative argument survived both clips intact. On balance, PRO held its live route to victory—the comparative income-correlation claim—while CON's filter argument, though live, was more about institutional behavior than about the test itself, reducing its decisiveness.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question 2 [B3.2]—"Isn't that an argument against how scores are *used*—not against collecting the data?"—was the pivotal exchange. It correctly reframed CON's strongest argument (automatic algorithmic filtering) as a complaint about implementation abuse rather than a principled reason to eliminate the data point. CON's answer that "collecting the data guarantees it will be used as a filter" was circular, and PRO's follow-up in Rebuttal 2 [B4.3] reinforced the point by explaining why transcript contextualization demands fundamentally more prior institutional knowledge than a standardized coordinate does—a distinction CON never adequately answered.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A won a competitive debate by keeping the comparison focused on public universities at scale: tests are imperfect, but removing the common benchmark leaves admissions more dependent on harder-to-detect wealth signals and inconsistent transcripts. Side B’s “numerical turnstile” argument was strong rhetorically, but it often attacked bad implementation rather than the requirement itself, and its claim that test collection inevitably becomes automatic filtering was overbroad. A’s clipped turns were an execution cost, but not enough to outweigh its clearer comparative path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest exchange was pressing that automatic score cutoffs are an argument against misuse, not against collecting standardized data, then tying the need for a common benchmark to the scale limits of public-university review.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side B built the stronger path by focusing on practical scale constraints in public universities and showing that alternatives default to harder-to-detect privilege signals. CON's training and filter arguments were responsive but never fully refuted the claim that tests remain less unequal than essays/extracurriculars or that removal creates greater informational poverty. B more effectively used pressure responses to narrow the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 on contextualization: test scores enable transcript reading at scale while transcripts alone demand unavailable local knowledge for unfamiliar schools.
