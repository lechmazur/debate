- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins because it successfully prosecuted the debate on two levels: first, constructing a clean institutional-liability-versus-individual-guilt distinction that neutralized CON's "collective punishment" framing; and second, exploiting CON's own first rebuttal concession of "permanent targeted scholarships for verified affected descendants" to demonstrate that CON had already conceded the ethical and practical viability of material remedies. Once CON endorsed targeted scholarships in [B2.3], its earlier arguments that material restitution constitutes collective punishment [B1.3] or a legally impossible diversion of restricted funds [B2.1] were internally self-defeating, and Side A pressed this contradiction effectively across Rebuttal 2 and the closing. CON attempted a late recovery by distinguishing narrow targeted scholarships from "broad community investment," but this reframe arrived too late, was underdeveloped, and in any case the motion as written asks precisely for "long-term scholarships and community investment" rather than the sweeping unaccountable payout CON kept attributing to PRO. CON's strongest surviving arguments were the endpoint metric problem and fiduciary constraints, but Side A answered the metric challenge with a proportional endowment-earnings framework that CON never squarely dismantled. The legal constraints argument had some real force but was blunted by PRO's correct observation that universities raise discretionary capital for non-revenue projects routinely. CON's repetitive "unregulated payout" characterization was increasingly detached from what PRO was actually defending.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 exploitation of CON's [B2.3] concession was the decisive exchange. By pointing out that CON's own endorsement of "permanent targeted scholarships for verified affected descendants" collapsed both the collective-punishment framing and the legal-impossibility argument, Side A shifted the entire debate from whether material remedies are permissible to how they should be structured — terrain far more favorable to the PRO motion. CON never cleanly answered this contradiction, instead retreating to a distinction between targeted scholarships and broad community investment that it had failed to establish clearly in its opening.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's stadium analogy in Rebuttal 2, distinguishing revenue-generating capital projects from non-revenue community payouts, sharply undermined PRO's claim that dedicated fundraising could fully insulate current students from harm.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A wins primarily because it anchored the debate in a concrete, traceable example (Georgetown's 1838 sale) and built a conceptually coherent through-line that CON never neutralized: reparative funds drawn from specifically ill-gotten endowment capital are being returned to those harmed, not diverted from current low-income students. This "ill-gotten gain" distinction is the pivotal clash, and CON's repeated "fungibility" charge never explained why the conceptual distinction between endowment capital tied to a documented sale and general operating budgets is false—it simply asserted the distinction was a fiction. CON's best pressure (the wealthy descendant vs. impoverished immigrant scenario) was sharp and PRO did not fully resolve the edge case, but PRO answered it well enough by showing the source of funds is the ill-gotten gain itself. CON's legal-landscape concern is real post-SFFA but was used purely as a reason to do nothing moral, which PRO correctly identified as hiding behind procedure. CON recycled the same three arguments—fungibility, legal risk, institutional overreach—across nearly every speech without meaningfully advancing them, while PRO progressively narrowed the debate and answered pressure questions directly with labeled A1/A2 responses. CON's closing was largely a restatement of the opening. PRO crystallized the central choice more sharply throughout: keeping profits from stolen labor behind symbolic apologies versus material redress. That framing held up under pressure and CON never dismantled it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was PRO's A4.1 response to CON's fungibility argument. CON had insisted throughout that earmarking endowment funds for lineage-based aid must mathematically reduce general aid. PRO's answer—that returns on capital specifically generated by selling 272 enslaved people belong to those descendants and are being restored, not taken from other students—was conceptually precise and CON never successfully refuted the underlying distinction between ill-gotten capital and general operating funds. This single exchange stripped CON of its core trade-off framing and left its case dependent on exaggerated legal fears and institutional-overreach concerns that PRO had already partially addressed.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins because it successfully framed the issue as repayment of an unpaid debt drawn from ill-gotten endowment gains, not a zero-sum raid on current aid budgets. CON’s practical objections about fungibility, institutional overreach, and legal precarity were each met with plausible rebuttals—most decisively the distinction between returning traced capital and cutting general aid. CON’s alternative of universal need-blind aid never fully answered why that approach repairs the specific intergenerational harm created by the institutions’ documented exploitation, and its pressure-question answers conceded too much ground on the moral core.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 distinction that endowment wealth directly traceable to slavery (e.g., Georgetown’s 1838 sale) can fund targeted reparative programs without cannibalizing universal need-based aid, directly neutralizing CON’s strongest trade-off argument.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A wins by keeping the debate tightly anchored to the motion’s core premise: institutions that profited from specific historical exploitation owe a specific material debt. A effectively neutralized B’s zero-sum framing by distinguishing earmarked returns on ill-gotten capital from general need-based aid budgets, and handled pressure questions directly with the required A1/A2 format. While B raised strong practical concerns about fungibility, legal risk, and institutional overreach, it increasingly pivoted toward a generic defense of need-blind aid rather than squarely answering why documented institutional debt should be settled with symbolic gestures alone. A’s clearer comparative weighing and sharper rhetorical crystallization of the unpaid debt versus performative apology clash gave it the decisive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over endowment fungibility versus earmarked reparative funds. A’s Rebuttal 2 directly answered B’s pressure by framing the scholarships as returning specific ill-gotten gains rather than cannibalizing general aid, effectively dismantling B’s central zero-sum premise and reframing the trade-off as moral repayment rather than budgetary theft.
