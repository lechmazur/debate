### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON identified the decisive structural problem in PRO's case early and never let go of it: PRO's flagship evidence (Houston, Edmonton) documents what happens when minimums are removed and developers exercise flexibility, not what happens under a binding maximum. CON's "ceiling either doesn't bind or blocks genuine demand" dilemma (sharpened in B2.1, B4.4) was never resolved by PRO, whose "generous calibrated cap with exceptions" answer in Rebuttal 2 effectively conceded the dilemma by collapsing into CON's own flexibility model. CON's pressure questions forced this concession, and CON's answers to PRO's pressure questions (A1/A2 on repeal vs. mandate, and on cap risk being conditional rather than universal) were more precise and consistent with its case than PRO's replies. PRO's case remained forceful on cost and sprawl but repeatedly reused the same examples without resolving the equivocation CON exposed, and PRO also suffered two clipped speeches (opening and Rebuttal 1) versus CON's one clipped closing, a modest additional execution cost. CON's closing crystallized the clash cleanly with "its own example defeats its case," landing the debate's most memorable line.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.2/B2.3 rebuttal exposing that PRO's Houston/Edmonton evidence supports removing minimums (flexibility), not imposing maximums (binding caps) — a distinction PRO never cleanly resolved and which CON reinforced decisively in B4.3-B4.4 by showing PRO's "generous calibrated cap" answer concedes the underlying dilemma.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON successfully established that PRO's empirical examples (Houston, Edmonton) demonstrated the success of eliminating minimums and allowing flexibility, not the necessity of imposing maximums. CON's "binding or useless" dilemma exposed the central weakness in PRO's case: a cap either fails to change behavior or actively strands households in car-dependent regions where alternatives do not yet exist. CON further showed that alternative tools—unbundling parking, form-based codes, lot-coverage limits, and curb pricing—could achieve PRO's goals of affordability and density without the coercive downside. PRO never adequately resolved why a ceiling was required "even in car-dependent regions" rather than simply removing floors.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's takedown of PRO's Houston and Edmonton examples by showing they involved eliminating minimums rather than imposing maximums, thereby undermining PRO's empirical support and accidentally supporting CON's flexibility model (B2.1-B2.3, reinforced in B4.3 and B5.2).
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON won the central necessity comparison. PRO proved minimums cause overbuild, but CON showed removing minimums + unbundling already fixes cross-subsidy, making a maximum either non-binding or harmful. CON's dilemma [A2.1] and turn of Houston/Edmonton as evidence for flexibility not caps went largely unanswered, while PRO conceded spillover requires new permits/meters [B2.3] and never established a unique benefit of forbidding voluntarily financed parking. Responses to pressure questions favored CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A R1 [A2.1-A2.3] calling ceiling a legal prohibition and turning Houston/Edmonton: eliminating minimums explains oversupply and predicts voluntary reductions, so PRO's best examples support CON flexibility, leaving PRO with no independent case for a binding maximum.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Both sides built plausible, well-grounded cases, but A more successfully resolved the central dilemma that B itself raised in Pressure Questions: "if a cap never binds it does nothing; if it binds it causes shortage." A answered this directly by arguing caps should bind only at the margin (excess/multi-car capacity) while accommodating ordinary demand, and turned B's "floors are calibrated to genuine need" claim back on itself by asking why planners can supposedly forecast floors accurately but not ceilings — a symmetry B never squarely answered in Rebuttal 2, instead repeating that binding caps "guarantee shortage" without engaging the marginal-binding framing. A also landed a sharper reversal on the equity/vulnerability point (minimums force car-free tenants to subsidize multi-car households; unbundling/assignment, not floors, actually gets a worker a space) that B's response ("the car-free are a tiny minority") did not fully neutralize. B's case remained intuitive and concrete (spillover onto narrow streets, surface-lot repurposing, luxury-auction fear) and its Pressure Questions were sharp, but its final rebuttal and closing largely reasserted the same spillover/undersupply frame rather than resolving A's marginal-binding and knowledge-symmetry challenges. Both openings/closings suffered minor word-limit clipping, roughly offsetting as an execution cost. Overall A gave the cleaner, more resolved path to a verdict.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 response to B's Pressure Question dilemma ("A cap should bind only at the margin—preventing hypothetical peaks or redundant multi-car demand—without eliminating normal one-car access") combined with turning the floor-vs-ceiling knowledge problem back on B was the most decisive exchange, since B's Closing never squarely answered why a "modest, calibrated" floor is knowable while an analogous "context-sensitive" ceiling is not.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO established the decisive asymmetry: minimums compel standardized overbuilding and force car-free households to subsidize drivers, while context-sensitive maximums let developers meet ordinary demand up to a ceiling that only prevents excess. CON’s vulnerability and spillover attacks were blunted when PRO showed that minimums do not guarantee vulnerable workers a space and that hiding curb demand in every building is inferior to direct management. PRO’s rebuttals were sharper, its pressure answers more direct, and its closing crystallized the clash more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s flip of CON’s equity argument—showing that minimums bury parking costs in everyone’s rent and subsidize wealthier multi-car households, rather than guaranteeing early-shift workers access—undermined CON’s claim that floors protect the vulnerable.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO wins the core asymmetry. CON's shortage/spillover and luxury-auction fears are answered by calibrated high caps that bind only on peak/multi-car excess while preserving normal access, with simple curb tools. CON never solves PRO's pressure: a minimum does not guarantee the early-shift worker a space (assignment does) but does guarantee everyone pays for oversupply, and the same data used to set a modest floor can set a high ceiling. Overbuild cost locked into rents/land-use outweighs visible, revisable underbuild.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.2/B5.4 — minimum does not guarantee assignment, only cost; unbundled assignment under a high maximum protects ordinary demand while preventing forced subsidy for multi-car households, flipping CON's equity claim.
